# 对程序缺陷视而不见｜Bug blindness

---

## 正文

我以前常常纳闷，为什么我看到的 bugs 比大多数人多那么多。我很容易就能观察到[每周数百到数千个 bugs](https://danluu.com/everything-is-broken/)，而且[似乎没有什么能 work](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/)，但和我交谈的大多数人都看不到[任何类似的情况](https://danluu.com/customer-service/)。很长一段时间，我以为这与我如何 use computers 有关，但随着时间推移，我意识到，主要原因是人们也在 hitting 同样的 bugs，只是没有注意到。

如果你不是 programmer，这也许是一种更好的看待世界的方式，但我认为，消除 quality/bug blindness 对 programmers 有帮助。我已经在很多朋友和熟人身上做到过这一点（只是通过指出 bugs）。几周之后，愿意留意的人往往也会开始注意到 bugs。

因为我会注意到这类事情，在我做过的好几份工作中，directors/VPs/execs/etc. 有时想从一个相对更可能注意到 issues 的人那里听到真实意见，就会请我 evaluate 某个东西（必要时还会 fix 这些 issues，或推动针对它们的 fixes）。有时我找不到任何 issues（很可能存在 issues，只是不属于我会注意到的那一类）。更多时候，我发现的 issues 介于“mild”和“moderate”之间。而有时，issues 是 severe 的，甚至可以说那个东西实际上根本不 work。

我觉得最后这一类有点令人费解，因为当我查找关于那个东西如何变成这种状态的讨论时，通常会看到一连串 internal comments，表示它很棒、works well，等等；但当我 open 它并试用时，却发现只有采用相当多 non-intuitive workarounds，它才会 works。十有八九，normal user 不仅无法 use 它，还会遇到糟糕得令人啼笑皆非或怒不可遏的体验，以至于要把这件事告诉朋友。

这篇文章我可能已经酝酿了十年左右，但一直迟疑着没有写出来，因为我心里始终有个疑问：我是否在不知不觉中，以某种方式 triggering 了大多数 users 都不会 hit 的奇怪 corner case behavior？但看到越来越多 product 在 launches 后，因为 users 遇到和我发现的完全相同的 issues 而惨遭失败，我不再认为，一般来说，我之所以 hitting bugs，是因为我做了 normal user 不会做的异常操作。如果一个 product 在我 use 它时显得 severely flawed，那它大概确实如此。而凭借 LLMs 的魔力，如今我甚至可以让 LLMs 在很多方面表现得像 normal users，并表明这些 issues 会在许多不同的 scenarios 中 reproduce。

### 几个例子

我不想列举那些以检查某个东西 worked 得有多好为本职工作的具体例子，因为即使这些 internal examples 的本意是建设性的、不追究责任的，在对外转发时，读者也不一定会这样理解，所以我会举几个没那么有趣、佐证也没那么充分的“随手”例子。

前段时间，我[写出了几次 web search queries 的 results](https://danluu.com/seo-spam/)，发现 Google、Bing 和 Kagi 给出的 results 都很差。总体而言，主要 search engines 都未能为这些 queries return 好的 results，而是 returned 满是 low-quality SEO spam 的 pages，以及一些实际上属于 scams 的 sites。顺便说一下，按上文提到的 scale，我会将这视为“moderate”，而不是“severe”（severe 大概是指 search engine 有一半时间 returns 500 errors、大多数 results 都是 scams，等等；我对 severe 的门槛是 normal user 很可能完全无法 use 那个东西，而不只是体验糟糕）。几乎没有人[1](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:R)反对我对 Google 和 Bing search results 的描述，但有人告诉我，我对 Kagi 的判断错了。有些人把他们实际得到的 search results 发给了我。每一次，我都没能在这些 search results 中看到一个好的 result（例如，对于 seasonal forecast query，search 没能 return 最新的 seasonal forecast；对于 tire result，没有一个 result 给出了 correct explanation，等等），而且里面充斥着 SEO spam。有一次，一个人把自己的 Kagi filters 列表和得到的 search results 都发给了我，并没有断言这些 results 是好是坏；但其他人通常坚持说 results 很好，尽管这些 results 既未能 link 到 matching result，又充斥着 spam。例外是 user 做了诸如将 GitHub pin 到 results 顶部这样的操作；对于目标是 download 在 GitHub 上 hosted 的 software 的 queries，这样做确实 worked，但对于文章中的其他 queries，当然就完全 fails 了，因为你不太可能在 GitHub 上找到你所在地区的 seasonal forecast，或关于 tire mechanics 的 correct explanaton。

从一般道理上说，我理解人们往往会看不到自己喜爱之物的缺点。例如，我在[看到 Volvo 在 out-of-sample crash tests 中的表现](https://danluu.com/car-safety/)后买了一辆 Volvo，因此有时会到 Volvo car forums 上 search，寻找自己问题的答案。十多年来，现有的 reliability data 一直表明（而且我认为，维修 Volvos 的 mechanics 所积累的 anecdotal experience 也支持这一点），Volvo reliability 处于 mediocre 到 poor 的水平；但当然，Volvo forums 上到处都是坚持认为 Volvos 属于最 reliable 的 cars、而 data 全都错了的人。

一个可能更贴近主题的例子是 Blackboard（course management software）。当年它还是 universities 用于 coursework 最广泛的 software 时，students 和 professors 都普遍不喜欢它。我认为，说它是我社交圈中最普遍遭人嫌弃的 software，并不为过（还有一些更遭人痛恨的 software，例如 Visual Source Safe，但这些 software 的使用范围都不够广，因而总体上并不是最普遍遭人嫌弃的）。Wikipedia page 写道

> Blackboard 已经成为“education 领域最不受欢迎——甚至最遭人厌恶——的 companies 之一。”

还写道

> 2011 年 12 月，Fast Company 报道，Amplicate customer opinion survey 的 respondents 中，有 93%“憎恨”这家 company。

当年我年轻得多，说话也没那么有分寸，有一次碰到一位在 Blackboard 工作的人，没多想就傻乎乎地脱口问了句类似于“做这个这么多人都不喜欢的 software，是什么感受？”的话。幸好，对方一点也没生气，反而真的感到困惑，因为他们认为这是一款广受喜爱、users 非常喜欢的 software。他们不太相信我说的可能是真的，于是我说了些话，表示只是我自己搞错了，随后我们便转向了别的话题。当时我年轻得多，也更天真，听说这款在我社交圈中大概最普遍遭人嫌弃的 software，在我遇到的那一位该公司 employee 看来（想必在其他 employees 看来也一样），竟是一款非常受欢迎的 software，我着实感到惊讶。

我能理解 Volvo forums 为什么会变成那样，因为[如今 cars 总体上已经足够 reliable，以至于人们通常不会遇到 car breakdowns](https://www.patreon.com/posts/106839697)，所以很容易有人这样想：“data 不可能是对的；毕竟，我的 car 从来没有 broken down。”更让我费解的是，一个人怎么能看着一组满是 spam 的 search results，随后飞快发出一条消息，解释这些 results 有多棒，即便他们确实是某个 search engine 的拥趸；又或者，一个人怎么会认为 users 普遍喜爱一款以不受欢迎而闻名的 software——我和每一个人谈起它，对方都会告诉我它有多糟糕（而且往往是主动抱怨），有新闻报道专门讨论人们有多不喜欢它，甚至连它的 Wikipedia page 都提到了这种近乎一致的反感。另一个类似 Blackboard 的例子，可能是 Discourse（forum software）的 web performance；[这篇文章](https://danluu.com/slow-device/)的灵感之一，就来自我与 Discourse employees 的讨论，他们认为 Discourse 拥有出色的 performance。我觉得这个例子很有意思，因为 Discourse 里面实际上存在会 slowed down 实际 page loads 的 code，[目的是在 LCP 等 web performance metrics 上 cheat](https://danluu.com/slow-device/#appendix-gaming-lcp)。这远远超出了[只是针对 benchmark 进行 optimizing](https://danluu.com/car-safety/)的程度，已经上升为真正的 cheating，不仅对 user 毫无益处，反而还 harmed 了 user。在某个层面上，implementing 这种 cheating、并指导 users 如何避免意外 subvert 这种 cheating 的 programmers，肯定知道自己的 app 的实际 performance 很差，但人们很容易在这类事情周围筑起心理屏障。

到如今，我不会说自己还感到惊讶，因为这种事情我见得太多，反倒是它不发生时，我才会觉得意外；但我仍然好奇，这类事情发生时，那个人脑子里究竟在想什么。

举一个非 programming 的例子，我们此前曾[在这篇讨论人们如何对“显而易见”的 facts 持有不同 perspectives 的文章中](https://danluu.com/impossible-agree/)提到，有一位 basketball player，从主观评价来看，普遍被认为是他那个 era 中最 dirtiest 的 player。NBA 不会 track 衡量 player dirtiness 的 objective measures，但他似乎在各种 measures 上都遥遥领先。例如，尽管 genital strikes 和 1950 年以前的 rebounds 一样，并不是 officially tracked stat，但他无疑保持着本世纪 punching、kicking、kneeing 或以其他方式 striking players 的 genitals 的 record（按 era-adjusted numbers 来算，他也应当保持 record；不过，由于 80s 和 90s 的 play 总体上 dirtier 得多，他也有可能并不保持 all-time record）。在讨论中，他所属 team 的大多数 fans 似乎都注意不到这一点；当这个 player strikes 其他 players 的 genitals 时，team 的 fans 对此浑然不觉，还为他的 contortions 辩护，以至于“natural rebounding motion”和“natural shooting motion”这两个说法都成了反复出现的笑话。

平均而言，人类非常擅长忽视自己喜爱之物的负面之处，其中也包括（而且往往尤其包括）自己的工作或自己 company 所做的工作。不知这是好事还是坏事，我似乎恰好相反，思绪会立刻转向我自己和自己工作中的 flaws。有好几次，有人因为一篇博客文章给我发消息，说些类似于“要是有人批评你的工作，你会怎么想？”或“要是有人说你的工作不好，你会怎么想？”的话。对于前者，我的想法是，我费了很大力气，才从那些能在我的 reasoning 中挑出漏洞的人那里获得批评，所以只要有人对我的工作提出哪怕稍微合理的批评，那都是件很棒的事。至于后者，我通常认为自己的工作充满了 major flaws，所以，呃，是啊，说它不好似乎很合理。我认为自己的工作中有些具体方面很有趣或不错，但总体而言，我不确定自己会把做过的任何一件事评为好。我不是说自己没有 blind spots，但我认为，在这个特定的 blind spot 上，我比大多数人稍微不那么容易中招[2](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:B)。

### 习惯性的 mitigations

如果回想我自己曾有过的类似 blind spots，有一件事立刻浮现在脑海里：小时候，我的一个朋友用了我的 computer。要理解这个故事，你得知道，那还是 [mechanical mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse#Mechanical_mice) 的年代。随着时间推移，碎屑会粘在 mouse ball 上，除非把它清理掉，否则就会导致它 track erratically。

朋友尝试用我的 computer 时，发现 mouse 根本没法用，因为 mouse pointer movement 看起来几乎是 random。我重新坐到 computer 前用 mouse 时，却完全没有任何问题，但当我留意自己是怎样用手 smoothly move 那个 pointer，让它沿 straight line 移动时，才发现我的手正在到处剧烈甩动。我意识到，随着碎屑逐渐积累在 mouse ball 上，我一定也在逐渐适应它，并且不知怎么地，通过做出 countervailing erratic movements 来 compensating for 这个 mouse 的 extremely erratic tracking[3](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:D)。我竟然能完全没注意到自己在这么做，这让我觉得很不可思议，也让我一直怀疑，自己如今是否也在做某种类似的事。

我有时会想起自己为了 work around bugs 而养成的所有 mitigations。例如，以前 opening 一个新的 Google Doc 时，我会立刻把想要的 title 填进 doc。不知从什么时候起，可能是十年前左右，Google Docs 加入了某种 delay，使得你刚 open 那个 doc 后，在 title box 中所做的 typing 会被 overwritten。于是我现在养成了这样的习惯：opening 一个 Google Doc 后，先做点别的事，然后再 change 那个 title。随着 Google Docs 不断加入更多 features，我也逐渐养成了一系列习惯，用来 avoid 各种 pitfalls（比如在“错误”的时机尝试 search，结果得到的是毫无用处的 native browser search，而不是 Google Docs search）。

我的感觉是，computer literacy 和 software literacy 中很大一部分，就是积累大量这类你会在 non-conscious level 自然而然做出的习惯。这些习惯往往非常依赖具体情境，比如我在 Microsoft 工作时养成的一个习惯：在 logging in 之前，先把 laptop 的 WiFi switch 拨到 off（我注意到其他人也会这么做）。这是因为有某个 service，经常会让你的 login fail，并提示“目前没有可用的 logon servers 来 service 这个 logon request”。但如果那个 service 根本无法 connect，这项 check 就会被 bypassed，你就可以直接 log in。

### Quality blindness（质量盲视）

这样的例子足以写满一篇文章，不过回到本文的主题，大家经常建议的一种尝试克服 quality blindness 的办法，是让人们 dogfood 自己的 software。On average，这比不 dogfooding 好得多，但它能发挥作用的前提，是人们没有摸索出（然后又忘记自己摸索过）能够 work around 那个 software 的各种 issues 的习惯。On average，programmers 很擅长 working around software foibles（在 pre-LLM 时代，要成为 effective programmer，你就必须有这种本事），所以如果不留心，programmers 很容易注意不到这类 issues。

反过来说，要让一个 app 对人们而言 easy to use，很大一部分似乎就是让这些古怪习惯变得没有必要。虽然听起来这应该很容易做到，但从我见过的人们尝试对此提供 feedback 的情况来看，大多数 developers 的本能反应似乎是：“啊？做 X 很容易啊，只要做 \[一套 complex sequence，其中的事情，任何普通人如果之前没有多次用过这个 app，除非有人专门向他们解释过，或他们见过别人这么做，否则根本不会想到\]”，或者：“啊？你没看到吗？在你 execute 第 261 页 Appendix B 中的 steps 之后，这方面的 instructions 已经清楚地写在 manual 的第 43 页了啊？”

话虽如此，我认为让人们摆脱 quality blindness 是做得到的，因为我已经做成过不少次。我觉得，只有当对方愿意接受时，这才真正有效，因为人们有无穷的能力去 willful blindness；但在对方愿意接受的情况下，仅仅指出那些他们没注意到的 issues，似乎就管用。几年甚至十年以后，有时还会有人告诉我，他们现在看哪里都有 bugs。

我认为这值得做，是因为我见过有着严重 quality blindness 的个人和 teams，ship 出来的东西因为 product quality issues，success 的机会大打折扣，甚至毫无机会[4](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:C)。明知且刻意地 trade off quality for speed[5](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:P) 是一回事，但在我见过的这些情况里，总是存在着一种 quality blindness：参与 project 的每个人都以为自己正在 shipping 非常 high quality 的东西，实际却并非如此。

这件事从来都不是无关紧要的，但随着 coding agents 的出现，它变得更加重要，因为虽然 churn out low quality software 比以往任何时候都容易，improve quality 也同样比以往任何时候都容易，无论是 [better performance](https://danluu.com/no-slow/)、[fewer bugs](https://danluu.com/ai-coding/)，还是其他方面。

但要做到这一点，你必须 [actually notice](https://mastodon.social/@slightlyoff@toot.cafe/117145660980388794) 这是可能的，也就是 [quality 可以被 improved](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/)。

### Appendix：对 advertising 的 blindness

Michael Malis（[Freshpaint](https://www.freshpaint.io/?utm_source=dl) 的 founder 和前 CEO）指出（这是在消息里说的，所以采用了消息式的格式）：

> 说个类似但又有所不同的 data point——在 advertising 方面，我也见过类似的 blindness。向别人解释 Freshpaint 时，我会告诉他们，我们帮助 hospitals 做 marketing
> 
> 我经常被问到的一个问题是，hospitals 为什么要做 marketing。奇怪的是，只要你留意，就会发现 hospitals 做了大量 marketing
> 
> 在 SF，到处都有 ucsf/sutter health/stanford 和各种 treatments 的 bus ads 与 billboards

这与 Michael 的评论和本文都不是同一个话题，不过我想说，我和不少完全不相信 ads 有效的人聊过；但在我曾工作过的一家大公司，我和一个我信任其 data methodology 与 judgement 的人聊过 ads A/B testing，在另一家公司，我也亲自看过 data。我认为，在那些情况下，支持 ads 提供 real lift（远远超出 ad 的 cost）的 causal evidence 很强。在我亲自查看的那个案例里，他们做了一次 geo-segmented A/B test，在一些 geos 买 ads，在其他 geos 则不买（这是在 worldwide 范围内进行的，regions 是 U.S. states、Canadian provinces 等）。之所以采用这种 geo-segmentation，是因为即使有 cross-device tracking，也无法 100% 确定某个人是否曾 exposed to an ad（当然，采用这种 segmentation 也仍然存在这个问题；我更倾向于采用更 clustered to population areas 的 segmentation，避免 splits 使人们相对容易从 boundary 的一侧到另一侧，例如通勤。不过，这类 contamination 通常意味着，可能的 true lift 比 estimated lift 更高），因此人们有时会做这些 geo-segmented A/B tests。

总之，在这些 A/B tests 中，仅凭 direct revenue gain，return on ad spend 就已经相当不错，而且 users 也有 gain，这似乎很可能在以后带来更多 revenue（后续的 revenue 没有被 analyzed）。我不了解一般而言的 ad effectiveness，也不知道你的具体 ads 是否 effective，但那种[被反复提起的观点](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/)，即 ads 总体上不起作用，在我看来似乎是错的。

回到 Michael 评论的话题，我认为 programmers 很容易注意不到 ads。我认识的 programmers 几乎全都用 ad blocker，而在现实生活中，他们的目光似乎也会直接掠过 ads，注意不到它们。我能理解这会怎样助长 ads 不起作用的想法。到底谁会看这些玩意儿？但从我与“普通人”的交往，以及我在 Google 工作时熟悉的 data 来看，很多人，或许是大多数人，甚至没有意识到许多 ads 就是 ads。当他们做 Google search，并 click 最上面的 resut 时，往往根本不知道，自己看到的并不是 Google“认为”的 best link，而是谁向 Google 付了最多钱来 buy 那个 ad slot，谁的 link 就出现在那里。

Em Chu 谈一种习惯性的 bug mitigation：

> 我相信你能为这一节搜集无穷的例子，但我就是想抱怨一下：在 waking up 并 unlocking 我的 laptop（mac）时，很容易让它进入一种“awake”却 unusable 的 state（black screen 上只有 cursor，或者类似情况），而且只能通过实际合上盖子再重新打开来 fixed。为了 work around 这个问题，我想自己通常会在 screen 亮起后等一秒，操作一下 trackpad，然后再 unlock 它。不过说实话，这大多是在潜意识里发生的；而且考虑到我每个月还是会 hit 这个 bug 几次，我显然还需要练习。

读到这里，我检查了自己是怎样打开 laptop 的，才意识到，为了 working around 其他 laptop bugs，我也养成了一些古怪的习惯。这里提到的具体 bug 并不会在我的 laptop 上 reproduce，而且看来，我可以停止那种为以前某台 laptop 形成的习惯性 mitigation 了。

## 原文

I used to wonder why I see so many more bugs than most people. I easily observe [hundreds to thousands of bugs per week](https://danluu.com/everything-is-broken/) and [nothing seems to work](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/), but most people I talk to don't see [anything like this](https://danluu.com/customer-service/). For a long time, I thought this had something to do with how I use computers but, over time, I've realized that it's mostly that people are hitting the same bugs and don't notice.

If you're not a programmer, that's probably a better way to see the world, but I think curing quality/bug blindness is helpful for programmers. I've done this with a lot of friends and acquaintances (just by pointing out bugs). After a few weeks, people who are so inclined tend to start noticing bugs as well.

Because I notice these kinds of things, I've had multiple jobs where directors/VPs/execs/etc. sometimes ask me to evaluate something when they want an actual opinion from someone who is relatively likely to notice issues (and fix them or drive fixes for them if necessary). Sometimes I won't find any issues (there are likely issues that just aren't the kind I notice). More often, I find issues that fall somewhere from "mild" to "moderate". And, sometimes, the issues are severe, to the point where one might even say the thing actually doesn't work.

I find this last category a bit mysterious, as when I look up discussions on how the thing got into this state, there's usually a stream of internal comments indicating that the thing is great, it works well, etc., but when I open up the thing and try it, it's in a state where the thing only works if you do quite a few non-intuitive workarounds. More likely than not, not only would a normal user not be able to use the thing, they'd have such a hilariously/infuriatingly bad experience that they'd tell their friends.

I've had this post in mind for maybe a decade or so, but I was hesitant to write it up because, in the back of my mind, I always wondered if I'm somehow triggering weird corner case behavior most users don't hit without realizing it. But after seeing more and more cases where the product launches and falls flat on its face because users run into the exact same issues I saw, I don't think that, in general, I'm hitting bugs because I'm doing unusual things a normal user wouldn't do. If a product seems severely flawed when I use it, it probably is. And with the magic of LLMs, nowadays, I can even have LLMs act like normal users in a lot of ways and show that the issues reproduce across many different scenarios.

### A few examples

I don't want to give any specific examples where it was my job to see how well the thing worked because, even if the internal examples are meant in a constructive, blameless, way, they may not always read that way when re-posted externally, so I'll give a few less interesting and less well supported "random" examples.

A while ago, I [wrote up the results of some web search queries](https://danluu.com/seo-spam/) and found poor results from Google, Bing and Kagi. In general, the major search engines failed to return good results for the queries and returned pages full of low-quality SEO spam as well as some sites that were actually scams. BTW, on the scale mentioned above, I would consider this "moderate" and not "severe" (severe would be something like, the search engine returns 500 errors half the time, the majority of results are scams, etc; my bar for severe is that a normal user likely won't be able to use the thing at all, not that they have a bad experience). Almost nobody[1](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:R) objected to my characterization of Google and Bing search results, but people told me that I was wrong about Kagi. In some cases, people sent me their actual search results. In every such case, the search results did not contain a good result that I could see (e.g., for the seasonal forecast query, the search failed to return an up-to-date seasonal forecast, for the tire result, there was no result with a correct explanation, etc.) and was full of SEO spam. In one case, a person passed me both their list of Kagi filters as well as the search results they got without making claims that the results were good or bad, but people generally insisted the results were good even though the results both failed to link to a matching result and were full of spam except in cases where the user did something like pin GitHub to the top of their results, which worked for the queries where the goal was to download software that's hosted on GitHub, but of course completely fails for the other queries from the post as you're unlikely to get your regional seasonal forecast or the correct explanaton of tire mechanics on GitHub.

In the abstract, I get that people who are fans of things tend to be blind to the thing's faults. For example, since I bought a Volvo after [seeing how they do in out-of-sample crash tests](https://danluu.com/car-safety/), I sometimes search for answers to my questions on Volvo car forums. For well over a decade, the reliability data that exists (and I think this is backed up by the anecdotal experience that mechanics who work on Volvos have) is that Volvo reliability is mediocre to poor, but of course Volvo forums are full of people who insist that Volvos are among the most reliable cars and that the data are all wrong.

An example that might be more central to the topic is Blackboard (the course management software). Back when it was the most widely used software by universities for coursework, the software was widely disliked by both students and professors. I think it would be fair to say that it was the most widely disliked software in my social circles (there was more strongly disliked software, like Visual Source Safe, but any more strongly disliked software wasn't widely used enough to be the most widely disliked overall). The Wikipedia page notes

> Blackboard had become "one of the most disliked — even detested — companies in education."

as well as

> In December 2011, Fast Company reported that 93% of respondents to the Amplicate customer opinion survey "hate" the company.

Back when I was much younger and had less of a filter, I ran into someone who worked at Blackboard and, without thinking, I stupidly blurted out something like "what's it like to work on this software that so many people dislike?". Luckily, the person I was talking to wasn't offended at all and, instead, they were actually confused because they thought it was widely loved software that users really liked. They didn't really believe what I said could be true and I made some comment indicating that it was just confusion on my part and then the conversation continued in a different direction. At the time, as someone much younger and more naive, I was really surprised to hear that the software that was probably the most widely disliked software in my social circles was thought to be really well-liked software by the one employee from the company I met (and, presumably other employees as well).

I can understand how the Volvo forums get to be how they are, in that [cars are reliable enough in general now that people generally don't experience car breakdowns](https://www.patreon.com/posts/106839697), so it's easy for someone to think something like "the data can't be right; after all, my car has never broken down". It's more of a mystery to me how somebody can look at a set of search results that are full of spam and then dash off a message explaining how great the results are, even if they're a fan of a particular search engine or how someone can think that users generally love software that's famous for being disliked, to the point that every single person I talk to about it tells me how bad it is (often in unprompted complaints), there are news articles that discuss how much people dislike the software, and the near-universal dislike for the software is mentioned on its Wikipedia page. Another Blackboard-like example might be Discourse (forum software) web performance, where one of the inspirations [for this post](https://danluu.com/slow-device/) was discussions with Discourse employees who thought that Discourse had great performance. I found that one interesting because Discourse actually had code in it that slowed down actual page loads [in order to cheat on web performance metrics like LCP](https://danluu.com/slow-device/#appendix-gaming-lcp). That went well beyond [just optimizing for a benchmark](https://danluu.com/car-safety/) and rose to the level of actual cheating that not only had no benefit to the user, it actually harmed the user. At some level, the programmers implementing that sort of cheating and advising users on how to not accidentally subvert the cheating must know that the actual performance of their app is poor, but it's very easy for people to put up mental barriers around this kind of thing.

By now, I wouldn't say that I'm surprised because I've seen this kind of thing enough that I would actually consider it surprising if it didn't happen, but I still wonder what's going on inside someone's head when something like this happens.

For a non-programming example, we previously noted [in this post on how people have different perspectives on "obvious" facts](https://danluu.com/impossible-agree/), there's a basketball player who, subjectively, is generally considered to be the dirtiest player of his era. The NBA doesn't track objective measures of player dirtiness, but he seems dominant on a wide variety of measures. For exampe, although, like rebounds before 1950, genital strikes aren't an officially tracked stat, he surely holds the record for punching, kicking, kneeing, or otherwise striking players in the genitals this century (he should also hold the record for era-adjusted numbers, but it's possible that he doesn't have the all-time record due to play being much dirtier overall in the 80s and 90s). In discussions, most fans of his team don't seem to notice this and the phrases "natural rebounding motion" and "natural shooting motion" have become running jokes from how oblivious the team's fans are when they justify this player's contortions when he strikes other players in the genitals.

On average, humans have a high ability to ignore negatives in things they're a fan of, including (and often especially) their own work or work their company does. For better or for worse, I seem to have the opposite of this and my thoughts immediately go to the flaws in myself and my work. A number of times, as a result of a blog post, someone has messaged me with something like "how would you like it if someone criticized your work?" or "how would you like it if someone said your work isn't good?" To the former, my thought is that I go to great lengths to get criticism from people who can poke holes in my reasoning, so it's pretty awesome if someone has remotely reasonable criticism of my work. And to the latter, I generally think my work is full of major flaws, so, uhh, yeah, it seems pretty reasonable to say it isn't good. There are particular aspects of my work that I think are interesting or good but, overall, I don't know that I'd rate anything I've done as good. I'm not saying I don't have blind spots, but I think I'm a bit less prone to this particular one than most people[2](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:B).

### Habitual mitigations

If I think about analogous blind spots I've had, one that jumps out at me is from when I was a little kid and a friend of mine used my computer. For this story to make sense, you have to know that this was in the [mechanical mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse#Mechanical_mice) era. Over time, detritus would get stuck to your mouse ball and cause it to track erratically unless you cleaned it out.

When my friend tried to use my computer he found it impossible to use the mouse because mouse pointer movement seemed almost random. When I sat down at the computer again and used the mouse I didn't have any problem using it at all, but on looking at what I was doing with my hand to smoothly move the pointer in a straight line, I was violently throwing my hand all over the place. I realized I must've adjusted to the detritus on the mouse ball over time as it accumulated and I was somehow compensating for the mouse's extremely erratic tracking by making countervailing erratic movements[3](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:D). I thought it was pretty amazing that I could not notice that I was doing this and I always wonder if I'm doing some equivalent thing today.

I sometimes think about all of the mitigations I've developed to work around bugs. For example, when opening a new Google Doc, I used to immediately put the title I wanted into the doc. At some point, maybe ten years ago or so, Google Docs added some kind of delay such that the typing you do into the title box right after you open the doc gets overwritten, so I now have this habit where, after opening a Google Doc, I do something else and then I change the title. Over time, as Google Docs has had more and more features added, I've developed a series of habits that avoid all sorts of pitfalls (such as trying to search at the "wrong" time and getting the useless native browser search instead of the Google Docs search).

My feeling is that a large fraction of computer literacy and software literacy is developing a large library of these habits that you just do at a non-conscious level. These are often quite specific to the situation, such as a habit I developed when I worked at Microsoft of flipping my laptop's WiFi switch to off before logging in (which I noticed other people doing as well). This was because there was some service, which would often fail your login with "There are currently no logon servers available to service the logon request”. But if that service couldn't connect at all, the check would be bypassed and you could just log in.

### Quality blindness

We could fill a post up with examples like that, but back to the main topic of the post, one commonly suggested way to try to overcome quality blindness is to have people dogfood their own software. On average, this is a lot better than not dogfooding, but it only works to the extent that people don't figure out (and then forget about) habits that work around whatever issues the software has. On average, programmers are pretty good at working around software foibles (you had to be in order to be an effective programmer pre-LLM), so it's very easy for programmers to not notice these kinds of issues if they're not paying attention.

On the flip side, a large part of making an app easy for people to use seems to mean making weird habits like these unnecessary. Although this sounds like it should be easy to do, from having seen people try to give feedback about this kind of thing, the reflexive reaction of most developers seems to be "huh? It's easy to do X, just do \[complex sequence of things that no normal person would think of if they hadn't used the app many times before unless it was specifically explained to them or they saw someone else do it\]" or "huh? Didn't you see that the instructions for this are clearly laid out in page 43 of the manual after you execute the steps in Appendix B on page 261?".

That being said, I think curing people of quality blindness is do-able because I've done it quite a few times. I think this only really works when the person is receptive, as people have infinite capacity for willful blindness but, in cases where people are receptive, just pointing out issues they didn't notice seems to work. Years or even a decade later, people will sometimes tell me they see bugs everywhere now.

The reason I think this is worth doing is that I've seen people and teams with a high degree of quality blindness ship things that have reduced or even no chance of success because of product quality issues[4](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:C). It's one thing to knowingly and deliberately trade off quality for speed[5](https://danluu.com/bug-blind/#fn:P), but when I've seen this happen there's always been a kind of quality blindness where everyone involved with the project thinks they're shipping something very high quality when that's not the case.

This has never been unimportant, but it's gotten more important with coding agents because, while it's easier than ever to churn out low quality software, it's also easier than ever to improve quality, whether that's [better performance](https://danluu.com/no-slow/), [fewer bugs](https://danluu.com/ai-coding/), etc.

But, to do this, you have to [actually notice](https://mastodon.social/@slightlyoff@toot.cafe/117145660980388794) that this is possible, that [quality can be improved](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/).
