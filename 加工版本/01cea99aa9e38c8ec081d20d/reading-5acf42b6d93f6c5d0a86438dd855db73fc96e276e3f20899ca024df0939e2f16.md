## 2026

## 为什么要写 book 或 essay，而不是制作 videos、reels 等？（2026 年 5 月 26 日）

_就一个我偶尔会重新思考的问题，写给自己的一则笔记。_

这是个好问题。Videos 和 reels _确实是_一种绝妙的 media form，既 powerful，又极其有趣。的确，你可以拿一个还算有趣的 script，layer in 一些相当 generic 的 visuals 和 audio，对大多数人（包括我）来说，它就会比单独的 script 有趣得多。我们享受这种 texture，也从中收获很多。而且，videos 和 reels 的 distribution 好得多，因此被数百万人看到的可能性也高得多。

那么，writing 应当扮演什么 role？

在某些方面，Writing 是更适合 thinking 的 medium。如果我想 develop 好的或 original 的 creative ideas，writing 会极其有帮助。Video 和 reels 也可以这样使用，但据我理解，那会更慢，也更困难。如果 distribution 是一个 goal，那么你_最终_或许会使用 writing 之外的 channels；但如果主要的 goal 是有值得说的东西（而不是成为 Mr. Beast），writing 就是一种极好的 medium。当然，它不是唯一的方式，但你确实需要以世上某种深入的活动为根基，而不是仅仅为了 social media 而做 social media。（或者，你可以把 [hacking 那个 system](https://drive.google.com/file/d/11dV24Mda_kNEBCLtPtBomWG4IkngvQKK/view?usp=sharing) 看作那种深入的活动。但这在我看来并不令人满足。）换句话说：认真的 writing，是把自己变成一个拥有值得分享的 ideas 和 understanding 的人的好方法。

把 Distribution 和 engagement 本身当作目的，是许多人采用的 default goals；social factors 和 platforms 所作的 design decisions 都在鼓励这一点。但这往往是一条有害的道路，会通向 audience capture，并让你忘记自己的 goals。问题是：你想 achieve 什么？你的投入有多坚定？对于某些 goals，distribution 和 engagement 非常值得追求；对于另一些，它们就远没有那么重要，甚至可能有害。

对我来说，眼下一个重要的 goal，是 build 一个_小型_ community，其中的人都在热切投入与我自己的 creative projects 有所 overlapping 的 creative projects。我一直没有为此付出足够的努力。这意味着直接给人发消息，意味着组织 workshops、retreats 等活动。这意味着要更加努力，为我的作品 fostering 好的 comments。这要一个人一个人地去做。这是把 Inklings，而不是 Beatles，当作 creative work 的 model。一切都围绕着这个问题：谁可能成为这个 project 的朋友？作为回应，我又想支持谁？这样做最终或许会 create 更广泛的 distribution 所需的 conditions。但那不是眼下要 focus on 的事。因此，把 writing 作为 primary focus 很合适，让 podcasts 之类的东西来支持它，即便后者更容易获得更多 distribution。

## 2024

## Framing stories：框架故事（2024 年 10 月 30 日）

Framing stories 在 fiction 和 film 中非常常见。它们本身却几乎从不令人难忘。我并不怎么在意年老的 Rose deWitt Bukater 或 treasure hunt（在 _Titanic_ 中），也不怎么在意 weather reporting（在 _Groundhog Day_ 中），或_诸如此类的东西_。即使在我喜爱的 stories 中，我也常常几乎记不起它们。我知道 _Life of Pi_ 中有一个 framing story，但记得不多——好像与一个动物园，以及从加拿大去印度有关。而且，我也不能说 Rob Reiner（或 William Goldman）在 _The Princess Bride_ 的 film version 中删去大部分 framing 是不对的。我猜，novel 的 framing 对 Goldman 很重要——那些关于生活并不公平的内容，以及它们与 story 的 contrast。但与 fairy tale 相比，framing 完全黯然失色，而 fairy tale 才是他心之所在。

这并不意味着 framing stories 没有意义。但它们的意义其实并不在于 _story_ 本身，通常它们是一种 bridging device，帮助把我们（reader 或 viewer）从我们的 world 带入 story world。有时，这种 transport 相当令人难忘——想想 Dorothy 被 transported 到 Oz，或 Lucy 走进 wardrobe，又在 Narnia 出现。但我们依然不怎么在意 framing。它是 story 的 infrastructure，而不是 story。

我最近很喜欢 _Piranesi_ 和 _I Who Have Never Known Men_，这两部作品都没有 framing story，尽管其中的 worlds 足够奇异，authors 肯定曾动过这个念头。但我认为，不用 framing 是正确的选择。事实上，在 _Piranesi_ 中，我们看到了一种 reverse framing：最终，我们重新进入自己的“real” world。fantasy world frames 了 real world。但这样做的 effect，是让 fantasy world 和 real world 都有趣得多。我记得整个 story arc。我不禁想，_Titanic_、_Groundhog Day_ 等作品如果没有 framing，会不会更好？

我上面说，我不在意 _Titanic_ 中的 framing。仔细想想，这不是真的。有两处我记得很深。一处是如今真正的 _Titanic_ 的 images。另一处是 treasure hunter 在结尾说：“我从未真正懂得，也从未让它走进心里”，意识到自己虽然痴迷多年，却从未真正理解 sinking 的 story。也许那个 framing 确实值得保留，比从 Jack 和 Rose 登船前的生活 story 讲起更好。又或者，以 Jack 救下正要跳下去的 Rose 作为开头会更好？你可以_让_那个 story 成立，不过可能需要一些 flashbacks，来 convey 那种 ship millieu 的恢宏。

novel _Cloud Atlas_ 试图让 framing stories 作为真正的 story 发挥作用：它几乎全是 framing story。事实上，在我看来，其中的 central story——“Sloosha's Crossin' an' Evrythin' After”——是这些 stories 中最不有趣、也最不令人难忘的。但这种 construction 在 film version 中被舍弃了，后者只是呈现六个 interwoven stories，却取得了好得多的 effect。

我上面写的内容，对 framing stories 相当否定。除了已经提到的 bridging，值得再想想它们还能 enable 什么。我认为，它们主要是给 writer 多得多的 narrative control。在 _Star Maker_ 或 _Last and First Men_ 这样的作品中，Stapledon 的 framing device 让他几乎可以去任何地方，向我们展示任何东西。_The Magician's Nephew_ 和那种（常见的）world-between-worlds 的 device 也是如此。Magic realist stories 有时也能 achieve 同样的 narrative control，通常是通过在一开始就 asserting 一个或多个 absurdities——Karl Marx 加入谈话，或一位 alien bounty hunter 加入，或_诸如此类_。一旦 reader 接受了这一点，就能接受灵活得多的 narrative。但 writer 也放弃了很多——Tolkien 所谓的 subcreation 所带来的感觉，大半就此丢失——如今是生活在 dreamworld 中，而不是一个 subcreated real world 中。

## 为了 discover 而 Teaching（June 25, 2024）

我听过一个故事：mathematician Kolmogorov 曾决定开一门课，讲授 Hilbert 的某个（unsolved）problem，并抱着一个不太严格的愿望性目标：solving 这个 problem。他希望这能帮助他组织这门课的讲授思路，同时大幅减轻失败的痛苦。结果，Kolmogorov 和班上的一位学生 Vladimir Arnold 真的 solved 了这个 problem。

我决定把这种 pattern 称为“为了 discover 而 teaching”——以某种强烈的 creative agenda 来推动课程的开展。我的许多 creative work 都是这样完成的。这通常很有趣，也极有收获（希望对学员也有好处；有时这些学员会成为 collaborators，就像 Arnold 那样）。我几乎整个 creative life 都在这么做，但直到今天才想到这个 terminology。有这样的 terminology 似乎很有用。

这个说法改编自一个有用的说法，是我在 2009 年从 Louise Dennys 那里学来的；她当时是 Random House Canada 的 executive publisher：“为了 discover 而 writing”。这也是我经常做的事，但两者相当不同。

## 你怎么看为 AI Safety 开展 Manhattan Project 这个想法？（September 14, 2023）

_（这是一篇详细笔记的大幅精简版，我可能会将原笔记整理后公开。）_

许多人都提出过“Manhattan Project for AI Safety”。它具体意味着什么，各个 proposal 的说法并不相同。但一个共同特征是，建议 governments 或 philanthropists 拨出数十亿（或更多）美元，用于某个旨在“solve safety”的 centralized project。通常的设想是在一个地点开展，并招募“所有 top experts”，让他们能集中精力研究这个 problem。例如，[Judea Pearl](https://twitter.com/yudapearl/status/1641978456513867776?s=20)、[Gary Marcus](https://twitter.com/GaryMarcus/status/1641823446308122624)、[Samuel Hammond](https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2023/05/08/manhattan-project-for-ai-safety-00095779) 等人都支持过这一想法的不同版本。这个想法对人们有足够大的吸引力，因此值得仔细思考：在什么条件下，用 Manhattan Project approach 应对 problems 是有希望的；在什么条件下，它会失败，甚至 retard progress。

我不打算在这里尝试作 thorough analysis，但确实想表达一个范围有限的看法：这个 project 的 centralized、top-down、all-encompassing 性质，似乎是个糟糕的主意。这些 qualities 对 Manhattan Project 来说是合理的，因为 physicists 对几乎所有相关 physics 都已经有了良好、往往甚至非常好的 models，也很清楚该如何实施一切。尽管仍需要许多 refinement，他们对整体情况已经了解很多，因此 centralized approach 是合理的。相比之下，对于 AI Safety，我们甚至连 basics 都知之甚少。如果想取得 progress，最好是扶持大量 parallel efforts，去 pursuing 截然不同的 ideas，而不是在一个地方搞 centralization。（这_并不_意味着其中某些 efforts 不应规模很大，只是不应 all-encompassing。）即便如此也存在 problems：safety 与 capabilities work 之间没有清晰的 division，而且许多 safety work _就是_ capabilities work。但至少作为一个关于如何在 safety 上取得 progress 的基本观点，这在我看来是清楚的。

-   每次提到 OpenAI，（大多来自 academic 界、但远非全都如此的）听众中就会传出紧张的笑声。他们：(a) 将 OpenAI 视为 leader；(b) 对此感到不安，觉得自己在许多方面落后了，而且不知道该怎么办。

-   information distribution 的 asymmetries 非常显著。作为局外人，我感觉几乎（！）每个人，除了在自己狭窄的 area 之外，都落后了相当多。

-   creative action 的 mechanism——在这群人中，往往就是 academic research group——对哪些 problems 能被认真 attacked 施加了如此大的 constrains，令人印象深刻。这让一个问题很值得思考：不同的 creative action 的 mechanisms 能够实现什么：academic groups 与 established companies（DeepMind、OpenAI、Anthropic），与新 startups，与古怪的 Twitter，与 open source，与 Discord，与\[等等\]。各自都有某些 strengths。这里有些（并非所有）academics 有些沮丧，似乎觉得自己的 action 的 mechanism——academic research group——只能为宏大的整体 vision 作出微小的贡献。许多人似乎觉得自己只是在当配角。

-   据我所知，利用 LLMs 做出的许多最有趣的东西，仍然出自古怪的 fringe communities。原来 twitter poasting 是 GPT-4 的良好 training！但我敢说，还有一些很棒的 communities 是我不知道的（Discord、Signal 等）。

-   LLMs 的 experimental anthropology 是一个非常有趣的 subject。奇怪的是，我们仍没有好的方式来谈论 universal 或 invariant properties——也就是在 model 发生合理 changes 时仍然 invariant 的 properties。

-   人们拥有怎样的秘密 knowledge 或 ability，能给他们带来 competitive advantage？这很值得思考。有些（并非所有！）与会者似乎抱着“money/compute 是 decisive”的 presumption，并有些无动于衷，认定 OpenAI、DeepMind 等在这方面拥有 natural monopoly。但这种 monopoly 是其他因素的 downstream。最明显的是：让 Alec Radford、David Silver、John Jumper 等人发挥作用；也许不那么明显的是，某些 beliefs。那些 beliefs 是什么，很值得思考。还可能存在什么其他 beliefs？

-   到目前为止：完全没有认真提及 safety。和往常一样，我感到这种古怪的张力。一方面：这在智识上和 1920 年前后的 quantum mechanics 一样令人兴奋（很可能更令人兴奋）。另一方面，ASI 令人恐惧。

## 任由 professionals 摆布的 Thinking（June 3, 2023）

social media 有一点令人不安：它让你和你的朋友与一支大军对抗——或者，至少并不明确站在同一边。这支大军由非常聪明、资源非常充足、co-ordinated 的人组成，他们想帮助 sculpt 你的 attention。他们希望你保持 _engaged_。Twitter、Instagram 或 TikTok 可以雇用一千名 behavioural psychologists、marketers、data scientists、product managers 等人，让你的 attention 服从他们的意志。

我说了“对抗”，但这不完全准确。事实上，你认为对 attention 的良好运用，与他们所做的事情之间，可能存在某种 overlap。但也可能存在巨大差异，而这相当令人不安。

politics 中也有类似的 phenomenon。我认识许多非常聪明、善于思考的人，他们只是随意参与 politics，大多是 US 的 Democratic party 的 partisans。我时不时会注意到，他们坚信的某件事，经调查后却完全是错的。（他们也相信许多看来正确的事情。）

据我所知，原因与 social media 的情况几乎一样：他们在不知不觉中，与一群规模庞大、非常聪明的人进行着 adversarial war；后者正（集体地）精心 sculpting 关于世界的 misleading narratives。当然，这与 Democratic party 本身无关——只不过我的朋友大多是 Democrats——如果你只是任何 political group 的普通 partisan，你的 thoughts 往往都会在一定程度上任由那个 group 内的 professionals 摆布。

即使大多数 professionals 都是致力于 truth 的好人，这些 effects 仍然 hold——事实上，我相信大多数 political parties 中的大多数人，以及 social media companies 中的大多数人，都是如此，当然都并非人人如此。

我想知道，这两种 effects 在多大程度上 sculpt 我自己的 thinking。我敢说，这远比我以为的要多，而我本来就觉得很可能已经相当多了。

## 关于 pure research 中的“task lists”的反思（2023年6月3日）

manager 或 engineer 的 orientation $$\*$$ 是面向一份 tasks 清单；creative researcher 的 orientation 则是面向一份 emotional provocations 和 half-baked hunches 的清单。

我有时会列出一份 task list，随后却发现它严重妨碍了我的工作。无论对这份清单做多少“improvement”，都无法改变这种情况：问题似乎恰恰就在于有一份 task list。这种时候，通常意味着我更适合列出一份让我有强烈感受的 weird-ass provocations 清单。

此外：“strong”几乎可以具有任何 valence。Fear 或 anger 往往和 inspiration、curiosity 或 fascination 一样有效！（不过，如果我的清单里大多是这些，我会很痛苦。）

而且，你不会逐项勾掉它们。你只是不断回到它们那里汲取动力。（嗯，有时它们也会失去自己的 emotional valence。）

这并不意味着我不使用 task lists。事实上，有很多砍柴挑水式的工作，在这些事情上，我觉得 task lists 极其有用。但也有很多事情，倘若我处于 on-task 的状态，反而意味着我在某种程度上做得不对。我需要让主要的 orientation 始终面向 weird hunches、让我着迷的事物、strange connections 等等，而不是面向 task list。

（受2020年末与 Sebastian Bensusan 的一次交谈启发。）

$$\*$$ 这些说法都是“consider a spherical cow”式的。我不是 manager 或 engineer，当然也不能代表所有 creative researchers。这是一个有趣的 speculative model。

## 关于 molecular nanotech 和 quantum computing 早期历史的随想（2023年6月3日）

有个问题我琢磨过不少：为什么 quantum computing 的发展相当顺利，而 molecular nanotechnology 却没有？

两者有很多相似之处：

-   两者都是在1980年代早期至中期被 proposed 的。
-   两者都有 Feynman 做出的 precursor work，尽管 key papers 是别人写的。
-   两者都令人兴奋，但也都有些 vague。quantum computing model 很快就被明确为一个非常 concrete goal；而 universal assemblers 之类的东西仍然是 somewhat vaguely specified（而且据我所知，至今仍然如此）。
-   在 Clinton nanotech initiative 之前，两者在最初约 ~10 年里都只吸引了少量 research interest，以及极少量 funder interest。
-   两者都在90年代中期左右受到 prominent scientists 的强烈 critiqued（针对 MNT 的是 Smalley，针对 QC 的是 Landauer 和 Unruh）。

一些不同之处：

-   在最初的十年里，MNT 的公众知名度更高，因为 Drexler 写过一本通俗读物。
-   MNT 在1990年代名义上获得了巨额资金注入——也就是 nanotech initiative。早期 MNT believers 的一个常见抱怨是，这项计划被别人挪用，偏离了“real” MNT。

我从早期 MNT believers 那里听过的故事（一遍又一遍又一遍）是：上述最后两点，加上 Smalley critique，共同扼杀了 MNT。我对此存疑。比如说，在1993年，我认为 MNT 的状况明显好于 QC。但我认为，主要区别可能在于：

-   Shor 在1994年 discovered 了一个“killer app”，即 factoring algorithm，而 NSA（以及其他人）非常想要它。Shor 宣布这一成果几天后，Gerard Milburn 对我说：“quantum computing 现在要变得重要了。”
-   这使得少数 first-rate labs 开始尝试做一些 simple proofs of quantum computing principle。这是千里之行的第一步。尤其是，有了 Shor 的 killer app 作为理由，现在就可以迈出第二步（以及第三步、第四步）experimental steps。
-   部分由于上述两点的推动，Landauer 和 Unruh 的 critiques 得到了极其认真的对待，人们也找出了一个 effective rebuttal。幸运的是，这需要一些关于 quantum mechanics 的非同寻常的新 ideas——结果，几十位 theorists（包括起了次要作用的我自己）参与进来，写出了几百篇 papers。在那关键的一年之后，Landauer 用一句冷面幽默的话作了 summary（但也表达了赞许）：“Quantum computing 今天比一年前近了20年。”相比之下，对 Smalley 意见的回应就远没有那么富有想象力。
-   同样形成对比的是：universal assemblers 之类的东西作为 goal，仍然相对有些 vague；MNT 没有某个特定的 killer app，是某个具体的、规模极大的客户迫切想要的；因此，top labs 并没有明确把 MNT 作为 goal 来开展工作。

我认为，对过去40年里 physics、chemistry 和 biology 的大量工作，一个相当公允的 summary 是：它们一直在朝 molecular nanotechnology 迈进。但它们是 piecemeal、bottom-up 地推进的，动力来自迈出接下来的 experimental steps：在这里稍微 improve control，在那里 improve sensitivity，我们能 control 哪些新东西，能 sense 哪些新东西？这段历程取得了令人无比惊叹的成功。而它也使我们来到这样一个位置：MNT（或某种更 powerful 的东西）开始显得是一个合理的 top-down goal。

## process 的严重 limits，以及围绕 taste 的禁忌（2023年6月3日）

关于 metascience 的讨论往往高度聚焦于 process。寻找更好的 granting strategies、更好的 incentives、更好的 values、更好的 hiring practices，等等。individuals 的 qualities 和 human knowledge 的实际 details，往往被当作一个 black box。这种关注点很奇怪，因为 process 能带你走的路终究有限。你无法靠 bureaucratize 就获得 blazing insight；世上没有 scaled-out chain Michelin three star restaurants。我们也没有制造 Albert Einsteins 的 production line。而我很少听到有人明确讨论 process 的 limits。

这让我想起关于 taste 的讨论中一个常见的特点：人们往往出于礼貌，默默假定参与讨论的人都拥有 good taste。在大多数情况下，这似乎极不可能；不同人的 taste quality 差异实在太大。这往往是此类谈话中没有说出口的 high-order-bit。我怀疑，坦率而非常具体地讨论每个人如何看待自己的 taste，通常会很有帮助；不是试图达成一种 Kumbaya 式的“我们都有 good taste”的共识，而是找出参与者之间 stark disagreement 的所在，以及这些 disagreements 的原因。

**后记：**从实际角度说，你必须相信自己的 taste。毕竟，你没有别人的 judgment 可以依靠。当然，你可以选择听从别人（有时是好事，有时是坏事），但在决定听从谁时，你仍然在 exercising taste。这有点像这样一个事实：没有 exercise program，实际上也是对 exercise program 的一种选择。当然，你可以选择尝试 _improve_ 自己的 taste。但那是另一个话题了！

## Outlining（拟写大纲）

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#outlining)

小时候，课堂上经常有人要求我“写一个 outline”。我们会练习这个。但我始终没能真正让它奏效。成年后，我有时听到自己敬佩的 researchers 和 writers 说，他们会做 outlines。我不禁想：他们是怎么做到的？

似乎和世界上其他所有人一样，我目前正在写一篇关于 AI existential risk 的 essay。我眼下正在完成的 section 讨论了常用 terms “artificial general intelligence”和“artificial superintelligence”存在的一些 problems。有时候写起来觉得很乏味：“哦，这应该很容易，我何必费这个劲，这些早就有人说过了。”可是，当我看其他人对这一点的 discussions 时，我觉得许多都是 outright wrong，而且没有哪个显得 fully satisfactory。

而当我真正动笔时，我就能看出原因：我会写下一个看似 obvious 的 sentence，然后意识到它有点不对，不完全 true。接着意识到，它在某个重要方面是 wrong。于是我会 improve 自己的 understanding，写下更 true 的东西。而这会 change 其余整个 argument，有时是以 subtle 的方式，有时则会 completely changing 它。而且不只是 argument 后面的部分：它常常会 change _一切_。关键是：只有深入这个 issue 的内里，我才能 discover 这一点[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn1)。如果我之前已经 outlined，那么到这里我就不得不扔掉自己的 outline。而这种事会一遍又一遍、一遍又一遍地发生……你懂我的意思。outlining 的问题在于，writing 是一个 transformative process；我 write，是为了 transform 自己的 understanding。而没有任何 outline 能经受住这样的 transformation。如果一个 outline “奏效”了，那就意味着我的 understanding 并没有被 transformed；尽管 outline 奏效了，writing 却失败了[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn2)。

（这也是我对看似乏味的 topics 有些怀疑的原因。有时，这意味着你应该 omit 这个 topic。但有时候，那里正有一个机会等着你：你产生了 illusion of understanding，因为你并没有在每个 level of abstraction 上真正 understood。而你想做的，是 break 那个 illusion，improving 自己的 understanding。）

所以：我不喜欢 outlines。不过，我确实会做一件与它非常接近的事。我会 sketch _很多很多_。我会 braindump 许多 rough ideas，organize 它们，把它们放进 hierarchies，围绕它们 riff，把它们 mash them up，尝试 opposites，尝试我能想到的最古怪的东西，也尝试我能想到的最 conventional 的东西。我想，这看起来很像 outlining。但它发生在**每一个** level of abstraction 上，从 highest 一直到围绕某些 points 展开极其 extensive 的 riffing，而这些 points 也许在一篇 shippable essay 里连一个 sentence 都不值得占据。有时候，这种 sketching 的某些部分最终会起到 outline 的作用。但那只是 _improved understanding_ 意外产生的 byproduct。outline 不是重点，improved understanding 才是。

9.  爱训人的人会指出上述所有 points 的 drawbacks。例如，如果这是学习 ask questions 的唯一方式，那当然不好。但在我看来，爱训人的人把这类评论本身当作目的；有时，他们似乎只是在享受炫耀自身优越感的机会。Wise use 会让 criticism 服务于 creative growth 和 development，而不是把它当作主要目的。

## AI overload / interface shock：AI 过载／界面冲击 (02-26-2023)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#ai_overload_interface_shock)

只是随想一下未来几年的 AI systems。即便只是这么短的一段时间，似乎也很可能变得相当古怪，更不用说长远了。

思考这件事颇具挑战，原因很多。部分原因在于：眼下有大量噪声，来自 capital 的涌入，以及随之而来的炒作、骗子和唱衰者。而且，尽管炒作不断，仍然很难指出一个至关重要、得到广泛使用的 product，其中 AI 无可争议地是决定性 element。不过，尝试理解这些事情似乎仍然很重要。

当然，未来尚未写定：它有待我们共同决定。但提出 generative 和 analytic models，来思考可能发生什么，是件有趣的事。下面就是这样一个 model。它并不是 prediction，只是一个有助于思考各种可能未来的 model。

具体来说：人们将普遍感到 _AI overload_ 或 _interface shock_[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn4)：随着社会中越来越大的一部分人不得不持续 learning 和 relearning 新的 AI systems，一遍又一遍……再一遍又一遍又一遍又一遍又一遍又一遍，便会产生这种冲击、困惑和茫然。

Copilot、Midjourney、DALL-E、StableDiffusion、GPT3、ChatGPT、Claude、GPT4、Bing，仅仅是最初的开端。

事实上，它们大多还只是些新奇事物，对 knowledge work 而言，还远未到几乎不可或缺的地步。但它们不久之后的 successors 似乎很可能成为 knowledge work 中几乎不可或缺的东西，甚至可能比今天的 Google search 重要得多。充分利用那些 systems，会像学习演奏一种容易上手的乐器：初学者能获得满足，而 mastery 的不断提升也会带来不断增加的 returns。结果便是：人们有强烈的 incentive 去更好地使用这类 systems；同时觉得自己_应该_不断进步，甚至_必须_不断进步。还有：这类 systems 的阵容将迅速变化，覆盖越来越多的 domains；而那些 systems 也不会是 fixed targets：它们将与各自的 user bases 迅速 co-evolve。就好像那件乐器会不断变化、变异，速度与你学习它一样快。

类似的事情已经发生在 programmers 身上：他们承受着一种 API overload：每年，他们都必须学会一批接一批不断出现的 frameworks 和 libraries。我听过许多 programmers 谈论这有多么令人不堪重负，有时甚至茫然无措。只不过，感到这种压力和茫然的将不再只有 programmers：这类 interface shock 将广泛蔓延，波及每一个能通过 mastery 这类 AI systems 在生活中获得实际 advantage 的人。而且，这一切将笼罩在双重情绪阴影之下：生计受到威胁；以及一种也许更深层的 identity threat，因为人们会重新审视自己对 intelligence 的感受，以及它在 sense of self 中的 role。在这个 model 中，我们所理解的 intelligence 可能发生显著变化：它将从直接“solving the problem”，转向“rapidly exploring and mastering interfaces”。类似的变化已经发生在 programming 中，但这次将覆盖范围广泛得多的 creative 和 knowledge work。

## 贫乏的 Moral Systems (01-20-2023)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#poverty_of_moral_systems)

[在一组](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#poverty_of_moral_systems)[最近的笔记中](https://michaelnotebook.com/otc/index.html)，我写到了自己对 deductive Moral Systems 的总体怀疑，并赞成一种更 exploratory / bricolage 的 approach。我把这描述为两种相互竞争的 traditions。其中一种 tradition 专注于 developing Moral Systems：

> ……这似乎在许多方面受到了 mathematics 的启发。找出一些颇为合理的 basic axioms 或 models。然后尝试 explore 并 understand 它们的 consequences。这还不完全是 proving theorems——这些 Systems 很少足够 unambiguous，因而做不到这一点。而是通过“making arguments”来弄清什么是 right。然后还有第二种 tradition，也就是 99.99% 的人所采用的：只是摸索着过日子，与亲友交谈，观察其他人，试着弄清如何在这个世界上生活，做一个好人，善待别人，也善待自己。
> 
> 我认为第二种 tradition 通常要 powerful 和 reliable 得多。原因在于，世界极其复杂，因此 experience 比任何这类 System 都丰富得多。在这种情况下，就目前而言，单是 exploring reality，在许多方面就远比这类 System 更具挑战。这就像试图从几个 simple ideas 出发 deduce biology，与坚定地 exploring the biosphere 之间的区别。实际的 biosphere——我们能够 explore 的 biosphere——极其复杂，而 exploring 它所带来的收获，迄今远远超过尝试从 theoretical first principles 出发 understand 事物。不过，Moral Systems 的一个 benefit 是，人们可以用一些在现实世界中无法轻易采用的方式来推演它们。巧妙的 thought experiments、不同寻常的问题——这些确实带来了 generative benefits。不过，虽然我毫不怀疑这能 generates novel moral ideas，但我怀疑它是否能 generates reliable moral insight。
> 
> 铺垫这么多，其实是想说：我从根本上非常怀疑任何强意义上的“Moral System”这一 notion，甚至怀疑这类 System 中 consistency 和 implication 之类的 notions……许多人想极其认真地将它们作为展开长篇 reasoning 和 action 的 basis。我认为这通常是一个错误，会为那些在任何聪明的 10 岁孩子看来都显然不对的 actions，提供复杂的、有时还出于私心的 justifications。当我这样说时，对 Moral Systems 感兴趣的人有时会想要辩论：在我看来，除了极少数例外，这样的辩论是在浪费我的时间。他们坚持使用第一种 tradition，而不是第二种。把与人的相处主要建立在他们的 actual experiences 上，其次才考虑 Moral Systems 或关于什么是 good 的 theories，要健康得多。

回想起来，我遗憾自己错过了给这一现象命名的机会：它指出的是 _Moral Systems 的贫乏_。具体而言，是与 exploring (moral) reality 相比的贫乏。Deduction 和 consistency 都是不错的 tools，但它们只是所需之物中的一小部分；如果你坚持依靠它们，处境就很不妙。Emerson 说得对：愚蠢的 consistency 是小心灵的恶魔；然而，许多 Moral Systems 似乎都把 consistency 当作一种 basic value 来依靠。

## Technological progress 是 instrumental；actual progress 是 internal to human experience (10-07-2022)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#tech_progress_is_instrumental)

在自己的网站“The Roots of Progress”上的一篇[书评](https://rootsofprogress.org/where-is-my-flying-car)中，Jason Crawford 写道：

> 大概直到 1950s，人们对未来的设想虽然各不相同，却都是乐观的。人们相信 progress，并认为 technology 正在带领我们走向更美好的世界。在一代人的时间里，这一点发生了变化，到 1960s 后期，这种转变已经十分明显。一种不相信 technology 或 progress 的“counterculture”兴起了：事实上，environmentalist movement 是这种 counterculture 的一个主要组成部分，其中很大一部分人认为 technology 和 industry 正在主动 destroying the Earth。

他在书评后面又写道，语气显然颇为赞同：“social activism \[例如 environmentalist movement 所从事的那种\] 是对 human capital 的 drain”。

这是一种奇怪的观点，似乎把 technological advance 与 progress 等同起来，并认为对这种 advance 的任何 inhibition 都是对 progress 的 drain。它之所以奇怪，是因为我认识的大多数 environmentalists 也相信 progress：他们希望下一代过上更好的生活，并对如何最好地实现这一点有自己的想法。事实上，像 Clean Air Act 这样的 modern environmental legislation 能够 passed，部分原因正是这些 activists 的努力。这样的 legislation 很可能已经挽救了数百万人的生命，并显著改善了数十亿人的生活。它也有 cost：我毫不怀疑，这样的 legislation 已经 inhibited technological development。也许这种 cost 比它所 prevented 的 harm 更严重。但至少也有可能，其 benefit 远远超过了 cost。也就是说，这样的 regulations 和 activism 很可能是 human progress 的一座 milestone，值得庆祝，而不是被斥为“对 human capital 的 drain”。

Technology 对于 improving human lives 往往是 instrumentally useful，但它作为 end in itself 并不是 intrinsically good。归根结底，progress 是 internal 的：它是 human lives 和 experience 的 quality 的 improvement；它根本不直接存在于 technology 之中。从这个意义上说，（仅举两个例子）Sermon on the Mount 或 abolitionist movement 所表达的 values，可以说比任何 technology 都代表着一种更 intrinsic 的 progress，因为它们更 directly change human experience。更广泛地说，我们最富想象力的 story-creators、moral entrepreneurs、artists 和 activists，都对 human progress 作出了巨大贡献。

当然，science 和 technology 是 progress 极其重要的 enablers。当你有充足的食物和 medicine、有良好的住房等等时，过上美好生活要容易得多。我只是想指出，我见过有人倡导的一些 fundamental assumptions，在我看来似乎是（错误的）。[我赞同 Effective Altruism 的](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/)这种 approach：“试图为‘什么是 good？’这个问题，做到 science 为‘世界如何运作？’这个问题所做到的事情。\[EA Community\] 并不提供一个答案，而是在发展一个旨在持续改进答案的 community。”当然，可以说，每个人都已经认为自己在为 progress 做这样的事：热情高涨的 technologists 和 anti-technology Luddites，都认为自己是在为“正确”的 progress 形式辩护。

回看上面的内容，我并不满意。就已经说到的部分而言，似乎还可以，但非常不完整。science 和 technology 的一个关键之处在于，它们提供了一顿 free lunch：随着我们的 understanding improves，human power 和 ability to act 也随之 increases。这并不总是好事：某些 technologies 基本上只有坏处。但许多 technologies 看来 on net 是 positive 的（尽管带有一些 negative effects）。因此，我不能说自己赞成 precautionary principle；它似乎主要是一种 _status quo_ bias。虽然我非常支持 science 和 technology，但我会本能地排斥某一类 myopic technologist：他们总是支持 new tech。不过，对于这些事情，我还没有更好的、有原则依据的思考方式。

## “explanation”在 AI 中的 role (09-30-2022)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#role_of_explanation_in_AI)

有一种针对当前 AI 工作的 critique，以这个 argument 的不同变体表达出来：“你看，其中一些 systems 作为 demos 很令人惊叹。但 creating 这些 systems 的人，对它们如何 work、为什么如此，几乎没有 detailed understanding。而在我们拥有这样的 understanding 之前，我们就不算真正地在 AI 上 making progress。”这个 argument 有时还伴随着一些（往往相当武断的）assertions，声称 science‘必须’具有哪些 characteristics。

我本能地对这样的 arguments 有些认同。我原来从事的 physics 领域，充满了关于事物如何 work 的 detailed、而且往往颇令人满意的 explanations。当然，许多其他 fields 也是如此。从历史上看，new technologies 往往_始于_ tinkering 和 intuitive folk models，但接下来的 technological progress，则由对 underlying phenomena 的大幅 improved explanations 所 enabled。即使对 solar system 的 understanding 相当模糊，你也能 build 一个 sundial；但要 build 一个 atomic clock，就需要对许多 phenomena 有 deep understanding。

AI 工作似乎正在试图 violate 这种历史上的 improvement model。是的，我们正在以 tinkering mode developing 看起来越来越好的 systems。但在 understanding 这些 systems 如何 work 方面，progress 似乎远远落在后面。Papers 往往包含一些相当缺乏说服力的 just-so “explanations”，解释 systems 如何 work（或其灵感来源）。但这种 explanation 的 standards 往往极低：它们确实只是 just-so stories。看到这一点，一些人便 conclude，AI 工作并不是“真正的”scientific progress，而只是一种幻象。

但我对此有所怀疑。我倾向于猜想，我们正处于一个 Feyerabendian 的“Anything Goes”时刻：关于 science“必须”如何 proceed 的 prior beliefs 正在被推翻。而我们回顾时，会纳闷自己当初为什么抱有那些 prior beliefs。

发生变化的 underlying thing，是 trying 和 evaluating systems 变得容易了。过去，如果你想 develop improved clocks，就必须费力地 build actual systems，再 rigorously test 它们。单是一个 new design，就可能需要数月或数年才能 build 和 test。Detailed scientific understanding 之所以重要，是因为它帮助你弄清楚该在（technological）design space 的哪个部分 search。当一种 new technology 的每个 instance 都很昂贵时，你就需要 detailed explanations 来告诉你到哪里 search。

相比之下，AI 中的许多 progress 对 search 采取了一种更加 agnostic 的 approach。它不是用 detailed explanations 来 guide search，而是结合使用：(a) general architectures；(b) trying 数万亿（或更多）种 possibilities，并由用于 improvement 的 simple ideas（例如 gradient descent）来 guided；以及 (c) _recognize_ progress 的 ability。这是一种截然不同的 experimentation mode，只有能进行 extremely rapid symbol manipulation 的 machines 出现后，它才成为可能。

在 clock 的例子中，这就 analogous to 拥有一个 universal constructor，能够 extremely rapidly trying 数万亿种不同的 clock designs、recognizing timekeeping 的 progress，并 iteratively making tiny improvements。我不确定在这里“数万亿”是否远远足够——three-dimensional material design 在 combinatorially 方面的要求非常高！但意思应该已经清楚：当 trying new designs 达到_某种_ scale 时，一种新的 progress mode 很可能就会成为可能，它与以往的 modes 大不相同，甚至可能 violating 以往关于 progress“应该”如何发生的 theories。人们会抱怨这些新的 horologists，抱怨他们不理解自己在做什么；与此同时，这些新的 horologists 却会在 building 具有前所未有的 accuracy 的 clocks。

要 critique 这个故事很容易。你可以反驳说：“哦，但 search-and-recognize 必然会在需要 genuine understanding 的 crucial points 遇到 bottleneck。”不过：(a) 请注意，这已经从 argument-of-principle 转变成了引用 _ad hoc_ bottlenecks 的 argument-of-practice；而且 (b) 提出这个 argument 的人，似乎并不是那些实际在 building better systems 的人，而后者才是我们最终关心的 argument-of-practice。我对它有些认同，但也仅限于非常有限的程度。

**关于意图的后记：**这里无意声称 originality。当然，我也不指望这些话能说服任何不愿被说服的人。这只是我在梳理自己的一些想法。

## 关于 risk 的 Stated 与 revealed preference

大约五六家 science funders 的 Funders 都对我说过类似的话：“哦，我很想 fund 更多 high-risk projects，但 scientists 就是不肯 submit，尽管我极力鼓励他们。他们实在太 conservative 了！”

我几乎可以肯定，在这些 funders 中的每一家，一份 safe、incremental 的 grant application 都比真正 high risk 的申请更有可能得到 funded。这些 funders 有点像那些告诉当地菜贩自己想要四季豆，却从来只买西红柿的人。一个明智的菜贩很快就会不再供应四季豆，而只摆出西红柿。

如果 funders 真心想要 high-risk projects，他们就必须确保这样的 projects 比 low-risk projects _更有可能_得到 funded。他们需要让那些 submitted low-risk work 却未成功的 applicants 离开时觉得：“天哪，我的 project 还不够 daring。”

这些都没有涉及 risk 什么时候是好的，以及它究竟是否是好的；那是另一个问题。我只是 observing，stated 与 revealed preferences 之间似乎经常有很大的 gap。他们并不购买自己声称想买的东西。

## 那些你不能说的事 (07-03-2022，修订于 07-13-2022)

有一组 generative questions，我觉得格外有帮助：那些无礼的问题。也就是问自己：在这里，问什么会被认为无礼、冒犯或不得体？什么事情如果是真的，会让人们不快？这里很难说清楚，因为有一个相关的想法，是我（郑重强调）并不是在谈论的：我指的不是通常的政治意义，也不是社会议题；在这些方面，自诩为大胆说真话的人，往往只是在重拾陈旧的偏见。我指的是很不一样的东西：当你开始深入熟悉一个 nascent field 时，你就可以开始问自己：“什么事情如果是假的，会真正让我不安？”或者“什么会真正打乱这里被视为理所当然的局面？”我发现，这个问题最有用的问法是：“在这里，我最害怕什么？”以及“在这里，什么会被认为冒犯或无礼？”我说的冒犯，不是针对个人，而是指 violating shared intellectual presumptions。

## 谁将 own AI？

一个 observation：许多（并非所有）顶尖 AI labs 的管理者，似乎本身并不是直接从事 AI 的 technical aspects 的 top talent。我对此有些惊讶。当真正 challenging technical problems 正在被 solved 时，在 technical problem solving 方面最出色，通常会带来 unique advantage。相比之下，tell a story、obtain capital，以及 assemble 具有基本上属于 commodity skills 的人员，这些能力相当常见。这就是为什么 Intel 和 Google 是由非常能干的 technical people 创办的，而不是由认定自己应该 own semiconductors 或 search 的 business people 创办的。

（澄清一下：Page 和 Brin 并不是 the Valley 有时所说的那种 technical——他们不是能够大量写出 code 的出色 commodity engineers。但在某些方面，他们比世界上的任何其他人都更理解 search 的 technical problem。后者才是我所说的 technically strong 的意思。）

与 quantum computing 作 contrast 很有意思。一些 quantum computing startups 是由非常出色的 technical people 管理的。另一些则由能力在于 raising money、telling stories、hiring 等方面的人管理。到目前为止，第一类似乎做得好得多，至少以 building a quantum computer 方面的 progress 来衡量是如此。

如我所说，我对 AI 事态的发展有些惊讶。也许我只是误解了情况。当然，我确信 Demis Hassabis 极其 technically capable（例如，DeepMind 早期的 hiring 就说明了这一点）。但总体而言，这让我觉得，许多最出色的 technical people 并不理解自己处于多么有利的位置。的确：许多人知道自己不会喜欢管理事务，因此不主动举手承担，是完全正确的。但其中也会有一部分人同样喜欢管理事务。如果看到更多由出色的 technical people 创办的 splinter labs，我不会惊讶。

我很好奇：你会给那些管理顶尖 AI labs 的人中的任何一位投票吗？你能想象他们成为历史上伟大而明智的 leaders 吗？

## note-taking 的 impulse (04-03-2022)

对于当下盛行的许多 note taking 做法，有一点我很难理解：它似乎经常完全没有任何真正的 creative sense。我工作时，通常（最终）是想做出某种 shippable 的东西。它源于我自己的 curiosity，但也有一种强烈的意愿，想为别人做出有价值的东西，哪怕受益的只有几个人。这既令人满足，也有助于 test 我是否真的在做有用的事情。

相比之下，有些人（并非所有人）的 note taking 似乎是为了 writing notes，而这些 notes 对任何人都没有多少明显的用处，包括他们自己。他们并没有在朝着什么成果推进；事实上，有时似乎只是觉得自己“应该” taking notes。这让我想起许多抱怨 memory systems 的人；他们往往其实也没有什么能好好用上它们的地方。这就像是在 cargo culting creative work。

以上这些，我已经尽可能说得直截了当了。这无疑不太公平。但它确实让我困惑。这常常就像煞费苦心地 practicing the piano，却只是为了 play chopsticks 或 scales。

当然，这些都与我无关。但我之所以产生兴趣，是因为 writing 是一种极其强大的 tool，能够 improve 一个人自己的 thinking。note-taking 的风潮看起来似乎应该是其中的一部分。但问题是：有没有人在这些 tools 的关键帮助下，writing 出了出色的 screenplays、books 或 essays，或者 making important discoveries？如果有，我很想知道他们是谁！

我觉得，很有可能它们只是被用于一些我凭直觉无法理解的方式。例如，我往往觉得 engineering 和 company-building 很难理解。虽然我当然钦佩那些把这些事情做得很好的人，但我自己在这些方向上没有多少 impulse。所以，也许这些 tools 在那里很有用，只是我看不出具体如何有用。那也会很有意思。

**补充：** 这并不是一个我非常坚定的看法。我的感觉是，这样的 systems 应该有某种极其强大的用途。但在我看来，大多数实际 uses 并不是很有意思。我怀疑只是自己没有看到那些有意思的 uses。

## AI safety：一个 oversimplified model (03-17-2022)

一个关于 AI Safety 的非常 oversimplified 的 3-level model。当然，我无意提出什么新东西或特别深刻的见解，只是写下来有助于让自己的思考稍微 precise 一点。

1.  narrow alignment problem，许多 technical AI safety work 似乎都 focuses 于此（“让我们确保我的 nuke 只在我希望它 goes off 时才 goes off，在我希望它 explode 的地方 explodes，_等等_”）。对于 AGI，这还有一个额外的 challenge（超出 nuke analogy 的范围）：human intent 往往是 ambiguous 的，而且很难 debug。我们以为自己想要 A；随后意识到想要的是 A'；然后是 A''，等等；接着我们发现，即使自己已经清楚想要什么，也很难 express，而且很容易被 misinterpret。这个 problem 是 interpersonal communication 与 programming debugging 的 combined generalization，还额外夹杂着 massive unexpected emergent effects 这个 problem（例如“我们没有意识到，optimizing 我们的 metrics 会意外 lead to authoritarian dictatorships”）。

2.  但即使你能神奇地 solve narrow alignment problem，你仍然面临 evil actors 会有 bad intent 这个 problem（“让我们确保 bad guys / bad orgs / rogue nations 没有 nukes”）。在这种情况下，Moore's Law 与 algorithms 的 progress 意味着，如果任何人拥有 AGI，那么很快每个（剩下的）人都会拥有 AGI。从这个意义上说，AI safety 是 eliminating evil actors 的一个 special case。

    -   这里的 caveat 是，你或许能做类似 non-proliferation treaties 的事，让最 powerful 的 actors 运用其 power，slow down 其他 actors acquiring AGI。作为 slowing 的手段，这很有用，不过从长远看，我怀疑它是否行得通。对 nukes 和 chemical weapons 做到这一点就已经够难了；software 会难得多。
3.  agency 的 problem：如果 AGIs become 拥有自己 goals 的 independent agents，而且它们比 humans powerful 得（多得）多，那么 humanity 作为一个整体，就会处在 powerless 者身处 powerful 者身旁的那种 standard historic situation 中。当 powerful entities 的 goals 与 powerless 者 overlap 时，通常都是 powerful entities 得到它们想要的东西，即便这会伤害 powerless 者。

毫无疑问，这个 model 有各种错误或遗漏。当然，我对围绕这些问题的思考所知甚少。尽管如此，我仍有一种或许错误的感觉：许多 AI safety 工作只是在边缘小打小闹，唯一真正可能奏效的，是现在就做类似 non-proliferation 的事。这很难——目前，许多强大的 economic、defense 和 research interests 都会反对。但这似乎是顺理成章的做法。请注意，ridicule 是 dismissing 这个 argument 的常见 tactic：proof-of-impossibility-by-failure-of-imagination。但这算不上什么 argument。说清楚一点：我也不认为 standard AI safety arguments 很好。但它们不必很好：这是一个少见的 case，我倾向于非常认真地对待 precautionary principle。

## “你应该”

颇为经常地，会有人对我说：“你应该\[做某某项目\]”。

这是好意。但它令我不快的程度，却出乎意料。建议中的项目往往需要好几年才能完成。往往还是某种显得乏味，或构思欠妥的项目。而背后的预设似乎是，我缺少项目点子，却有大把时间。（上次核对数量时，我的清单上大约有 5,000 个项目点子）。

我的建议是：如果你喜欢这个点子，喜欢到要建议我花时间去做，那么_你_就应该去做。如果你不想做，你应该反思一下为什么不想。

与此同时，一种不那么令人恼火的说法是：“有一个非常有趣的项目，我很希望看到有人去做，那就是……”

## 面向广泛受众的 science 中的 Affect (12-24-2021)

我不喜欢的几种常见 affects：

-   那种令人啧啧惊叹的 flowery affect，充满悦耳、浮夸却没多少意义的措辞。
-   那种 medicinal affect：“看看这个，它会让你成为一个更好的人。”常常自鸣得意、摆出施惠姿态、居高临下。我怀疑没有人觉得自己在做这种作品，但这却是一种出奇流行的选择。
-   那种 mysterian affect。少量来一点也无妨——我们不知道的东西多得很，描述得好时也极其有趣[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn5)。但当 woo-woo affect 占据主导时，就令人不适了。Mysteries 通常在联系着我们已知的东西来理解时最有趣。

我喜欢并欣赏的 affective qualities 是：curious、appreciative、exploring、humorous，努力 deepen understanding，同时意识到，即使我们非常自信，我们的 understanding 通常也很不完整。最好的作品常常 reveals hidden beauty，让 unexpected relationships 变得 vivid。这非常能拓展人的眼界。

幸运的是，具备这些 qualities 的作品正处于黄金时代。

有一种常见的 boundary case 特别让我烦恼。Carl Sagan 的《Cosmos》中有不少 flowery affect（“billions and billions”等等）。但它通常是在表达一个 deeply insightful 的 core idea。许多其他人也使用类似的 flowery affect——有时甚至拉满到 11——但 core ideas 却弱得多。那就完全行不通了。

## 论 discovery 与 experiment 的 invention

（围绕我阅读 David Wootton 的《The Invention of Science》所作的发散思考）

在 chess 中，一个 grandmaster 和一个彻头彻尾的 beginner，在某个 position 中很可能会 play 同一个 move，甚至同一串 series of moves。然而，这个 action 背后的 reasons——这些 moves 的 theory 和 context——会完全不同。

Magnus Carlsen 的 playing pawn to e4 与我的 playing pawn to e4 并不相同——远远谈不上相同！

许多 actions 中都会出现 analogous phenomenon：由于周围的 context，表面上“相同”的 action 可能有截然不同的 meaning。有时，我们希望一个 verb（或其对应的 noun）指的是 literal action；有时，我们希望一个 verb/noun 指的是更完整的 context。

我一直在联系 discovery 和 experiment 这两个 notions 思考这件事。要 make precise 它们在什么意义上是现代 notions，并不容易。当然，我毫不怀疑，即使追溯到 prehistoric times，人们偶尔也会 carried out 一些 literal actions，与我们今天会称作 discovery 或 experiment 的事情十分相似。然而，尽管它们与我们今天所说的 discovery 或 experiment 很可能存在 literal similarity，更完整的 context 却非常不同。

在这两种情况下，周围的 theory（人们如何思考它，它意味着什么）和周围的 context 都有所不同。

我不打算一一列举——那很容易就能写成一本书！

但我尤其着迷于 discovery 的 irreversible nature——现代意义上的 discovery 必须涉及一个 near-irreversible act of amplification，使 knowledge 得以 spread 到世界各地，成为我们 collective memory 的一部分。

这或许在 pre-historic times 偶然发生过——比如，fire 很可能就是如此。但今天，我们有许多 institutions，其 role 很大程度上就是促成这种 irreversible act of amplification。

## 思考我从 David Wootton 的《The Invention of Science》中学到了什么 (12-21-2021)

到目前为止，或许最重要的一点是，scientific revolution 的一个主要部分在于：_discovery 变得 near-irreversible_。在 Brahe 或 Galileo 等人之前，许多人可能已经有过他们的某些 insights。但那些 insights 大多_并未 spread 到整个 culture_。

这种 spread 是由 novel technologies（journals、printing press、citation）+ 一种新的 political economy（关于 citation、priority、replicability 的 norms，以及 reputation economy）共同实现的。其结果是为新 ideas 提供了一个强大的 amplifier。而 amplification 是一种格外具有 one way 性质的 process：idea 这个精灵一旦出瓶，就很难再塞回去。

我忘了这是谁说的（Merton？），但某件事物的 discoverer 并不是第一个 discover 它的人，而是_最后一个_，也就是其 discovery 使这个 idea 得以 spread 到整个 culture 的那个人。这需要一个 idea amplifier，一个使 idea 变得广为人知的 irreversible process。

## Information overload 是 information 带来的 benefits 的后果 (12-21-2021)

一种常见的说法是，information overload 是太多 knowledge 被过快 produced 的结果。这种说法追忆着一段（想象出来的、令人怀念的）历史，那时被 produced 的 knowledge 要少得多。

这种说法隐含着一个错误。

“Information overload”根本上是一种感受。如果你对 ideas 和 information 没什么兴趣，也没什么用途，你就不会感到多少 information overload。

但假设你是一个能从 ideas 中获益良多的人。每多花一个小时阅读、吸收好的 lectures 或\[等等\]，你都觉得大有裨益。如果你身处一个竞争激烈的 intellectual field，一个掌握更多 information 就能获得 competitive advantage 的领域，情况可能尤其如此。你自然会感到很大的压力，既要选好读什么，也要读得更多。这就是 information overload 的感受。

换句话说，information overload 的感受并不是因为 knowledge 太多而产生的。它来自这样一个事实：花更多时间吸收 knowledge 可能带来很高的 returns，由此形成压力，让你不断投入更多时间；此外，对于该吸收什么，并不存在 natural ordering。

这之所以重要，一个原因是人们常常以为，只要使用恰到好处的 tool、approach 或 system，information overload 就会消失。事实上，更好的 tooling 往往会提高吸收 information 的 returns，因此实际上可能 increase information overload。这并非 universally true——能 increase 你的 informational self-efficacy 感受的 tools，可能会 reduce overload 的感受。但上述说法仍有相当多的道理。

（顺带一提，我想推荐 Ann Blair 的书 [Too Much to Know](https://smile.amazon.com/Too-Much-Know-Scholarly-Information/dp/0300165390/ref=sr_1_2?crid=2G8MDPCXK2Y0E)，它讲述了现代以前的 information overload。）

## “experimental program”作为我们 improve 对世界的 understanding 时的 first-class object (12-21-2021)

有一种关于 history of science 的 caricature，认为 comparing theory to experiment 这一 notion 起源于 16 世纪。（常被归于 Bacon）。当然，这是（一种严重的）caricature 和 oversimplification；显然，我们史前的祖先就会从经验中学习！如果无法从这样的祖先（或者，比如 Pythagoras 这样的 mystic）到一位对于如何 devise 自己的 experimental program 有着非常 sophisticated ideas 的现代 scientist，大致画出一条直线，我倒会感到震惊。

这种 discrepancy 让我很不舒服。自从第一次听到这个说法以来，大概是在 7 或 8 岁时，它就一直让我不舒服。总有什么地方有那么一点不对劲。

我怀疑，主要的 transition 在于开始拥有 experimental program，即对于如何 explore nature 有了某种（theory！）的 notion。跟一位 scientist 聊聊，他们会有数百个关于可能的 experiments 的 ideas，对于 costs、benefits、variations 和 failure modes 等等也有详细的思考。现代的新意在于，人们 understanding 到这些事情值得思考。换句话说：我们一直都在做 experiments。只是在 modern science 中，experimental programme——一组关于如何 explore nature 的 ideas——已成为我们 improve 对世界的 understanding 时的 first-class object。

## Internalized goals，而不是 written goals，才是 valuable 的 (12-21-2021)

关于 written goals 的 value，人们已经写了很多。我越来越觉得这是个错误。Goals 只有在变得 deeply internalized、成为你的一部分时，才是 valuable 的。Writing 的帮助，在于它有助于实现这一目的。

## funding 关于 risk 的 work 所具有的 reflexive nature (11-27-2021)

在理想世界中，funders 会运用我们对于如何 reason about risk 的最佳 understanding，来 make decisions，决定 fund 什么 research。（当然，我们所处的世界与此相去甚远，funders 似乎大多仍在运用对于 risk 的 pre-modern understanding。）这种情况有个有趣的方面：当然，他们 fund 的 research 随后可能真正改变我们对于如何最好地思考 risk 的 understanding；在一个重要方面，它会是 reflexive 的。

## Creative workers 应当 design 自己的 filing system (11-25-2021，修订于 11-27-2021)

这样做的 returns 总让我震惊。这是使用 org-mode 的一个 value：你不必使用 computer 的 file system（你对它的 control 非常有限），而可以 design 自己的。更重要的是，可以对那个 filing system 不断 re-design、re-design，再 re-design。design 和 re-design 其实都是 creative acts。而且它们的重要性出人意料。

一个设想中的书名特别让我觉得有趣：“通过 Filing 过上更好的生活”。不过，从某种意义上说，Dave Allen 已经写了这本书，只是他将其命名为“Getting Things Done”。但它并非面向 creative workers。

## merging files 的惊人 value (11-25-2021)

我相信这对许多人来说显而易见，但这是我最近才 discovered 的事：把关于 closely related subjects 的 files collecting up（而且常常 merging），竟有意想不到的 value。

有些 topics 我会一再回顾，而相关的 files 散落在我的 hard disk 各处。Systematically collecting 它们，并在许多情况下 merging 它们，带来了出人意料的帮助。

这在实物世界里是我们经常做的事（把所有袜子放在放袜子的抽屉里）。在 creative thought 的 expressions 的世界中，这样做的 value 似乎至少同样大。

## Programmable money 和 smart contracts 是 powerful ideas，因此很可能会长久存在 (11-21-2021)

围绕这个问题，存在一道奇怪而巨大的分歧：crypto 是真的吗？许多信奉者高喊“这就是未来”。许多厌恶它的人高喊“这全是骗局”。但这个问题本身大体上就是个错误。一个更好的 fulcrum question 是：decentralized programmable money 和 smart contracts 会长久存在吗？具体来说，一个 functioning 的 programmable money 和 smart contracts 的 system，是否会 enable 非同寻常的（而且非常 valuable 的）新 co-ordination behavior 形式？我相信答案显然是肯定的。这并不意味着今天的许多或所有 cryptocurrencies 都不会消失；但它确实意味着，未来几乎肯定会包含这些 ideas 中某些 ideas 的某种后继形式。

## Tech : basic research（技术与基础研究） (11-17-2021)

在 tech 中，exert power（或 act）的 capacity 是 fundamental 的；understanding 是 instrumental 的。

在 basic research 中，understanding 是 fundamental 的；exert power 或 act 的 capacity 是 instrumental 的。

在每个领域，我偶尔都会遇到一些人，他们似乎不仅认为自己的观点是 self-evidently correct 的，而且几乎无法相信竟会有人持不同看法。但在我看来，两者在很大程度上都是（由群体共同持有的）values。

把它类比为古罗马 : 古希腊，很有意思。

## research 与 writing 之间的 relationship (11-17-2021)

在 writing 任何一种 essay 或 book（无论是否属于 research）时，我发现自己（几乎总是）必须按 linear order 来 write first draft。

这与 research 存在强烈的 tension，因为在 research 中，你试图尽可能 improve 自己的 understanding。这不是能按 linear order 做到的事。它更像一种 stochastic upward spiral。从这个意义上说，来回跳转是合理的。你试图 write 一些（你希望）会出现在 final piece 中的 snippets，并尽可能寻找可以 improve 的 pieces。

归根结底，一项 research 需要某种 strong core insight，某个重要而 new 的 idea 或 evidence。通常你开始时并没有这些。所以，你在 exploring、向下深挖、试图 understand、发掘并试图 crystallize 出局部的 understanding 精华，直到你觉得自己确实拥有了某种 strong core insight，拥有了 written report 的 foundation。到那时，你就可以尝试 linear draft。

## Memory systems 作为 concentrating 你的 experience 的一种方式 (10-11-2021)

日常生活中会发生数量惊人的事情。只有极少数似乎真正重要。Memory systems 让你把那些事情 distill 出来，从而一再回顾它们。它们是 concentrating 你的 experience 的一种方式。

## Feedback rules、selection effects、end states（反馈规则、选择效应、终态；2021 年 10 月）

一个 person、organization 或 ecosystem 遵循什么 feedback rule 来 govern change、learning 和 growth？具体而言，那个 feedback rule 会 select for 什么？它所 selects for 的东西，在很大程度上 determines 最后会得到什么。

可以给出 infinite number 的 examples。

(1) political media 是否遵循一种 feedback rule，来 rewards 人们对 government 的 understanding 在 quality 上的 improvements？不是，除非是偶然如此，或仅在某些 narrow ways 上如此；据我所知，关注 political media 的人，往往最终只在一个由（非常 narrow 的）issues 组成的 small subset 上 more informed，而在许多 crucial ways 上反而 less informed。具体而言，他们更有可能相信那些 serve 这个 feedback rule 的 misconceptions。这一点强烈适用于所有 parties，而且几乎不需要 dishonesty，只需要平常的糊涂就够了。

一个我个人觉得惊人的 example：数量之多令人吃惊的人（包括 media 从业者）真心相信，Facebook caused Trump 2016。尽管事实上，Trump 只把 budget 的一小部分花在 Facebook 上，而且其中大部分还是在 election cycle 后期花的。mainstream media 在 cause Trump 方面所起的作用要大得多。我说的不只是 Fox，而是 CNN-NYT-etc-etc-etc，是整个群体，当然也包括起了 significant role 的 Fox。Facebook 确实在 at the margin 上起了作用（许许多多其他事情也一样）。但一个重要得多的 effect，是 Trump 知道如何 manipulate mainstream media。而 mainstream media 似乎没有办法理解这一点——这不在 govern 它们如何 change 的 feedback loop 之内。事实上，情况恰恰相反：Trump 几乎肯定为它们带来了 revenue；它们受到的激励，是让 Trump 这样的 candidate 出现。大多数 media 从业者似乎理解这一点——一些最有名望的 executives 曾强调过这一点——但他们却没有把它与“Facebook caused Trump”是一个 false narrative 这一事实联系起来。并不是他们在撒谎。他们相信这个 narrative，是因为 systemic incentives。

我知道，许多人会把上一段当作我疯了的 evidence。我当然不是想说 Facebook 没起作用；但它只是许多 factors 之一；它的作用几乎肯定比 mainstream media 小得多；而 mainstream media 不理解这一点，相当大程度上是因为它们的 incentives。当然，这个说法 violates conventional narratives。而且我并没有提供 detailed argument，只给出了我的 conclusions。也许我错了。但我不这么认为。而当你听大多数人试图给出他们关于为什么 Facebook caused Trump 的 argument 时，它很快就会瓦解为一些 assertions：要么是错的，要么是 based on 极其 weak 的 evidence[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn6)。

(2) 在 universities 中，grant overhead 位于 feedback loop 之内。结果就是，无论 grant agencies select for 什么，universities 都会 systematically select for 同样的东西。这是一种（极其强大的）centralizing force。在大多数 individual given instances 中，它的作用很 weak。但经过数十年，这个 effect 是 cumulative 的，而且极其巨大。说 research 是 centrally controlled 未免太过，但朝这个方向发展的 tendencies 无疑很 strong。

## Why write a book or essay rather than make videos, reels etc? (May 26, 2026)

_A note to myself on a question I occasionally return to._

It's a good question. Videos and reels _are_ a marvellous media form, powerful and powerfully interesting. Indeed, you can take a moderately interesting script and layer in some fairly generic visuals and audio and it becomes far more interesting than the script alone to most people (including me). We enjoy and get a lot out of the texture. And videos and reels have far better distribution, so the likelihood of being seen by millions of people is much higher.

What then is the right role for writing?

Writing is in some ways a better medium for thinking in. If I want to develop good or original creative ideas, writing is extraordinarily helpful. Video and reels can also be used this way, but my understanding is that it's slower and more challenging. If distribution is a goal then you may use channels other than writing _eventually_, but if the principal goal is to have something worth saying (rather than Mr. Beast), writing is an excellent medium. It's not the only way, of course, but you do need grounding in some deep activity in the world, not just social media for social media's sake. (Alternately, you can view [hacking that system](https://drive.google.com/file/d/11dV24Mda_kNEBCLtPtBomWG4IkngvQKK/view?usp=sharing) as the deep activity. But it seems unsatisfying to me.) Put a different way: serious writing is a great way of changing you into a person with ideas and understanding worth sharing.

Distribution and engagement for their own sake are default goals many people adopt, encouraged both by social factors, and by design decisions made by the platforms. But they're often a damaging path toward audience capture, and losing sight of your own goals. The question is: what do you want to achieve? How firmly committed are you? For some goals, distribution and engagement are highly desirable; for others, they are much less important, and may even be damaging.

For me, an important goal right now is to build a _small_ community of people who are intensely engaged with creative projects overlapping my own. I haven't been putting enough effort into that. It means messaging people directly, it means organizing workshops, retreats, and so on. It means working much harder on fostering good comments on my work. It'll be done one person at a time. It's the Inklings as a model for creative work, not the Beatles. All around the question: who is a likely friend of the project? Who do I want to support in turn? Doing this may eventually create the conditions for broader distribution. But it's not the thing to focus on right now. And so writing is a good primary focus, with things like podcasts supporting it, even if they get more distribution more easily.

## Framing stories (October 30, 2024)

Framing stories are very common in fiction and film. They're almost never memorable in their own right. I don't much care about old Rose deWitt Bukater or the treasure hunt (in _Titanic_), or about weather reporting (in _Groundhog Day_) , or _etc etc etc_. Often I can barely remember them, even in stories I love. I know there was a framing story in _Life of Pi_, but I don't remember much – something about a zoo, and going to India from Canada. And I can't say that Rob Reiner (or William Goldman) wasn't correct to throw out most of the framing in the film version of _The Princess Bride_. I suspect the novel's framing mattered to Goldman – all that stuff about life not being fair, and the contrast to the story. But the framing fades entirely compared to the fairy tale, which is where his heart is.

This doesn't mean framing stories don't have a point. But the point is not actually _story_ per se, it's usually a bridging device to help bring us (the reader or viewer) from our world into the story world. Sometimes the transport is quite memorable – think of Dorothy being transported to Oz, or Lucy going into the wardrobe and emerging in Narnia. But we still don't much care about the framing. It's infrastructure for the story, not story.

I recently enjoyed both _Piranesi_ and _I Who Have Never Known Men_, and in both cases there's no framing story, though the worlds are strange enough that the authors must have been tempted. But I think they made the right choice by having no framing. Indeed, in _Piranesi_ we have a reverse framing, where eventually we re-enter our "real" world. The fantasy world frames the real world. But it has the effect of making both the fantasy world and the real world much more interesting. I remember the whole story arc. I wonder if _Titanic_, _Groundhog Day_, and so on would have been better with no framing?

I said above that I don't care about the framing in _Titanic_. Upon reflection that's not true. I remember two bits strongly. One is the images of the actual _Titanic_, today. And the other is of the treasure hunter saying at the end that "I never got it, I never let it in", realizing that despite years of obsession he'd never really understood the story of the sinking. And maybe that framing really is worth it, better than starting with some story about Jack and Rose's lives before boarding the boat. Or perhaps it would have been better to start with Jack saving Rose as she was about to jump? You could _make_ that story work, though probably some flashbacks would be needed, to convey the majesty of the ship millieu.

The novel _Cloud Atlas_ tries to make framing stories work as actual story: it's almost all framing story. Indeed, the central story, "Sloosha's Crossin' an' Evrythin' After", is in my opinion the least interesting and memorable of the stories. But this construction was thrown out in the film version, which simply presents six interwoven stories, to much greater effect.

What I've written above is pretty negative about framing stories. It's worth reflecting on what they enable, beyond the bridging already mentioned. I think mostly they give the writer much more narrative control. In something like _Star Maker_ or _Last and First Men_, Stapledon's framing device lets him go almost anywhere and show us anything. It's similar in _The Magician's Nephew_ and the (common) device of a world-between-worlds. Magic realist stories sometimes achieve the same narrative control, typically by asserting one or more absurdities early on – Karl Marx joins the conversation, or an alien bounty hunter, or _etc_. Once a reader has accepted that, they can accept a much more flexible narrative. But the writer gives up a lot, too – much of the sense of what Tolkien has called subcreation – living now in a dreamworld, rather than a subcreated real world.

## Teaching to discover (June 25, 2024)

I've heard a story that the mathematician Kolmogorov once decided to teach a class on one of Hilbert's (unsolved) problems, with the loose aspirational goal of solving the problem. He hoped this would help organize his approach to the class, while much lowering the pain of failure. In the event, Kolmogorov and one of the students in the class, Vladimir Arnold, actually solved the problem.

I've decided to call this pattern "teaching to discover" – running classes with some strong creative agenda driving the class. Much of my creative work has been done this way. It's usually fun and extremely rewarding (and, hopefully, good for class members; sometimes those class members become collaborators, like Arnold). I've been doing it nearly all my creative life, but the terminology only occurred to me today. It seems useful to have such terminology.

The phrase is an adaption of a useful phrase I learned in 2009 from Louise Dennys, then the executive publisher at Random House Canada: "writing to discover". This is also something I frequently do, but is quite distinct.

## What do you think about the idea of a Manhattan Project for AI Safety? (September 14, 2023)

_(Much condensed version of a detailed note, which I may clean up and make public.)_

Many people have proposed a "Manhattan Project for AI Safety". Precisely what this means differs from proposal to proposal. But a common feature is to suggest governments or philanthropists put aside billions (or more) dollars for some centralized project meant to "solve safety". The idea is usually to run it out of one location, and to recruit "all the top experts" so they can work on the problem intensively. Variations on this idea have been endorsed, for instance, by [Judea Pearl](https://twitter.com/yudapearl/status/1641978456513867776?s=20), [Gary Marcus](https://twitter.com/GaryMarcus/status/1641823446308122624), [Samuel Hammond](https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2023/05/08/manhattan-project-for-ai-safety-00095779), and others. The idea exerts enough hold over people that it's worth thinking carefully about conditions under which a Manhattan Project approach to problems is promising, and the conditions under which it will fail, or even retard progress.

I won't venture a thorough analysis here, but do want to register one narrow opinion: the centralized, top-down, all-encompassing nature of the project seems a terrible idea. Those qualities made sense for the Manhattan Project, since physicists already had good and often very good models of nearly all the relevant physics, and a good idea of how to carry everything out. While much refinement was still necessary, they understood a lot about the broad picture, and a centralized approach made sense. For AI Safety, by contrast, we understand very little about even the basics. If you want to make progress, it's better to foster lots of parallel efforts, pursuing very different ideas, not centralization in one place. (This _does not_ mean some of those efforts shouldn't be quite large, just not all-encompassing.) Even that has problems: there is no clean division between safety and capabilities work, and much safety work _is_ capabilities work. But at least as a basic point about making progress on safety it seems clear to me.

-   Every time OpenAI is mentioned there is nervous laughter from the (mostly, though far from entirely, academic) audience. They: (a) see OpenAI as the leader; and (b) are nervous about that fact, feel behind in many ways, and don't know what to do about it.

-   The asymmetries of information distribution are very striking. As an outsider, it seems as though almost(!) everyone is quite behind, except in their narrow area.

-   It's striking how much the mechanism of creative action – often, in this crowd, the academic research group – constrains what problems can seriously be attacked. It makes it interesting to ponder what can be achieved by different mechanisms of creative action: academic groups vs established companies (DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic) vs new startups vs weird Twitter vs open source vs Discord vs \[etc\]. Each has certain strengths. There is some despondency among some (not all) academics here, who seem to feel that their mechanism of action – the academic research group – can only contribute in minor ways to the grand overall vision. Many seem to feel they're playing second fiddle.

-   Much of the most interesting stuff I know of being done with LLMs continues to be coming out of weird fringe communities. Turns out twitter poasting is good training for GPT-4! But I'll bet there are some great communities I don't know of (Discord, Signal etc).

-   The experimental anthropology of LLMs is a very fun subject. It's curious that we still don't have good ways of talking about universal or invariant properties – properties invariant across reasonable changes in model.

-   Interesting to ponder what secret knowledge or ability people have that give them a competitive advantage? The presumption of some (not all!) attendees seems to be "money/compute is decisive", and to be a bit apathetic, assuming OpenAI, DeepMind etc have a natural monopoly there. But that monopoly is downstream of other things. Most obviously: enabling people like Alec Radford, David Silver, John Jumper etc; perhaps less obviously, of certain beliefs. Interesting to ponder what those beliefs were. And what other beliefs there might be?

-   So far: absolutely no serious mention of safety. As so often, I feel this weird tension. On the one hand: this is as intellectually exciting as (probably more so than) quantum mechanics circa 1920. On the other hand, ASI is terrifying.

## Thinking at the mercy of professionals (June 3, 2023)

One alarming thing about social media is that it pits you and your friends against – or, at least, not clearly with – an army of very smart, very well resourced, co-ordinated people who want to help sculpt your attention. They want you to be _engaged_. Twitter or Instagram or TikTok can employ a thousand behavioural psychologists, marketers, data scientists, product managers, and so on to bend your attention to their will.

I said "against", but that's not quite right. In fact, there may be some overlap between what you consider a good use of your attention and what they do. But there may also be large differences, and that's pretty alarming.

A similar phenomenon occurs in politics. I know a lot of very smart, thoughtful people who are casually engaged in politics, mostly as partisans of the Democratic party in the US. And periodically I'll note that they strongly believe something that upon investigation turn out to be plain wrong. (They also believe a lot of things which seem to be correct.)

The reason, as far as I can tell, is pretty much the same as in the case of social media: they are unknowingly engaged in an adversarial war with a very large, very smart group of people who are (collectively) carefully sculpting misleading narratives about the world. This has nothing to do with the Democratic party, of course – it just happens most of my friends are Democrats – if you're a casual partisan of any political group your thoughts tend to be somewhat at the mercy of the professionals within that group.

These effects hold even if most of the professionals are great people with a commitment to truth – indeed, I believe this is true of most, though certainly not all, people in most political parties, and of most, though certainly not all, people at social media companies.

I wonder about how much both effects sculpt my own thinking. I'll bet it's a lot more than I think, and I think it's likely to be quite a bit.

## Reflections on "task lists" in pure research (June 3, 2023)

The manager's or engineer's orientation \[\*\] is toward a list of tasks; the creative researcher's is toward a list of emotional provocations and half-baked hunches.

I've sometimes composed a task list, and then found it getting badly in the way of my work. No amount of "improvement" of the list changes the situation: the problem seems to be with having a task list at all. When that's the case it usually means I'm better off with a list of weird-ass provocations which I feel strongly about.

Furthermore: "strong" can be with almost any valence. Fear or anger often work just as well as inspiration or curiosity or fascination! (Though I would be miserable if that's mostly what my list contained.)

And you don't check 'em off. You just keep going back for fuel. (Well, sometimes they lose their emotional valence.)

This doesn't mean I don't use task lists. In fact, there's a lot of chop wood, carry water type work, and I find task lists invaluable there. But there's also a lot of stuff where if I'm on-task, I'm somehow failing. And I need to keep the primary orientation toward weird hunches, things which fascinate me, strange connections, and so on, not toward the task list.

(Inspired by a conversation with Sebastian Bensusan in late 2020.)

\[\*\] This is all in the vein of "consider a spherical cow". I'm not a manager or engineer, and I certainly can't speak for all creative researchers. It's a fun speculative model.

## Musings on the early history of molecular nanotech and quantum computing (June 3, 2023)

A question I've wondered a fair bit about: why did quantum computing take off somewhat smoothly, while molecular nanotechnology did not?

There's many similarities:

-   Both were proposed in the early-to-mid-1980s.
-   Both had precursor work done by Feynman, although the key papers were written by others.
-   Both were exciting, but a little vague. The quantum computing model got firmed up pretty quickly into a very concrete goal; things like universal assemblers remained (and remain AFAIK) somewhat vaguely specified.
-   Both had just a small amount of research interest in the first ~10 years or so, and a tiny amount of funder interest, before the Clinton nanotech initiative.
-   Both were strongly critiqued by prominent scientists circa the mid-90s (Smalley for MNT, Landauer and Unruh for QC).

Some differences:

-   MNT had more of a public profile in that first decade, because Drexler had written a popular book.
-   MNT nominally got a huge injection of funds in the 1990s – the nanotech initiative. A standard complaint of early MNT believers is that this was co-opted by others away from "real" MNT.

The story I've heard (over and over and over) from early MNT believers is that the combination of these last two points, plus the Smalley critique, killed MNT. I doubt it. In 1993, say, I think MNT was in a significantly better state than QC. But I think the big differences were likely:

-   Shor discovered a "killer app" in 1994, the factoring algorithm, which the NSA (and others) really wanted. A few days after Shor announced it, Gerard Milburn told me "quantum computing is going to matter now".
-   This led to a small number of first-rate labs trying to do some simple proofs of quantum computing principle. It was the single step that began the journey of a thousand miles. In particular, second (and third and fourth) experimental steps could now be taken, with the justification of Shor's killer app.
-   Motivated in part by the last two points, the critiques of Landauer and Unruh were taken very seriously, and an effective rebuttal was worked out. Fortunately, some extraordinary new ideas about quantum mechanics were needed – as a result, dozens of theorists (including myself, in a minor role) got involved, and wrote hundreds of papers. Landauer's dry (but approving) summary after the crucial year: "Quantum computing is 20 years closer today than it was a year ago". By contrast, the reply to Smalley's comments was far less imaginative.
-   Also by contrast: things like universal assemblers remained relatively somewhat vague as a goal; MNT didn't have a particular killer app that a specific really big customer desperately wanted; and as a result top labs weren't explicitly working on MNT as a goal.

I think a pretty fair summary of much of the last 40 years of physics and chemistry and biology is that it's been working toward molecular nanotechnology. But they've done so piecemeal, bottom-up, motivated by taking next experimental steps: improve control here a bit, improve sensitivity there, what new things can we control, what new things can we sense? That journey has been absolutely astoundingly successful. And it puts us at a point where MNT (or something more powerful) starts to look like a sensible top-down goal.

## The severe limits of process, and the taboo around taste (June 3, 2023)

Discussions of metascience often focus heavily on process. Identifying better granting strategies, better incentives, better values, better hiring practices and so on. The qualities of individuals and the actual details of human knowledge are often treated as a black box. It's a curious focus, because process only takes you so far. You can't bureaucratize your way to blazing insight; there are no scaled-out chain Michelin three star restaurants. We don't have a production line for Albert Einsteins. And I rarely hear the limits of process discussed explicitly.

I'm reminded of a feature common in discussions of taste: there's often a tacit polite assumption that participants in the discussion all have good taste. This seems highly unlikely in most cases; the quality of taste varies so wildly between people. This is often the unspoken high-order-bit in such conversations. I suspect it would often be helpful to have a frank and very concrete discussion of how each person thinks about their own taste, not trying to come to a Kumbaya "we all have good taste" agreement, but rather identifying points of stark disagreement between participants, and the reasons for those disagreements.

**Postscript:** As a practical matter you must believe in your own taste. After all: you have no-one else's judgment to fall back on. Of course, you may elect to defer to others (sometimes good, sometimes bad), but in so doing you are still exercising taste in who you defer to. It's a bit like the fact that having no exercise program is, in fact, a choice of exercise program. Of course, you may choose to try to _improve_ your taste. But that's another subject!

## Outlining

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#outlining)

As a kid I was told often in class to "write an outline". We'd practice it. And I could never really make it work. As an adult, I sometimes hear from researchers and writers I admire that they make outlines. And I wonder: how do they do it?

Like seemingly everyone else in the world, I'm currently writing an essay on AI existential risk. The section I'm finishing right now discusses some problems with the commonly-used terms "artificial general intelligence" and "artificial superintelligence". And it sometimes feels tedious to write: "Oh, this should be easy, why am I bothering, this has all been said before". Except, when I look at other discussions of this point, I think many are outright wrong, and none seem fully satisfactory.

And when I actually write, I can see why: I'll write a sentence that seemed obvious, then realize it's a little off, not quite true. Then realize that it's wrong in an important way. And I'll improve my understanding, and write something more true. And it'll change the whole rest of the argument, sometimes in subtle ways, sometimes completely changing it. Not just later bits of the argument, either: often it changes _everything_. And the thing is: I can only discover this by getting right down into the guts of the issue[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn1). If I'd outlined, I would have had to throw out my outline at this point. And it happens over and over and over and over and… you get the idea. The trouble with outlining is that writing is a transformative process; I write in order to transform my own understanding. And no outline survives contact with such transformation. If an outline "worked" it would mean my understanding hadn't been transformed; while the outline would have worked, the writing would have failed[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn2).

(This is why I'm also somewhat suspicious of tedious-seeming topics. Sometimes that means you should omit the topic. Sometimes, though, there's an opportunity waiting: you have an illusion of understanding caused by not really having understood at every level of abstraction. And what you want is to break that illusion, improving your understanding.)

So: I'm not a fan of outlines. I do, however, do something closely adjacent. I sketch _a lot_. I'll braindump many rough ideas, organize them, put them in hierarchies, riff on them, mash them up, try opposites, try the weirdest stuff I can, try the most conventional stuff I can. I suppose it looks quite a bit like outlining. But it's at **every** level of abstraction, from the highest right down to very extended riffing on points that might not merit a single sentence in a shippable essay. Sometimes parts of this sketching eventually function _as_ an outline. But that's an accidental byproduct of _improved understanding_. The outline isn't the point,the improved understanding is.

## AI overload / interface shock (02-26-2023)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#ai_overload_interface_shock)

Just musing about the next few years of AI systems. Even that short term seems likely to get pretty weird, never mind the long term.

It's challenging to think about, for many reasons. In part because: there's a lot of noise right now, due to the influx of capital and concomitant hype and grifters and naysayers. And despite the hype it's still hard to point to a crucial widely-used product where AI is inarguably the decisive element. But it still seems important to try to understand.

Of course, the future isn't yet written: it's up to us to collectively decide. But it's fun to come up with both generative and analytic models to think about what may happen. Here's one such model. It's not meant as a prediction, just a useful model for thinking about possible futures.

It's this: there will be a widespread sense of _AI overload_ or _interface shock_[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn4): the shock and sense of confusion and bewilderment which occurs as a rapidly growing chunk of society has to keep learning and relearning new AI systems, over and over… and over and over and over and over and over and over.

Copilot, Midjourney, DALL-E, StableDiffusion, GPT3, ChatGPT, Claude, GPT4, Bing, are the very beginning.

Indeed, they're mostly still curiosities, not yet near-essential for knowledge work. But it seems plausible that their near-term successors will be near-essential for knowledge work, perhaps even much more important than Google search is today. Getting the most out of those systems will be like learning to play an easy-to-pick-up musical instrument: satisfying for beginners, but increasing mastery will pay increasing returns. The result: a strong incentive to get better with such systems; and a sense that one _should_ be getting better, indeed, even that one _must_ get better. Furthermore: there's going to be a rapidly changing cast of such systems, over more and more domains; and those systems won't be fixed targets: they will rapidly co-evolve with their respective user bases. It's as though the musical instrument will change and mutate, as fast as you're learning it.

Something like this already happens to programmers: they suffer a kind of API overload: every year, they must pick up a steady stream of frameworks and libraries. I've heard many programmers talk about how overwhelming (and sometimes bewildering) it is. Only it won't just be programmers feeling this overwhelm and bewilderment: this type of interface shock will spread widely, to everyone for whom mastery of such AI systems offers a real advantage in their lives. And it will be done under twin emotional shadows: threats to livelihood; and a perhaps even deeper sense of identity threat, as people re-evaluate their feelings about intelligence and its role in their sense of self. In this model, what we think of as intelligence may change significantly: it will move from "solving the problem" directly to "rapidly exploring and mastering interfaces". A similar change has already occurred in programming, but it'll be across a much broader class of creative and knowledge work.

## The poverty of Moral Systems (01-20-2023)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#poverty_of_moral_systems)

[In a](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#poverty_of_moral_systems) [recent set of notes](https://michaelnotebook.com/otc/index.html) I wrote of my general suspicion of deductive Moral Systems, and in favor of a more exploratory / bricolage approach. I characterized this as being about two competing traditions. One tradition is focused on developing Moral Systems:

> …this seems inspired in many ways by mathematics. Figure out some pretty reasonable basic axioms or models. And then try to explore and understand their consequences. It's not quite proving theorems – the Systems are rarely unambiguous enough for that. But "making arguments" to figure out what is right. And then there's the second tradition, which is what 99.99% of human beings use: just muddling through, talking with friends and family, watching other people, trying to figure out how to live in the world, to be a good person, to do right by others and by oneself.
> 
> I think the second tradition is usually much more powerful and reliable. And the reason is that the world is immensely complicated, and as a result experience is much richer than any such System. It's a situation where, for now, simply exploring reality is in many respects far more challenging than such a System. It's the difference between attempting to deduce biology from a few simple ideas, and determinedly exploring the biosphere. The actual biosphere – the biosphere we can explore – is immensely complicated, and exploring it has (so far) been much more rewarding than attempting to understand things from theoretical first principles. With that said, a benefit of the Moral Systems is that one can push them in ways you can't (easily) in the world. Clever thought experiments, unusual questions – those are genuine generative benefits. But while I have no doubt that generates novel moral ideas, I have my doubts about whether it generates reliable moral insight.
> 
> That's a lot of throat-clearing to say: I'm fundamentally very suspicious of any strong notion of a "Moral System" at all, or even of notions like consistency and implication in such a System… many people want to take them very seriously as a basis for extended lines of reasoning and action. I think that's usually a mistake, resulting in complex (and sometimes selfishly motivated) justifications for actions which would seem obviously wrong to any intelligent 10 year old. When I say this, people interested in Moral Systems sometimes want to debate: such debate seems to me (with rare exceptions) a bad use of my time. They're insisting on using the first tradition, rather than the second. And it's much healthier to relate primarily to people's actual experiences, and only secondarily to Moral Systems or theories of what is good.

Upon reflection, I regret the missed opportunity to name this phenomena: it's pointing out _the poverty of Moral Systems_. In particular: the poverty compared with exploring (moral) reality. Deduction and consistency are fine tools, but they're only a small part of what is needed, and if you insist on relying on them, you're in very poor shape. Emerson was right: a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of small minds; yet many Moral Systems seem to rely on consistency as a basic value.

## Technological progress is instrumental; actual progress is internal to human experience (10-07-2022)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#tech_progress_is_instrumental)

In a [book review](https://rootsofprogress.org/where-is-my-flying-car) on his website "The Roots of Progress", Jason Crawford writes:

> Through maybe the 1950s, visions of the future, although varied, were optimistic. People believed in progress and saw technology as taking us forward to a better world. In the span of a generation, that changed, with the shift becoming prominent by the late 1960s. A “counterculture” arose which did not believe in technology or progress: indeed, a major element of the counterculture was the environmentalist movement, much of which saw technology and industry as actively destroying the Earth.

Later in the review he states, apparently approvingly, that "social activism \[like that done by the environmentalist movement\] is a drain on human capital".

It's a curious point of view, which seems to equate technological advance with progress, and considers any inhibition of such advance as a drain on progress. What makes it curious is that most environmentalists of my acquaintance also believe in progress, in the sense that they want a better life for the next generation, and have ideas about how best to achieve it. Indeed, it was due in part to such activists that modern environmental legislation like the Clean Air Act was passed. Such legislation has likely saved millions of lives, and considerably improved billions of lives. It's had a cost: I have no doubt such legislation has inhibited technological development. Maybe that cost is worse than the harm it has prevented. But it's at least plausible that the benefit has been far more than worth the cost. That is, it's plausible that such regulations and activism are a milestone in human progress, which should be celebrated, not decried as "a drain on human capital".

Technology is often instrumentally useful for improving human lives, but it's not intrinsically good as an end in itself. Ultimately progress is internal: an improvement in the quality of human lives and experience; it doesn't reside directly in technology at all. In that sense, the values expressed by (just to pick two examples) the Sermon on the Mount or the abolitionist movement arguably represent a more intrinsic form of progress than any technology, because they more directly change human experience. More broadly, our most imaginative story-creators and moral entrepreneurs and artists and activists have contributed enormously to human progress.

Of course, science and technology are extremely important enablers of progress. It's far easier to live a good life when you have abundant food and medicine; when you have good housing, and so on. I'm merely making a point about what seems to me some (mistaken) fundamental assumptions I've seen advocated. [I'm sympathetic to Effective Altruism's](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/) approach of "attempt\[ing\] to do for the question 'what is the good?' what science has done for the question 'how does the world work?'. Instead of providing an answer \[the EA Community\] is developing a community that aims to continually improve the answer." Of course, arguably that's what everyone already thinks they're doing for progress: the gung ho technologists and the anti-technology Luddites all think they're arguing for the "correct" form of progress.

Looking at the above, I'm dissatisfied. It seems fine as far as it goes, but badly incomplete. A key thing about science and technology is that they provide a free lunch: as our understanding improves it increases human power and ability to act. That's not always a good thing: certain technologies are mostly just bad. But many seem on net to be positive (albeit with some negative effects). For this reason I can't say I'm a fan of the precautionary principle; it seems mostly like _status quo_ bias. And while I'm very pro science and technology, I instinctively recoil from a certain type of myopic technologist who is always in favor of new tech. But I don't yet have a better principled way of thinking about these things.

## The role of "explanation" in AI (09-30-2022)

](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#role_of_explanation_in_AI)

There's a critique of current work on AI expressed as variations on the argument: "Look, some such systems are impressive as demos. But the people creating the systems have little detailed understanding of how they work or why. And until we have such an understanding we're not really making progress on AI." This argument is then sometimes accompanied by (often rather dogmatic) assertions about what characteristics science "must" have.

I have some instinctive sympathy for such arguments. My original field of physics is full of detailed and often rather satisfying explanations of how things work. So too, of course, are many other fields. And historically new technologies often _begin_ with tinkering and intuitive folk models, but technological progress is then enabled by greatly improved explanations of the underlying phenomena. You can build a sundial with a pretty hazy understanding of the solar system; to build an atomic clock requires a deep understanding of many phenomena.

Work on AI appears to be trying to violate this historic model of improvement. Yes, we're developing what seem to be better and better systems in the tinkering mode. But progress in understanding how those systems work seems to lag far behind. Papers often contain rather unconvincing just-so "explanations" of how the systems work (or were inspired). But the standards of such explanation are often extremely low: they really are just-so stories. Witnessing this, some people conclude that work in AI is not "real" scientific progress, but is rather a kind of mirage.

But I wonder. I'm inclined to suspect we're in a Feyerabendian "Anything Goes" moment here, where prior beliefs about how science "must" proceed are being overthrown. And we'll wonder in retrospect why we held those prior beliefs.

The underlying thing that's changed is the ease of trying and evaluating systems. If you wanted to develop improved clocks in the past you had to laboriously build actual systems, and then rigorously test them. A single new design might take months or years to build and test. Detailed scientific understanding was important because it helped you figure out which part of the (technological) design space to search in. When each instance of a new technology is expensive, you need detailed explanations which tell you where to search.

By contrast, much progress in AI takes a much more agnostic approach to search. Instead, of using detailed explanations to guide the search it uses a combination of: (a) general architectures; (b) trying trillions (or more) of possibilities, guided by simple ideas (like gradient descent) for improvement; and (c) the ability to _recognize_ progress. This is a radically different mode of experimentation, only made possible by the advent of machines which can do extremely rapid symbol manipulation.

In the clock case it'd be analogous to having a universal constructor capable of extremely rapidly trying trillions of different clock designs, recognizing progress in timekeeping, and iteratively making tiny improvements. I'm not sure "trillions" would be nearly enough there – three-dimensional material design is very combinatorially demanding! But the point should be clear: at _some_ scale of trying new designs, a new mode of progress would likely be possible, quite different from prior modes, and perhaps violating prior theories of how progress "should" happen. People would complain about these new horologists and how they didn't understand what they were doing; in the meantime, the new horologists would be building clocks of unprecedented accuracy.

It's easy to critique this story. You can come back with: "Oh, but search-and-recognize will inevitably be bottlenecked at crucial points that need genuine understanding". But: (a) notice how this has shifted from an argument-of-principle to an argument-of-practice citing _ad hoc_ bottlenecks; and (b) the people making this argument don't seem to be the ones actually building better systems, which is the argument-of-practice we ultimately care about. I'm sympathetic to it, but only up to a very limited point.

**Postscript on intent:** No intent to claim originality here. And I certainly don't expect this to convince anyone who doesn't want to be convinced. This is just me working a few thoughts out.

## Stated versus revealed preference for risk

Funders at maybe half a dozen science funders have told me variations on: "Oh, I'd love to fund more high-risk projects, but scientists won't submit them, even though I strongly encourage it. They're so conservative!"

I am near certain that at each of those funders a safe, incremental grant application is far more likely to be funded than something that's actually high risk. The funders are a bit like people who tell their local grocer that they want green beans, but only ever purchase tomatoes. A sensible grocer would rapidly stop offering the beans, and just set out the tomatoes.

If funders genuinely want high-risk projects, they must ensure such projects are _more likely_ to be funded than low-risk projects. They need unsuccessful applicants who submitted low-risk work to walk away feeling "gosh, my project wasn't daring enough".

None of this addresses when and whether risk is good, which is a separate issue. I'm just observing that there seems to often be a large gap between stated and revealed preferences. They don't purchase what they say they want to buy.

## AI safety: an oversimplified model (03-17-2022)

A very oversimplified 3-level model of AI Safety. Certainly not intended to be new or especially insightful, it's just helpful for me to write down to make my thinking a tiny bit more precise.

1.  The narrow alignment problem, which seems to be where a lot of technical AI safety work focuses ("let's make sure my nuke only goes off when I want it to go off, explodes where I want it to explode _etc_"). For AGI this has the additional challenge (beyond the nuke analogy) that human intent is often ambiguous and difficult to debug. We think we want A; then realize we want A'; then A'' etc; then we discover that even once it's clear to us what we want, it's hard to express, and easy to misinterpret. This problem is a combined generalization of interpersonal communication and programming debugging, with the additional problem of massive unexpected emergent effects thrown in (of the type "we didn't realize that optimizing our metrics would accidentally lead to authoritarian dictatorships").

2.  But even if you can magically solve the narrow alignment problem, you still have the problem that evil actors will have bad intent ("let's make sure bad guys / bad orgs / rogue nations don't have nukes"). In this case, Moore's Law & progress in algorithms means if anyone has AGI, then pretty soon everyone (left) will have AGI. In this sense, AI safety is a special case of eliminating evil actors.

    -   The caveat to this is if you can do something like the non-proliferation treaties, whereby the most powerful actors use their power to slow down other actors from acquiring AGI. Useful as a slowing, though over the long run I doubt it will work. It's hard enough to do with nukes and chemical weapons; software is going to be much harder.
3.  The problem of agency: if AGIs become independent agents with goals of their own, and they're (vastly) more powerful than humans, then humanity as a whole will be in the standard historic situation of the powerless next to the powerful. When the powerful entities' goals overlap with the powerless, usually the powerful entities get what they want, even if it hurts the powerless.

No doubt there's all kinds of things wrong with this model, or things omitted. Certainly, I'm very ignorant of thinking about this. Still, I have the perhaps mistaken sense that much work on AI safety is just nibbling round the edges, that the only thing really likely to work is to do something like non-proliferation right now. That's hard to do – many strong economic and defense and research interests would, at present, oppose it. But it seems like the natural thing to do. Note that ridicule is a common tactic for dismissing this argument: proof-of-impossibility-by-failure-of-imagination. But it's not much of an argument. To be clear: I don't think the standard AI safety arguments are very good, either. But they don't need to be: this is a rare case where I'm inclined to take the precautionary principle very seriously.

## On the invention of discovery, and of experiment

(Riffing on my read of David Wootton's book "The Invention of Science")

In chess, a grandmaster and a total beginner may well play the same move, or even the same series of moves, in a position. And yet the underlying reasons for the action – the theory and context of the moves – will be completely different.

Magnus Carlsen playing pawn to e4 is not the same – not remotely the same! – as me playing pawn to e4.

An analogous phenomenon occurs with many actions: ostensibly the "same" action may have quite a different meaning, due to the surrounding context. And sometimes we want a verb (or its corresponding noun) to refer to the literal action, and sometimes we want a verb/noun to refer to the fuller context.

I've been thinking about this in connection with the notions of discovery and of experiment. It's difficult to make precise the sense in which they are modern notions. Certainly, I have no doubt that even going back to prehistoric times people occasionally carried out literal actions very similar to what we would today call discovery or experiment. Yet, while there may well be a literal similarity to what we call discovery or experiment today, the fuller context was very different.

In both cases, there are differences in both the surrounding theory (how people think about it, what it means), and in the surrounding context.

I won't try to enumerate all those – it could easily be a book!

But I am particularly fascinated by the irreversible nature of discovery – a discovery in the modern sense must involve a near-irreversible act of amplification, so knowledge is spread around the world, becoming part of our collective memory.

This may accidentally have occurred in pre-historic times – it probably happened with fire, for instance. But today we have many institutions much of whose role is about making this irreversible act of amplification happen.

## Reflecting on what I've learned from David Wootton's "The Invention of Science" (12-21-2021)

Perhaps the most important thing so far is that a major part of the scientific revolution was that _discovery became near-irreversible_. Many people prior to Brahe or Galileo or _etc_ may have had some of their insights. But those insights for the most part _did not spread throughout the culture_.

This spread was accomplished by a combination of novel technologies (journals, the printing press, citation) + a new political economy (norms about citation, priority, replicability, and the reputation economy). The result was a powerful amplifier for new ideas. And amplification is a peculiarly one way process: it's difficult to put an idea genie back in the bottle.

I forget who said it (Merton?) but the discoverer of something is not the first person to discover it, but the _last_, the person whose discovery results in the spread of the idea throughout the culture. That requires an idea amplifier, an irreversible process by which an idea becomes broadly known.

## Information overload as a consequence of the benefits of information (12-21-2021)

A common story is that information overload is the result of too much knowledge being produced too quickly. It harks back to an (imagined, nostalgic) history in which much less knowledge was being produced.

There's an error implicit in this story.

"Information overload" is fundamentally a feeling. If you're a person with little interest in or use for ideas and information, you won't feel much information overload.

But suppose you're a person who benefits a great deal from ideas. Every extra hour you spend reading or imbibing good lectures or \[etc\] you find of great benefit. This may be true especially if you're in a competitive intellectual field, a field where mastery of more information gives you a competitive advantage. You'll naturally feel a great deal of pressure to choose well what you read, and to read more. That's the feeling of information overload.

In other words, the feeling of information overload isn't produced by there being too much knowledge. It's produced by the fact that spending more time imbibing knowledge may produce very high returns, creating pressure to spend ever more time on it; furthermore, there is no natural ordering on what to imbibe.

One reason this matters is because people often think that if they use just the right tool or approach or system, information overload will go away. In fact, better tooling often improves the returns to imbibing information, and so can actually increase information overload. This isn't universally true – tools which increase your sense of informational self-efficacy may reduce the sense of overload. But there's more than a grain of truth to it.

(Parenthetically, let me point to Ann Blair's book [Too Much to Know](https://smile.amazon.com/Too-Much-Know-Scholarly-Information/dp/0300165390/ref=sr_1_2?crid=2G8MDPCXK2Y0E), an account of information overload before the modern age.)

## The "experimental program" as a first-class object in how we improve our understanding of the world (12-21-2021)

There is a caricature of the history of science in which the notion of comparing theory to experiment originated in the 16th century. (Often ascribed to Bacon). Of course, this is a (gross) caricature and oversimplification; obviously, our prehistoric ancestors learned from experience! I'd be shocked if it's not possible to draw pretty much a straight line from such an ancestor (or, say, a mystic like Pythagoras), to a modern scientist with very sophisticated ideas about how they devise their experimental program.

This discrepancy bugs me. It's been bugging me since I first heard the story told, probably at about age 7 or 8. There's something ever-so-slightly off.

I suspect that the main transition is in having an experimental program at all, some notion of a (theory!) of how to explore nature. Talk to a scientist and they'll have hundreds of ideas for possible experiments, detailed thoughts on costs and benefits and variations and failure modes and so on. What's new in the modern era is the understanding that it's worth thinking about those things. Put a different way: we've always done experiments. It's just that in modern science an experimental programme – a set of ideas about how to explore nature – has been a first-class object in how we improve our understanding of the world.

## Internalized goals, not written goals, are valuable (12-21-2021)

A lot has been written about the value of written goals. Increasingly, I think that's a mistake. Goals are only valuable if they've become deeply internalized, part of you. Writing is helpful insofar as it helps achieve that end.

## Creative workers should design their own filing system (11-25-2021, revised 11-27-2021)

## The surprising value of merging files (11-25-2021)

I'm sure this is obvious to many people, but it's something I only discovered recently: the unexpected value of collecting up (and often merging) files on closely related subjects.

There are certain topics I come back to over and over again, in files spread all over my hard disk. Systematically collecting them up and in many cases merging them has been surprisingly helpful.

## Programmable money and smart contracts are powerful ideas, and so are likely here to stay (11-21-2021)

There's this strange, huge fault line around the question: is crypto real? Lots of believers, shouting "this is the future". Lots of haters, shouting "it's all a scam". But the question itself is mostly a mistake. A better fulcrum question is: are decentralized programmable money and smart contracts here to stay? In particular, would a functioning system of programmable money and smart contracts enable extraordinary (and very valuable) new forms of co-ordination behavior? I believe the answer is obviously yes. This doesn't mean many or all of today's cryptocurrencies won't fade out; but it does mean the future will almost certainly involve some descendant of some of these ideas.

## The relationship between research and writing (11-17-2021)

When writing any kind of essay or book (research or non), I find that I (nearly always) have to write the first draft in linear order.

This is in strong tension with research, where you are trying to improve your understanding as much as possible. That's not something that can be done in linear order. It's more of a stochastic upward spiral. In that sense, it makes sense to bounce backward and forward. You're trying to write snippets that (you hope) will be in the final piece, and trying to find pieces to improve wherever possible.

Ultimately, a work of research requires some strong core insight, some important idea or piece of evidence that is new. Usually you don't have that when you start. And so you are exploring, digging down, trying to understand, unearthing and trying to crystallize out partial nuggets of understanding, until you feel you really have some strong core insight, a foundation for a written report. At that point you can attempt the linear draft.

## Memory systems as a way of concentrating your experience (10-11-2021)

In everyday life an astounding number of things happen. A tiny few seem really significant. Memory systems let you distill those things out, so that you will return to them again and again. They're a way of concentrating your experience.

## Feedback rules, selection effects, end states (Oct 2021)

What feedback rule does a person, organization, or ecosystem follow to govern change and learning and growth? In particular, what does that feedback rule select for? What it selects for determines much about what one gets.

An infinite number of examples may be given.

(1) Does the political media follow a feedback rule that rewards improvements in the quality of people's understanding of government? No, except incidentally and in certain narrow ways; as far as I can tell, people who follow political media often end up more informed on a small subset of (very narrow) issues, and less informed in many crucial ways. In particular, they're much more likely to believe misconceptions that serve the feedback rule. This applies strongly to all parties, and requires little in the way of dishonesty, just ordinary muddleheadedness.

An example which personally I find amazing: a surprising number of people (including people in the media) genuinely believe Facebook caused Trump 2016. This is despite the fact that Trump spent only a small fraction of his budget on Facebook, and most of that late in the election cycle. The mainstream media did far more to cause Trump. I don't mean just Fox, I mean CNN-NYT-etc-etc-etc, the entire set, including, of course, a significant role for Fox. It's certainly true that Facebook played a role, at the margin (as did many, many things). But a much more significant effect was Trump knowing how to manipulate the mainstream media. And the mainstream media seem to have no way of understanding that – it's not inside the feedback loop that governs how they change. In fact, quite the reverse: Trump almost certainly drove revenue for them; they are incented to have a candidate like Trump. Most members of the media seem to understand this point – it's been emphasized by some of the most prominent executives – but then don't connect it the fact that "Facebook caused Trump" is a false narrative. It's not that they're lying. They believe the narrative because of systemic incentives.

I realize the last paragraph will be treated by many as evidence I'm off my rocker. I'm certainly not trying to say Facebook didn't play a role; but it was one of many factors; it was almost certainly a much smaller role than the mainstream media; and the mainstream media doesn't understand this, in considerable part because of their incentives. Certainly, this story violates conventional narratives. And I haven't provided a detailed argument, merely my conclusions. Maybe I'm wrong. But I don't think so. And when you hear most people try to give their argument for why Facebook caused Trump, it quickly dissolves into assertions which are either wrong, or based on extremely weak evidence[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/sporadica.html#fn6).

(2) In universities, grant overhead is inside the feedback loop. And the result is that universities systematically select for whatever grant agencies select for. This is a (massive) centralizing force. It's weak in most individual given instances. But over decades the effect is cumulative, and enormous. It's too strong to say research is centrally controlled, but there's certainly strong tendencies in that direction.
