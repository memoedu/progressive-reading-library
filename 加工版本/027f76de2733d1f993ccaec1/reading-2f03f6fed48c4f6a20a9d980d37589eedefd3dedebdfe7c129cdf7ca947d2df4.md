# 一些 methodological notes

---

## 正文

在这里，我提出一些关于 consciousness 的想法，尤其要 arguing against 这样一种观点：consciousness 在某种意义上并非 real，或者是一种 illusion。

Descartes 是 _cogito ergo sum_ 的著名提出者。他 argued，即使你的 senses 受到 deceived，你仍然可以确定自己是 conscious。因此，在我们持有的 beliefs 中，consciousness 会是 Tier 1 truth。至少对我而言，情况确实如此。

请考虑以下这一组事物：quantum field theory、theory of relativity、neuroscience、cognitive science、evolutionary theory、external world realism、physicalism、naturalism 和 materialism。

现在，这里还有另一件事物：consciousness。

我宁愿把第一组事物扔进垃圾桶、付之一炬，也不愿放弃把 consciousness 视为 real 的事物。既然我刚才这样说了，有人可能会认为，我不该继续 debate 这个问题，因为试图改变我的想法毫无意义。但这并不 true。我的想法仍然可以改变。因此，作为一个 fallible 的人，我在理论上可能会 mistaken，给某个事物 assigning 一个类似于 Dirac's delta 的 prior。但我将 argue，实际情况并非如此，我并没有 mistaken。

请注意，我并没有说 rejecting consciousness 就等于 rejecting 第一组中的那些事物。accepting eliminativism 除了与 consciousness 存在 conflict 之外，并没有什么 incoherent 之处。因此，如果一个人仅仅 rejects 那一点，就不必 reject 对上述 sciences 的任何其他 beliefs。

## 一些 methodological notes

我们 believing 事物时，如何才算 justified？我见过的最佳回答是 foundationalism 的一种形式：如果在我们看来 P，并且我们的 belief in P 没有 defeaters，那么我们 believing in P 就是 justified（[Huemer 2007](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2007.00002.x/full)、[Moretti 2015](https://academic.oup.com/analysis/article-abstract/75/2/296/164984/Phenomenal-Conservatism1?redirectedFrom=fulltext)）。例如，在这个 framework 中，我们会这样处理 Müller-Lyer illusion：在我们看来，一个 arrow 比另一个 bigger，但我们的 seeming 有一个 defeater，即我们已经 measured 这些 arrows。因此，我们 believing 这些 arrows 就是它们看上去的样子，并不是 justified。在这样做时，我们 appealed to 一个更 solid 的 seeming：ruler 是 straight，我们的眼睛在 measuring 这些 arrows 时作出了 accurate judgements，等等。对于 consciousness，default position 是：我们确实是 conscious，而且 consciousness 是 real。但这个 position 是否存在 defeaters？正如我在上面提到的，要 dislodge 对 consciousness realism 的 belief，我们需要一个比“我是 conscious”更 stronger 的 premise，而这样的 premises 并不存在。当然，eliminativist 并不同意。

一个人也可以转而采取 coherentist approach，并以 coherence 作为 criterion of truth。如果这样做，他可能会发现，eliminativism 与 science 非常 coheres，而 consciousness realism 则更成问题，仿佛一个悬在外面的 isolated 事物，远离那一大簇关于 physical world 的 interconnected beliefs。我并不喜欢 coherentism，但一个人即使是 coherentist，也不必 committed to eliminativism。或许仍然可以 argued：consciousness 与其他每一个 belief 都 tightly linked，因为要有 beliefs，我们就必须是 conscious。

## Hard Problem（难题）

Michael Huemer 对 mind-body problem 的 exposition，可以很好地引入[我想讨论的问题](http://www.owl232.net/mind.htm)

> 请考虑以下五个 tenable theses：
> 
> | 1. | 对于任何 system，关于 whole 的每个 fact 都是 parts 的 nature 和 relations 的 necessary consequence。 | | 2. | 人是由 atoms made of 的。 | | 3. | Atoms 是 purely physical objects，除了 physical properties 和彼此之间的 physical relations，别无其他。 | | 4. | 人具有 mental states。 | | 5. | 任何 ascribing 一个 mental predicate 的 statement，都不能从任何一组 purely physical descriptions 中 derived。 |

关于 consciousness 及其与 brain 的 relation，不同 positions 会 accept 或 reject 其中的一些。它们不能全部同时被 held，但每一个看起来都 plausible。Premise one 本质上是 ontological reductionism 的一个 statement，在我看来它是 true。Premise two 和 premise three 是我们对 universe 的了解（可以用 fields、quarks 等来 Substitute）。Premise three 通过 introspection 看起来是 true。Premise five 可以理解为：

> 在 (5) 中，“derived”指的是“logically derived”，也就是 fundamental theorem of calculus 可以从 axioms of arithmetic 和一些 definitions 中 derived 的那个意义上的 derived。它并不是指“caused”。这意味着，不可能从某人或任何其他事物的 physical description 中 deduce 这个人的 conscious experiences 的 quality。如果给出了 universe 的一个 physical description，那么，例如，指出某人处于 pain 之中，始终会是一项 additional information。$$...$$
> 
> 最后，还有 (5)。有什么 reason 认为它是 true 呢？我们可以将它与一些 similar principles 进行 compare，以理解大致的意思。在 moral philosophy 中，有一个有时被称为 Hume's law 的 principle，它说，不可能从 descriptive judgement 中 derive 一个 normative judgement。normative judgement 是关于什么是 good 或 bad、right 或 wrong 的 judgement，而 descriptive judgement 基本上就是除此之外的任何 judgement。另一种说法是，你不能从一个“is”中 deduce 一个“ought”：你不能仅仅根据 what is the case 来 derive what ought to be the case。这个 principle 几乎得到普遍承认。而它只是一个更 general 的 pattern 的一部分。例如，你不能从任何一组不 describe distances 的 statements 中 derive 一个 describing distances 的 statement。你不能从任何一组 non-color statements 中 derive 一个关于 colors 的 statement。你不能从 non-geometrical 的 statements 中 derive geometrical statements。一般而言，如果一个 inference 的 conclusion 谈的是一件事，而 premises 谈的是另一件事，那么这个 inference 就是 invalid。同样，你不能从 physical description 中 derive 一个 mental description，这是一个 conceptual truth。毕竟，只要考虑一些 physical concepts，例如 spatial/geometrical properties、mass、force 和 electric charge。认为这些 concepts 能以某种方式被用来 explain 处于 pain 之中是什么感受，这 plausible 吗？无论你如何谈论 particles 的 masses、positions 和 forces，你都不会因此对任何事物 ascribed 任何 mental states。

Eliminativists 会 deny 4。Cartesan dualists 会 deny 2。Idealists 会 deny 2-3。Property dualism 可能会 deny 1 或 3，而 mind/brain identity theory 可能会 deny 1-5。Panpsychists 则 reject 3。

有些人说，决定哪些 systems 是 conscious，取决于我们自己。这并不 run counter to premise five。像“我把 exhibit 某种 external behaviour、以某种方式 internally constituted，并具有一定 degree 的 [Phi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_information_theory) 的 systems 视为 conscious”这样的说法，其中已经包含了对 consciousness 的 reference。我不同意这个 proposal，但它仍然会构成对其中一个 premises 的 rejection。

## materialist 的舞步

那么，假设我们想认真应对上面的问题，并且想 accept consciousness。一个通情达理的 materialist 会怎么说？确实，人可以通过 embracing eliminativism，以 denying 显而易见之事为代价，获得内心的平静；也可以停留在 uncertainty 的状态，并保留对 consciousness 的 belief。我所说的 materialist dance，指的是一个人在考虑 consciousness 的 possible explanations 时，可能发生的 positions 转换。

于是，我们开始思考 consciousness 这个 idea。我们了解一些 physics，而且我们不 believe，也不想 believe 任何古怪或玄乎的东西。我们也想保留对 consciousness 的 belief，因此首先 rejecting eliminativism。

然后，我们苦思冥想，想到 consciousness 其实是对 brain activity 的某些 patterns 的 poetic name。这就是 identity theory。它不 require 对 physics 作任何 modification，而且允许人说 consciousness 是 real。不错，是不是？

Sean Caroll 在他的上一本书 The Big Picture 中为这个 view 作了 defends。

> 如果 reality 中有哪一个 aspect 会 causes 人们 doubt 一种 purely physical 且 naturalist 的世界 conception，那就是 consciousness 的 existence。而要说服 skeptics 可能很难，因为即使最乐观的 neuroscientist 也不声称拥有一套 complete 且 comprehensive 的 consciousness theory。相反，我们所拥有的是一种 expectation：当我们确实获得这种 understanding 时，它将与 Core Theory 的 basic tenets 完全 compatible——是 physical reality 的一部分，而不是与之分离的东西。
> 
> 为什么应当有这样的 expectation？这部分归结于对我们的 credences 进行 Bayesian reasoning。unified physical world 这一 idea 在许多情境中都取得了巨大成功，我们完全有理由认为，它也能够 account for consciousness。不过，我们也可以提出一个 positive case，说明 alternatives 并不怎么行得通。如果说，理解 consciousness 如何能被顺畅地纳入 physical reality 已经不容易，那么想象它还能是什么别的东西就更难了。我们在这里的主要目标不是 explain consciousness 究竟如何 work，而是说明，在一个由 impersonal laws of nature 所 governed 的世界里，它是_能够_ work 的。
> 
> 在本章和下一章中，我们将着重介绍 consciousness 的一些使其特殊的 features。接下来的几章，我们将考察一些 arguments：无论 consciousness 是什么，它都必须不只是谈论遵循 conventional laws of physics、处于 motion 中的 ordinary matter 的一种方式。我们将发现，这些 arguments 没有一个很有说服力；我们也将比最初更加确信，我们 human beings 连同 thoughts、emotions 以及其他一切，都是 natural world 不可分割的一部分。

在讨论过程中，Carroll 不得不承认一些古怪的事情：例如，Chinese room 是 conscious 的；或者，一大群彼此 passing messages、simulating 某个人的 brain 的人，也是 conscious 的（也就是说，在这些 individuals 各自的 individual consciousnesses 之上，还存在某种 conscious 的东西）

> 想象一下，对一个人的 connectome 进行 mapping，不仅是在某一时刻，而是随着它在一生中的 develops 来进行。然后——既然我们已经投身于毫无实际可行性的 thought experiments——想象我们把这个人一生中每一次 signal crosses a synapse 都毫无遗漏地 record 下来。把所有这些 information Store 在 hard drive 上，或者写在（数量大得荒唐的）纸张上。这个关于一个人的 mental processes 的 record 本身会是“conscious”的吗？我们真的需要随时间发生的 development，还是说，一个人的 brain 的 physical state 的 evolution 的 static representation，就能 capture consciousness 的 essence。
> 
> 这些 examples 虽然异想天开，却具有说明作用。是的，用某种完全不同的 substance（无论是 neuristors 还是人）来 reproducing brain 的 processes，当然应该算作 consciousness。但不，把东西 printing 出来，形成那些 processes 的 static representation，则不应该。
> 
> 从 poetic-naturalism 的 perspective 来看，当我们谈论 consciousness 时，并不是在 discovering universe 中某种 fundamental 的东西。这不像 searching for causes 某种已知 disease 的 virus；在那种情况下，我们十分清楚自己在找哪类东西，只是想用 instruments 来 detect 它，以便 describe 它是什么。与“entropy”和“heat”一样，“consciousness”和“understanding”这些 concepts，是我们为了让自己能以更 useful、更 efficient 的 descriptions 来描述世界而_发明_的。我们应当据此 judge 一种关于 consciousness 究竟是什么的 conception：它是否提供了一种 useful 的谈论世界的方式——一种能够 accurately fits the data，并对正在发生的事情 offers insight 的方式。

> 作为 poetic naturalists，我们要做的基本上就是这些。consciousness 的 attributes，包括我们的 qualia 和 inner subjective experiences，都是谈论我们称为 human beings 的 collections of atoms 的 effective behavior 的 useful 方式。Consciousness 不是 illusion，但它也不意味着偏离我们目前所理解的 laws of physics。

> 根据 poetic naturalism，philosophical zombies 根本是 inconceivable 的，因为“consciousness”是谈论某些 physical systems 的 behavior 的一种特定方式。“experiencing the redness of red”这个 phrase，属于我们用来谈论 underlying physical system 的 emergent behavior 的 higher-level vocabulary 的一部分，而不是与 physical system 分离的东西。这并不意味着它不是 real；我的 redness experience 是完全 real 的，你的也是。它之所以是 real，与 fluids、椅子、大学和法典之所以是 real，完全是同一种意义——也就是说，在某个 domain of applicability 内，它们在对 natural world 的某一部分进行 successful description 时，发挥着 essential role。
> 
> 我们的 mental experiences 或 qualia 实际上不是 separate 的_东西_，而是我们针对 ordinary physical things _所讲述的故事_中的 useful parts；这个 idea 让许多人难以接受。

你也可以在这里加入 Daniel Dennett 的 view。

按照这个 view，consciousness 不是 really real，而只是 merely real。这难道不像 eliminativism 吗？Caroll 在书中并没有真正回答。不过，一种 charitable interpretation 大概会是：“是的，当然，consciousness 在 strong sense 上不是 real，但我们仍然可以讲述关于它的故事，并用这个词谈论 universe 的某些部分。”可以比较一下 ethics 中 ethical relativist 的 position：“是的，当然，ethics 在 moral realist 的意义上不是 real，但把这个或那个说成 right 或 wrong，作为‘社会这么认为’的简写，是 useful 的。”这最终分别 amounts to eliminativism 和 nihilism，只不过裹上了一层诗意的糖衣。Poetic naturalism 并不试图掩饰这一点：它的名字里甚至就有 poetic。不过，一旦被追问，poetic naturalist 或 identity theorist 就不得不承认，按照 real 的通常含义，对他们来说 consciousness 并不是 real。没有 qualia，没有 thoughts，没有 feelings，没有 beliefs。只有 neurons 之间的 interactions，别无其他。

但这样一来，我们就全都是 philosophical zombies 了。选择用不同的方式称呼事物，并不 change fundamental reality 的本来面貌。把勺子叫作叉子，并不会使它成为叉子。说“atoms 以这种方式发生的 movement”就是 consciousness，并不会使它成为 consciousness（在 realist 的意义上）。因此，zombies 就不仅是 conceivable 的东西，而是真正 actual 的！确实，当 eliminativist Brian Tomasik 被问到是否 believes 我们全都是 zombies 时，他回答，是的，我们就是。

这可能让人难以接受，于是人们可能转向 epiphenomenalism。这里，我们又看到了那些熟悉的说法：“brain 是一个 complex system，诸如此类”，或者“Consciousness 是某些 biological systems 所做的一种特殊事情，不能仅仅通过 replicating 其 functioning 就在 in silico 中 replicated”（[Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_naturalism) 和 [Pigliucci](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1S-7CWOLOtLRDmMiS7LtVxELssUi3OI1-UcrPAzGMuH4/pub)）。我曾经 believe 这一点。

如果我们这样做，就已经 abandoned materialism，进入了 dualism。有些人可能会抗议，坚持说 epiphenomenalism 仍然是 materialist 的，但在 academic philosophy of mind 中，materialism 指的是 type-A 或 type-B theories。有人可能会说，那又怎样？

问题恰恰在于，如果 consciousness 是 epiphenomenal 的，那么 causes 我谈论 consciousness 的就不是 consciousness。如果我们 subscribe to epiphenomenalism，Consciousness 就不在 causing 任何事情。Eliezer Yudkowsky 在[这里](http://lesswrong.com/lw/p7/zombies_zombies/)提出了这个 argument。这 leads to 一种古怪的情况：我的 zombie 会写出与我所写相同的东西，却并不是 conscious 的。这难道不古怪吗？如果我的 zombie 不是 conscious 的，它为什么会谈论 consciousness？同样，如果 consciousness 并没有 causing _我_写下关于 consciousness 的文字（在 epiphenomenalism 中，causality 只沿一个 direction 运行），那么我为什么会写关于 consciousness 的文字？

我在[这里，](https://nintil.com/2016/07/10/p-zombies-are-still-undead/)写过一篇回应 zombies 的 inconceivability 的文章，但 epiphenomenalism 仍然是一个令人困扰的 view。我可以 conceive 一个由 zombies 构成的 universe：它们做我们所做的一切，却全都是关于 consciousness 的 eliminativists。但在一个像我们这样的世界中，每个人都做着同样的事情，这似乎会使我们 believe，我们围绕 consciousness 所做的事情与它本身无关。我认为，我之所以写关于 consciousness 的文字，是因为我是 conscious 的。但也许我写关于 consciousness 的文字，只是因为我无法自已，而且我还受到了 deluded：假定 determinism，我的 mind 就只是一台误以为自己在 causing 事情、实际上却并未如此的摄像机。那会是一种 illusion。如果是这样，也就没那么古怪了。假定“每个人都是 mechanical robot 的 view”，我们退后一步，把一切看作原原本本的 laws of physics 在发挥作用，那么我可以把自己看成一个被 physical forces 和 stimuli 推来推去、写着东西的 body，同时还有这台作为我的 mind 的摄像机看着一切。当然，当一个被动的旁观者很无聊，于是它 forms beliefs，以为自己实际上在主导一切。我不是在 endorsing 这个 view，只是在说明，consciousness 怎么可能并没有 causing 我的文字，从而说明 zombies 怎么会是 conceivable 的。

不过，假设我们不喜欢这种 epiphenomenal explanation，因为我们确信 consciousness 确实会做些事情，就像我（以及 Eliezer）似乎所认为的那样。于是，我们跳上了 interactionist dualist 这条船。紧接着，我们就面对 QFT argument。Sean Carroll [再次说道](http://www.preposterousuniverse.com/blog/2008/02/18/telekinesis-and-quantum-field-theory/)：

> 在与用 mind 弯曲勺子相关的 meter-sized scales 上，所允许的最强 possible new force 大约会是 gravity 的 _one billionth_。而且别忘了，gravity 实在太 weak，无法弯曲勺子。
> 
> 就是这样。我们已经说完了。深刻的教训是，虽然 science 并非无所不知，但也不是“怎样都行”。有一些 well-defined 的 physical phenomena 的 regimes，我们在其中_确实_知道事物如何运作，仅此而已。要寻找新的、出人意料的 phenomena，就得去那些 regimes 之外。你不需要制定繁复的 double-blind protocols，来评判那些自称 psychics 的人的 abilities。我们对 laws of physics 的 knowledge 已经 rules them out。与此相反的 Speculations 并非大胆的先见者的领地，而是疯子的梦想。

虽然他是在 argues against telekinesis，但我们可以把这个 argument extend 到 consciousness。如果 consciousness causes 什么，那么我们应该能够 detect 那个 force。根据 wikipedia 上的一张[方便查阅的图表](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction)，在 small scales 上，gravity 原本就比其他 forces 极其 weak，因此，更 weaker 的东西对 brain 的 effect 就会是 negligible，被其他 forces 淹没。不过，这仍然并不 rule out 那种 possibility。所需要的也许只是一种 minuscule force，或者一种 consciousness-field！Implausible 并不等于 impossible！

注意，Carroll 说：

> 任何可能隐藏在那里的 new forces，要么（远远）过于 short-range，无法 effect 日常物体，要么（远远）过于 weak，无法产生 readily observable effects。

而我们可能只需要作用于 brain 中的 ions 或 atoms 的 tiny forces。于是，interactionist 可能会想看看 Bohm、Penrose、Popper 或 Stapp 等人的 proposals，它们提出某种 quantum effect mediating consciousness（也就是那句臭名昭著的话：‘Quantum mechanics 看起来很古怪，consciousness 也是，也许它们是 related 的’）。但这在某些人听来可能太诡异了，于是 interactionism 就成了 materialist dance 中又一个被抛弃的 view。

于是，我们接着转向 [neutral monism](http://nautil.us/issue/47/consciousness/is-matter-conscious)。在 consciousness 难题的 possible solutions 中，它是最不容易理解的一个。我对其 motivation 的理解是这样的：想象 universe 中充满了四处运动的球。球之间的箭头，也就是 laws of physics，describe 这些球的 interactions。但这些球是什么？它们的 intrinsic nature 是什么？或者，用 [David Pearce](https://www.physicalism.com/) 最喜欢的说法，是什么向 equations 中吹入了火焰？于是，在这些球的内部，我们找到了 phenomenal properties。所有东西里面都会有一点点 consciousness：panprotopsychism。这个 view preserves 原样的 laws of physics，保留了对 consciousness 的 belief，而且似乎不会碰到‘没有任何东西 causes 我关于 consciousness 的文字’这个问题，因为它 claims，physics 就是 consciousness，而不是一个 epiphenomenon。

为了理解这一点，这里的想法是：如果我们说 universe 是由 fields 构成的，那么那些 fields 归根结底就是 phenomenal experience 的 fields（是的，这听起来仍然很古怪）。你的头部正在以一种非常 specific 的方式 physically implementing 某种非常 complex calculation，而成为那个正在被 implemented、被‘unfolded by Nature’的 calculation，是有某种感受的。

Brian Tomasik 在[这里](http://reducing-suffering.org/not-type-f-monist/)对此作了 reply。基本上，neutral monism 的问题在于，由于它让 laws of physics 保持 unchanged，我们仍然无法将自己对 consciousness 的 belief causally trace 到 consciousness 本身。第二个问题是，consciousness 的 micro-particles 仍然必须被 aggregated 成一个 whole unified consciousness（combination problem）。

如果这在你看来仍然很古怪，那你就没辙了。于是，你可能开始考虑，自己最初关于 consciousness 是 real 的 supposition，也许是 misguided 的。于是，你开始考虑 eliminativism。

## 支持 eliminativism 的 argument

你可能会这样想：science 能把事情弄对，而 human beings 在 thinking 时是 biased 的，也会 make mistakes；对于这个诡异的 conscious 东西，也许 science 这座宏伟大厦是 right 的，比你是 right 的更有可能。而且 consciousness 看起来与你关于 nature 的 network of beliefs 实在相距甚远！我们可以想象某种 causal graph，其中 nature 中的一切都通过 forces of nature 与一切 connected，从而产生漂亮的 bidirectional interactions……然后，却冒出这么一个你不知道该放在哪里的东西。也许根本没有这个东西！过去，我们曾经怀疑 life 是否有什么神奇之处，还以为存在某种 elan vital，构成了 inanimate 与 alive 之间的 difference，而现在我们知道，从头到尾都只是 atoms 及其 interactions。Consciousness 最后也会被发现是同样的东西，是另一种 elan vital；真要说有什么不同，那就是它比最初那个还更 intuitive。

不过，还是 Cogito ergo sum。而且，既然 consciousness 对我们来说是 obvious 的，eliminativist 就 bears the burden of proof。因此，eliminativist 接下来便试图寻找针对‘我 feel conscious’这一 premise 的 arguments。

## 针对 realism 的 Critiques 与 countercritiques

A1: 没有 phenomenal properties，就没有 seeming；我们不可能 seem to be conscious，却并不是 conscious。如果某个东西在我们看来似乎是什么，我们可能会弄错 seeming 的 content，但不会弄错 seeming 本身。

R1: 想想 visual illusions。它们 seem 是一种样子，但其实并不是。它 perceptually seems 是一种样子，却 doxastically seems 是另一种样子（我们并不 believe 眼睛向我们展示的东西，而是 believe 我们已经 determined 为 actual case 的东西，例如 Müller-Lyer illusion 中的 lines 是 equal 的）。我们可以有自己拥有 phenomenal states 的 perceptual seeming，但我们可以 correct 那个 intuition，仍然把 consciousness 看作一种 illusion。而且，我（Rob）deny：如果你 doxastically instantiate 一个 property，你就因此 instantiate 了它；也就是说，你对 consciousness 的 belief 并不意味着你是 conscious 的。有这样一个案例：一名女子被告知有人会用冰块触碰她，但实际触碰她的却是一根烧热的火钳。她可能 seem 自己 feels 一种 burning sensation，但她可能正在 instantiating cold qualia。

## 再谈 Yudkowsky 的 argument

现在，我要更仔细地看看 Eliezer Yudkowsky 反对 epiphenomenalism 的 argument。回想一下，在上面链接的那篇我写的文章中，我 argue，他的 argument 并不 refute zombies。但它是否 refute epiphenomenalism 呢？

他的 argument，简而言之，是在说：

1.  Consciousness 似乎会 cause 某些事情，例如我们 feel 很高的 temperatures 时会大喊（我们对 qualia 的 awareness 是大喊的 cause）
2.  为了 know 某个东西，你必须与它处于 causal contact
3.  如果 epiphenomenalism 是对的，那么我们谈论 consciousness 的原因，与我们是 conscious 这一 fact 无关
4.  Chalmers 将 consciousness postulates 为一个 separate reason，但也明确指出，（在 epiphenomenalism 中）我们谈论 consciousness 有另一个 separate reason (3)。那么，为什么还要 postulate consciousness？如果你 postulate 它，至少要让它有可能做些什么（interactionism）

我同意第 1 点和第 3 点。第 4 点是 problematic 的，因为 consciousness 并不是被 postulated 出来的，而是被 assumed 为一项有待 explained 的 data。Epiphenomenalism 随后被 proposed 为一种让 physics 保持原样的 explanation。

第 2 点可能更加 problematic。我可以在未曾与 objects 接触的情况下，know 它们的一些 properties（例如，如果 A is bigger than B，而且 B is bigger than C，那么 A is bigger than C）。设想在我们的 light-cone 之外的一个行星上，一座房子里的一个箱子里有一块石头，因此我不可能曾与它接触。即便如此，我仍然会 know 关于它的一些东西。但有人随后可以 argued，这只是关于事物之间 relations 的 knowledge，而不是关于事物的 properties 的 knowledge。诚然，epiphenomenalism 看起来并非如此，因此，要绕过 consciousness 缺乏 causal effect 这一点，并不是 trivial 的。

但即便如此，这看起来还是很奇怪。认为 consciousness 会做些什么的 belief，可以很容易地作为 illusion 被 explained away（从而 defeating 第 1 点的 seeming）。但这样一来，我们说“在我看来，我是 conscious”的原因，就与“在我看来，我是 conscious”这一 fact 无关了。回想一下，我们的 body 完全在 laws of physics 的引导下活动（在 epiphenomenalism 下），而且 by definition，它对 consciousness 一无所知（既没有 causal contact，brain 中也没有独立于 consciousness 的 mental states）。这个 body 怎么会谈论和书写一种它没有理由 believe in 的 property？而且，为什么它后来竟然像魔法般地被发现确实拥有那种 property？我的意思是，你可以 argued，由于某种原因，存在一种普遍的 cognitive failure，使 advanced mental architectures outputs 关于某个东西的内容。但这个随机说出的东西，怎么竟然真的 exists！这似乎就像说一只会飞的魔法独角兽 exists，而且是粉红色、epiphenomenal 的一样不大可能。可你又怎么可能 argued，这样的东西 exists？你不能说是独角兽告诉你的，因为它是 causally impotent 的。因此，如果你说它 exists，而别人问你为什么，你就无法给出任何理由。而如果它结果真的 exist，那将是一个奇迹般的巧合。同样的道理也适用于 consciousness。或许有办法挽救 epiphenomenalism，但这个 argument 确实相当具有毁灭性。Chalmers 本人近年来似乎也已经不再坚持它。

## 最后的话

随着时间推移，我们会获得更多关于 brain 如何 works，以及是否存在可能 mediate mental 与 brain 之间 interaction 的 special forces 的 information。希望这些能帮助 solve 这个 problem。目前，eliminativists 与 realists 之间的 debate 有些无望。我承认，我无法理解怎么会有人 disbelieve in consciousness。那想必得是 insane minds 才会如此，但 eliminativists 远非 insane，他们聪明而且深思熟虑。在看过双方的说法后，我总觉得 Graziano 或 Dennett 这样的人所作的评论和 arguments 是 missing the point，或者 begging the question。他们大概也这样看待 realist。但围绕如此 basic intuition 的 conflict，想必就是这样。通常，我们会借助 shared intuitions，就 uncertain propositions 达成一致。但在这里，即使我们几乎对所有事情都意见一致，也无法如此轻易地化解这个 disagreement。

最后我还想说，如果你对此还有任何想法，请一定告诉我。另外，如果你认定 consciousness 显然以 X 方式 works，而我没看出来就是愚蠢，那你大概错了，尽管如果你真的对了，我会很高兴。我猜，大多数受过教育的读者会觉得自己接近 epiphenomenalism，并试着 defend 它，让它听起来更 plausible。我在这里没有为所有 views 投入足够时间，因为重点不是澄清什么是答案，而是澄清什么不是答案。结束时我想强调，我的意图不是说“我已经解决了这个问题，答案就在这里”，而更像是在说“请帮助继续理解那使 understanding 本身成为可能的东西，但首先请接受，确实有这样的东西存在”。

    > 这就把我们带到了 phenomenal binding/combination problem。binding problem 听起来对 non-materialist physicalism 来说是个非常坏的消息：solution 看似不可能，这促使 David Chalmers 转向 dualism。恰恰相反，这是个非常好的消息。我们终于可以摆脱 philosophising——以及就 physical 的 intrinsic nature 能否是 experiential 交换 untestable intuitions——转而 extract novel, precise, experimentally falsifiable predictions。而且它们确实是 empirical predictions，不只是 retrodictions：乍看之下，我们的 phenomenally bound minds 与 CNS 的 microstructure 之间的 structural mismatch 是 unbridgeable，正如 David Chalmers 所认识到的那样。Phenomenal binding 是 classically impossible。然而，如果 non-materialist physicalism 是 true，那么我们的 phenomenally bound minds 与（最终的）QFT 的 formalism 之间就必须存在 perfect structural match。任何了解 decoherence 的强大威力（参见 https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.2635.pdf) 在“warm, wet and noisy”的 CNS 中如何发挥作用的人，都能告诉你，像 https://www.physicalism.com/#6 这样的 interferometry experiment 最多只能 yield functionless "noise" 的 nonclassical interference signature。如果我在极力推介这个 proposal（唉，这让人听起来更像个怪人，而不是一个不偏不倚的求真者），那并不是因为我确信 answer 是 correct，而是因为我担心，否则用来 test 它所需的 interferometry experiment 将永远不会有人去做。

## 原文

Here I present some thoughts about consciousness, especially arguing against the idea that consciousness is somehow not real, or an illusion.

Descartes was the famous originator of _cogito ergo sum_. He argued that even if your senses were deceived, you could still be sure that you were conscious. And so consciousness would be the Tier 1 truth amongst the beliefs we hold. This is indeed the case for me, at least.

Consider the following set of things: quantum field theory, the theory of relativity, neuroscience, cognitive science, evolutionary theory, external world realism, physicalism, naturalism, and materialism.

Now here there is another thing: consciousness.

I'd rather bin and commit to the flames the first set of things rather than give up consciousness as a real thing. Given that I just said this, one could think that I should not debate the issue further, as it would be pointless to change my mind. But this would be not true. My mind can still be changed. So I, as a fallible being, can in theory be mistaken in assigning something a prior resembling a Dirac's delta. But I'll argue that this is not the case, and that I am not mistaken.

Note that I have not said that rejecting consciousness amounts to rejecting the things in the first set. There is nothing incoherent in accepting eliminativism except for its conflict with consciousness. So if one rejects just that, one need not reject any further beliefs in the mentioned sciences.

## Some methodological notes

How are we justified in believing things? The best answer I have seen is a form of foundationalism: We are justified in believing in P if it seems to us that P, and there are no defeaters for our belief in P ([Huemer 2007](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2007.00002.x/full), [Moretti 2015](https://academic.oup.com/analysis/article-abstract/75/2/296/164984/Phenomenal-Conservatism1?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). In this framework, as an example, we would deal with the Müller-Lyer illusion by saying that it seems to us that one arrow is bigger than the other but that we have a defeater for our seeming: that we have measured the arrows. And so we would not be justified in believing that the arrows are what they seem. In doing this, we have appealed to a more solid seeming: that the ruler is straight, that our eyes made accurate judgements when measuring the arrows, etc. With consciousness, the default position is that we are indeed conscious and that consciousness is real. But are there defeaters for that position? As I mentioned above, to dislodge the belief in consciousness realism, we need a stronger premise than 'I am conscious', and there are no such premises. The eliminativist, of course, disagrees.

One could instead take a coherentist approach, and use coherence as a criterion of truth. If one does this, one may find that eliminativism coheres very well with science and that consciousness realism is more of an issue, a dangling isolated thing away from a big cluster of interconnected beliefs about the physical world. I am no fan of coherentism, but one need not be committed to eliminativism if one is a coherentist. Perhaps it could be argued that, nonetheless, consciousness is tightly linked to every other belief via the fact that to have beliefs we have to be conscious.

## The Hard Problem

Michael Huemer's exposition of the mind-body problem will serve as a good introduction to t[he issue that I want to discuss](http://www.owl232.net/mind.htm)

> Consider the following five tenable theses:
> 
> | 1. | For any system, every fact about the whole is a necessary consequence of the nature and relations of the parts. | | 2. | People are made of atoms. | | 3. | Atoms are purely physical objects, with nothing but physical properties and physical relations to one another. | | 4. | People have mental states. | | 5. | No statement ascribing a mental predicate can be derived from any set of purely physical descriptions. |

Different positions regarding consciousness and its relation to the brain will accept of reject some of these. All of these cannot be held at the same time, but all of them seem plausible. Premise one essentially is a statement of ontological reductionism, which seems true to me. Premise two and premise three is what we know about the universe (Substitute for fields, quarks, etc). Premise three seems true by introspection. Premise five can be taken to mean:

> In (5), "derived" means "logically derived," that is, derived in the sense in which the fundamental theorem of calculus can be derived from the axioms of arithmetic and some definitions. It does not mean "caused". This means that it would not be possible to deduce the quality of someone's conscious experiences from a physical description of him or of anything else. If a physical description of the universe is given, it will always be an additional piece of information, e.g., to note that someone is in pain. \[...\]
> 
> Finally, there is (5). What reason is there for thinking that is true? Well, we can compare it with a number of similar principles to get the general idea. In moral philosophy, there is a principle sometimes called Hume's law that says it is not possible to derive a normative judgement from a descriptive judgement. A normative judgement is a judgement about what is good or bad, right or wrong, and a descriptive judgement is basically anything else. Another way this is stated is that you cannot deduce an "ought" from an "is": you can't derive what ought to be the case solely on the basis of what is the case. This principle is almost universally acknowledged. And it is merely part of a more general pattern. For example, you can't derive a statement describing distances from any set of statements that don't describe distances. You can not derive a statement about colors from any set of non-color statements. You can't derive geometrical statements from non-geometrical ones. And generally, if you have an inference in which the conclusion talks about one thing and the premises talk about something else, the inference is invalid. In the same way, it is a conceptual truth that you cannot derive a mental description from a physical description. After all, just consider some physical concepts, such as spatial/geometrical properties, mass, force, and electric charge. Is it plausible that there is any way that these concepts could be used to explain what it feels like to be in pain? Say whatever you like about masses, positions, and forces of particles, you will not have ascribed any mental states to anything.

Eliminativists will deny 4. Cartesan dualists will deny 2. Idealists will deny 2-3. Property dualism might deny 1 or 3 and the mind/brain identity theory could deny 1-5. Panpsychists reject 3.

Some people say that deciding what systems are conscious is up to us. This does not run counter to premise five. Saying things like "I consider as conscious systems that exhibit such and such external behaviour, that are internally constituted in such way, and that have a certain degree of [Phi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_information_theory)" include a reference to consciousness in them. I disagree with this proposal, but it will still amount to the rejection of one of the premises.

## The materialist dance

So let's suppose that we want to grapple with the problem above, and we want to accept consciousness. What would a sensible materialist say? Indeed, one can achieve peace of mind by embracing eliminativism at the cost of denying the obvious, or one can remain in a state of uncertainty and keep belief in consciousness. By the materialist dance I mean the switch in positions that may happen to one as one considers possible explanations for consciousness.

So we begin pondering the idea of consciousness. We know things about physics, and we don't believe or want to believe in anything weird or spooky. We also want to keep our belief in consciousness, so we begin by rejecting eliminativism.

Then we think hard and come up with the idea that consciousness is really a poetic name for certain patterns of brain activity. This is the identity theory. It doesn't require any modification to physics, and it allows one to say that consciousness is real. Nice, isn't it?

Sean Caroll in his last book, The Big Picture, defends this view.

> If there is any one aspect of reality that causes people to doubt a purely physical and naturalist conception of the world, it’s the existence of consciousness. And it can be hard to persuade the skeptics, since even the most optimistic neuroscientist doesn’t claim to have a complete and comprehensive theory of consciousness. Rather, what we have is an expectation that when we do achieve such an understanding, it will be one that is completely compatible with the basic tenets of the Core Theory—part of physical reality, not apart from it.
> 
> Why should there be any such expectation? In part it comes down to Bayesian reasoning about our credences. The idea of a unified physical world has been enormously successful in many contexts, and there is every reason to think that it will be able to account for consciousness as well. But we can also put forward a positive case that the alternatives don’t work very well. If it’s not easy to see how consciousness can be smoothly incorporated as part of physical reality, it’s even harder to imagine how it could be anything else. Our main goal here is not to explain how consciousness does work, but to illustrate that it _can_ work in a world governed by impersonal laws of nature.
> 
> In this chapter and the next we’ll highlight some of the features of consciousness that make it special. Then over the following few chapters we’ll examine some arguments that, whatever consciousness is, it has to be more than simply a way of talking about ordinary matter in motion, obeying the conventional laws of physics. What we’ll find is that none of those arguments is very persuasive, and we’ll be left with a greater conviction than we started with that we human beings are part and parcel of the natural world, thoughts and emotions and all.

In the process of the discussion, Carroll is forced to admit some weird things: For example, that the Chinese room is conscious, or that a giant army of people passing messages to each other, simulating the brain of some, is also conscious (as in, there being something conscious above the individual consciousnesses of the individuals)

> Imagine mapping a person’s connectome, not only at one moment in time but as it develops through life. Then—since we’re already committed to hopelessly impractical thought experiments—imagine that we record absolutely every time a signal crosses a synapse in that person’s lifetime. Store all of that information on a hard drive, or write it down on (a ridiculously large number of) pieces of paper. Would that record of a person’s mental processes itself be “conscious”? Do we actually need development through time, or would a static representation of the evolution of the physical state of a person’s brain manage to capture the essence of consciousness.
> 
> These examples are fanciful but illustrative. Yes, reproducing the processes of the brain with some completely different kind of substance (whether neuristors or people) should certainly count as consciousness. But no, printing things out onto a static representation of those processes should not.
> 
> From a poetic-naturalism perspective, when we talk about consciousness we’re not discovering some fundamental kind of stuff out there in the universe. It’s not like searching for the virus that causes a known disease, where we know perfectly well what kind of thing we are looking for and merely want to detect it with our instruments so that we can describe what it is. Like “entropy” and “heat,” the concepts of “consciousness” and “understanding” are ones that we _invent_ in order to give ourselves more useful and efficient descriptions of the world. We should judge a conception of what consciousness really is on the basis of whether it provides a useful way of talking about the world—one that accurately fits the data and offers insight into what is going on.

> As poetic naturalists, that’s basically what we’ll be doing. The attributes of consciousness, including our qualia and inner subjective experiences, are useful ways of talking about the effective behavior of the collections of atoms we call human beings. Consciousness isn’t an illusion, but it doesn’t point to any departure from the laws of physics as we currently understand them.

> According to poetic naturalism, philosophical zombies are simply inconceivable, because “consciousness” is a particular way of talking about the behavior of certain physical systems. The phrase “experiencing the redness of red” is part of a higher-level vocabulary we use to talk about the emergent behavior of the underlying physical system, not something separate from the physical system. That doesn’t mean it’s not real; my experience of redness is perfectly real, as is yours. It’s real in exactly the same way as fluids and chairs and universities and legal codes are real—in the sense that they play an essential role in a successful description of a certain part of the natural world, within a certain domain of applicability.
> 
> The idea that our mental experiences or qualia are not actually separate _things_, but instead are useful parts of certain _stories we tell_ about ordinary physical things, is one that many people find hard to swallow.

You can also insert here Daniel Dennett's view.

On this view, consciousness is not really real, but merely real. Isn't this like eliminativism? Caroll doesn't really answer in the book. But a charitable interpretation would be something like "Yes, sure, consciousness is not real in the strong sense, but we can still tell stories about it and talk about certain parts of the universe using that word". Compare, in ethics, the position of the ethical relativist: "Yes, sure, ethics is not real in the moral realist sense, but it is useful to say that this or that is right or wrong as a shorthand for 'society thinks so'". This in the end amounts to eliminativism and nihilism, respectively, if only sugarcoated with poetry. Poetic naturalism doesn't attempt to hide this: it even has poetic in its name. But when pressed, the poetic naturalist, or the identity theorist, will have to concede that on the common meaning of real, consciousness is not real for them. There are no qualia, no thoughts, no feelings, no beliefs. Only interactions between neurons, and nothing else.

But then, we are all philosophical zombies. Choosing to call things in different ways does not change the way fundamental reality is. Calling a spoon a fork does not make it into a fork. Saying 'the movement of atoms in this way' is consciousness does not make it so (in the realist sense). And so zombies would then be not only something conceivable, but actually actual! Indeed Brian Tomasik, an eliminativist, when asked if he believes that we are all zombies, he says yes, we are.

This might seem hard to swallow, so one then may turn to epiphenomenalism. Here we see the familiar stories of 'The brain is a complex system blah blah' or 'Consciousness is a special thing that certain biological systems do, and that cannot be replicated in silico just by replicating its functioning' ([Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_naturalism) and [Pigliucci](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1S-7CWOLOtLRDmMiS7LtVxELssUi3OI1-UcrPAzGMuH4/pub)). I used to believe this.

If we do this, we have abandoned materialism, and entered dualism. Some people might protest and insist that epiphenomenalism is still materialist, but in academic philosophy of mind, materialism means type-A or type-B theories. So what?, one might say.

Well so it happens that if consciousness is epiphenomenal, then what causes me to talk about consciousness is not consciousness. Consciousness is not causing anything if we subscribe to epiphenomenalism. Eliezer Yudkowsky makes the argument [here](http://lesswrong.com/lw/p7/zombies_zombies/). This leads to the weird situation where my zombie would be writing the same things as I write, but without being conscious. Wouldn't that be weird? Why would my zombie be talking about consciousness if it is not conscious? Equally, if consciousness is not causing _me_ to write about consciousness (in epiphenomenalism, causality  runs only in one direction), then why am I writing about consciousness?

I wrote an answer to the inconceivability of zombies [here,](https://nintil.com/2016/07/10/p-zombies-are-still-undead/) but epiphenomenalism is still a troubling view. A universe of zombies that do everything we do, but are all eliminativists about consciousness is something I can conceive. But a world like ours where everyone does the same seems to lead us to believe that what we do regarding consciousness doesn't have to do with it. I think that I write about consciousness because I am conscious. But maybe I write about consciousness because I can't help it, and in addition I am deluded: assuming determinism, my mind is only a camera that thinks mistakenly it is causing things, but it actually is not. That would be an illusion. If so, it is not that weird. Assuming the 'everyone is a mechanical robot view', where we step back and see everything as the raw laws of physics doing their job, I can see myself as a body being pushed around by physical forces and stimuli, writing stuff, while there is this camera that is my mind looking at everything. Of course, being a passive spectator is boring, so it forms beliefs that it is actually in charge. I am not endorsing it, just showing how could it be that consciousness is not causing my words, and thus how zombies are conceivable.

But let's say that we don't like the epiphenomenal explanation because we are convinced that consciousness does something, like I (and Eliezer) seem to do. Then we jump into the interactionist dualist ship. We are then immediately faced with the QFT argument. Sean Carroll [again](http://www.preposterousuniverse.com/blog/2008/02/18/telekinesis-and-quantum-field-theory/):

> On meter-sized scales, relevant to bending a spoon with your mind, the strongest possible allowed new force would be about _one billionth_ the strength of gravity. And remember, gravity is far too weak to bend a spoon.
> 
> That’s it. We are done. The deep lesson is that, although science doesn’t know everything, it’s not “anything goes,” either. There are well-defined regimes of physical phenomena where we _do_ know how things work, full stop. The place to look for new and surprising phenomena is outside those regimes. You don’t need to set up elaborate double-blind protocols to pass judgment on the abilities of purported psychics. Our knowledge of the laws of physics rules them out. Speculations to the contrary are not the provenance of bold visionaries, they are the dreams of crackpots.

While he argues against telekinesis, one could extend the argument to consciousness. If consciousness causes stuff, then we should be able to detect that force.  According to a [handy chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction) at wikipedia, gravity is extremely weak compared to other forces to begin with at small scales, and so something even weaker would have a negligible effect on the brain, swamped by other forces. But still, this doesn't rule out that possibility. A minuscule force, or a consciousness-field might be all that is required! Implausible is not impossible!

Note that Carroll says that:

> any new forces that might be lurking out there are either (far) too short-range to effect everyday objects, or (far) too weak to have readily observable effects.

And we might only need tiny forces acting on ions or atoms in the brain.The interactionist might want then to look at proposals like Bohm's, Penrose's, Popper's or Stapp's that propose some sort of quantum effect mediating consciousness (the infamous 'Quantum mechanics seems weird, and so does consciousness, maybe they are related'). But this may sound too spooky to some ears, and so interactionism is another view that is abandoned in the materialist dance.

So we then turn to [neutral monism](http://nautil.us/issue/47/consciousness/is-matter-conscious). Out of the possible solutions to the conundrum of consciousness, it is the least obvious to understand. My understanding of its motivation is this: Imagine the universe filled with balls moving around. Arrows between the balls, the laws of physics, describe the interactions of the balls. But what are the balls? What is their intrinsic nature? Or to use [David Pearce](https://www.physicalism.com/)'s favourite expression, what breathes fire into the equations?. And so inside those balls, we find phenomenal properties. Everything would be have a tiny bit of consciousness in it: panprotopsychism. This view preserves the laws of physics as they are, keeps the belief in consciousness, and doesn't appear to run into the 'Nothing causes my writings on consciousness' problem, as it claims that physics is consciousness, not an epiphenomenon.

To get your head around it, the idea here is that if we say that the universe is made of fields, those fields are ultimately fields of phenomenal experience (yes, it still sounds weird). Your head is physically implementing some very complex calculation in a very specific way, and there is something that feels like to be that calculation, being implemented and 'unfolded by Nature'.

Brian Tomasik has a reply to this [here](http://reducing-suffering.org/not-type-f-monist/). Basically, what would be wrong with neutral monism is that, as it leaves the laws of physics unchanged, still cannot causally trace our belief in consciousness to consciousness itself. A second problem is that the micro-particles of consciousness still have to be aggregated into a whole unified consciousness (combination problem).

If this still seems weird to you, you are out of luck. So you might then consider that your initial supposition, that consciousness is real, is perhaps misguided. And so you begin to consider eliminativism.

## The argument for eliminativism

You might think something like science gets things right, and human beings are biased and make mistakes when thinking, and maybe it is more likely that this great edifice of science is right than you are about this spooky conscious thing. Plus consciousness seems like something really removed from your network of beliefs about nature! One can picture a sort of causal graph with everything in nature connected to everything by the forces of nature so that you get nice bidirectional interactions... and then there's this thing that you don't know where to put. Maybe there is no thing! In the past we have wondered if there was something magical about life, and we thought that there was this elan vital that made the difference between inanimate and alive, and now we know that it was atoms and their interactions all along. Consciousness will turn out to be the same thing, another elan vital that, if anything, is more intuitive than the original.

...

...

Cogito ergo sum tho. And given that consciousness is obvious to us, the eliminativist bears the burden of proof. Thus, the eliminativist then tries to find arguments against the 'I feel conscious' premise.

## Critiques and countercritiques of realism

Rob Bensinger [posted recently an article](https://nothingismere.com/2017/02/23/phenomenal-consciousness-is-a-quasiperceptual-illusion-objections-and-replies/) , the immediate cause of me writing this, where he tries to do that.

Bensinger considers a series of arguments. Here I will summarise the argument he is replying to, then his response, then mine. (A is argument, R is Reply, C is counter reply)

A1: There is no seeming without phenomenal properties, we can't seem to be conscious yet not be. If something seems something to us, we might be mistaken about the content of the seeming, but not of the seeming itself.

R1: Think visual illusions. They seem one way, but they aren't. It perceptually seems one way, but doxastically seems another way (We don't believe what our eyes show us, we believe what we have determined to be the actual case, e.g. that the lines are equal in the Müller-Lyer illusion). We can have the perceptual seeming that we have phenomenal states, but we can correct that intuition, and see consciousness as an illusion still. And I (Rob) deny that if you doxastically instantiate a property you therefore instantiate it, that is, your belief in consciousness does not mean you are conscious.  There is a case where a woman is told she will be touched with an ice cube, but is touched by a hot poker instead. It might seem to her that she feels a burning sensation, but she might be instantiating cold qualia.

C1: I would interpret the perceptual/doxastic distinction differently. I would say that we have a seeming that X, and then there are defeater for the seeming. A belief would be something justified by, but different from, the seeming. In both cases, having belief or seemings are mental properties, which require qualia. If one has a seeming, one is then conscious. The hot poker example just proves that we can be confused about the contents of consciousness, not about having consciousness in the first place. This is not to deny that we cannot be confused about having consciousness itself: Daniel Dennett and other eliminativists are in such state of confusion. To put it in another way, this is like trying to argue against consciousness by arguing that any of our mental states might not track reality because a laplacian demon might be misleading us. But there is still a conscious thing being deluded in that scenario!

A2: (Looks very similar to A1)

R2:

> However, once it is granted that both doxastic and perceptual error is possible in the absence of the phenomenal, I see no principled way to rule out the possibility that zombies are capable of quasiperceptual metarepresentations as well, including erroneous ones. And such metarepresentations, I believe, do hold some promise of _explaining_ the apparent presence of phenomenal properties in our stream of consciousness, as opposed to ignoring or flatly denying this pre-doxastic semblance.

This is something that I deny, of course. Zombies cannot make doxastic errors, as knowledge as commonly understood requires consciousness (cf. Searle). About what zombies can or cannot do, it seems weird for an eliminativist to talk about zombies that way: after all, we are all zombies to begin with (according to Brian Tomasik at least), so if we are capable of quasiperceptual metarepresentations, zombies are. But the argument still seems confused: there is still talk of phenomenal properties in a stream of consciousness, and I insist that appearence of phenomenal properties refutes eliminativism.

## Yudkowsky's argument, again

I now take a closer look at Eliezer Yudkowsky's argument against epiphenomenalism. Recall that in the post linked above that I wrote, I argue that his argument does not refute zombies. But does it refute epiphenomenalism?

His argument, in a nutshell is saying:

1.  Consciousness seems to cause stuff like us yelling if we feel very hot temperatures (Our awareness of the qualia is the cause of the yelling)
2.  In order to know about something, you must be in causal contact with it
3.  If epiphenomenalism is right, then the reason why we talk about consciousness has nothing to do with the fact that we are conscious
4.  Chalmers postulates consciousness as a separate reason but makes it explicit that (in epiphenomenalism) there is a separate reason why we talk about consciousness (3). Why postulate consciousness then? If you postulate it, at least make it possible that it does something (interactionism)

I agree with point 1 and point 3. Point 4 is problematic because consciousness is not postulated but assumed as a piece of data to be explained. Epiphenomenalism is then proposed as an explanation that leaves physics as it is.

Point 2 could be more problematic. I know some properties about objects without having been in contact with them (If A is bigger than B and if B is bigger than C, then A is bigger than C, for example). Imagine there is a rock inside a box inside a house in a planet outside of our light-cone, so I cannot possible have come in contact with it. Still, I would know something about it. But one could then argue that such thing only is knowledge of relations between things, not of properties of things. Admittedly, epiphenomenalism doesn't look like this, so it is not trivial to work around the lack of causal effect of consciousness.

But still, it looks weird. The belief that consciousness does something could be explained away as an illusion without much trouble (defeating the seeming of point 1). But then the reason we say "It seems to me that I am conscious" has nothing to do with the fact that "It seems to me that I am conscious". Recall, our body is going around fully guided by the laws of physics (under epiphenomenalism) and does not know anything about consciousness by definition (Both no causal contact and no mental states in the brain separate from consciousness). How comes this body is talking and writing about a property it has no reason to believe in? And why does it then turn out, magically, that it does indeed have that property? I mean, you could argue that for whatever reason there is a widespread cognitive failure that makes advanced mental architectures outputs stuff about something. But how comes this random something actually exists! It seems as likely as saying that a flying magical unicorn exists and is pink and epiphenomenal. But then how could you possibly argue that such a thing exists? You couldn't say that the unicorn tells you so, because it is causally impotent. So if you say that it exists and you are asked why, you cannot give any reason. And if it turns out that it does exist, it would be a miraculous coincidence. Same applies for consciousness. There might be a way of recovering epiphenomenalism, but the argument is indeed quite devastating. Chalmers himself seems to have moved away from it in the recent years.

## Final words

As times goes on, we will have more information about how the brain works, and whether there are or not any special forces that might mediate the interaction between the mental and the brain. Hopefully that might help solve this problem. Currently, the debate between eliminativists and realists is a bit hopeless. I admit that it eludes me how it can be that there are people who disbelieve in consciousness. Surely those must be insane minds, but eliminativists are far from insane, they are clever and thoughtful. Seeing both sides, I always see the comments and arguments of people like Graziano or Dennett as missing the point, or begging the question. They probably think the same of the realist. But it must be like that for a conflict over such a basic intuition. Typically, we would use shared intuitions to agree on uncertain propositions. But here, even if we agree on almost everything, we cannot fix this disagreement as easily.

One final thing that I will say is that if you have any further idea about this, please do tell me. Also, if you assume that consciousness obviously works in way X and that I am stupid for not seeing it, you are probably wrong, though I would be glad if you are actually right. I'm guessing that most educated readers will feel close to epiphenomenalism and will try to defend it and make it sound more plausible. I have not devoted here enough time to all of the views because the point was not to clarify what is the answer, but what is not. I end this remarking that my intention has not been to say 'I have solved this, here's the answer', but more like 'Help continue the work of understanding that which enables understanding itself, but please accept that there is such a thing to begin with'.

    > I disagree with your 3). Note that we do not currently know what the human brain's 'consciousness algorithm' looks like, yet we are quite sure that whatever it turns out to be will not invalidate our claim to being conscious. Therefore whatever your alternative algorithm that perfectly mimics consciousness without actually being conscious looks like, it could have been our implementation to begin with. These ideas are conflict: to insist that a perfect consciousness imitator is nonetheless not truly conscious, we would have to admit that whether we are conscious ourselves depends on the algorithm our minds use, and that if it turns out to match that of our hypothetical unconscious imitator then we must not be conscious.
    > 
    > The solution is to realise that 'conscious' is a word describing the behaviours we think of as conscious, not a specific underlying implementation (since we call ourselves conscious without knowing what our implementation is), so by definition any consciousness imitator is truly conscious. However, this doesn't mean we are guaranteed to be able to easily and effectively check for consciousness -- we have to think of tests that capture everything we mean by the word. And of course a consciousness imitator might only become conscious when it's trying to persuade us that it is, returning to unconsciousness the rest of the time.
