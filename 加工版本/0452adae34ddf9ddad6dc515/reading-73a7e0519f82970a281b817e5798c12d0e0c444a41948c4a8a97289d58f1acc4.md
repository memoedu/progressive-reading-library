# 误判人才｜Misidentifying talent

---

## 正文

\[点击折叠 / 展开关于 sports 的部分\] 以下是 talent scouts 的一些笔记：

-   Recruit A（招募对象 A）：
    -   ... 将会是一个真正的 specimen，有机会拥有 Dave Parker 那样的 body。Facially 看起来像 Leon Wagner。body flexibility 很 Good。hands 非常 large。
-   Recruit B（招募对象 B）：
    -   Outstanding physical specimen——big athletic frame，带有 broad shoulders 和 long, solid arms and leg。他的 step 有 Good bounce，body control 则是 above avg。Good strong face。
-   Recruit C（招募对象 C）：
    -   Hi butt，longish arms & legs，leanish torso，像一匹小马驹
    -   \[另一位 scout\]：Wiry、loose，agility 很 good，face 也 good
    -   \[又一位 scout\]：Athletic looking body，loose、rangy，slightly bow legged。

脱离上下文，你可能会以为他们是在 scouting actors 或 models，但这些人其实是 baseball players（“A”是 Lloyd Moseby，“B”是 Jim Abbott，“C”是 Derek Jeter），而且都是相当出色的选手（Lloyd Moseby 或许只有大约四年算得上非常出色的 player，但与大多数被 scouted 的 players 相比，这已经让他非同寻常了）。如果你读一读其他 baseball scouting reports，就会看到许多评论，谈论某个人有一张“good face”、他们长得像谁、他们的 butt 看起来怎么样，等等。

基本上人人都想 hire 有 talent 的人。但即使在 baseball 这个 hiring talent 的 returns 既 obvious 又 high、而且在美国 major sport 中最容易 quantified 的领域，人们也因为依赖错误磨炼出来的 gut feelings，而在长达一个世纪的时间里犯下相当明显的错误；这些 gut feelings 又严重依赖 unconscious 以及 conscious biases。稍后，我们会看看 baseball hiring 对其他领域意味着什么，不过首先，让我们看看，那些最终并没有真正取得成就的 players，怎么也会得到与未来 superstars 类似的 scouting reports（不关心 sports 的 programmers 可以把它视为 interview feedback 的对应物）。例如，以下是关于 [Adam Eaton](https://www.baseball-reference.com/players/e/eatonad01.shtml) 的评论：尽管他曾被视为那一代最炙手可热的 prospects（potential hires）之一，但按 pro standards 来看，他是个表现不佳的 player：

-   Scout 1：Medium frame/compact/firm。是个非常出色的 athlete / shows quick "cat-like" reactions。overall body strength 是 Excellent。Medium hands / medium length arms / 带有 strong forearms ... 这个 Player 是个 tough competitor。这家伙的 make-up 中有些老派斗牛犬般的劲头。
-   Scout 2：Good body，frame 还有 develop 的空间。Long arms 和 big hands。Narrow face。留着鬓角，戴帽子是军人风格。Slope shoulders。Strong inlegs ... 也 played basketball。Good athlete .... Attitude 是 excellent。看不出他会 breaking down。是全国 top HS pitchers 之一
-   Scout 3：6'1"-6'2" 180，upper and lower half 都 solid。还可以再增加 15 而不造成不利影响

另一方面，scouts 也会根据 physical appearance 贬低那些后来证明很出色的 players，例如以下这些担心 Albert Pujols 的 weight 的 scouts：

-   Scout 1：Heavy, bulky body。lower half 有 Extra (weight)。未来有 (weight) problem。Aggressive hitter，具有 mistake HR power。往往是个 hacker。
-   Scout 2：Good bat (speed)，带有 very strong hands。Competes well，并且 battles at the plate。Contact 似乎 fair。Swing 有时会变得有点 long。会 over pull。他没有 hit the ball hard to CF or RF。Weight 迟早会成为问题。

Pujols 最终成为有史以来最出色的 baseball players 之一（[目前按 WAR ranked 32nd](https://web.archive.org/web/20220209005630/https://www.baseball-reference.com/leaders/WAR_career.shtml)）。他的 weight 并没有成为问题，但如果你读一读其他 heavy 或 short 的出色 players 的 scouting reports，就会发现他们经常被 underrated。当然，baseball scouting reports 并非只看人的 appearances，但 scouts 通常会因自己认为 athlete 应该是什么样子而产生很强的 biased 判断。

由于在 baseball 中使用 stats 已经“赢了”（如今 top teams 都雇用了成批的 statisticians），而“old school”的人又不愿承认这一点，我们经常看到有人声称，使用 stats 并不会真正带来与过去不同的 outcomes。但这种说法错得如此离谱，以至于人们举出的 examples 通常都是 [self-refuting](https://www.patreon.com/posts/62223485) 的。例如，Sports Illustrated 对此是这样说的：

> Media 和 Internet 上的 draft prognosticators 喜欢大肆渲染 Madrigal 身上那种“scrappy little battler”的一面，声称 modern sabermetrics 帮助 scouts 纳入了以前被 overlooked 的 smaller players。当然，这完全是胡扯。一个 players \[原文如此\] 的 abilities 决定了他对 scouts 的 appeal——而不是 height 或 bulk——而 smaller、shorter players 一直都是 baseball 的常见成员，从 Mel Ott 到 Joe Morgan，再到 Kirby Puckett 和 Jose Altuve，都是如此。

用这些 examples 来支持 scouting，实在令人费解，因为众所周知，Kirby Puckett 尽管交出了 statistically dominant performances，却被 scouts overlooked；他只是因为一次偶然的巧合才得以成为 baseball player：Twins 的 [farm system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_team) 的 assistant director 去看自己儿子参加的一场 baseball game，在同一场比赛中看到了 Kirby Puckett，这才促成 Twins drafting Kirby Puckett，而他此后撑起了这支 franchise 长达十年。

Joe Morgan 同样以被 overlooked 而闻名，也只是由于偶然的巧合才得以成为 professional baseball player。Morgan 在高中交出了 statistically dominant numbers，却因为 height 而被忽视。由于没有被 pro team drafted，他去了 Oakland City College，在那里再次交出出色的 numbers，却依然被忽视。一支 team 注意到他，是两个巧合共同作用的结果。首先，一支新的 baseball team 成立了，这支新 team 需要补齐 team 及其相关 farm system 的人员，这意味着他们需要大量 players。其次，这支新的 baseball team 需要 hire scouts，于是 hired Bill Wight（他以前并不从事 scout 工作）担任 scout。Wight 后来因不像几乎所有其他 scout 那样抱有 appearance bias 而出名，还因为 signing 那些“长相古怪”的 baseball players 而被嘲笑。Bill 说服这支新的 baseball team “hire”了不少被 overlooked 的 players，其中包括 Joe Morgan。

Mel Ott 同样以被 overlooked 而闻名，也只是由于巧合才得以成为 professional baseball player。他在高中时如此 dominant，以至于会在业余时间为 adult semi-pro teams 打球。然而，当他毕业时，pro baseball teams 因为他太 small 而不愿要他，于是他在一家木材公司找了份工作，并为 company team 打球。木材公司的老板对他的 baseball skills 印象深刻，而 Ott 幸运的是，这位木材公司的老板与一支 baseball team 的老板既是生意伙伴又是朋友，实际上帮 Ott 在一支 pro baseball team 中谋得了一个 position，最终成就了[按 WAR ranked 的有史以来第 20 出色的 baseball career](https://web.archive.org/web/20220208221047/https://www.baseball-reference.com/players/o/ottme01.shtml)[1](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:J)。大多数 short baseball players 恐怕没有遇上这种偶然的好运；每有一个人遇上，很可能就有许多人没有遇上。如果看看有多少几乎被忽视、却又幸运的 players 交出了足以让他们成为 all-time greats 的 numbers，那么很可能，打过 amateur 或 semi-pro baseball、原本可能成为有史以来最伟大 players 的人中，绝大多数都被忽视了，没有去打 professional baseball（如果这听起来不太可信，那么在阅读后面关于 chess、go 和 shogi 的部分时，不妨想想：如果根据人们在 major tech companies 中对一个人怎样才算 cognitively skilled 的看法，把所有看起来不像应该很出色的 players 都 removed，会发生什么；然后再看看，all-time-greats 还剩下多大的 fraction）。

决定为一支 baseball team “hire”谁，是一项 high stakes decision，牵涉数百万乃至更多美元（按 2022 年的美元价值计算），但 teams 并没有尝试认真 quantify productivity，而是根据各种各样的 irrelevant factors 来决定 draft（hire）谁。与任何 major sport 一样，baseball productivity 比大多数现实活动中的 productivity 更容易 quantify，因为 game 比“真实”的 problems 简单得多。而且，在美国的 major sports 中，baseball 是最容易 quantify 的 sport，但[这并没有阻止 baseball teams 用一个世纪的时间，过度看重 height 和 race 等视觉上显而易见的 criteria](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)。

前些天，我又想起了这件事，因为前些天我在 Twitter 上看到一个 thread，一位非常成功的人在其中讲述自己是如何起步的，说自己尽管 unqualified，却靠游说进入了一所 elite institution，并用这个故事得出结论：elite gatekeepers 基本上只是在 scouting for talent，而你只需要向人们展示你有 talent：

> 我生活中一个与 college 有关的例子是，我以糟糕的 grades 和糟糕的 SAT scores 成功进入了 CMU（我参加 test 时得了流感 :/）
> 
> 我花了一个月，了解 CMU 的 CS department 的一切，然后开车过去，直接找 professors 交谈。我刚到 campus 时，entrance office 问了我的 GPA 和 SAT，然后就让我离开。但我设法与一位 professor 交谈，他把我转给他的上司，如此 recursively，直到我和学校的 vice president 谈了起来。他问我，凭什么觉得自己足够好，能来 CMU，我说：“我不确定自己够不够好。其他这些孩子都真的很聪明。我现在就可以离开。”他打断了我，并提醒我，能走进那个房间需要多大的 agency。
> 
> 他当场给了我一封手写的 acceptance letter ... 我觉得有一个秘诀，至少对 gatekeepers 来说是这样：他们通常只是在寻找 high agency 和 talent。

我也从其他成功人士那里听过这种故事，而他们往往会对这一切意味着什么得出 bimodal conclusions。一些人得出结论，认为世界正确地 recognized 了他们的 talent，而且世界就是这样运作的；talent 会得到 recognized 和 rewarded。另一些人则得出结论，认为在 talent 能否得到 rewarded 这件事上，世界相当 random；许多具有 similar talents、采用 similar strategies 的人被略过了，而他们只是幸运地因为自己的 talent 得到了 rewarded[2](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:B)。

还有一次让我想起以前的 baseball scouting reports，是听说我的一位朋友如何走到今天这一步的时候；她现在是加拿大一所顶尖大学的 engineering professor。我们就叫她 Jane 吧。Jane 在她如今任职 professor 的大学读 undergrad 时，有时走在 campus 上，会有人好心地问她：“你迷路了吗？”有时，这是因为她作为一个女人，身处 engineering building 时看起来不像来对了地方。另一些时候，则是因为她看起来、说起话来都像来自加拿大乡下的人。有一次，一名保安以为她是个游荡进 campus 的无家可归者。几年之后，在 college 朋友的帮助下，她学会了穿合适的衣服、采用合适的 mannerisms，从而能被当作“对的那类人”；朋友们向她解释，一个人应该怎样说话、怎样穿衣。但她年轻时，人们对她的第一印象是 admin assistant，如今的第一印象则是 professor 的妻子，因为他们没料到她所在的 department 会有女性 professor。她已经相当成功，但与一个外表符合预期的人相比，她为此付出了多得多的努力。

至于在她的经历中，她的 gate keepers 是否真的只是在寻找 agency 和 talent，她曾经因为从未听说过“玉米热狗”而 failed 一场 civil engineering exam；她还曾勉强 passed 一门 intro programming class，那门课的 professor 宣布，任何还不知道如何 program 的人都会 fail。

那次 corn dog exam failure，是因为 civil engineering exam 中有一道题，要求 students design 一个 corn dog dispenser。我的朋友从未听说过 corn dog，于是问 professor，corn dog 是什么。那位 professor 不相信她不知道 corn dog 是什么，还当着全班的面斥责她，认为她问的这个问题显然不可能是认真的。由于不知道 corn dog 是什么，她 designed 了一个把 corn 放进 hot dog 里、再 dispensed 内含 corn 的 hot dog 的东西，结果 failed，因为那并不是 corn dog。

事实证明，civil engineering 和 programming 的 gatekeepers 并不只是在寻找 agency，而是在寻找来自合适 background 的人。我怀疑，这与那位当场 admitted 一个 promising student 的 CMU professor 并没有多大不同；只不过，很多人会把“一个 smart teenage boy，能讲出一套自己的 grades 和 SAT scores 为什么不好的故事”pattern match 为“promising potential prodigy”，却把“一个来自加拿大乡村的女孩，在 high school 班里拿 top grade，以前没怎么用过 computer，穿得像加拿大乡村的穷人，因为父母基本上抛弃了她和弟弟，她得一边抚养弟弟、一边支付 college 的费用”pattern match 为“不属于 engineering 的 homeless person”。

另一件让我想起 baseball scouting reports 有多可笑的事，是前些时候我与 Ben Kuhn 的一次交谈。

**我**：真奇怪，big tech companies 里，处在我这个 level（senior staff engineer）的男性，怎么有那么多人都这么 tall。在我最近的记忆里，在这个 level 或更高 level 的男性中，我想我只和一位比我 shorter 的人一起开过会。我也只比 U.S. average shorter 1"！而且，那个比我 shorter 的人已经 worked remotely 至少十年了，所以我不知道大家是否真的留意过他的 height。management track 上的人似乎还要 taller。看看那些和我一起开过会的 VPs，他们肯定都至少有 6' tall。  
**Ben**：也许我能在 big tech company 当 VP。我有 6' tall！  
**我**：哦，我想我之前并不知道 6' tall 到底有多高。那些和我开会的 VPs 明显比你 taller。他们大概至少有 6'2"？  
**Ben**：哇，作为 minimum 来说，那真是很 tall 了。6'2" 在 U.S. adult male 中是 96%-ile。

我和在各种 big companies（tech companies、consulting companies 等）工作的 successful people 聊过这件事，一些按通常标准已经算 tall、身高 6' 或 6'1" 的男性告诉我，他们在重要会议中经常是房间里 shortest 的男性。6'1" 还略低于 baseball player 的 median height。height 与 consultant 或 programmer 的 success 似乎比它与 baseball 中的 success 更 correlated，这有点奇怪，因为在 baseball 中，height 会直接带来 advantage。一种可能的 explanation 是 [halo effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halo_effect)：人们对 tall 或看起来有 authoritative 气质的人的 positive associations，会促成这些人的 success。

我在网上看到这类讨论时，总有人指出，这是因为 height 与 cognitive performance 是 correlated 的。但如果我们看看关于 IQ 的 literature，这种 correlation 并没有 strong 到足以 explain 这类现象。如果看看那些不通过 IQ test、而是通过其他方式直接 tested 人的 mental acuity 的 fields，我们也能 observe 到这一点。例如，在 chess 中，大多数 top players 都在 average height 附近，两个方向上都有一些 outliers。即使不详细查看 data，这也应当是预料之中的，因为 height 与 IQ 之间的 correlation 是 weak 的，其中很大一部分 correlation 来自 low end 的 relationship[3](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:Q)；而 IQ 与各种 mental tasks 中的 performance 之间的 correlation 也同样是 weak 的（有人会说，按 social science standards 来看，它是 strong 的，[但从实际 explanatory power 来看，即使在 population level 上，它也非常 weak](https://danluu.com/percentile-latency/)，在 individual level 上就更 weaker 了）。[再具体看看 chess，我们就能发现，正如预期的那样，这种 correlation 是 weak 的](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2016/12/22143817/Burgoyne-Sala-Gobet-Macnamara-Campitelli-Hambrick-2016.pdf)。

由于 correlation 是 weak 的，而且 average height 附近的人远多于不在这个范围的人，我们应当预期，大多数 top chess players 都在 average height 附近。[如果看看近代历史上最 dominant 的 chess players，Carlsen、Anand 和 Kasparov](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/chess-players/do-chess-players-tend-to-be-short)，他们分别是 5'8"、5'8" 和 5'9"（如果查看不同 sources，它们声称的 heights 会相差几 inches，但仍在相当 normal 的 range 内；人们经常夸大 heights；如果看看那些尝试通过照片或亲眼进行真实 comparisons 的人，measured heights 往往低于人们自称的 height[4](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:O)）。

go 和 shogi players 的 heights 稍难查到，但在我能查到 heights 的人中，[这个列表里现代最顶尖的 players](https://gameofgo.app/learn/top-ten-go-players)（Lee Sedol、Yoshiharu Habu）似乎大致都在 normal range 内；和 chess 一样，那些属于 elite players、但不属于 best of all time 的人中，两个方向上都有一些 outliers。

如果 height 或 appearance 中的其他 factors 确实与 mental performance 非常 strongly correlated，我们应当看到，在 chess 这类相对直接 measure mental performance 的 activities 中，height 与 performance 之间的 correlation，远 stronger 于 height 与 career success 之间的 correlation；然而，情况恰恰相反。这似乎表明，height 带来的 halo effect，比任何与 height correlated 的 underlying benefits 都要 stronger。

如果看看这样一些 activities：人们获准真正展示 skills 之前，要经过相当多的 gatekeeping，但 performance 可以相当 accurately 地 measured，而且 hiring 更好的 employees 会对 company performance 产生 immediate、measurable、direct impact，例如 [baseball 和 hockey，我们就能看到，即使关于 data-driven approaches 如何发现人们 intuition 中巨大漏洞的公开讨论早已出现，人们在此后几十年里仍然选择相信 gut instinct，而不是 data](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)。

再看看 programming：要 accurately measure individual performance，其难度介于极其困难和不可能之间；individual performance 对 company success 的 impact，也远不如 sports 中那么 direct。那么，我们该如何 estimate talent assessment 的 accurate 程度？

pessimistic view 是，我们似乎没有理由预期 talent assessment 会比 sports 中更好：在 sports 中，相当 accurate、rigorous 的 performance assessments 公开文章出现了几十年，companies 才开始认真对待 talent assessment。而 programming 的 talent assessment 甚至还没有发展到能有人为整个 industry 的人员写出 accurate evaluations 的程度，所以，我们甚至还没有开始那个长达几十年的过程：companies 奋力坚持根据 personal opinions，而不是 accurate measurements 来 evaluating 人。

Jobs 在多个 levels 上都有相当于 old school baseball scouting reports 的东西。在 hiring stage，有多个 levels 的 filters，encode 了人们的 biases。关于这一点，一项经典 study 是 [Bertrand 和 Sendhil Mullainathan](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9873/w9873.pdf) 的 paper；它发现，resumes 上“white sounding”的 names，比“black sounding”的 names 获得更多 interviews 的 callbacks，而且 resume 上有一个“white sounding”的 name，会 increased resume 上更好 credentials 带来的 returns。此后，人们进行了这项 study 的许多 variants，例如，[带有 white sounding names 的 resumes，表现优于带有 Asian sounding names 的 resumes](https://www.utpjournals.press/doi/pdf/10.3138/cpp.2017-033)，[在根据 CVs 进行 evaluated 时，CVs 上名字为 white sounding 的 professors，会被 evaluated 为比名字为 black 和 Asian sounding 的 professors 有更好的 interpersonal skills](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Juan-Madera-2/publication/266589756_The_Problem_for_Black_Professors_Judged_Before_Met/links/54381fea0cf24a6ddb922d57/The-Problem-for-Black-Professors-Judged-Before-Met.pdf)，等等。

关于 promotions 和 leveling 的 literature 要弱得多，但我和其他一些人身处 highly selected environments，实际上需要经过多轮 screening，每一轮面对的都是经过越来越严格 selected 的人，例如 VPs、senior（指“senior staff”+）ICs、elite universities 的 professors 等；我们都 observed 到，针对 height 的 filtering，与 baseball 中一样 severe，或更加 severe，但没有 basketball 中那么 severe。

这就很奇怪，因为在 chess 这类“promotion”criteria 直接根据 performance 来 selected 的 mental endeavors 中，height 似乎只与 success 非常 weakly correlated。相关 literature 中的一个主要问题是，social scientists 通常只看 averages。许多 studies 只是给出一个 correlation coefficient。运气好的话，他们可能会给出一张 graph，针对每一个 height，给出某个东西的 average。这是最简单的做法，但只能让我们对实际情况有一个非常 coarse 的 understanding。

因为我喜欢弄清事物如何运作，包括 organizations 和人们的 opinions，所以我曾（非正式地、口头地）polled 过许多 engineers，问他们如何看待其他 engineers。我发现，opinions 存在大量 clustering，从而形成了一些 clusters，其中的人对于谁做出了 excellent work 大致有 agreement。在每个 cluster 内，人们经常会对 engineers 的 ranking 有不同意见，但对于谁属于“good to excellent”，通常有 agreement。

其中一个 cluster 的人，会看人们 produced 的 output，并据此 judging 人（这是我的 opinion；当然，这也可能只是我自己的 biases）。另一个 cluster 的人，会看 height 与 confidence 的某种 combination，并据此 judging 人。很长时间里，后者都让我困惑（早在我想到写这篇文章之前，我就已经习惯性地向人们提出这类问题，并 collating data；在我 recognized 这个 pattern 之前，我一直觉得很奇怪：那么多人明明有 good technical judgment——他们能做出 good work，也能提出展现 good technical judgment 的 comments，足以证明这一点——却对那么多经常说出 blatantly incorrect 的话、produced 出 poorly working 甚至 non-working systems 的人，给出很高的 evaluated）。另一个 cluster 则围绕 credentials，例如某人上过什么 school、被 leveled 到什么 level，或者曾在哪些 prestigious companies 工作。一个人的 judgment 可能来自多个 clusters，例如，有些人既会赞扬做出 excellent technical work 的人，也会赞扬 tall 且 confident 的人。在更高 levels 上，由于 judge 人们的 work 变得更困难，依据人们的 output 来作出 judgment 的人，相对就更少了。

我在曾任职的 startup 做这项 evaluation collating exercise 时，基本上只有一个 cluster，而且它依据的是人们的 output；对于谁是 top engineers，大家有相当广泛的 consensus。但在我工作过的任何一家 large companies，我都没有见过这种情况。我不会说，这就意味着那家 startup 的 evaluation 是 fair 的（也许我们所有人都受到了同样的 biases 影响），但至少我们没有受到那些最明显的 biases 影响。

回到大公司，如果我们看看 reform promotion system 需要什么，就会发现，在存在 biased 的情况下很难做到，因为许多 engineers 个人都是 biased。有些公司让 committees 处理 promotions，以 reduce bias，但这个 system 的主要 inputs 仍然带有很强的 biases。committee 使用人们的 recommendations 作为 input，而其中许多人让这些 biases 比他们的 technical judgment 具有更大的 weight。即使我们假设引入一个 system，identified 哪些人的 judgments 与并非 directly relevant to performance 的 factors 是 highly correlated，并且不给那些 recommendations 任何 weight，人们的 opinions 也常常会 limit 一个人能做的 work。我听到一些 junior 人员抱怨，他们无法 get promoted，因为他们的 work 不 fulfill promo criteria。当他们请求获准去做可能让自己 get promoted 的 work 时，却被告知他们太 junior，不能做那种 work。他们通常会被困在自己的 level，直到找到一位足够相信他们的 potential 的 manager，愿意给他们一些如果做得好就可能 result in a promo 的 work。与此 interacts 的另一个 factor 是，如果你表现良好和/或有很高的 "promo velocity"，也就是频繁地 getting promoted，就更容易 transfer 到有 high-impact work 可做的 team；而如果你表现不佳，或者甚至只是 promo velocity 很低、并没有表现得特别差，就会更难。在更高的 levels，不能做 high-impact work 的情况并不常见，但要 separate out individual performance 与 biases 各自的 impact 也非常困难，因为 performance 在很大程度上取决于你能 influence 谁；如果你需要 at scale 地做到这一点，就会涉及尝试 influence 那些 biased 的人，而要在更高的 levels get promoted，通常就需要如此。bias 的 nested、multi-level 的 impact，使人很难以能够 remove bias 的 impact 的方式去 change system。

虽然从整体上看这个 system 时很容易悲观，但看看自己作为个人能做什么，也很容易乐观起来。要像 Bill Wight（那位因 recommending "funny looking" baseball players 而闻名的 scout）那样，忽略其他人错误地认为重要的东西，其实相当容易[5](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:F)。[我曾在一家这样做的公司工作，而它的 engineering team 是我工作过的所有公司中最好的，远远超过其他公司](https://danluu.com/culture/#appendix-centaur-s-hiring-process)。他们的做法是忽略其他公司在意的 criteria，例如，[hiring 来自 non-elite schools 的人，而不是 focusing on pedigree](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1425514112642080773)、[不因人们没有练习在 whiteboard 上 solving 那些工作实践中并不会 solve 的 abstract problems，就将他们 ruling people out](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/)、[不设与 job performance 无关的 cultural fit criteria](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1447322487583244289)（他们确实在意人们是否 self-directed，以及在获得很高程度的 independence 时能否 function effectively），等等。[6](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:C)

## 原文

\[Click to collapse / expand section on sports\] Here are some notes from talent scouts:

-   Recruit A:
    -   ... will be a real specimen with chance to have a Dave Parker body. Facially looks like Leon Wagner. Good body flexibility. Very large hands.
-   Recruit B:
    -   Outstanding physical specimen – big athletic frame with broad shoulders and long, solid arms and leg. Good bounce in his step and above avg body control. Good strong face.
-   Recruit C:
    -   Hi butt, longish arms & legs, leanish torso, young colt
    -   \[different scout\]: Wiry loose good agility with good face
    -   \[another scout\]: Athletic looking body, loose, rangy, slightly bow legged.

Out of context, you might think they were scouting actors or models, but these are baseball players ("A" is Lloyd Moseby, "B" is Jim Abbott, and "C" is Derek Jeter), ones that were quite good (Lloyd Moseby was arguably only a very good player for perhaps four years, but that makes him extraordinary compared to most players who are scouted). If you read other baseball scouting reports, you'll see a lot of comments about how someone has a "good face", who they look like, what their butt looks like, etc.

Basically everyone wants to hire talented folks. But even in baseball, where returns to hiring talent are obvious and high and which is the most easily quantified major U.S. sport, people made fairly obvious blunders for a century due to relying on incorrectly honed gut feelings that relied heavily on unconscious as well as conscious biases. Later, we'll look at what baseball hiring means for other fields, but first, let's look at how players who didn't really pan out ended up with similar scouting reports (programmers who don't care about sports can think of this as equivalent to interview feedback) as future superstars, such as the following comments on [Adam Eaton](https://www.baseball-reference.com/players/e/eatonad01.shtml), who was a poor player by pro standards despite being considered one of the hottest prospects (potential hires) of his generation:

-   Scout 1: Medium frame/compact/firm. A very good athlete / shows quick "cat-like" reactions. Excellent overall body strength. Medium hands / medium length arms / w strong forearms ... Player is a tough competitor. This guy has some old fashioned bull-dog in his make-up.
-   Scout 2: Good body with frame to develop. Long arms and big hands. Narrow face. Has sideburns and wears hat military style. Slope shoulders. Strong inlegs ... Also played basketball. Good athlete .... Attitude is excellent. Can't see him breaking down. One of the top HS pitchers in the country
-   Scout 3: 6'1"-6'2" 180 solid upper and lower half. Room to pack another 15 without hurting

On the flip side, scouts would also pan players who would later turn out to be great based on their physical appearance, such as these scouts who were concerned about Albert Pujols's weight:

-   Scout 1: Heavy, bulky body. Extra (weight) on lower half. Future (weight) problem. Aggressive hitter with mistake HR power. Tends to be a hacker.
-   Scout 2: Good bat (speed) with very strong hands. Competes well and battles at the plate. Contact seems fair. Swing gets a little long at times. Will over pull. He did not hit the ball hard to CF or RF. Weight will become an issue in time.

Pujols ended up becoming one of the best baseball players of all time ([currently ranked 32nd by WAR](https://web.archive.org/web/20220209005630/https://www.baseball-reference.com/leaders/WAR_career.shtml)). His weight wasn't a problem, but if you read scouting reports on other great players who were heavy or short, they were frequently underrated. Of course, baseball scouting reports didn't only look at people's appearances, but scouts were generally highly biased by what they thought an athlete should look like.

Because using stats in baseball has "won" (top teams all employ stables of statisticians nowadays) and "old school" folks don't want to admit this, we often see people saying that using stats doesn't really result in different outcomes than we used to get. But this is so untrue that the examples people give are generally [self-refuting](https://www.patreon.com/posts/62223485). For example, here's what Sports Illustrated had to say on the matter:

> Media and Internet draft prognosticators love to play up the “scrappy little battler” aspect with Madrigal, claiming that modern sabermetrics helps scouts include smaller players that were earlier overlooked. Of course, that is hogwash. A players \[sic\] abilities dictate his appeal to scouts—not height or bulk—and smaller, shorter players have always been a staple of baseball-from Mel Ott to Joe Morgan to Kirby Puckett to Jose Altuve.

These are curious examples to use in support of scouting since Kirby Puckett was famously overlooked by scouts despite putting up statistically dominant performances and was only able to become a baseball player through random happenstance, when the assistant director of the Twins [farm system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_team) went to watch his own son play in a baseball game his and saw Kirby Puckett in the same game, which led to the Twins drafting Kirby Puckett, who carried the franchise for a decade.

Joe Morgan was also famously overlooked and only managed to become a professional baseball player through random happenstance. Morgan put up statistically dominant numbers in high school, but was ignored due to his height. Because he wasn't drafted by a pro team, he went to Oakland City College, where he once again put up great numbers that were ignored. The reason a team noticed him was a combination of two coincidences. First, a new baseball team was created and that new team needed to fill a team and the associated farm system, which meant that they needed a lot of players. Second, that new baseball team needed to hire scouts and hired Bill Wight (who wasn't previously working as a scout) as a scout. Wight became known for not having the same appearance bias as nearly every other scout and was made fun of for signing "funny looking" baseball players. Bill convinced the new baseball team to "hire" quite a few overlooked players, including Joe Morgan.

Mel Ott was also famously overlooked and only managed to become a professional baseball player through happenstance. He was so dominant in high school that he played for adult semi-pro teams in his spare time. However, when he graduated, pro baseball teams didn't want him because he was too small, so he took a job at a lumber company and played for the company team. The owner of the lumber company was impressed by his baseball skills and, luckily for Ott, the owner of the lumber company was business partners and friends with the owner of a baseball team and effectively got Ott a position on a pro baseball team, resulting in the [20th best baseball career of all time as ranked by WAR](https://web.archive.org/web/20220208221047/https://www.baseball-reference.com/players/o/ottme01.shtml)[1](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:J). Most short baseball players probably didn't get a random lucky break; for every one who did, there are likely many who didn't. If we look at how many nearly-ignored-but-lucky players put up numbers that made them all-time greats, it seems likely that the vast majority of the potentially greatest players of all time who played amateur or semi-pro baseball were ignored and did not play professional baseball (if this seems implausible, when reading the upcoming sections on chess, go, and shogi, consider what would happen if you removed all of the players who don't look like they should be great based on what people think makes someone cognitively skilled at major tech companies, and then look at what fraction of all-time-greats remain).

Deciding who to "hire" for a baseball team was a high stakes decision with many millions of dollars (in 2022 dollars) on the line, but rather than attempt to seriously quantify productivity, teams decided who to draft (hire) based on all sorts of irrelevant factors. Like any major sport, baseball productivity is much easier to quantify than in most real-world endeavors since the game is much simpler than "real" problems are. And, among major U.S. sports, baseball is the easiest sport to quantify, but [this didn't stop baseball teams from spending a century overindexing on visually obvious criteria such as height and race](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/).

I was reminded of this the other day when, the other day, I saw a thread on Twitter where a very successful person talks about how they got started, saying that they were able to talk their way into an elite institution despite being unqualified and use this story to conclude that elite gatekeepers are basically just scouting for talent and that you just need to show people that you have talent:

> One college related example from my life is that I managed to get into CMU with awful grades and awful SAT scores (I had the flu when I took the test :/)
> 
> I spent a month learning everything about CMU's CS department, then drove there and talked to professors directly when I first showed up at the campus the entrance office asked my GPA and SAT, then asked me to leave. But I managed to talk to one professor, who sent me to their boss, recursively till I was talking to the vice president of the school he asked me why I'm good enough to go to CMU and I said "I'm not sure I am. All these other kids are really smart. I can leave now" and he interrupted me and reminded me how much agency it took to get into that room.
> 
> He gave me a handwritten acceptance letter on the spot ... I think one secret, at least when it comes to gatekeepers, is that they're usually just looking for high agency and talent.

I've heard this kind of story from other successful people, who tend to come to bimodal conclusions on what it all means. Some conclude that the world correctly recognized their talent and that this is how the world works; talent gets recognized and rewarded. Others conclude that the world is fairly random with respect to talent being rewarded and that they got lucky to get rewarded for their talent when many other people with similar talents who used similar strategies were passed over[2](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:B).

Another time I was reminded of old baseball scouting reports was when I heard about how a friend of mine who's now an engineering professor at a top Canadian university got there. Let's call her Jane. When Jane was an undergrad at the university she's now a professor at, she was sometimes helpfully asked "are you lost?" when she was on campus. Sometimes this was because, as a woman, she didn't look like was in the right place when she was in an engineering building. Other times, it was because she looked like and talked like someone from rural Canada. Once, a security guard thought she was a homeless person who had wandered onto campus. After a few years, she picked up the right clothes and mannerisms to pass as "the right kind of person", with help from her college friends, who explained to her how one is supposed to talk and dress, but when she was younger, people's first impression was that she was an admin assistant, and now their first impression is that she's a professor's wife because they don't expect a woman to be a professor in her department. She's been fairly successful, but it's taken a lot more work than it would've for someone who looked the part.

On whether or not, in her case, her gate keepers were just looking for agency and talent, she once failed a civil engineering exam because she'd never heard of a "corn dog" and also barely passed an intro programming class she took where the professor announced that anyone who didn't already know how to program was going to fail.

The corn dog exam failure was because there was a question on a civil engineering exam where students were supposed to design a corn dog dispenser. My friend had never heard of a corn dog and asked the professor what a corn dog was. The professor didn't believe that she didn't know what a corn dog was and berated her in front of the entire class to for asking a question that clearly couldn't be serious. Not knowing what a corn dog was, she designed something that put corn inside a hot dog and dispensed a hot dog with corn inside, which failed because that's not what a corn dog is.

It turns out the gatekeepers for civil engineering and programming were not, in fact, just looking for agency and were instead looking for someone who came from the right background. I suspect this is not so different from the CMU professor who admitted a promising student on the spot, it just happens that a lot of people pattern match "smart teenage boy with a story about why their grades and SAT scores are bad" to "promising potential prodigy" and "girl from rural Canada with the top grade in her high school class who hasn't really used a computer before and dresses like a poor person from rural Canada because she's paying for college while raising her younger brother because their parents basically abandoned both of them" to "homeless person who doesn't belong in engineering".

Another thing that reminded me of how funny baseball scouting reports are is a conversation I had with Ben Kuhn a while back.

**Me**: it's weird how tall so many of the men at my level (senior staff engineer) are at big tech companies. In recent memory, I think I've only been in a meeting with one man who's shorter than me at that level or above. I'm only 1" shorter than U.S. average! And the guy who's shorter than me has worked remotely for at least a decade, so I don't know if people really register his height. And people seem to be even taller on the management track. If I look at the VPs I've been in meetings with, they must all be at least 6' tall.  
**Ben**: Maybe I could be a VP at a big tech company. I'm 6' tall!  
**Me**: Oh, I guess I didn't know how tall 6' tall is. The VPs I'm in meetings with are noticeably taller than you. They're probably at least 6'2"?  
**Ben**: Wow, that's really tall for a minimum. 6'2" is 96%-ile for U.S. adult male  

When I've discussed this with successful people who work in big companies of various sorts (tech companies, consulting companies, etc.), men who would be considered tall by normal standards, 6' or 6'1", tell me that they're frequently the shortest man in the room during important meetings. 6'1" is just below the median height of a baseball player. There's something a bit odd about height seeming more correlated to success as a consultant or a programmer than in baseball, where height directly conveys an advantage. One possible explanation would be due to a [halo effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halo_effect), where positive associations about tall or authoritative seeming people contribute to their success.

When I've seen this discussed online, someone will point out that this is because height and cognitive performance are correlated. But if we look at the literature on IQ, the correlation isn't strong enough to explain something like this. We can also observe this if we look at fields where people's mental acuity is directly tested by something other than an IQ test, such as in chess, where most top players are around average height, with some outliers in both directions. Even without looking at the data in detail, this should be expected because correlation between height and IQ is weak, with much the correlation due to the relationship at the low end[3](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:Q), and the correlation between IQ and performance in various mental tasks is also weak (some people will say that it's strong by social science standards, [but that's very weak in terms of actual explanatory power even when looking at the population level](https://danluu.com/percentile-latency/) and it's even weaker at the individual level). [And then if we look at chess in particular, we can see that the correlation is weak, as expected](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2016/12/22143817/Burgoyne-Sala-Gobet-Macnamara-Campitelli-Hambrick-2016.pdf).

Since the correlation is weak, and there are many more people around average height than not, we should expect that most top chess players are around average height. [If we look at the most dominant chess players in recent history, Carlsen, Anand, and Kasparov](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/chess-players/do-chess-players-tend-to-be-short), they're 5'8", 5'8", and 5'9", respectively (if you look at different sources, they'll claim heights of plus or minus a couple inches, but still with a pretty normal range; people often exaggerate heights; if you look at people who try to do real comparisons either via photos or in person, measured heights are often lower than what people claim their own height is[4](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:O)).

It's a bit more difficult to find heights of go and shogi players, but it seems like the absolute [top modern players from this list](https://gameofgo.app/learn/top-ten-go-players) I could find heights for (Lee Sedol, Yoshiharu Habu) are roughly in the normal range, with there being some outliers in both directions among elite players who aren't among the best of all time, as with chess.

If it were the case that height or other factors in appearance were very strongly correlated with mental performance, we would expect to see a much stronger correlation between height and performance in activities that relatively directly measure mental performance, like chess, than we do between height and career success, but it's the other way around, which seems to indicate that the halo effect from height is stronger than any underlying benefits that are correlated with height.

If we look at activities where there's a fair amount of gatekeeping before people are allowed to really show their skills but where performance can be measured fairly accurately and where hiring better employees has an immediate, measurable, direct impact on company performance, such as [baseball and hockey, we can see that people went with their gut instinct over data for decades after there were public discussions about how data-driven approaches found large holes in people's intuition](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/).

If we then look at programming, where it's somewhere between extremely difficult and impossible to accurately measure individual performance and the impact of individual performance on company success is much less direct than in sports, what should our estimate of how accurate talent assessment be?

The pessimistic view is that it seems implausible that we should expect that talent assessment is better than in sports, where it took decades of there being fairly accurate and rigorous public write-ups of performance assessments for companies to take talent assessment seriously. With programming, talent assessment isn't even far enough along that anyone can write up accurate evaluations of people across the industry, so we haven't even started the decades long process of companies fighting to keep evaluating people based on personal opinions instead of accurate measurements.

Jobs have something equivalent to old school baseball scouting reports at multiple levels. At the hiring stage, there are multiple levels of filters that encode people's biases. A classic study on this is [Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9873/w9873.pdf)'s paper, which found that "white sounding" names on resumes got more callbacks for interviews than "black sounding" names and that having a "white sounding" name on the resume increased the returns to having better credentials on the resume. Since then, many variants of this study have been done, e.g., [resumes with white sounding names do better than resumes with Asian sounding names](https://www.utpjournals.press/doi/pdf/10.3138/cpp.2017-033), [professors with white sounding names are evaluated on CVs are evaluated as having better interpersonal skills than professors with black and Asian sounding names on CVs](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Juan-Madera-2/publication/266589756_The_Problem_for_Black_Professors_Judged_Before_Met/links/54381fea0cf24a6ddb922d57/The-Problem-for-Black-Professors-Judged-Before-Met.pdf), etc.

The literature on promotions and leveling is much weaker, but I and other folks who are in highly selected environments that effectively require multiple rounds of screening, each against more and more highly selected folks, such as VPs, senior (as in "senior staff"+) ICs, professors at elite universities, etc., have observed that filtering on height is as severe or more severe than in baseball but less severe than in basketball.

That's curious when, in mental endeavors where the "promotion" criteria are directly selected by performance, such as in chess, height appears to only be very weakly correlated to success. A major issue in the literature on this is that, in general, social scientists look at averages. In a lot of the studies, they simply produce a correlation coefficient. If you're lucky, they may produce a graph where, for each height, they produce an average of something or other. That's the simplest thing to do but this only provides a very coarse understanding of what's going on.

Because I like knowing how things tick, including organizations and people's opinions, I've (informally, verbally) polled a lot of engineers about what they thought about other engineers. What I found was that there was a lot of clustering of opinions, resulting in clusters of folks that had rough agreement about who did excellent work. Within each cluster, people would often disagree about the ranking of engineers, but they would generally agree on who was "good to excellent".

One cluster was (in my opinion; this could, of course, also just be my own biases) people who were looking at the output people produced and were judging people based on that. Another cluster was of people who were looking at some combination of height and confidence and were judging people based on that. This one was a mystery to me for a long time (I've been asking people questions like this and collating the data out of habit, long before I had the idea to write this post and, until I recognized the pattern, I found it odd that so many people who have good technical judgment, as evidenced by their ability to do good work and make comments showing good technical judgment, highly evaluated so many people who so frequently said blatantly incorrect things and produced poorly working or even non-working systems). Another cluster was around credentials, such as what school someone went to or what the person was leveled at or what prestigious companies they'd worked for. People could have judgment from multiple clusters, e.g., some folks would praise both people who did excellent technical work as well as people who are tall and confident. At higher levels, where it becomes more difficult to judge people's work, relatively fewer people based their judgment on people's output.

When I did this evaluation collating exercise at the startup I worked at, there was basically only one cluster and it was based on people's output, with fairly broad consensus about who the top engineers were, but I haven't seen that at any of the large companies I've worked for. I'm not going to say that means evaluation at that startup was fair (perhaps all of us were falling prey to the same biases), but at least we weren't falling prey to the most obvious biases.

Back to big companies, if we look at what it would take to reform the promotion system, it seems difficult to do when biased because many individual engineers are biased. Some companies have committees handle promotions in order to reduce bias, but the major inputs to the system still have strong biases. The committee uses, as input, recommendations from people, many of whom let those biases have more weight than their technical judgment. Even if we, hypothetically, introduced a system that identified whose judgments were highly correlated with factors that aren't directly relevant to performance and gave those recommendations no weight, people's opinions often limit the work that someone can do. A complaint I've heard from some folks who are junior is that they can't get promoted because their work doesn't fulfill promo criteria. When they ask to be allowed to do work that could get them promoted, they're told they're too junior to do that kind of work. They're generally stuck at their level until they find a manager who believes in their potential enough to give them work that could possibly result in a promo if they did a good job. Another factor that interacts with this is that it's easier to transfer to a team where high-impact work is available if you're doing well and/or having high "promo velocity", i.e., are getting promoted frequently and harder if you're doing poorly or even just have low promo velocity and aren't doing particularly poorly. At higher levels, it's uncommon to not be able to do high-impact work, but it's also very difficult to separate out the impact of individual performance and biases because a lot of performance is about who you can influence, which is going to involve trying to influence people who are biased if you need to do it at scale, which you generally do to get promoted at higher levels. The nested, multi-level, impact of bias makes it difficult to change the system in a way that would remove the impact of bias.

Although it's easy to be pessimistic when looking at the system as a whole, it's also easy to be optimistic when looking at what one can do as an individual. It's pretty easy to do what Bill Wight (the scout known for recommending "funny looking" baseball players) did and ignore what other people incorrectly think is important[5](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:F). [I worked for a company that did this which had, by far, the best engineering team of any company I've ever worked for](https://danluu.com/culture/#appendix-centaur-s-hiring-process). They did this by ignoring the criteria other companies cared about, e.g., [hiring people from non-elite schools instead of focusing on pedigree](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1425514112642080773), [not ruling people out for not having practiced solving abstract problems on a whiteboard that people don't solve in practice at work](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/), [not having cultural fit criteria that weren't related to job performance](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1447322487583244289) (they did care that people were self-directed and would function effectively when given a high degree of independence), etc.[6](https://danluu.com/talent/#fn:C)

### Appendix: other factors

This post used height as a running example because it's both something that's easy to observe is correlated to success in men which has been studied across a number of fields. I would guess that social class markers / [mannerisms](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1480616193761288194), as in the Jane example from this post, have at least as much impact. For example, a number of people have pointed out to me that the tall, successful, people they're surrounded by say things with very high confidence (often incorrect things, but said confidently) and also have mannerisms that convey confidence and authority.

Other physical factors also seem to have a large impact. There's a fairly large literature on how much the halo effect causes people who are generally attractive to be rated more highly on a variety of dimensions, e.g., [morality](https://www.gwern.net/docs/sociology/2021-klebl.pdf). There's a famous ask metafilter (reddit before there was reddit) answer to a quesiton that's something like "how can you tell someone is bad?" and the most favorited answer (I hope for ironic reasons, although the answerer seemed genuine) is that they have bad teeth. Of course, [in the U.S., having bad teeth is a marker of childhood financial poverty](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1477001184904814593), not impoverished moral character. And, of course, gender is another dimension that people appear to filter on for reasons unrelated to talent or competence.

Another is just random luck. To go back to the baseball example, one of the few negative scouting reports on Chipper Jones came from a scout who said

> Was not aggressive w/bat. Did not drive ball from either side. Displayed non-chalant attitude at all times. He was a disappointment to me. In the 8 games he managed to collect only 1 hit and hit very few balls well. Showed slap-type swing from L.side . . . 2 av. tools

Another scout, who saw him on more typical days, correctly noted

> Definite ML prospect . . . ML tools or better in all areas . . . due to outstanding instincts, ability, and knowledge of game. Superstar potential.

Another similarly noted:

> This boy has all the tools. Has good power and good basic approach at the plate with bat speed. Excellent make up and work-habits. Best prospect in Florida in the past 7 years I have been scouting . . . This boy must be considered for our \[1st round draft\] pick. Does everything well and with ease.

There's a lot of variance in performance. If you judge performance by watching someone for a short period of time, you're going to get wildly different judgements depending on when you watch them.

If you read the blind orchestra audition study that everybody cites, the study itself seems poor quality and unconvincing, but it also seems true that blind auditions were concomitant with an increase in orchestras hiring people who didn't look like what people expected musicians to look like. Blind auditions, where possible, seem like something good to try.
