# 偏差正常化｜Normalization of deviance

---

## 正文

你有没有提起过某件自己觉得完全正常的事，结果对方却大吃一惊？每当我描述一些同事们都觉得正常的事时，这种情况就总会发生。不知为什么，和我交谈的人脸上愉快的微笑，会变成惊恐的僵笑。下面是几个有代表性的例子。

有一家公司，可能是我工作过的最好的地方，兼具 Valve 和 Netflix 最好的部分。同事们都很棒，而且你几乎拥有完全的自由，想做什么就做什么。但作为这种 culture 的 side effect，他们在第一年里可能就会失去一半的 new hires，有些是 voluntarily 离开，有些是 involuntarily 离开。完全正常，对吧？下面还有一些轶事，我工作过的地方的人都认为它们完全正常。而且往往不只是正常，甚至值得称赞。

有一家公司对 infrastructure 极其保密。比如，有个 team 担心，如果向 hardware vendor reported bugs，这些 bugs 就会 get fixed，而他们的 competitors 就能 use the fixes。Solution：索要 firmware，自己 fix bugs！最近，我认识的一群公司外部人士，试图 reproduce 这家公司今年早些时候 published 的 paper 中的 algorithm。这群人发现，他们无法 reproduce 那个 result，而且 paper 中的 algorithm 导致了异乎寻常的 instability；被问及此事时，一位 authors 回答说：“嗯，我们有些 tweaks 没有放进 paper”，并拒绝分享这些 tweaks。也就是说，为了避免泄露 details，这家公司故意 published 了一个 unreproducible result，一切照常。这家公司通过严格执行 firing leakers 的 policy 来 enforces secrecy。在 orientation 时，他们会举出因 leaking 而被 fired 的人的例子（比如，有人泄露了某个办公室里即将举办一场音乐会的消息），还会在 company all hands 上宣布因 leaks 而进行的 firings。这些 policies 的结果是，我认识好几个人，他们甚至不敢把诸如 health insurance 的 updated info 之类的 emails forward 给配偶，生怕 forwarding 错了 email 而被 fired；他们宁可换一台 computer，重新敲一遍 email 再转交，或者用 phone 拍下 email。

在一个办公室里，有一天我问起，为什么我几乎从没见过某两个人同时出现在同一个房间。别人告诉我，他们的积怨已经持续了十年，而且情况其实已经改善了——多年来，他们真的不能待在同一个房间，因为其中一个人会气得失去理智，做出令人后悔的事；但如今局面已经缓和到，偶尔能在办公室的同一侧，甚至同一个房间里见到他们。这两个人也不是什么无关紧要的人。他们正是这个办公室仅有的两个 teams 的两位 managers。

有一家公司的 culture 古怪到，当我坐下来想写一篇关于它的文章时，发现自己[写下的内容不仅超过了其他任何单篇文章，甚至超过了其他所有文章的总和](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:N)（如今已经远超 100k 字，相当于一本中等篇幅的书）。也是在这家公司，最近有人向我解释，我们不 using data to make decisions，而是靠 political connections，这是多么棒的一件事；而且，making decisions based on data 这种想法反正也只是个神话，根本没人那样做。还是这家公司，为了让我加入而告诉我的四件事，全部都是假的，最后的工作偏偏就是我明确说过不想做的那一件。我加入这家公司时，我的 team 好几个月都没有 use version control，要让所有人都 use version control，着实经历了一番斗争。虽然我赢了这场斗争，但在让大家在 checking in 之前先 run a build 这件事上，我输了，更不用说 run tests 了，所以 build 每天都会 broken 好几次。当我提到，我认为这对我们的 productivity 是个问题时，有人告诉我，这没关系，因为它对每个人的影响都一样。既然唯一重要的是我的 stack ranked productivity，我就不该在意它对整个 team 的影响；这对每个人来说都很正常，就意味着没有什么可担心的。

有一家公司发起了多项大规模 initiatives，要 recruit 更多 women 进入 engineering roles，但[在 recruiter screens 中，women 仍会因为不够 technical 而 get rejected](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)，此前她们被问到的问题是：“你的经验涉及 algorithms，还是只是 coding？”我以为，我的 referral 加上非常有力的 recommendation，应该能避免这种事，但并没有。

有一家公司，我在那里参与过一项四人工作，budget 达数亿美元，每年产生的 impact 达十亿美元，但申请购买几百美元的东西，却经常要花上几个月，或者遭到拒绝。

你可能会想，是不是我工作过的地方都格外糟糕。没错，这些公司通常都被认为是还不错的工作场所，其中两家还被认为是最好的工作场所之一，但也许只是我碰巧去了被高估的地方。不过，当我听别人讲其他公司如何运作时，即使是 engineering 声誉极佳的地方，我也会有同样的经历，只不过这次震惊的是我，而觉得自己的故事很正常的，是和我交谈的人。

有些公司会 use [@flaky](https://github.com/box/flaky)，其中包括 SF Bay area 绝大多数使用 Python 的 unicorns。如果你不知道这是什么，它是一个 library，让你可以给那些烦人的、有时 pass、有时 fail 的 flaky tests add 一个 Python annotation。我问过来自三家不同公司的多位同事和前同事，问他们觉得这会做什么，他们都猜它会 re-runs the test 多次，只要其中任何一次 runs fail，就 reports a failure。很接近，但不完全是。要 use @flaky 来实现这一点，在技术上是可能的，但实际上，它被用来 re-run the test 多次，只要其中任何一次 runs pass，就 reports a pass。created @flaky 的公司实际上是一家 storage infrastructure 公司，而这个 library 在它最大的 competitor 那里也被广泛 used。

有一家公司以出色的 engineering practices 闻名，但我上次查看时，它的 reliability 只有 2 9s，而原因完全可以从它的 engineering practices 预见。这已经是接连第二件无法 deanonymized 的事，因为有多家公司都符合描述。这里，我说的不是那些想成为下一个 reddit 或 twitter 的公司，对它们来说，reliability 只有 1 9 显然也完全没问题。我说的是那些出售 platforms、让其他公司依赖它们的公司；一旦发生 outage，dependent companies 就会在整个 outage 期间 pause operations。多家 build infrastructure 的公司，都采用了会让 reliability 落到 2 9s 的 practices。

据我所见，许多这样的公司，一开始几乎全身心地专注于 product growth。这完全合理，因为公司刚 founded 时，价值几乎为零；它们懒得去做那些能 protect them from losses 的事，比如良好的 ops practices，或者真正拥有 security，因为根本没什么可失去的（嗯，除了不可避免的 security breach 发生时的 user data；如果你和 unicorns 的 security 人员聊过，就会知道这些事确实会发生）。

结果就是形成了一种 culture：人们 hyper-focused 于 growth，却 ignore risk。即使公司已经 grown 到价值远超十亿美元、已经有东西可以失去，这种 culture 往往还是会延续。从 Google、Amazon 或其他拥有扎实 ops practices 的地方来到这些公司的人，无不震惊。他们常常试图修复问题，随后在发现自己无力改变什么时离开。

Google 如今的 ops 和 security practices，可能是所有 tech company 中最好的。说一句“你应该像 Google 一样认真对待这些事”很容易，但看看他们是怎么走到这一步的，更有启发。如果你查看 codebase，就会发现各种 services 的 names 都以 z 结尾，数量多得出奇的 variables 也是如此。有人告诉我，这是因为很久以前，有人想 add monitoring。让 `google.com/somename` expose monitoring data 并不怎么 secure，所以他们加了一个 z。`google.com/somenamez`。为了 security。就在这家如今 security 全球最好的公司。他们如今的 security 做得如此之好，以至于我聊过的好几个人（他们全都是在这件事发生后才加入的）都坚决否认这件事曾经发生过，尽管他们给出的理由实在说不通（比如，为了 avoid name collisions），而我的消息来源，恰恰是事情发生时就在场的人。

Google 从在 names 末尾加 z，走到拥有全球最好的 security，并不是因为有人发表了一番振奋人心的演讲，或者写了一篇令人信服的文章。他们是在几次丢脸之后才做到的，这让那些想把事情做“对”的人，有了 fix fundamental process issues 的筹码。几乎每一家我所知道、拥有良好 practices 的公司，都是同样的故事。Microsoft 多年来一直是 security 界的笑话，直到多次灾难性的 exploits 迫使他们认真对待 security。这么说听起来很简单，但如果你和当时在那里的人聊过，就会知道，这场改变极其惨烈。尽管有来自高层的 mandate，仍有一些人进行了激烈的 political pushback，他们的立场是：公司一路走到 2003 年的地位，也没在 security 这类 practices 上浪费时间。既然一直行之有效，为什么要改？

每个 industry 都能看到这类事。tech 人员经常提起的一个经典例子，就是 doctors 和 nurses 的 hand-washing。众所周知，germs 是存在的，而 properly washing hands 能极大地 reduces the odds of transmitting germs，从而 significantly reduces hospital mortality rates。尽管如此，受过训练的 doctors 和 nurses 仍常常不这样做。这就需要 Interventions。提醒大家 wash their hands 的标牌可以 save lives。但如果有人站在 hand-washing stations，要求经过的人 wash their hands，就能有更多 lives 被 saved。人们可以无视标牌，却无法无视强制他们 wash their hands 的人。

这和 tech companies 中多次引入更好的 practices 的尝试如出一辙。如果你告诉大家应该这样做，会有一点帮助。如果你通过 code review 来 enforce 更好的 practices，帮助就大得多。

data 清楚地表明，人类真的很不擅长花时间去做那些已被充分理解、无可争辩地能 reduce the risk of rare but catastrophic events 的事。我们会 rationalize，认为 taking shortcuts 才是正确、合理的做法。对此有一个 term：normalization of deviance。它在许多其他 contexts 中都得到了充分研究，包括 healthcare、aviation、mechanical engineering、aerospace engineering 和 civil engineering，但我们却看不到在 software 的 context 中讨论它。事实上，我从未见过这个 term 被用在 software 的 context 中。

我们能不能从别人的错误中学习，而不是每个错误都亲自犯一遍？这个 industry 的现状让这听起来不太可能，但我们还是试试看吧。[John Banja 有一篇很好的 summary paper，讨论 healthcare 中的 normalization of deviance](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2821100/)，其中的经验教训，我们可以尝试 apply 到 software development 中。需要注意的是，由于 Banja 关注的是 patient outcomes，因此这与 devops failure modes 有着密切的 analogy，但 normalization of deviance 也会发生在那些不那么直接 analogous 的 cultural contexts 中。

这篇 paper 的第一个 section 详细介绍了 healthcare 以及其他领域的多起 disasters。下面是一个典型的 example：

> 作者曾以 expert witness 的身份参与一起 catastrophic negligence case，其中一名 anesthesiologist 应一名 surgeon 的要求 turning off 一台 ventilator，因为这名 surgeon 想给 patient 的 abdomen 拍一张 x-ray（Banja, 2005, pp. 87-101）。这台 ventilator 原本只应 off 几秒钟，但 anesthesiologist 忘了 turn it back on，或者以为自己已经 turned it back on，实际上却没有。这名 patient 处于 without oxygen 的状态足够长，以致出现 global anoxia，进而陷入 vegetative state。她始终没有 recovered，9 天后被 disconnected from artificial ventilation，又过了 2 天便 died。后来人们发现，operating room 中的 anesthesia alarms 和 monitoring equipment 被故意 programmed 为一种 “suspend indefinite” mode，因此 anesthesiologist 没有收到关于 ventilator problem 的 alerted。可悲的是，本来用来 prevent 这种惨剧的 instrumentality 竟被 disabled，可能是因为 operating room 的工作人员觉得持续不断的蜂鸣声令人烦躁不安。

因为 notifications 太多、太烦人，就 Turning off 或 ignoring 它们？一次 erroneous manual operation？这简直可能直接出自我能想到的不少公司的 post-mortem，只不过其 result 是一场悲惨的死亡，而不是数百万美元的损失。如果你[读过很多 tech post-mortems](https://danluu.com/postmortem-lessons/)，那么 Banja 的 paper 中每个 example 都会让你感到熟悉，尽管细节有所不同。

这一 section 最后写道，

> 这些 disasters 通常 reveal 的是，造成它们的 factors 往往有着“[漫长的 incubation periods，其典型表现是 rule violations、在无人察觉中 accumulated 的 discrepant events，以及关于 hazards 的 cultural beliefs；这些因素共同 prevented 了那些本可能 staved off harmful outcomes 的 interventions](http://www.jstor.org/stable/223506?seq=1#page_scan_tab_contents)”。此外，尤其令人震惊的是，多次 rule violations 和 lapses 竟能 coalesce，从而使一场 disaster 得以 occurrence。

这同样可能出自一篇关于 technical failures 的文章。这使得接下来探讨这些 failures 为什么 happen 的 section 显得值得一读。文中给出的 reasons 是：

#### 这些 rules 既愚蠢又 inefficient

paper 中的 example 是给 newborns delivering medication。为了 prevent “drug diversion”，nurses 必须在 computer 上 enter 自己的 password，才能 access medication drawer、取出 medication，并 administer correct amount。为了确保第一名 nurse 没有偷拿 drugs，如果还有任何 drug 剩余，就应由另一名 nurse 观察这一 process，然后在 computer 上 enter 自己的 password，表明自己亲眼见证了 drug 被 properly disposed of。

这听起来很熟悉。有多少 technical postmortems 是以“有人 skipped 了一些 steps，因为它们 inefficient”开头的？比如，“programmer force pushed 了一份 bad config 或 bad code，因为他们确信不会出任何问题，于是 skipped staging/testing”？臭名昭著的 [2014 年 11 月 Azure outage](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/final-root-cause-analysis-and-improvement-areas-nov-18-azure-storage-service-interruption/) 正是因此发生的。大约同一时期，Azure 某个竞争对手的一名 dev overrode 了不应 push 一份 fails tests 的 config 这条 rule，因为他们知道这份 config 不可能是 bad。当这导致 canary deploy 开始 failing 时，他们又 overrode 了不能带着 failure 从 canary deploy 到 staging 的 rule，因为他们知道自己的 config 不可能是 bad，所以 failure 肯定来自别的地方。那份 postmortem revealed，这份 config 是 technically correct 的，但 exposed 了 underlying software 中的一个 bug；这份 config 所 revealed 的 latent bug 没有 Azure bug 那么 severe，完全是运气好。

人类不擅长 reasoning failures 如何 cascade，因此我们 implement 关于何时 deploy 才 safe 的 bright line rules。但让我们难以 reason 何时 deploy 才 safe 的同一个原因，也让这些 rules 显得愚蠢而 inefficient。

#### Knowledge 是 imperfect 且 uneven 的

人们不会自然而然地知道什么才应该是 normal，而当新人被 onboarded 时，他们学会那些已经 normalized 的 deviant processes，与学会 reasonable processes 一样容易。

[Julia Evans](http://jvns.ca/) 向我描述了这件事如何发生：

_新人加入_  
**新人**：卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧槽  
**老员工**：是啊，我们知道，我们也很担心  
**新人**：卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧……  
_新人习惯了_  
_新人 #2 加入_  
**新人 #2**：卧槽 卧槽 卧槽 卧槽  
**新人**：是啊，我们知道。我们也很担心。

这里真正隐蔽而有害的是，人们会真的认同那个让人喊“卧槽”的观念，并在整个职业生涯中将它传播到其他地方。有一次，我[在一个经常 broken 的 open source project 上做了一些工作，并被告知 broken build 是 normal 的，而且他们做得比 average 更好](https://danluu.com/julialang/)，之后我算了算，发现那个 project 基本上是同类中最差的，于是写了些东西，谈论[用相对较少的 effort，就可能拥有一个几乎总能 passes 的 build](https://danluu.com/broken-builds/) 这一想法。我收到的最常见回应是：“哇，那家伙肯定在和 superstar programmers 共事。但现实一点吧。我们每个人每周都会 break the build 至少几次。”仿佛在 checking code in 之前 running tests（甚至只是 attempting to compile）都需要超人的能力。但人们一旦确信某种 deviation 是 normal 的，往往就会变得非常执着于这一观念。

#### 我是为了我的 patient 好，才 breaking the rule

paper 中的 example 是某个人 breaks 了 finding a vein 时应该 wear gloves 的 rule。其 reasoning 是，wearing gloves 会让 find a vein 变得更难，这可能导致他们不得不用 needle 在 baby 身上 stick 多次。这很难反驳。谁也不想让 baby 多受 pain！

我能想到的第二严重的 outage，发生在有人注意到一个 database service 出现 slowness 的时候。他们向这个 service pushed 了一个 fix，为了 prevent service degradation 继续 spreading，他们 ignored 了应该进行 proper、slow、staged deploy 的 rule。相反，他们把这个 fix pushed 到了所有 machines。这很难反驳。谁也不想让自己的 customers 遭遇 degraded service！不幸的是，这个 fix exposed 了一个 bug，导致了一场 global outage。

#### 这些 rules 并不 apply to 我／你可以 trust 我

> 大多数人都 perceive themselves as good and decent people，因此他们能够把自己的许多 rule violations 理解为对 problematic situations 完全 rational 且 ethically acceptable 的 responses。他们认为自己没有做错任何事，而在面对相反的 evidence 时，会感到愤怒，而且常常会 fiercely defend themselves。

随着 companies 逐渐壮大，它们最终不得不 impose security，防止每个 employee 都能够 access 几乎所有东西。而在大多数 companies，发生这种情况时，总有些人会非常不高兴。“你不 trust 我吗？如果你 trust 我，为什么要 revoking 我对 X、Y 和 Z 的 access？”

Facebook 曾长期[让所有 employees 都能 access 每个人的 profile](http://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424052702304898704577478483529665936)，这件事广为人知；你甚至可以找到一些 HN comments，表明有些 recruiters 会明确把这作为在 Facebook 工作的一项 perk 来介绍。而且，我能想到不止一家备受推崇的 unicorn，即使已经经历了第一次或第二次严重的 security breach，所有人仍然能够 access 几乎所有东西。要获得足够的 political capital，去 restrict 人们对那些自认为需要知道、或有权知道的东西的 access，是很难的。许多时髦的 startups 都有“trust”和“transparency”之类的 core values，这使得人们很难反对 universal access。

#### Workers 害怕 speak up

有些人，我根本不会向他们提供 feedback，因为我无法判断他们能否坦然接受，而且话一旦说出口，就不可能收回。在这篇 paper 中，作者举了一个例子：一位 doctor 的 handwriting 很差，别人请他说明自己写了什么时，他就会变得很刻薄。结果，人们不再询问，而是靠猜。

在大多数 company cultures 中，人们对提供 feedback 感到别扭。每个人都听说过这样的 project：早就应该被 terminated，却因为没有人愿意提供 explicit feedback，又拖延了几个月甚至几年。即使 cultures 不鼓励刻薄、鼓励 feedback，这仍然是个 problem：友善的 cultures 在 speaking up 方面的 issues，似乎并不比刻薄的 cultures 少，甚至可能更多。在有些地方，人们害怕 speak up，因为他们会遭到刻薄之人的攻击。在另一些地方，他们害怕，是因为自己会被贴上刻薄的标签。这是个难题。

#### Leadership withholding 或 diluting 关于 problems 的 findings

在这篇 paper 中，这表现为：随着 information 沿 chain of command 向上传递，flaws 和 weaknesses 被 diluted。一个例子是，supervisor 可能会采取 sub-optimal actions，以免自己在 superiors 眼里显得很糟糕。

第一次看到这种事情发生时，我很震惊。那时我大概刚毕业半年或一年。我发现我们正在做一件明显 non-optimal 的事，于是向 group 中的 senior person 提了出来。他告诉我，他并不反对我的看法，但如果按我的方式去做，而出现了 failure，那就会非常丢脸。他承认，我的方式 reduced 了 failure 的 chance，而且不会让 failure 的 technical consequences 更糟，但更重要的是，我们不能丢脸。如今我已经工作了十年，对人们如何以及为什么玩这种游戏有了更深的理解，但我仍然觉得这很荒唐。

#### Solutions（解决办法）

假设你注意到，自己的 company 有一个 problem，我听过大多数 companies 的人抱怨它：人们因为 heroism 和 putting out fires 而 get promoted，而不是因为 preventing fires；人们因为 shipping features 而 get promoted，而不是因为做 critical maintenance work 和 bug fixing。你要如何改变这种情况？

最简单的 option，就是自己做正确的事，不理会周围发生的事情。这会产生一些 positive impact，但你的 impact 的 scope 必然 limited。接下来，你可以说服自己的 team 做正确的事：对于那些我觉得非常重要、而且是 sticky 的 practices，我曾经这样做过几次，这样一旦事情运转起来，我就不必继续花精力说服大家。

但如果 incentives 与你背道而驰，要让人们一直做正确的事，就需要持续付出努力，而且这种努力可能是 unsustainable 的。在这种情况下，problem 就变成了说服某个人改变 incentives，然后确保这种 change 能够 works as designed。如何说服别人值得讨论，但这个话题既漫长又复杂，超出了这篇 post 的 scope。至于如何让 change 奏效，无论是在我工作过的地方，还是在那些我非常了解其 internal politics 的地方，我都见过许多“显而易见”的 mistakes 反复发生。

Small companies 就容易多了。我在一家 100 人的 company 工作时，hierarchy 是 individual contributor (IC) -> team lead (TL) -> CEO。仅此而已。CEO 很少干预，但只要他想让某件事发生，它就会发生。关键在于，他很清楚每个人都在做什么，基本上能够 real-time 地 adjust rewards。如果你为 company 做了一件很棒的事，你很可能会 get a raise。不是等九个月后下一个 performance review cycle 到来，而是基本上立刻就会得到。并不是所有 small companies 都能 effectively 做到这一点，但只要有合适的 leadership，它们就可以。这对 large companies 来说是不可能的。

在 large company A (LCA)，他们存在我们正在讨论的 problem，于是上面下达了一项 mandate，要求对从事 critical 但 low-visibility 的 grunt work 的人给予更好的 reward。employees 太多，下达 mandate 的人无法直接做出所有关于 compensation 和 promotion 的 decisions，但他可以 review survey data、spot check decisions，并提供 feedback，直到情况 normalized。我的 subjective perception 是，这家 company 从未成功地让枯燥的 maintenance work 与光鲜的新 projects achieve parity，但已经足够接近，以至于那些想确保事情 worked correctly 的人，不必为了这样做而 significantly damage 自己的 careers。

在 large company B (LCB)，ICs 都同意，对 creating new features 的 reward 比做 critical grunt work 更丰厚，是 problematic 的。我与 managers 交谈时，他们也常常同意。但即便如此，get promoted 的人仍然 disproportionately 是那些 ship 光鲜新东西的人。我看到 management 在 LCB 尝试了许多 cultural 和 process changes。其中大多数都表现为拥有响亮 titles 的人发表宣言。对于真正重要的事情，他们可能会制作一段视频，并通过要求大家在观看后参加 multiple choice quiz 来 enforce compliance。我在其他 ICs 身上观察到的 net effect 是，人们谈论 management 与 ICs 的日常生活有多么脱节。但出于与 normalization of deviance 发生时相同的 reasons，这些 information 似乎根本无法传到 upper management。

这最终竟成了一个关于 incentives 的 problem，有点好笑。作为一个 industry，我们花大量时间思考如何 incentivize consumers 去做我们想让他们做的事。但随后，我们却建立起一些 incentive systems，大家普遍认为它们在 incentivizing 我们做错误的事，而我们之所以这样做，是传话游戏与 cargo cult diffusion 共同作用的结果。Microsoft 如日中天时，我们 copied 他们的 interview process，问 brain-teaser interview questions。如今 Google 如日中天，我们又 copy 他们的 interview process，问 algorithms questions。如果你看看那些比 Google 更年轻的时髦 companies，就会发现它们中的大多数基本上都在 copy 他们的 ranking/leveling system，只做了一些细小的 tweaks。好消息是，与以前大家 copied 的许多 companies 不同，Google 对自己的大多数 processes 都进行了大量思考，并做出了 data driven decisions。坏消息是，Google 在许多方面都是 unique 的，这意味着他们的 reasoning 往往并不能 generalize，而且[人们常常在 practices 已经在 Google 被 deprecated 很久之后，仍然对它们进行 cargo cult](https://danluu.com/why-ecc/)。

这种 diffusion 也会发生在 technical decisions 上。Stripe [built a reliable message queue on top of Mongo](https://www.hakkalabs.co/articles/trouble-with-event-processing-try-the-method-that-stripe-has-mastered-with-mongodb)，于是我们也 build [reliable](https://aphyr.com/posts/284-jepsen-mongodb) message [queues](http://stackoverflow.com/questions/9274777/mongodb-as-a-queue-service) on top of [Mongo](https://aphyr.com/posts/322-jepsen-mongodb-stale-reads)[1](https://danluu.com/wat/#fn:J)。这一路往下，全都是 cargo cults[2](https://danluu.com/wat/#fn:P)。

这篇 paper 有专门的 sub-sections，讨论如何 prevent normalization of deviance，我建议完整阅读。

-   要 Pay attention to weak signals
-   要 Resist 那种想要 unreasonably optimistic 的冲动
-   要 Teach employees 如何 conduct emotionally uncomfortable conversations
-   System operators 需要在 speaking up 时感到 safe
-   要 Realize，oversight 和 monitoring 是 never-ending 的

让我们看看其中第一条“pay attention to weak signals”如何与一个具体例子 interacts：新人刚加入 company 时发出的“WTF WTF WTF”。

如果一位 VP 认定某件事情搞砸了，人们通常会听。这是一个 strong signal。而当人们不听时，VP 知道该动用哪些手段让事情发生。但新人刚进来时，几乎必然不知道自己能动用哪些手段让事情发生，也不知道该找谁谈。他们发出的是很容易被 ignored 的 weak signals。等到他们对 system 的了解足以发出 strong signals 时，他们已经适应了。

“Pay attention to weak signals”听起来确实是个好建议，但我们该如何做到呢？Strong signals 少之又少，因此很容易得到关注。Weak signals 却非常多。我们如何 filter out 那些不重要的 signals？又如何让整个 team 或 org 真正这样做？这类 questions 无法用 generic 的方式 answered；这需要认真思考。我们大多把这些心思花在了别处。Startups 花大量时间思考 growth，尽管它们都会告诉你，自己非常重视 engineering culture，但 revealed preference 表明并非如此。除了少数例外，big companies 也没什么不同。在 LCB，我看过 competitive analysis slide decks，真是令人惊叹。他们会审视数百个 products 的每一个细节，确保从 onboarding 到与 competing products 的 interop，一切都尽可能让 users 满意。只要有任何一个 screen 比任何 competitor 的更 complex 或更 confusing，人们就会不高兴，并设法 fix 它。这相当令人佩服。然而，当 LCB 为我所在 org 的 employees 进行 onboards 时，其中三分之一的人至少缺少 alias/account、办公室或 computer 中的一样，而且这种情况可能持续数周甚至数月。Competitive analysis slide decks 讲述 onboarding 有多重要，因为你只有一次留下第一印象的机会；但 employees 在 onboarded 时得到的印象，却是 company 根本不在乎他们，而且 quotidian processes 普遍 broken 是 normal 的。LCB 连 employee onboarding 的基本环节都做不好，更不用说 acculturation 这种真正 complex 的事情。这是可以理解的——user growth 或 attrition 之类的 external metrics 是 measurable 的，而如何判断你是否正在对人们进行 acculturating，使他们不会 ignore weak signals，这类 targets 则更软性、更难 determine，但这并不意味着它们不那么重要。人们写了大量文章，讨论使用更花哨的 languages，或 TDD、agile 等 techniques，如何让你的 teams 更 productive，但拥有 strong engineering culture 才是大得多的 force multiplier。

感谢 Sophie Smithburg 和 Marc Brooker 向我介绍 Normalization of Deviance 这个 term，也感谢 Kelly Eskridge、Leah Hanson、Sophie Rapoport、Sophie Smithburg、Julia Evans、Dmitri Kalintsev、Ralph Corderoy、Jamie Brandon、Egor Neliuba 和 Victor Felder 提供 comments/corrections/discussion。

## 原文

Have you ever mentioned something that seems totally normal to you only to be greeted by surprise? Happens to me all the time when I describe something everyone at work thinks is normal. For some reason, my conversation partner's face morphs from pleasant smile to rictus of horror. Here are a few representative examples.

There's the company that is perhaps the nicest place I've ever worked, combining the best parts of Valve and Netflix. The people are amazing and you're given near total freedom to do whatever you want. But as a side effect of the culture, they lose perhaps half of new hires in the first year, some voluntarily and some involuntarily. Totally normal, right? Here are a few more anecdotes that were considered totally normal by people in places I've worked. And often not just normal, but laudable.

There's the company that's incredibly secretive about infrastructure. For example, there's the team that was afraid that, if they reported bugs to their hardware vendor, the bugs would get fixed and their competitors would be able to use the fixes. Solution: request the firmware and fix bugs themselves! More recently, I know a group of folks outside the company who tried to reproduce the algorithm in the paper the company published earlier this year. The group found that they couldn't reproduce the result, and that the algorithm in the paper resulted in an unusual level of instability; when asked about this, one of the authors responded “well, we have some tweaks that didn't make it into the paper” and declined to share the tweaks, i.e., the company purposely published an unreproducible result to avoid giving away the details, as is normal. This company enforces secrecy by having a strict policy of firing leakers. This is introduced at orientation with examples of people who got fired for leaking (e.g., the guy who leaked that a concert was going to happen inside a particular office), and by announcing firings for leaks at the company all hands. The result of those policies is that I know multiple people who are afraid to forward emails about things like updated info on health insurance to a spouse for fear of forwarding the wrong email and getting fired; instead, they use another computer to retype the email and pass it along, or take photos of the email on their phone.

There's the office where I asked one day about the fact that I almost never saw two particular people in the same room together. I was told that they had a feud going back a decade, and that things had actually improved — for years, they literally couldn't be in the same room because one of the two would get too angry and do something regrettable, but things had now cooled to the point where the two could, occasionally, be found in the same wing of the office or even the same room. These weren't just random people, either. They were the two managers of the only two teams in the office.

There's the company whose culture is so odd that, when I sat down to write a post about it, I found that I'd [not only written more than for any other single post, but more than all other posts combined](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:N) (which is well over 100k words now, the length of a moderate book). This is the same company where someone recently explained to me how great it is that, instead of using data to make decisions, we use political connections, and that the idea of making decisions based on data is a myth anyway; no one does that. This is also the company where all four of the things they told me to get me to join were false, and the job ended up being the one thing I specifically said I didn't want to do. When I joined this company, my team didn't use version control for months and it was a real fight to get everyone to use version control. Although I won that fight, I lost the fight to get people to run a build, let alone run tests, before checking in, so the build is broken multiple times per day. When I mentioned that I thought this was a problem for our productivity, I was told that it's fine because it affects everyone equally. Since the only thing that mattered was my stack ranked productivity, so I shouldn't care that it impacts the entire team, the fact that it's normal for everyone means that there's no cause for concern.

There's the company that created multiple massive initiatives to recruit more women into engineering roles, [where women still get rejected in recruiter screens for not being technical enough](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/) after being asked questions like "was your experience with algorithms or just coding?". I thought that my referral with a very strong recommendation would have prevented that, but it did not.

There's the company where I worked on a four person effort with a multi-hundred million dollar budget and a billion dollar a year impact, where requests for things that cost hundreds of dollars routinely took months or were denied.

You might wonder if I've just worked at places that are unusually screwed up. Sure, the companies are generally considered to be ok places to work and two of them are considered to be among the best places to work, but maybe I've just ended up at places that are overrated. But I have the same experience when I hear stories about how other companies work, even places with stellar engineering reputations, except that it's me that's shocked and my conversation partner who thinks their story is normal.

There's the companies that use [@flaky](https://github.com/box/flaky), which includes the vast majority of Python-using SF Bay area unicorns. If you don't know what this is, this is a library that lets you add a Python annotation to those annoying flaky tests that sometimes pass and sometimes fail. When I asked multiple co-workers and former co-workers from three different companies what they thought this did, they all guessed that it re-runs the test multiple times and reports a failure if any of the runs fail. Close, but not quite. It's technically possible to use @flaky for that, but in practice it's used to re-run the test multiple times and reports a pass if any of the runs pass. The company that created @flaky is effectively a storage infrastructure company, and the library is widely used at its biggest competitor.

There's the company with a reputation for having great engineering practices that had 2 9s of reliability last time I checked, for reasons that are entirely predictable from their engineering practices. This is the second thing in a row that can't be deanonymized because multiple companies fit the description. Here, I'm not talking about companies trying to be the next reddit or twitter where it's, apparently, totally fine to have 1 9. I'm talking about companies that sell platforms that other companies rely on, where an outage will cause dependent companies to pause operations for the duration of the outage. Multiple companies that build infrastructure find practices that lead to 2 9s of reliability.

As far as I can tell, what happens at a lot these companies is that they started by concentrating almost totally on product growth. That's completely and totally reasonable, because companies are worth approximately zero when they're founded; they don't bother with things that protect them from losses, like good ops practices or actually having security, because there's nothing to lose (well, except for user data when the inevitable security breach happens, and if you talk to security folks at unicorns you'll know that these happen).

The result is a culture where people are hyper-focused on growth and ignore risk. That culture tends to stick even after company has grown to be worth well over a billion dollars, and the companies have something to lose. Anyone who comes into one of these companies from Google, Amazon, or another place with solid ops practices is shocked. Often, they try to fix things, and then leave when they can't make a dent.

Google probably has the best ops and security practices of any tech company today. It's easy to say that you should take these things as seriously as Google does, but it's instructive to see how they got there. If you look at the codebase, you'll see that various services have names ending in z, as do a curiously large number of variables. I'm told that's because, once upon a time, someone wanted to add monitoring. It wouldn't really be secure to have `google.com/somename` expose monitoring data, so they added a z. `google.com/somenamez`. For security. At the company that is now the best in the world at security. They're now so good at security that multiple people I've talked to (all of whom joined after this happened) vehemently deny that this ever happened, even though the reasons they give don't really make sense (e.g., to avoid name collisions) and I have this from sources who were there at the time this happened.

Google didn't go from adding z to the end of names to having the world's best security because someone gave a rousing speech or wrote a convincing essay. They did it after getting embarrassed a few times, which gave people who wanted to do things “right” the leverage to fix fundamental process issues. It's the same story at almost every company I know of that has good practices. Microsoft was a joke in the security world for years, until multiple disastrously bad exploits forced them to get serious about security. This makes it sound simple, but if you talk to people who were there at the time, the change was brutal. Despite a mandate from the top, there was vicious political pushback from people whose position was that the company got to where it was in 2003 without wasting time on practices like security. Why change what's worked?

You can see this kind of thing in every industry. A classic example that tech folks often bring up is hand-washing by doctors and nurses. It's well known that germs exist, and that washing hands properly very strongly reduces the odds of transmitting germs and thereby significantly reduces hospital mortality rates. Despite that, trained doctors and nurses still often don't do it. Interventions are required. Signs reminding people to wash their hands save lives. But when people stand at hand-washing stations to require others walking by to wash their hands, even more lives are saved. People can ignore signs, but they can't ignore being forced to wash their hands.

This mirrors a number of attempts at tech companies to introduce better practices. If you tell people they should do it, that helps a bit. If you enforce better practices via code review, that helps a lot.

The data are clear that humans are really bad at taking the time to do things that are well understood to incontrovertibly reduce the risk of rare but catastrophic events. We will rationalize that taking shortcuts is the right, reasonable thing to do. There's a term for this: the normalization of deviance. It's well studied in a number of other contexts including healthcare, aviation, mechanical engineering, aerospace engineering, and civil engineering, but we don't see it discussed in the context of software. In fact, I've never seen the term used in the context of software.

Is it possible to learn from other's mistakes instead of making every mistake ourselves? The state of the industry make this sound unlikely, but let's give it a shot. [John Banja has a nice summary paper on the normalization of deviance in healthcare](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2821100/), with lessons we can attempt to apply to software development. One thing to note is that, because Banja is concerned with patient outcomes, there's a close analogy to devops failure modes, but normalization of deviance also occurs in cultural contexts that are less directly analogous.

The first section of the paper details a number of disasters, both in healthcare and elsewhere. Here's one typical example:

> A catastrophic negligence case that the author participated in as an expert witness involved an anesthesiologist's turning off a ventilator at the request of a surgeon who wanted to take an x-ray of the patient's abdomen (Banja, 2005, pp. 87-101). The ventilator was to be off for only a few seconds, but the anesthesiologist forgot to turn it back on, or thought he turned it back on but had not. The patient was without oxygen for a long enough time to cause her to experience global anoxia, which plunged her into a vegetative state. She never recovered, was disconnected from artificial ventilation 9 days later, and then died 2 days after that. It was later discovered that the anesthesia alarms and monitoring equipment in the operating room had been deliberately programmed to a “suspend indefinite” mode such that the anesthesiologist was not alerted to the ventilator problem. Tragically, the very instrumentality that was in place to prevent such a horror was disabled, possibly because the operating room staff found the constant beeping irritating and annoying.

Turning off or ignoring notifications because there are too many of them and they're too annoying? An erroneous manual operation? This could be straight out of the post-mortem of more than a few companies I can think of, except that the result was a tragic death instead of the loss of millions of dollars. If you [read a lot of tech post-mortems](https://danluu.com/postmortem-lessons/), every example in Banja's paper will feel familiar even though the details are different.

The section concludes,

> What these disasters typically reveal is that the factors accounting for them usually had “[long incubation periods, typified by rule violations, discrepant events that accumulated unnoticed, and cultural beliefs about hazards that together prevented interventions that might have staved off harmful outcomes](http://www.jstor.org/stable/223506?seq=1#page_scan_tab_contents)”. Furthermore, it is especially striking how multiple rule violations and lapses can coalesce so as to enable a disaster's occurrence.

Once again, this could be from an article about technical failures. That makes the next section, on why these failures happen, seem worth checking out. The reasons given are:

#### The rules are stupid and inefficient

The example in the paper is about delivering medication to newborns. To prevent “drug diversion,” nurses were required to enter their password onto the computer to access the medication drawer, get the medication, and administer the correct amount. In order to ensure that the first nurse wasn't stealing drugs, if any drug remained, another nurse was supposed to observe the process, and then enter their password onto the computer to indicate they witnessed the drug being properly disposed of.

That sounds familiar. How many technical postmortems start off with “someone skipped some steps because they're inefficient”, e.g., “the programmer force pushed a bad config or bad code because they were sure nothing could go wrong and skipped staging/testing”? The infamous [November 2014 Azure outage](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/final-root-cause-analysis-and-improvement-areas-nov-18-azure-storage-service-interruption/) happened for just that reason. At around the same time, a dev at one of Azure's competitors overrode the rule that you shouldn't push a config that fails tests because they knew that the config couldn't possibly be bad. When that caused the canary deploy to start failing, they overrode the rule that you can't deploy from canary into staging with a failure because they knew their config couldn't possibly be bad and so the failure must be from something else. That postmortem revealed that the config was technically correct, but exposed a bug in the underlying software; it was pure luck that the latent bug the config revealed wasn't as severe as the Azure bug.

Humans are bad at reasoning about how failures cascade, so we implement bright line rules about when it's safe to deploy. But the same thing that makes it hard for us to reason about when it's safe to deploy makes the rules seem stupid and inefficient.

#### Knowledge is imperfect and uneven

People don't automatically know what should be normal, and when new people are onboarded, they can just as easily learn deviant processes that have become normalized as reasonable processes.

[Julia Evans](http://jvns.ca/) described to me how this happens:

_new person joins_  
**new person**: WTF WTF WTF WTF WTF  
**old hands**: yeah we know we're concerned about it  
**new person**: WTF WTF wTF wtf wtf w...  
_new person gets used to it_  
_new person #2 joins_  
**new person #2**: WTF WTF WTF WTF  
**new person**: yeah we know. we're concerned about it.  

The thing that's really insidious here is that people will really buy into the WTF idea, and they can spread it elsewhere for the duration of their career. Once, after doing some work [on an open source project that's regularly broken and being told that it's normal to have a broken build, and that they were doing better than average](https://danluu.com/julialang/), I ran the numbers, found that project was basically worst in class, and wrote something about the idea that [it's possible to have a build that nearly always passes with relatively low effort](https://danluu.com/broken-builds/). The most common comment I got in response was, "Wow that guy must work with superstar programmers. But let's get real. We all break the build at least a few times a week", as if running tests (or for that matter, even attempting to compile) before checking code in requires superhuman abilities. But once people get convinced that some deviation is normal, they often get really invested in the idea.

#### I'm breaking the rule for the good of my patient

The example in the paper is of someone who breaks the rule that you should wear gloves when finding a vein. Their reasoning is that wearing gloves makes it harder to find a vein, which may result in their having to stick a baby with a needle multiple times. It's hard to argue against that. No one wants to cause a baby extra pain!

The second worst outage I can think of occurred when someone noticed that a database service was experiencing slowness. They pushed a fix to the service, and in order to prevent the service degradation from spreading, they ignored the rule that you should do a proper, slow, staged deploy. Instead, they pushed the fix to all machines. It's hard to argue against that. No one wants their customers to have degraded service! Unfortunately, the fix exposed a bug that caused a global outage.

#### The rules don't apply to me/You can trust me

> most human beings perceive themselves as good and decent people, such that they can understand many of their rule violations as entirely rational and ethically acceptable responses to problematic situations. They understand themselves to be doing nothing wrong, and will be outraged and often fiercely defend themselves when confronted with evidence to the contrary.

As companies grow up, they eventually have to impose security that prevents every employee from being able to access basically everything. And at most companies, when that happens, some people get really upset. “Don't you trust me? If you trust me, how come you're revoking my access to X, Y, and Z?”

Facebook famously [let all employees access everyone's profile](http://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424052702304898704577478483529665936) for a long time, and you can even find HN comments indicating that some recruiters would explicitly mention that as a perk of working for Facebook. And I can think of more than one well-regarded unicorn where everyone still has access to basically everything, even after their first or second bad security breach. It's hard to get the political capital to restrict people's access to what they believe they need, or are entitled, to know. A lot of trendy startups have core values like “trust” and “transparency” which make it difficult to argue against universal access.

#### Workers are afraid to speak up

There are people I simply don't give feedback to because I can't tell if they'd take it well or not, and once you say something, it's impossible to un-say it. In the paper, the author gives an example of a doctor with poor handwriting who gets mean when people ask him to clarify what he's written. As a result, people guess instead of asking.

In most company cultures, people feel weird about giving feedback. Everyone has stories about a project that lingered on for months or years after it should have been terminated because no one was willing to offer explicit feedback. This is a problem even when cultures discourage meanness and encourage feedback: cultures of niceness seem to have as many issues around speaking up as cultures of meanness, if not more. In some places, people are afraid to speak up because they'll get attacked by someone mean. In others, they're afraid because they'll be branded as mean. It's a hard problem.

#### Leadership withholding or diluting findings on problems

In the paper, this is characterized by flaws and weaknesses being diluted as information flows up the chain of command. One example is how a supervisor might take sub-optimal actions to avoid looking bad to superiors.

I was shocked the first time I saw this happen. I must have been half a year or a year out of school. I saw that we were doing something obviously non-optimal, and brought it up with the senior person in the group. He told me that he didn't disagree, but that if we did it my way and there was a failure, it would be really embarrassing. He acknowledged that my way reduced the chance of failure without making the technical consequences of failure worse, but it was more important that we not be embarrassed. Now that I've been working for a decade, I have a better understanding of how and why people play this game, but I still find it absurd.

#### Solutions

Let's say you notice that your company has a problem that I've heard people at most companies complain about: people get promoted for heroism and putting out fires, not for preventing fires; and people get promoted for shipping features, not for doing critical maintenance work and bug fixing. How do you change that?

The simplest option is to just do the right thing yourself and ignore what's going on around you. That has some positive impact, but the scope of your impact is necessarily limited. Next, you can convince your team to do the right thing: I've done that a few times for practices I feel are really important and are sticky, so that I won't have to continue to expend effort on convincing people once things get moving.

But if the incentives are aligned against you, it will require an ongoing and probably unsustainable effort to keep people doing the right thing. In that case, the problem becomes convincing someone to change the incentives, and then making sure the change works as designed. How to convince people is worth discussing, but long and messy enough that it's beyond the scope of this post. As for making the change work, I've seen many “obvious” mistakes repeated, both in places I've worked and those whose internal politics I know a lot about.

Small companies have it easy. When I worked at a 100 person company, the hierarchy was individual contributor (IC) -> team lead (TL) -> CEO. That was it. The CEO had a very light touch, but if he wanted something to happen, it happened. Critically, he had a good idea of what everyone was up to and could basically adjust rewards in real-time. If you did something great for the company, there's a good chance you'd get a raise. Not in nine months when the next performance review cycle came up, but basically immediately. Not all small companies do that effectively, but with the right leadership, they can. That's impossible for large companies.

At large company A (LCA), they had the problem we're discussing and a mandate came down to reward people better for doing critical but low-visibility grunt work. There were too many employees for the mandator to directly make all decisions about compensation and promotion, but the mandator could review survey data, spot check decisions, and provide feedback until things were normalized. My subjective perception is that the company never managed to achieve parity between boring maintenance work and shiny new projects, but got close enough that people who wanted to make sure things worked correctly didn't have to significantly damage their careers to do it.

At large company B (LCB), ICs agreed that it's problematic to reward creating new features more richly than doing critical grunt work. When I talked to managers, they often agreed, too. But nevertheless, the people who get promoted are disproportionately those who ship shiny new things. I saw management attempt a number of cultural and process changes at LCB. Mostly, those took the form of pronouncements from people with fancy titles. For really important things, they might produce a video, and enforce compliance by making people take a multiple choice quiz after watching the video. The net effect I observed among other ICs was that people talked about how disconnected management was from the day-to-day life of ICs. But, for the same reasons that normalization of deviance occurs, that information seems to have no way to reach upper management.

It's sort of funny that this ends up being a problem about incentives. As an industry, we spend a lot of time thinking about how to incentivize consumers into doing what we want. But then we set up incentive systems that are generally agreed upon as incentivizing us to do the wrong things, and we do so via a combination of a game of telephone and cargo cult diffusion. Back when Microsoft was ascendant, we copied their interview process and asked brain-teaser interview questions. Now that Google is ascendant, we copy their interview process and ask algorithms questions. If you look around at trendy companies that are younger than Google, most of them basically copy their ranking/leveling system, with some minor tweaks. The good news is that, unlike many companies people previously copied, Google has put a lot of thought into most of their processes and made data driven decisions. The bad news is that Google is unique in a number of ways, which means that their reasoning often doesn't generalize, and that [people often cargo cult practices long after they've become deprecated at Google](https://danluu.com/why-ecc/).

This kind of diffusion happens for technical decisions, too. Stripe [built a reliable message queue on top of Mongo](https://www.hakkalabs.co/articles/trouble-with-event-processing-try-the-method-that-stripe-has-mastered-with-mongodb), so we build [reliable](https://aphyr.com/posts/284-jepsen-mongodb) message [queues](http://stackoverflow.com/questions/9274777/mongodb-as-a-queue-service) on top of [Mongo](https://aphyr.com/posts/322-jepsen-mongodb-stale-reads)[1](https://danluu.com/wat/#fn:J). It's cargo cults all the way down[2](https://danluu.com/wat/#fn:P).

The paper has specific sub-sections on how to prevent normalization of deviance, which I recommend reading in full.

-   Pay attention to weak signals
-   Resist the urge to be unreasonably optimistic
-   Teach employees how to conduct emotionally uncomfortable conversations
-   System operators need to feel safe in speaking up
-   Realize that oversight and monitoring are never-ending

Let's look at how the first one of these, “pay attention to weak signals”, interacts with a single example, the “WTF WTF WTF” a new person gives off when the join the company.

If a VP decides something is screwed up, people usually listen. It's a strong signal. And when people don't listen, the VP knows what levers to pull to make things happen. But when someone new comes in, they don't know what levers they can pull to make things happen or who they should talk to almost by definition. They give out weak signals that are easily ignored. By the time they learn enough about the system to give out strong signals, they've acclimated.

“Pay attention to weak signals” sure sounds like good advice, but how do we do it? Strong signals are few and far between, making them easy to pay attention to. Weak signals are abundant. How do we filter out the ones that aren't important? And how do we get an entire team or org to actually do it? These kinds of questions can't be answered in a generic way; this takes real thought. We mostly put this thought elsewhere. Startups spend a lot of time thinking about growth, and while they'll all tell you that they care a lot about engineering culture, revealed preference shows that they don't. With a few exceptions, big companies aren't much different. At LCB, I looked through the competitive analysis slide decks and they're amazing. They look at every last detail on hundreds of products to make sure that everything is as nice for users as possible, from onboarding to interop with competing products. If there's any single screen where things are more complex or confusing than any competitor's, people get upset and try to fix it. It's quite impressive. And then when LCB onboards employees in my org, a third of them are missing at least one of, an alias/account, an office, or a computer, a condition which can persist for weeks or months. The competitive analysis slide decks talk about how important onboarding is because you only get one chance to make a first impression, and then employees are onboarded with the impression that the company couldn't care less about them and that it's normal for quotidian processes to be pervasively broken. LCB can't even to get the basics of employee onboarding right, let alone really complex things like acculturation. This is understandable — external metrics like user growth or attrition are measurable, and targets like how to tell if you're acculturating people so that they don't ignore weak signals are softer and harder to determine, but that doesn't mean they're any less important. People write a lot about how things like using fancier languages or techniques like TDD or agile will make your teams more productive, but having a strong engineering culture is much larger force multiplier.
