# The Longevity FAQ: Making of

## 正文

我刚刚发布了 [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity/)。这是我第一次在博客中涉足 molecular biology。说来，我从高中之后就没有真正学过 biology，所以下面就讲讲，我是如何——以及为什么——从零开始，为一个 complex topic 撰写 FAQ 的。

首先，为什么要写这个话题？最初的一个动机是，我曾多次说过，我认为未来更多的 progress 会来自 life sciences，而不是 physics，但我在 biology 方面的知识非常、非常匮乏，从未认真了解过它。另一方面，ageing 多年来一直是我心里隐约关注的一个话题；早在 2009 年左右，我就读过 Aubrey de Grey 的书 _Ending Aging_。最近，因为想进一步了解它，我注意到，当时缺少一种像后来这份 FAQ 那样的东西：从 first principles 出发（至少就 cell 的 functioning 而言），解释究竟发生了什么，解释 core concepts 及其 relation，指出 contradictory evidence、观点随时间发生的各种变化，以及 nuanced results，还有 therapies。

于是，为了开始获取这些背景知识，我开始阅读 Molecular Biology 领域_那本公认的_教科书，Molecular Biology of the Cell。虽然我更喜欢 papers，认为它们比书籍更能高效地传播知识，但教科书尤其适合用来进入一个新领域。教科书会采用从最基础内容开始的 structured approach，而且还会告诉你一些你起初根本不知道自己想了解的事情。

但因为缺乏 biology 最基础的知识，我还不得不借助 Khan Academy 的 biology 课程，以及网上各种各样的资源，来补充教科书。当然，我没有学完整本教科书，只读了我认为相关的章节。这让我具备了一些知识，能够理解接下来要看的话题，但同时，我在非常基础的知识上仍然存在缺口（例如，我并不知道一个作为 [adaptive immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V\(D\) 基础的 key process）J_recombination))

David Sinclair 的书是促使我撰写 FAQ 的直接契机。我读完这本书后，对其中一些 conclusions 产生了疑虑：他似乎非常轻视 ROS 和 DNA damage 的 role，转而支持他自己的 theory，把 sirtuins 和 epigenetics 放在核心位置。在一定程度上，我的担忧是有根据的：他确实淡化了前两者的 role。这里要说明，我的目的并不是对这本书进行 fact-check；总体而言，我并不怀疑它的 main argument。书中已经提到了 aging 叙事里的许多关键角色：telomeres、mitochondria、reprogramming、sirtuins、senescence，等等，而且链接了许多令人惊讶的 results，以及像 _Hallmarks_ paper 这样的 foundational work，所以起初我用它来指导我的资料搜集。

通常，我写某个话题时，只会通过 literature reviews 或 meta-analysis 来 search 我需要的信息，查看近期的 work，然后直接在博文里写。

FAQ 的第一个版本就是这样开始的，但写到一半时，我决定尝试另一种方式。我知道，过去惯常的写作方式会让我遗漏一些 studies，而且通常也不利于文章的风格和 argument 的行文脉络；因为在写到相关章节之前，我并不知道自己将要写些什么。这次，我改为删除原文档的大部分内容，并决定用一个 Notion spreadsheet（尽管 Notioneers 可能不赞成这样描述 Notion）来 store 我正在读的 papers，用它们涵盖的话题和发表年份给它们 tagging。这样，我就能记下每篇 paper 的 core results，轻松地按领域对它们 filter，并按年份 sort，从而看出 literature 随时间如何演变；一般来说，all else equal，较新的 work 应当更 correct，因为多出来的时间可能让更多 arguments 得到考虑，让更多 evidence 得以出现。

在这个过程中，我开发的一个 tool 帮了忙，它拥有一个收录了大多数 scientific papers 的 database（超过 150 million 篇）。虽然我还没有公开所有 API routes 和 features，但我已经可以执行这样的 queries：“Find 所有 cite X 和 Y 的 papers”。如果 papers X 和 Y 彼此 contradict，而你想找到一篇 synthesises 两者的 paper，或者解释 disagreement 究竟在哪里的 paper，这就很有用。

于是，在某个时候，我坐下来写出了文章的结构；首先，我列出了一些 basic concepts，并链接了相关的 caveats 和 studies，这些正是我刚开始时希望能有的东西，然后我解释了 core concepts；之所以选这些 concepts，是因为它们在我读的 papers 里反复出现。

接下来，我安排了一个 therapies 章节；这里主要启发我的是 Laura Deming 的 FAQ，以及那些专门单独考察 anti-aging therapies 的 papers；我决定取这些 therapies 的 sets 的 union，舍弃其中一些似乎不太相关的。

在寻找 studies 时，如果发现某种东西似乎好得令人惊讶，比如 senolytics 或 rapamycin，我就会尝试 search “rapamycin ineffective”或“senolytics negative effects”这样的 terms；后者让我找到了一些有趣的 speculation，探讨为什么可能会这样。总体而言，我采用了标准的 Nintil method：

1.  查看这个话题的 literature reviews
2.  针对你不理解的话题展开了解并进行 search
3.  对于你怀疑的 results，或者不同 reviews 彼此 contradict 的地方，寻找针对那些具体要点的 literature reviews。
4.  沿着 citation graph 继续查找，直到最新的 evidence 都已被 reviewed
5.  在这个过程中，记下 disagreement 有多大、papers 通常出现在哪些 journals、哪些 papers 是大家都 cites 的，等等。

对于单篇 paper，存在一个该读什么的问题。最开始，面对那些非常 general 的 literature reviews，答案是全部都读。对于范围更 narrow 的 literature reviews，我承认自己会跳过一些章节，把注意力放在 figures、introduction 和 conclusion 上；这些 reviews 有时会过于深入某个特定话题，对我的帮助就没有那么大了（这些内容不会写进 FAQ，而且反正我也会忘掉细节）。有时我会有具体的问题；FAQ 的一个章节简短提到了 adults 和 young individuals 中 senescent cells 的 %。这是我写 FAQ 时一直好奇的事情，因为人们常常 assertion 说，“只需少量 senescent cells，就能产生 large effects”，但这里的少量究竟是多少？于是我为此查找 papers，在这种情况下，我只取了那个 number，并读了它所在的段落。

尝试 reconcile literature 中的 disagreements 时，会遇到一个该相信谁的问题。有时，提出不同意见的 paper 发表得较晚，而再往后，又能找到两位作者共同撰写的 joint paper，在其中对 results 给出 interpretation（monkey studies 就是这种情况）。另一种情况是，后来的 paper 说，较早的 paper 因为 methods 不佳而存在缺陷，或者较早的 paper 声称得到的 results 并不 generalizable。我引用的关于 sirtuins 在 flies 中的 effect 的 Whitaker (2013) paper 就是这种情况。如果后来的 paper references 了较早的 work，并解释了 disagreement，那么 prima facie，我愿意把它当作事实接受。

但还有一种问题：你会发现似乎存在不同的 researchers 群体（free radicals 的 researchers 可能就是这样），他们往往在不同的 journals 上 publish，并把不同的东西视为正确，尽管这看起来很奇怪。当然，这里的问题可能是一方没有读过另一方的 evidence，或者某一方的 arguments 就是不够好。ROS 章节就是这种情况。在这里，我能做到的最好办法，就是把这一节写得更长，记下不同时期人们在 reviews 中声称得到的 results；但我无法对这一节抱有很强的信心；要做到这一点，我就必须更深入地钻研，更好地理解不同的 assays 和各项 evidence。所以，如果我要对 ROS 的 role 做更深入的 review，我会先记下具体的 claims（例如，在任何 animal model 中，antioxidant intake 都不会 lengthen lifespan，superoxide dismutase knockout 会 reduces lifespan），记下所提供的 evidence，再逐一检查每个 fact 的 discussion。然后，在掌握各条信息之后，我就能看看它们如何 logically fit together，并判断谁是对的。

发表之后，因为知道这个话题对我来说是新的，文章必然会有许多错误，我承诺每天都根据当天从读者反馈中获得的 corrections 来 update FAQ。发表后的第 2 天，我还决定把 FAQ 发给几位有声望的 ageing researchers，看看他们是否有 corrections 要补充（截至撰写本文时，他们还没有回复）。

最初的反响似乎不错，reddit 上的一位 biogerontologist 说，它看起来[相当不错，满分 10 分可得 (8-9) 分](https://www.reddit.com/r/longevity/comments/er3x6b/nintil_the_longevity_faq_a_summary_of_the_current/)；同样，我也在 twitter 上收到了一些非常[好](https://twitter.com/AdamMarblestone/status/1219398228996763648)的[回应](https://twitter.com/anderssandberg/status/1219654582814507009)。文章中的 changelog 表明，原文里有不少错误，我已经作了纠正。事后复盘时发现，大多数严重错误都出现在 FAQ 最初版本就有的章节中，那些内容是在我全面阅读 literature 之前就开始写的。显而易见的改进办法是，等所有内容都 reviewed 之后再开始写。第二类错误源于缺乏 biology 的基础知识。这是意料之中的。

归根结底，我本可以选择更谨慎一些，在决定为一个开始之前几乎一无所知的 complex topic 撰写 FAQ 之前，花更多时间阅读基础内容。但我反而认为，不妨先试一试，再利用随后收到的反馈来指导自己了解遗漏了什么，这样更高效。结果，我发现自己在 immune system、cancer 的 mechanics、atherosclerosis 和 inflammation 方面的知识存在薄弱之处；这些内容可能会写进 FAQ 的更新版本。

我对最终结果相当满意；我原先预计，这会是一份质量还不错、但包含一些错误的 FAQ，而且会随着时间逐渐完善，事实也确实如此。

## 原文

So I just published the [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity/). This represents my first blogging foray into molecular biology. So it happens, I hadn't really studied biology since high school, so here's how -and why- I went from zero to writing a FAQ on a complex topic.

Why write about it, in the first place? An original motivation is that I've said a bunch of times that I see more progress in the future coming from the life sciences rather than physics, yet my knowledge in biology was very, very lacking, not having ever seriously looked into it. On the other hand ageing had been a topic in the background for me for years; back in 2009 or so I had read Aubrey de Grey's book _Ending Aging_. More recently, and wanting to learn more about it, I noticed that there was a lack of something like what eventually became the FAQ, something that explains, from first principles (At least as far as the functioning of the cell is concerned) what is going on, explains core concepts and their relation, points to contradictory evidence, the different changes in views over time, and nuanced results, plus therapies.

So to start getting that background knowledge started reading _the_ textbook on Molecular Biology, the Molecular Biology of the Cell. As much as I'm more of a fan of papers as being more efficient media for the transmission of knowledge than books; textbooks in particular are the best way to get into a new field. A textbook has a structured approach starting from the very basics, and it will also tell you things you didn't know you wanted to know in the first place.

But lacking super-basic knowledge of biology, I had to resort too to the Khan Academy biology courses plus assorted resources online to complement the textbook. I didn't work through the whole textbook, of course, only sections that I thought were relevant. This left me with some knowledge to understand topics I would be looking at, but at the same time I ended up with gaps in very basic knowledge (e.g. I wasn't aware of a key process that underlies the [adaptive immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V\(D\)J_recombination))

David Sinclair's book was the immediate trigger for me writing the FAQ, I had read the book and ended up wary of some conclusions, he seemed very dismissive of the role of ROS and DNA damage, in favor of his own theory that puts sirtuins at epigenetics at the core, To some extent my concerns were valid: He was indeed downplaying the role of the former. My intent, to be clear here, was not to fact-check the book; overall I was not skeptical of the main argument. The book already mentioned many of the key protagonists in aging story: telomeres, mitochondria, reprogramming, sirtuins, senescence, and more, and it linked to many surprising results plus foundational work like the _Hallmarks_ paper, so I initially used that to inform my information gathering.

Usually when I write about topics, I just search for the information I need in the form of literature reviews or meta-analysis, looking at recent work and writing in the blogpost directly.

The first version of the FAQ started like this, but halfway through it I decided to try a different approach. I knew that my usual way of going about writing has led in the past to missing some studies, and in general favouring a subpar style and flow of the argument; as I don't know what I will be writing about until I've gotten to the relevant section. Instead, what I did here was to delete most of the original document, and decided to use a Notion spreadsheet (Though the Notioneers may object to Notion being described in such way) to store the papers I was reading, tagging them with the topics they covered and the year of publication. That way I could take notes with the core results from each paper, and be able to easily filter them by area and sort them by year, enabling me to see how the literature evolved over time; generally all else equal more recent work should be more correct, as the extra time may have allowed for more arguments to be considered and more evidence to come through.

I was aided in this quest by a tool I developed, which has a database of most scientific papers(Over 150 million), While I haven't made public all the API routes and features yet, I was able to do queries like "Find all papers that cite X and Y" which is useful if papers X and Y contradict each other and one wants to find a paper that synthesises them both, or otherwise explains what the disagreement is about.

So at some point I sat down and wrote a structure for the article; first I laid down some basic concepts, with the relevant caveats and studies linked, the sort of thing I would have liked to have when I started, then I explained core concepts; I picked up these concepts because they tended to appear over and over in the papers I was reading.

Then, I had a therapies section; here I was largely inspired by Laura Deming's FAQ and the papers that looked at precisely anti-aging therapies in isolation; I decided to take the union of those sets of therapies, discarding some that seemed less relevant.

In looking for studies, when finding that something seemed surprisingly good, as is the case for senolytics or rapamycin, I tried searching for terms like "rapamycin ineffective" or "senolytics negative effects"; the latter led to some interesting speculation as to how that may be. In general I applied the standard Nintil method:

1.  Look at literature reviews on the topic
2.  Expand and search about topics you don't understand
3.  For results you are skeptical about, or that different reviews contradict each other, find literature reviews for those concrete points.
4.  Follow the citation graph until the most recent evidence has been reviewed
5.  In the process, take note of how much disagreement there is, the journals papers tend to appear on, what are some papers everyone cites, etc.

For an individual paper, there's the question of what to read. At first, with the very general literature reviews, the answer is everything. In the more narrow literature reviews, I admit I'll skip sections and focus on the figures, introduction, and conclusion; usually these sometimes get too deep into a particular topic and it wouldn't help me that much (It won't make it into the FAQ and I will forget the details anyway). Sometimes I would have concrete questions; in a section of the FAQ there is a brief reference to the % of senescent cells in adults and young individuals. This is something I had wondered during writing the FAQ, as it's a common assertion that "just a few senescent cells can have large effects", but how much few is that few? So I looked up papers for that, and in that case I just took the number and read the paragraph where it appeared.

A problem that occurs when attempting to reconcile disagreements in the literature is who to trust. Sometimes the disagreeing paper came later and then even later one can find a joint paper by both authors where they give an interpretation to the results (As was the case with the monkey studies). Another case is that a later paper says the earlier paper was flawed due to poor methods, or that the earlier paper claimed results that were not generalizable. This was the case for the Whitaker (2013) paper I cite on the effect of sirtuins in flies. If the later paper references earlier work and explains the disagreement, I'm prima facie willing to accept that as the truth.

But there's an issue where one finds what seems to be different communities of researchers (This might be the case with the free radicals researchers) who tend to publish on different journals and accept different things as true, odd as this may seem. Of course, here the problem may be that one side has not read the other's evidence, or that one side's arguments are just not good. This was the case in the ROS section. Here, the best I could do is do a longer section, noting down the results people have claimed over time in reviews; but I cannot have a lot of confidence in this section; to do so I'd have to dig deeper and understand better the different assays and pieces of evidence. So if I did a deeper review of the role of ROS I'd begin by noting down specific claims (e.g. antioxidant intake does not lengthen lifespan, in any animal model, superoxide dismutase knockout reduces lifespan), noting down the evidence given and one by one check the discussion for each fact. Then once I have individual pieces of information, I could see how they could logically fit together and determine who is right.

After publication, as I knew I was bound to have many mistakes, this being a topic new to me, I made the commitment to update every day the FAQ with whatever corrections I have sourced that day from feedback I'm receiving from readers. On day 2 after publication I also decided to send the FAQ to various reputed ageing researchers, to see if they have any corrections to ad (As of the writing of this, they haven't replied).

Early response seemed good, a biogerontologist on reddit said it looks [pretty good (8-9) out of 10](https://www.reddit.com/r/longevity/comments/er3x6b/nintil_the_longevity_faq_a_summary_of_the_current/), likewise I got some very [good](https://twitter.com/AdamMarblestone/status/1219398228996763648) [responses](https://twitter.com/anderssandberg/status/1219654582814507009) on twitter. The changelog in the post reveals that there were a number of mistakes in the original that I've corrected. Doing a post-mortem, most of the serious mistakes were to be found in sections of the FAQ that were in the initial version, which I started to write before a comprehensive reading of the literature. The obvious fix here is to wait until everything has been reviewed to start writing. The second class of mistakes was due to lacking basic knowledge about biology. This was to be expected.

At the end of the day, I could have chosen to be more careful and spend more time reading about the basics before deciding to write a FAQ on a complex topic I had little idea about prior to starting. But instead, I judged more efficient to try anyway and then use the feedback I would receive as guide to know what I was missing. As a result, I have identified weaknesses in my knowledge of the immune system, the mechanics of cancer, atherosclerosis, and inflammation; which might make it into an updated version of the FAQ.

I am fairly satisfied with the final result; I predicted that it would be a reasonably good FAQ that would contain some mistakes, and that it would get polished over time, and that has been the case.
