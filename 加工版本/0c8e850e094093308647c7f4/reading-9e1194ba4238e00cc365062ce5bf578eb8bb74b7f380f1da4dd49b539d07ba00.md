# I did a meditation and it sort of changed my personality?!

---

## 正文

2025 年 3 月，我参加了一次 [Jhourney](https://jhourney.io/) meditation retreat，这是我一生中最深刻的经历之一，也深深地 reshaped 了我与自己相处的方式。

如果你在 retreat 之前告诉我会发生这些事，我是不会相信的。我以前做过一些 meditation——包括一次 10-day vipassana retreat——虽然它并不是 _毫无_作用，但我还是觉得不过如此。我很抗拒花那么多时间盯着自己的脑袋里面看：我似乎很难真正相信通过那种方式学到的任何东西，因为那是一个如此 complicated system。而且，我的 brain 原本似乎就运转得挺好。

我读过一些 [serious](https://x.com/nickcammarata) [meditators](https://sashachapin.substack.com/) 的文章，他们似乎有着另一种 experience，充满了 insights 和 bliss，但我并没有特别被那种 experience 吸引。那么多的 navel-gazing 和 [wireheading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirehead_%5c%28science_fiction%5c%29) 让我觉得很自我放纵；何况他们看起来足够古怪，不管他们在做什么，大概都不会对我有效。

后来，生活仿佛模仿起了 [Bay Area House Party](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/every-bay-area-house-party?hide_intro_popup=true)，Jhourney 出现了：这家 meditation retreat startup 承诺带你 speedrun 到 bliss 的部分（[当时的宣传语](https://web.archive.org/web/20250217013113/https://www.jhourney.io/)：“改变人生的 meditative bliss，以前需要数千小时才能学会。我们用一周教会你。”）当我听到，不仅那些我一直在读其文章的 serious meditators 强烈推荐它，连几位更讲求实际的同事也这么说时，我决定去看看大家究竟在热捧什么。

* * *

Jhourney 教授一种 meditation，名义上 focused on 被称为 jhanas 的 altered states。Jhanas 是 blissful-feeling states：如果你 release 了足够多可以称作 tension、[clenching](https://howtounclench.com/) 或 [tanha](https://x.com/nickcammarata/status/1561191706124603395) 的东西，再 focus on 一种 enjoyable open-hearted sensation，比如 smiling、一段 joyful memory，或者对所爱之人 feeling care，你就可以“relax into”这些 states。如果做得对，你就能启动一个 enjoying the enjoyment 的 feedback loop，最终进入 deeply absorbed euphoria 的 state。

我说 Jhourney 是 _名义上_ focused on 那些东西，是因为 jhanas 实际上有点像改善生活和 relationships 的特洛伊木马，而后者不知为何就是没有 wireheading 那么好卖。Jhourney 的非正式口号是：“为了 bliss 而来，为了 personal growth 而留下。”之所以会有这种 growth，是因为你在 jhana meditation 中试图 release 的许多 tension，都是 repressed 或“stuck” feelings 的 downstream：有些很小（“我不想听到那边那个声音”，导致 head 和 neck 的 micro-clenching），有些很大（“我不能让自己 feel 这种 sadness，不然我会哭，那会很 shameful”，导致各种 face 和 chest muscles 的 tensing，而这些 muscles 原本会帮助你哭出来）。为了 get into jhana states，许多人（比如我！）最终不得不努力 untangling knots of emotional clenching，而这通常会带来许多 growth。

* * *

我对这次 Jhourney retreat 的第一个体验，就是刚到那里便拿到了一本 80-page 的 meditation instructions 手册。

这已经让我有点震惊了，因为我从没读过任何自己觉得写得好的、关于 spirituality 或 emotions 的东西。我读过的关于 mind 的“最好”的内容，也不过是空洞的 pop-psychology，用吸引人的废话把老生常谈填充起来，直到最后不知怎么就成了一本卖得出去的书。其余的则与 astrology 没什么区别——它们从来没有清楚到足以让我判断是否适用于自己，也就是说，它们似乎并没有提出 falsifiable predictions。至于 spiritual writing，大多给我的感觉都是 word-association 式的胡言乱语，作者随手抛出一些氛围对味的词，我却不确定他们自己是否真的理解这些词。说真的，我之前参加的那次 meditation retreat，居然还安排大家观看一段长达一小时的录像，里面那位如今已经去世的创办人在讲自己如何能够 sense quantum vibrations。

Jhourney 手册没有这些问题，因为它是 software engineers 写的。它 epistemically conservative，而且清晰得惊人，完全没有胡言乱语。为了帮助大家理解那些 inherently fuzzy language，他们从多个角度解释每件事。他们给出了清楚的 [gears-level](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B7P97C27rvHPz3s9B/gears-in-understanding) explanations，说明自己为什么认为这些东西会如此运作。他们甚至专门岔开去解释自己的 teaching philosophy，让 students 明白自己 _为什么_ 要做正在做的事情。这是出色的 pedagogy。

结合书面的 instructions 和现场 meditation sessions 中的 teachings，我很快就能到达一个感觉像是通往 jhana 的“入口匝道”的位置。但每次到了那里，我都会因为接下来该怎么做而变得 stressed 和“grabby”，然后从匝道上“掉下来”。

到了 retreat 的第 4 天，我对此感到非常 frustrated，而且这种感受开始自己形成 loop。我因为无法 reach jhana 而 frustrated，又因为这种 frustration 让事情更加困难而 frustrated。我尝试了 Jhourney 推荐的应对 negative emotions 的方式，也就是一种 self-compassion meditation。但无论怎么尝试，它都没能真正 resonate；我无法 cultivate 任何 self-compassion 的 emotional experience。

事实上，我意识到，自己想不起来 _曾经_ 有过任何真正的 receiving compassion 的 emotional experience，而这大概是有点糟透了。

我的不少朋友也参加了同一次 retreat。因为我根本无法获得 self-compassion，我当然就拿这个发现来担心：他们会不会因为我自身有毛病、meditating 很困难而评判我，从而又给这个灾难性的 feedback loop 添上更多层。

第 4–6 天相当难熬。Jhourney 每天都会让我们填写一份调查，问：“如果把普通的一天算作 5，以 1-10 打分，你今天过得怎么样？”我给第 6 天打了 1.5。（值得肯定的是，Jhourney 知道这意味着我需要额外帮助，安排了很多与不同 facilitators 进行 1:1 交流的时间，尽管这些都没有带来多大改变。）

* * *

到了这时，在一次拼命试图靠思考摆脱 feelings 困境的过程中，我开始反复琢磨 [elephant 和 rider 的 metaphor](https://www.creativehuddle.co.uk/post/the-elephant-and-the-rider)。这个想法是：人们通常会 identify with 自己 mind 中发生的一切，但这实际上是一种 confusion；更准确的理解是，把“你”，也就是 conscious part，看作一个小小的 human rider，坐在一头庞大而行动迟缓、常常自顾自行动的 elephant 身上。真正属于 rider 的 action space 的，只有诸如“对 elephant 说话”“向 elephant visualize mental images”“把 attention direct 到 experience 的某个特定 aspect”等事情。其他一切——包括所有 emotions 和许多 thoughts——都是 elephant 的工作。

在这个 frame 下，self-compassion meditation 对我不起作用，其实并不是“我”的错。self-compassion 的 emotional experience 是否发生，取决于 elephant，而“我”（rider）唯一能做的，就是继续温柔地说那些话、做那个 technique，或者诸如此类的事，然后等待 elephant 按它自己的节奏 steer。

大约就在那时，我决定放弃 Jhourney curriculum，只做那些看起来会让我感觉舒服的事。✻✻ 讽刺的是，Jhourney 在手册的多个地方多少都推荐过这么做，但我太执着于遵守规则，以至于没有遵守规则中告诉我停止遵守规则的那部分。我打破了保持沉默的规则，向一位同样参加 retreat 的亲密朋友寻求一些 reassurance。重新开始 meditating 时，我给自己编了一个 technique：想象把 love 和 compassion 送给 elephant，而不是“我自己”。结果这竟然像个作弊码：我立刻 felt 一股涌出的 warmth，而我对自己说的那些宽慰的话，也开始 emotionally resonate。

我真希望能更好地描述那之后发生的事，但遗憾的是，那已经是好一阵子以前了，而且涉及许多 subtle mental movements；再加上，当你的一部分 psyche 正在 liquefying 时，很难追踪正在发生什么。我记得有一种仿佛“走进”了对 elephant 的 compassion 和 love 这种 feeling 之中的感觉，它占据我越来越多的 attention，感觉也越来越 deep、rich、meaningful。然后是某种 energy 的涌动，以及一次 full-body frisson；我后来发现，当我的 brain rearranged itself 时，常常会出现这种现象。†† 我不认为按照 Jhourney 的 rubric，这严格 qualifies as 一个 jhana，因为它并不是 stable 和 self-sustaining 的，但除此之外，它的感觉很接近他们对 first jhana 的描述。

当我起身走到外面时，我逐渐注意到，世界的感觉很不一样。我意识到，自己一直带着一种像“冰箱嗡鸣”般的 subtle body tension，而它现在消失了。在 retreat center 四处走走、看看风景，感觉美妙极了，仿佛我可以花上几天，就这样融化在景色里。热饮区的 Lipton 薄荷茶好喝得不合情理。有时我会无缘无故因为 joy 或 gratitude 而眼眶湿润。我感到了与朋友之间深得多的 care 和 connection。

也许这样说听起来有点黏糊糊的，还丢失了个人特质，好像我吃了某种 drug，把平常的自己替换成了一滩 love 之类的东西。实际 experience 恰恰相反；不知为何，我感觉这一切的发生，正是因为 _做自己变得更容易了_。那些 care 和 connection 并非来自某滩外来的 love；它们之所以流露出来，是因为一大堆 psychic gunk 脱落了，让真正的 Ben-ness 的 essence 更容易显露出来。

* * *

然后，我~凭着新获得的对 emotions 和 self-compassion 的 mastery，从此幸福地生活了下去~手忙脚乱地试图把这一切 integrate 回正常生活。

retreat 之后，我注意到的一个重大变化是，我的 emotions 变得 intense 了 2–3×。我觉得这最终是 net positive，但它让回去工作变得很困难——我担任着一个 stressful 的 role，managing 一个 large team，而且还得补上一周与外界失联期间落下的工作，这让人更加 stressed。更不妙的是，突然之间，如果有人在 Slack 上惹人烦，有时我会气得想从耳朵里喷火。

接下来的几周里，这种 intensity 回落了，我的 moment-to-moment experience 的变化大概有 80% 恢复了正常（例如，我不再在平常的情境下 spontaneously tearing up）。不过，有些变化留了下来：

-   我的许多 shame/cringe reflex 变成了 self-compassion reflex。以前，一点小失误就会让我特别难堪（比如，怎么说呢，忘记锁公共卫生间的门，结果有人走了进来）。现在，遇到这些事时，我会非常清楚地感到：“哎呀，这不过是普通人都会犯的错，其他几百万人也犯过，对方很可能根本没放在心上。”
-   我变得更容易被艺术打动。以前，我大概一年才会被一首诗或一段音乐感动落泪一次，现在变成了一周一次。
-   我对自己更 subtle 的 emotional responses 也一直保持着更强的觉察。我记得，有次听一段以前大概听过 100 次的 instrumental folk music，突然注意到那段 melody 让我感到 deeply nostalgic，随后意识到，这比我以往听音乐时的感受，在 [feelings wheel](https://www.feelingswheel.com/) 上向外多了 two levels。
-   我仍然觉得，自己对 distracted 或 stimulated 的需要比以前少得多。以前，只要没有 fully focused 于某项任务（比如做家务或走路时），我就会以 2× speed 听 podcasts。现在，我完全不想这样做，而且真要听 podcasts 时，2× speed 会让我觉得不舒服。同样，我开始不喜欢很多餐馆的食物，却非常享受简单的家常饭菜，以前我会嫌这些饭菜太寡淡。
-   朋友们仍然会提到，我的 body language 不一样了。一位朋友说：“posture 更 upright，shoulders 更 open，更 fluid，也更 gentle。”另一位形容我：“‘离开得更少了’，也就是说，在 interactions 中，‘不在场’的时间更少了。”

这些变化都很好，但更好的是，许多 mindset 和 relational shifts 也留了下来。

* * *

在 Mindset 方面，我想只能说，我成了 feelings-pilled 的人。

如果你在 retreat 之前问我，emotions 到底是怎么回事，我大概会这样回答：

> Positive emotions 很好，不过，你不该让它们过多地 distract 你，使你偏离 long-term goals。Painful emotions 是对 stimuli 的 responses，属于我们的 evolutionary inheritance，但在今天大多是 maladaptive，因为我们所处的 environment 与它们产生时的 environment 太不一样了。假设你有足够好的 self-control，不会因它们而 act out，那么它们的 main effect 就是让你变笨；你应该等自己冷静下来后，再去接触让你 emotional 的事情，以此 mitigate 这种影响。

我现在的看法更接近下面这样（这仍然是一个 oversimplified strawman，现实要复杂得多）：

> 所有 emotions 都是 unconscious/intuitive cognition 中重要而美好的 components。‡‡ 我确实是说 _所有_，甚至包括我们的文化非常不喜欢的那些——例如，anger 是一个 sign，表明你在乎的某件事物正受到 threatened，需要 protecting；而 fully welcoming 它，能帮助你更清楚地知道究竟什么需要 protecting，以及该怎么做。如果你想进一步了解 negative emotions 中美好而 constructive 的 aspects，我推荐 [The Language of Emotions](https://karlamclaren.com/the-language-of-emotions-book/) 这本书、[Joe Hudson 的 podcast](https://www.youtube.com/@ArtofAccomplishment/featured)（搜索你感兴趣的 emotion），或者这里有几篇文章，分别概述了他对 [anger](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/anger)、[fear](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/fear) 和 [shame](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/shame) 的看法。不过，它们有时会 create impulses，让我们想做一些实际上并非好主意的事情。在通过 socializing 让自己不去 follow 那些 impulses 的过程中，我们受到的训练是：通过 repressing 它们，或围绕它们 clenching up，以痛苦的方式试图让 emotions 本身消失。与直觉相反，repression 和 clenching 实际上会使 emotions 以某种 partially metabolized state “停留下来”，产生 negative side-effects，例如 anxiety、thought-looping、numbness、blind spots、impaired reasoning，以及唤起 secondary emotions 作为 defenses，等等。另一种与这些 emotions 相处、从长远来看对你更好的方式，是弄明白如何 welcome 和 accept 它们。这最终会让它们感觉不那么糟，并让它们带给你更多属于好主意的 impulses、更少属于坏主意的 impulses。但这并不像“停止 repressing 就好了”那么简单。Welcoming emotions 需要 fully feeling 它们；如果它们很 strong，这可能会令人过于 overwhelming，除非你有一种 deep felt sense of safety，否则就无法奏效。这种 safety 可以通过许多不同方式获得，例如 therapist、朋友、某些 meditation，等等。如果你无法获得足够的 safety，暂时 repressing 或 clenching 也许就是你最好的选择。

让我改变这里这些看法的主要原因，是我在 retreat 期间花了好几天，密切关注自己的 emotional experience。例如，retreat 刚开始不久，一位 facilitator 声称，thought-looping 通常是 stuck 或 repressed emotion 的一个 sign。我当时非常怀疑，但后来，我有过几次 _非常清楚_ 的经历：围绕某件事 thought-looping，独自找个私密的地方为此哭一场，然后 thought spiral 立刻停止了，我与那件事的关系也变得不同。§§ 在 retreat 中，比在日常生活中更容易看清这一点：大量 meditation 既让我更容易注意到自己的 thought processes，也让我更快地“unstick”或 welcome 底层的 emotions。

同样，就在 retreat 之前，我结束了一段 long-term relationship，对此一直有很多 internal conflict 和 self-doubt。我的 therapist 经常对我说这样的话：“如果你充分 felt 了自己的 feelings，你就会对这个决定感到 clear。”我当时觉得这简直不可理喻（呃，这本来就是个非常艰难的决定，而且有很多 tradeoffs，我根本不知道该如何 weigh！）。但在 retreat 的最后一天，我注意到，自己清楚多了：那段 relationship 中许多我不喜欢的事情，确实存在，也确实重要；我对结束它的选择也有信心得多了。

当然，我早就听过很多人说“这种决定不能 logically 做，你必须倾听自己的内心”之类的话，但我始终没有真正理解他们在说什么。回过头看，我只是 _没有能力_ 做出“倾听自己的内心”这个 internal move，因为 [管道被太多 repressed 的积垢堵住了](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/theres-trauma-and-then-theres-gnar)。我真希望那些简短的说法后面能再加一句，比如：“当然，倾听自己的内心可能说起来容易，做起来难得多，所以，如果你觉得这很难，或者不清楚该怎么做，这里有一些可以起步的做法；也请做好准备，你可能要花很多小时（甚至很多年），才能弄明白自己的内心想说什么。”

这种新的 mindset 确实改变了我与自己的 emotions 相处的方式。虽然我仍然经常觉得它们 painful，但已经比以前少了很多，而且现在，我的出发点是：它们并不“真的”是 painful 或坏的，而最好的应对方式，是弄明白如何 accept 它们、welcome 它们，并找到其中的 wisdom 或 beauty。总体而言，这种感觉好得多——遇到 stressful 或艰难的事情时，我不再只是不知所措地挣扎，而更像是有了某种方向感或总体上的 orientation，这真的让人感到 comforting 和 grounding。

* * *

我还注意到，这些思考自身 emotional difficulty 的方式，很多也可以作为有用的 metaphors，帮助我思考人与人之间的 emotional dynamics。就像 resisting 自己的 emotions 是一种常见的 coping strategy，但最终效果并不好一样，resisting _别人的_ emotions 也是一种非常常见、却有害的 pattern；我猜，许多（大多数？）relational damage 都与此有很大关系。例如，我意识到，我刚刚结束的那段 relationship 的 root problem，很大一部分在于我们双方都缺少 accept 彼此许多 emotions 的 capacity，而当时我还无法清楚表达这一点。现在，我认为，[明确以 welcoming 彼此 emotions 为重点的 practices](https://autotranslucence.wordpress.com/2019/05/07/emotional-waste-collection-the-care-and-feeding-of-relationship-intimacy/)，是长期 nurturing relationships 的一种非常有价值的工具。

事实上，我开始注意到，_许多_ 棘手的问题——无论是我个人生活中的问题，还是作为 manager 遇到的那类问题——都部分源于人们 resisting 某种 emotions。Alice 很难沟通，因为她经常滔滔不绝地讲不重要的细节，因为她想证明自己什么都懂，因为她对于不被认真对待，有许多 stuck fear/resentment。Bob 和 Clara 对 strategy 一直有 disagreement，因为 Clara 不够在乎 Bob 的意见，不愿参与讨论，因为 Bob 曾经对她很刻薄；Clara 从未抱怨，因为她一直在 avoiding 那种受到伤害的 feeling。Dave 的 team 正在 underperforming，但作为 exec sponsor 的 Erin 不愿 manage him out，因为她害怕 conflict，也害怕让那些喜欢 Dave 的 reports 感到 angry。

当然，一个问题由 stuck emotions 引起，并不意味着 unsticking 它们就是解决问题的最佳方式；但这仍然是一个非常有用的 lens，能帮助我理解正在发生的事情。

* * *

另一种保留下来的 shift 发生在我的 relationships 中。我的 friendships 有了巨大的改善，几个不同的因素促成了这一点：

-   我更清楚自己想从 close friendships 中获得什么——主要是一种 strong felt sense of safety and care——也感到这是我生活中缺失的一大块。
-   我也开始更善于 feeling 这些东西。随着我的许多 shame reflex 消解，总体而言，和人相处时我感到更 safer 了——对于别人会如何回应我的行为，我的 intuitive predictions 变得更 positive。我也更善于注意到并珍惜别人向我 expressed care 的不同方式。
-   因为总体上觉得更 safer，我也更愿意 take risks 或 be vulnerable，这让 closeness 建立得更快。一位朋友说，我似乎更有勇气了。

这也意味着，我的“交友类型”变了——我更在乎 emotional connection，而不那么在乎 intellectual connection。幸运的是，虽然我生活的圈子非常崇尚思考，我仍然找到了许多热心而宽厚的人，并逐渐结交了一群在这两方面都很出色的 close friends。到目前为止，这是最让我开心的变化。

我的 dating life 也发生了很大变化：

-   我开始能够感受到对别人强烈得多的 attracted。例如，那年晚些时候，我喜欢上了一个人，那种 crush 比我高中时经历过的任何 crush 都更强烈。
-   我接连有过一些 anxious-attachment-ish experiences，这是以前从未有过的，不过它们随着时间推移变得温和多了。‖‖ 我不完全确定这是怎么回事；我想，这部分与 calibrating 到我能感受到的新的 attraction level 有关。
-   我上面提到的额外的 safety 和勇气，在 dating 中也很有帮助：对于沟通棘手的事情，或身处不寻常的情境，我自在多了（例如，不属于“在 app 上认识一个人，约几次会，然后 become exclusive”这种 standard script 的情境）。一位朋友评论说，我似乎有了更多 degrees of freedom。

* * *

总体而言，这些变化真的非常美好，但也相当 intense。变得更加 emotionally aware，解决了我的许多问题，比如想要更 deep 的 friendships，或不明白自己的 relationship 为何 unsatisfying。但结果是，打赢那场 boss fight 只是让所有敌人都 level up 了。我原来的问题被可怕的新问题取代了，比如我和那些新近建立起 deep 关系的朋友有时会有 conflicts，或者我对 dating 对象的喜欢会超过对方对我的喜欢。

在我的生活中，对于这些变化的 impact，我感受最复杂的部分是工作。retreat 大约六个月后，我出现了相当严重的 burnout，离开了 managing 一个 big team 的 role，转到一个明显没有那么 ambitious 的 IC role。我不认为 retreat 是我 burnout 的 main cause——main cause 是，我在自己 managing 的 area 中没有足够的 background，无法产生我想要的那种 impact，此外还夹杂着一些难以相处的 working relationships。但结果是，我新的 psyche 也变得更不擅长 repressing 或 avoiding emotion 了，这让一切都更加困难。

我把 repression 看作 [technical debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_debt) 或 [deferred maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deferred_maintenance) 在 emotional 层面的 equivalent：这是一种 avoiding immediate cost 的方式，在这里，这个 cost 就是 feeling uncomfortable feelings，代价则是以后要付出更高的 cost 来清理它。¶¶ 就像 tech debt 或 deferred maintenance 一样，如果你以后会有更多 resources 来处理问题，那么 repression 或 avoidance 有时也可能是正确的 strategy！我的 role 让我不断遇到一些低程度的 painful 事情，比如别人对我 disappointed 或 annoyed，我对别人 disappointed 或 annoyed，projects 进展不顺，办公室零食被换成更差的零食，等等。由于 avoid 或 repress 的能力变弱了，这些事情出现时，我不得不更充分地 feel 它们，这真的很 draining。它也需要相当多的时间和 attention，以至于我不得不 defer 自己个人生活中的许多 maintenance。

即使现在我觉得自己的 energy levels 已经基本 recovered，我仍然不确定自己是否想回到漩涡中心。不仅仅是因为我得重新开始 deferring maintenance；我也更加 aware 到许多自己_已经_ deferred、而且非常想清理的 maintenance——比如前面提到的 investing in friendships。

如果我只是在追求自己的 personal growth 和 happiness，我会说这个变化显然是好的。我的生活感觉更加 richer，也更加 whole，而接受一个没有那么 ambitious 的 role，迫使我 release 了对于拥有一份“光鲜”的工作或 career 的几层 ego attachment，这让我感到深深的 freeing。但是……我也非常在乎自己对世界的 impact，而且我认为[接下来的几年很可能会是一个 critical period](https://ai-2027.com/research/timelines-forecast)，我的工作在这期间可能会尤其 high-leverage。从这个角度看，此刻对全力投入工作感到 resistant，是件令人难过的事。

* * *

我很不确定该从这一切中得出什么。下面是一些零散的 reflections。

-   在参加这次 retreat 之前，我绝不会说自己在 self-compassion 或 self-esteem 上有问题，因为当我直接向自己提问时，我能够说出正确的 self-compassionate 话语。但从事后的视角看，我显然确实有很大的 self-compassion 问题；即使我能说出那些话，我也并没有真正 feeling 它们，或像它们是真的那样行动。真正拥有 self-compassion 的 embodied experience 和 habits，是我在现实生活中最接近 [The Vitamin](https://www.tumblr.com/caracalliope/730830836356775936/moreover-everyone-gathers-around-to-be) 的体验。✻✻✻✻ “我最深、最隐秘的幻想是，我倒在街上，被紧急送进医院。他们做了一堆 tests，发现我严重 deficient 于某种 vitamin。然后我开始 taking 那种 vitamin，成为世上最快乐、最聪明的人，因为我所有的问题都是这一种 deficiency 导致的。"
-   Jhourney 正在做一件非常酷的事，而且在几个 axes 上远胜于大多数／所有其他 meditation retreats（可获得的 support 的数量、pedagogical excellence）。不过，你大概不该期待自己也会有_如此_改变人生的体验；我猜，就 long-term effects 而言，我的 outcome 大概属于 95th-percentile-plus。我仍然认为，作为 meditation 和 embodied emotional awareness 的 introduction，它非常棒。
-   我觉得自己在时间上真的很幸运：如果 retreat 早一天结束，我想我就不会经历那个改变人生的部分了。这让我非常后怕。
-   起初，我由此得出结论，认为自己应该特别投入 meditation。后来我又参加了两次 retreats（另一次 Jhourney retreat，以及一次 Dustin DiPerna 的 mahamudra introduction retreat），但它们都远没有产生那么大的 effect。第二次 Jhourney retreat 对 reinforcing 我的 daily practice 很有帮助，而且我确实认为，daily meditation practice 对 stabilizing 那些后来变得 permanent 的变化有帮助，但就连这一点，我也不是完全确定。
-   Jhanas 肯定是真的。另外，听到有人告诉我一件听起来完全是假的事情（“只用你的 brain，就能按需 achieve bliss”），然后拿出一本 80 页的 engineering manual 和关于 success rates 的细致 data 来支持它，这感觉相当不可思议。总体而言，这让我更有可能相信，其他各种“woo” phenomena 也是真的，至少就人们正在 accurately describing 自己的 experiences 这一点而言是如此。（我仍然相信 universe 按照 physics 运行，但许多 woo 事物都有 physics-compatible explanations，尤其是如果你相信人的 intuitions 有时能做出一些神乎其神的事情。）
-   我在标题中把这描述为“chang\[ing\] 我的 personality”，但我不想夸大它的 magnitude。我确实经常在聊天中被人问到：“所以，你怎么会这么 <like X>？”然后我会告诉他们：“我几年前参加过一次奇怪的 meditation retreat。”不过，personality 也有许多不同的 aspects，即使其中许多 aspects 对我来说完全 changed 了，大部分仍然没有变化，所以从 absolute 的意义上说，我仍然非常像过去的自己。
-   哪些 flags 应该提醒你，也许你该更多地审视自己的 emotions？下面这些事情，我觉得可能是线索（回头看，其中许多都符合我当时的情况）：
    -   你经常 stressed，并 stuck 在 thought loops 中
    -   你很容易 annoyed 或 frustrated
    -   你的 scarcity mindset 过多——你经常担心，这已经是自己这辈子能拥有的最好的工作、relationship 或其他什么，而这种担心并没有 fundamentals 的支持
    -   你寻求大量 distraction 或 stimulation；仅仅活在当下并不让你觉得很愉快
    -   对于自己想要什么，你感到许多 internal conflict 或 uncertainty
    -   你非常不喜欢自己的一些部分，或希望它们有所不同
    -   你觉得生活中缺少某种 depth、richness 或 meaning，却说不准究竟缺了什么

我也仍然有许多问题：

-   这一连串的 experiences……是好的吗？
    -   我非常确信，它会有益于我自己的 long-term life happiness。但它发生的时机意味着，在这个可能是 humanity 最 critical 的 periods 之一的阶段，我变得不那么愿意承担 intense 的工作；即使这个变化是 positive 的，这也让我感觉不好。我有点不确定该如何 balance 这些。
    -   我肩头的 [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham) 说，我本该继续 [playing push-pin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-pin_%5c%28game%5c%29#References_in_philosophy)。我肩头的，呃……[deva？](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_%5c%28Buddhism%5c%29) 则说，如果我追随自己的内心，我对世界的 impact 会更大，即使它不那么 predictable 或 legible。我觉得这两种说法我都不太信。
-   在这之前，我似乎处在一个带有大量 numbing 和 repression 的 personality [attractor basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor#Basins_of_attraction) 中。这究竟是怎么发生的？更重要的是，未来我／我们怎样才能尽量让更少的人遇到这种情况？
    -   关于这一点，我有一些模糊的线索（比如，我小时候很容易被惹哭，因此在学校经常受欺负），但远没有一幅完整的图景（比如：每个有过那种经历的人都会这样吗？如果不是，是什么造成了差别？）
-   从 Mechanistically 的角度看，elephant meditation 做了什么？它为什么会那么突然地起作用，并 create 如此 lasting 的 shift？（事实上，大部分 lasting shift 真的是它带来的吗？其中又有多少其实来自我在 cognitive level 上学到的东西？）
-   紧接着那之后，我进入的那个 glowy、“更容易成为 Ben 的 essence”的 state，是否能够被 stabilized（或者其中一些 aspects 能够被 stabilized）？如果可以，该怎么做？
-   更一般地说——接下来我该走向哪里？我确信，做一周合适类型的 introspection 或 meditation 之类的事，可以 unlock 很棒的东西，而且感觉还有更多很棒的东西等待 unlock，但我希望自己有一种比 semi-randomly 尝试更好的方式，来找到通往它们的路。

* * *

一位读过草稿的人问，关于这段历程，我会对过去的自己，或像我这样的人说些什么。归根结底，我会将其归结为：

-   你很可能有许多 feelings，是你拒绝 welcome 进自己 experience 的，而这很可能与你生活和 relationships 中许多 unsatisfying 的事情有关。

-   你可以在 safe context 中，逐渐向那些 uncomfortable 的部分给予 loving attention，以此 improve 这种情况；出发点是想要了解和 care for 自己，而不是 fix 自己。

-   当这很难时，记住这不是你的错。你只是 rider；elephants 总归会按 elephant 的方式行事。让它去它需要去的地方，并继续尽你所能地爱它。

## 原文

In March 2025, I went on a [Jhourney](https://jhourney.io/) meditation retreat that was one of the most profound experiences of my life, and deeply reshaped how I related to myself.

If you had told my pre-retreat self that this was going to happen, I wouldn’t have believed you. I’d done some meditation before—including a 10-day vipassana retreat—and while it didn’t do _nothing_, I was underwhelmed. I felt pretty resistant to spending that much time staring at the inside of my head: it seemed really hard to actually trust anything I learned that way, because it was such a complicated system. And besides, my brain seemed to work fine the way it was.

I’d read some writing by [serious](https://x.com/nickcammarata) [meditators](https://sashachapin.substack.com/) who seemed to be having a different experience, filled with insights and bliss, but it wasn’t one that I felt particularly drawn to. That much navel-gazing and [wireheading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirehead_%5c%28science_fiction%5c%29) felt self-indulgent, and anyway they seemed weird enough that whatever they were doing probably wouldn’t work for me.

Then, in a moment of life imitating [Bay Area House Party](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/every-bay-area-house-party?hide_intro_popup=true), we got Jhourney, a meditation retreat startup promising to speedrun you to the bliss part ([tagline at the time](https://web.archive.org/web/20250217013113/https://www.jhourney.io/): “Life-changing meditative bliss used to take thousands of hours to learn. We teach it in a week.”) When I heard them strongly recommended not only by the serious meditators I’d been reading, but also by a couple more hard-nosed coworkers, I decided to see what the hype was about.

* * *

Jhourney teaches a style of meditation nominally focused on altered states called jhanas. Jhanas are blissful-feeling states that you can “relax into” if you release enough of what you might call tension or [clenching](https://howtounclench.com/) or [tanha](https://x.com/nickcammarata/status/1561191706124603395), and then focus on an enjoyable open-hearted sensation like smiling, a joyful memory, or feeling care for a loved one. If done right, you can start a feedback loop of enjoying the enjoyment, and end up in a state of deeply absorbed euphoria.

I say Jhourney is _nominally_ focused on that because the jhanas are actually kind of a Trojan horse for improving your life and relationships, which somehow doesn’t sell as well as wireheading. Jhourney’s unofficial motto is “come for the bliss, stay for the personal growth.” The growth happens because a lot of the tension you’re trying to release in jhana meditation is downstream of repressed or “stuck” feelings, either small ones (“I don’t want to be hearing that sound over there” leading to micro-clenching the head and neck) or big ones (“I can’t let myself feel this sadness or I’ll cry and that would be shameful” leading to tensing the various face and chest muscles that would otherwise be helping you cry). In order to get into jhana states, a lot of people (like me!) end up having to work on untangling knots of emotional clenching, and this usually leads to a lot of growth.

* * *

My first experience of my Jhourney retreat was showing up and being handed an 80-page handbook of meditation instructions.

This already blew my mind a little, because I had never read anything about spirituality or emotions that I considered good. The “best” stuff I’d read about the mind was vapid pop-psychology, padding out truisms with engaging fluff until the result somehow became a marketable book. The rest was indistinguishable from astrology—it was never clear enough that I could tell whether it applied to me, which meant it didn’t seem to make falsifiable predictions. As for spiritual writing, it mostly felt like word-association mumbo-jumbo, with authors throwing around words that had the right vibes but that I wasn’t sure they themselves even really understood. Heck, the previous meditation retreat I’d been on had included watching an hour-long recording of the now-dead founder talking about how he could sense quantum vibrations.

The Jhourney handbook didn’t have these problems, because it was written by software engineers. It was epistemically conservative and shockingly lucid, completely mumbo- and jumbo-free. To help with the inherently fuzzy language, they explained everything from multiple angles. They gave clear [gears-level](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B7P97C27rvHPz3s9B/gears-in-understanding) explanations of why they thought things worked the way they did. They even took a detour to explain their teaching philosophy so that students were oriented about _why_ they were doing what they were doing. It was masterful pedagogy.

Between the written instructions and the teachings from live meditation sessions, pretty soon I could get to a point that felt like the “on-ramp” to a jhana. But every time I got there, I would get stressed and “grabby” about what to do next, and “fall off” the ramp.

By the time I got to day 4 of the retreat, I was feeling really frustrated about this, in a way that was starting to loop on itself. I was frustrated that I couldn’t reach jhana, and frustrated that the frustration was making things harder. I tried Jhourney’s recommended way of working with negative emotions, which was a self-compassion meditation. But no matter how much I tried, it didn’t really resonate; I couldn’t cultivate any emotional experience of self-compassion.

In fact, I realized I couldn’t remember _ever_ having had a real emotional experience of receiving compassion, and that that was probably kind of fucked up.

A bunch of my friends were on the same retreat. Since I had no access to self-compassion, of course I used this realization to start worrying that they would judge me for having a hard time meditating because I was fucked up, adding even more layers to the feedback loop of doom.

Days 4–6 were pretty bad. Every day Jhourney gave us a survey of “how was your day on a scale of 1-10 with 5 as a normal day?” I rated day 6 a 1.5. (To their credit, Jhourney knew this meant I needed extra help and offered lots of 1:1 time with different facilitators, although none of it made that much difference.)

* * *

At this point, in one of my desperate attempts to think myself out of my feelings pickle, I started thinking a lot about the [metaphor of the elephant and the rider](https://www.creativehuddle.co.uk/post/the-elephant-and-the-rider). The idea is that people usually identify with everything that happens in their mind, but this is actually a confusion; it’s more accurate to think of “you,” the conscious part, as a small human rider perched atop a large lumbering elephant that often does its own thing. The only things that are actually in the rider’s action space are things like “say words to the elephant,” “visualize mental images to the elephant,” “direct attention to a particular aspect of experience,” etc. Everything else—including all emotions and many thoughts—is the elephant’s job.

In this frame, the fact that the self-compassion meditation wasn’t working for me wasn’t really “my” fault. Whether the emotional experience of self-compassion happened was up to the elephant, and the only thing “I” (the rider) could do was to keep gently saying the words or doing the technique or whatever, and wait for the elephant to steer, at its own pace.

Around that point I decided to give up on the Jhourney curriculum and just do whatever seemed like it would feel nice.✻✻ Ironically, Jhourney more or less recommends doing this in various parts of the handbook, but I was too into following rules to follow the part of the rules where they told me to stop following the rules. I broke the rule of silence to ask for some reassurance from a close friend who was also on the retreat. When I got back to meditating, I made up a technique for myself where I imagined sending love and compassion to the elephant instead of to “myself.” This turned out to be a cheat code: I immediately felt an outpouring of warmth, and the reassuring things I was telling myself started to emotionally resonate.

I really wish I could describe better what happened after that, but unfortunately it was a while ago and involved a lot of subtle mental movements, plus it’s hard to track what’s happening while a chunk of your psyche is liquefying. I remember a sort of sense of “going inside” the feeling of compassion and love for the elephant, having it take up more and more of my attention, and feel more and more deep, rich and meaningful. Then some kind of rush of energy and a full-body frisson that I’ve learned often happens when my brain has rearranged itself.†† I don’t think this strictly qualifies as a jhana by Jhourney’s rubric, because it wasn’t stable and self-sustaining, but it otherwise felt close to how they describe first jhana.

When I got up and walked outside, I gradually noticed that the world felt pretty different. I realized that I’d always carried a “refrigerator hum” of subtle body tension that was now gone. Walking around the retreat center looking at the views felt amazing, like I could have spent days just melting into the landscape. The Lipton peppermint tea at the hot drink station tasted unreasonably good. I would sometimes tear up with joy or gratitude for no reason. I felt much deeper care and connection with my friends.

Maybe that makes it sound a little gooey and impersonal, like I took a drug that replaced my normal self with a puddle of love or something. The actual experience was the opposite; somehow it felt like this was all happening because it was _easier to be myself_. The care and connection wasn’t coming from some foreign love puddle; it came out because a bunch of psychic gunk had sloughed off and made it easier for the real essence of Ben-ness to shine through.

* * *

And then I ~used my newfound mastery of emotions and self-compassion to live happily ever after~ flailed around trying to integrate everything back into normal life.

One of the big changes I noticed after the retreat was that my emotions got 2–3× more intense. I think this was net positive in the end, but it made coming back to work hard—I had a stressful role managing a large team, and I also needed to catch up from a week of being incommunicado, which was extra stressful. And it wasn’t exactly helpful that suddenly, if someone was annoying on Slack, it would sometimes make me want to shoot flames out my ears.

Over the next couple weeks, the intensity settled back down and the changes in my moment-to-moment experience went maybe 80% of the way back to normal (e.g., I stopped spontaneously tearing up in normal circumstances). Some of them stayed, though:

-   A lot of my shame/cringe reflex turned into a self-compassion reflex. I used to get super embarrassed by minor mistakes (things like, I don’t know, forgetting to lock a public bathroom door and having someone walk in). Now those things come with a very clear sense of “gosh, what a normal-person type of mistake that millions of other people have also made, it probably didn’t even register for the other person.”
-   I became much more easily moved by art. I went from being moved to tears by a poem or piece of music maybe once a year to once a week.
-   I stayed much more aware of my more subtle emotional responses. I remember listening to some instrumental folk music I’d probably listened to 100 times before, suddenly noticing that the melody felt deeply nostalgic and then realizing that was two levels further out on the [feelings wheel](https://www.feelingswheel.com/) than I’d ever felt for music.
-   I still felt much less of a need to be distracted or stimulated than before. I used to listen to podcasts at 2× speed whenever I wasn’t fully focused on a task (e.g. if I was doing chores or walking). Now I have no desire to do this, and when I do listen to podcasts, 2× speed feels unpleasant. Similarly, I started to dislike a lot of restaurant food, and to really enjoy very simple home-cooked meals that I would have considered too bland before.
-   Friends continued to mention that my body language was different. One noted: “more upright posture, more open shoulders, more fluid and gentle.” Another described me as “‘leaving less,’ as in, spending less time ‘out of the room’ in interactions.”

These were great changes, but even better, a bunch of mindset and relational shifts also stuck around.

* * *

Mindset-wise, I guess I’d just say I got feelings-pilled.

If you’d asked me what the deal with emotions was before the retreat, I probably would have said something like:

> Positive emotions are nice, although you shouldn’t let them distract you too much from your long-term goals. Painful emotions are responses to stimuli that are part of our evolutionary inheritance, but are largely maladaptive today because we’re in an environment so different from where they arose. Assuming you have good enough self-control not to act out on them, their main effect is to make you dumber, which you should mitigate by not engaging with whatever makes you emotional until you’ve chilled out.

My new view is more like the following (this is still an oversimplified strawman and reality is a lot messier):

> All emotions are important and beautiful components of unconscious/intuitive cognition.‡‡ I do mean _all_, even ones that our culture really doesn’t like—for example, anger is a sign that something you care about is threatened and needs protecting, and fully welcoming it can help bring clarity about what exactly needs protecting and how. If you’re curious to learn more about the beautiful and constructive aspects of negative emotions, I recommend the book [The Language of Emotions](https://karlamclaren.com/the-language-of-emotions-book/), [Joe Hudson’s podcast](https://www.youtube.com/@ArtofAccomplishment/featured) (search for the emotion you’re interested in), or here are some specific articles summarizing his views on [anger](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/anger), [fear](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/fear), and [shame](https://aoa.zkf.io/topics/shame). However, they sometimes create impulses to do things that aren’t actually good ideas. In the process of socializing ourselves not to follow those impulses, we’ve been trained to try to make the emotions themselves go away in painful ways, by repressing them or clenching up around them. Unintuitively, repression and clenching actually cause the emotions to “stick around” in some sort of partially metabolized state, which has negative side-effects, like anxiety, thought-looping, numbness, blind spots, impaired reasoning, bringing up secondary emotions as defenses, etc. A different way of working with these emotions, that’s better for you in the long term, is to figure out how to welcome and accept them. This eventually leads to them feeling less bad, and giving you more impulses that are good ideas and fewer bad ones. But it’s not as simple as “just stop repressing.” Welcoming emotions requires fully feeling them; if they’re strong, that can be too overwhelming to work unless you have a deep felt sense of safety. That safety can be provided in many different ways, e.g. a therapist, friends, some kinds of meditation, etc. If you don’t have access to enough safety, repressing or clenching temporarily might be your best option.

The main thing that changed my view here was spending days at the retreat paying close attention to my own emotional experience. For example, near the beginning of the retreat, one of the facilitators claimed that thought-looping was usually a sign of a stuck or repressed emotion. I was really skeptical, but then I had several _very clear_ experiences of thought-looping around something, going off to a private place to cry about it, and immediately stopping the thought spiral and relating to it differently.§§ It was a lot easier to see this clearly on the retreat than in normal life: doing lots of meditation both made it easier to notice my own thought processes, and also faster to “unstick” or welcome the underlying emotions.

Similarly, I’d ended a long-term relationship right before the retreat, which I’d felt a lot of internal conflict and self-doubt about. My therapist had often told me things like “if you felt enough of your feelings you’d feel clear about this decision,” which sounded crazy to me at the time (uh, it’s a really hard call and there are a lot of tradeoffs that I don’t know how to weigh!). But on the last day of the retreat I noticed that I felt much clearer that a lot of the things I didn’t like about that relationship were real and important, and felt much more confident in my choice to end it.

Of course, I’d already heard plenty of people say things like “you can’t make this kind of decision logically, you have to listen to your heart,” but I never really understood what they were talking about. In retrospect, I just _didn’t have the ability_ to do the “listen to your heart” internal move, because [the pipes were clogged by too much repressed gunk](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/theres-trauma-and-then-theres-gnar). I wish those soundbites had included a second sentence like “of course listening to your heart can be much easier said than done, so if it feels hard or unclear for you, here are some first steps to get started with, and be prepared for it to take you many hours (or years) to figure out what your heart is trying to say.”

This new mindset really shifted the way I related to my emotions. While I still often experience them as painful, it’s much less than I used to, and now I start from the assumption that they’re not “actually” painful or bad, and that the best thing to do with them is to figure out how to accept them, welcome them, and find the wisdom or beauty in them. Overall this feels a lot better—when I run into something stressful or hard, instead of just flailing around not knowing what to do about it, it feels more like I have some kind of sense of direction or overall orientation, which is really comforting and grounding.

* * *

I also noticed that a lot of these ways of thinking about emotional difficulty within myself were also useful metaphors for thinking about emotional dynamics between people. Just like resisting your own emotions is a common coping strategy but ultimately doesn’t work very well, resisting _other people’s_ emotions is also a really common but damaging pattern, which I would guess is very involved in a lot of (most?) relational damage. For example, I realized that a big part of the root problem of the relationship that I had just ended was that both of us were missing the capacity to accept a bunch of each other’s emotions, which was something I hadn’t been able to articulate clearly at the time. I now think [practices explicitly focused on welcoming each other’s emotions](https://autotranslucence.wordpress.com/2019/05/07/emotional-waste-collection-the-care-and-feeding-of-relationship-intimacy/) are a really valuable tool for nurturing relationships over time.

In fact, I started to notice that a _lot_ of sticky problems—both in my personal life, but also the type that I ran into as a manager—were partly downstream of people resisting emotions of one type or another. Alice is hard to communicate with, because she frequently monologues about unimportant details, because she wants to prove she understands everything, because she has a bunch of stuck fear/resentment about not being taken seriously. Bob and Clara have a persistent disagreement about strategy, because Clara doesn’t care enough about Bob’s opinion to engage, because Bob was cruel to her once; Clara never complained because she’s avoiding the feeling of having been hurt by it. Dave’s team is underperforming but Erin, the exec sponsor, doesn’t want to manage him out, because she’s scared of the conflict and of making Dave’s reports, who like him, angry.

Of course, just because a problem is caused by stuck emotions doesn’t mean that unsticking them is the best way to solve it; but it was still a really useful lens to view what was going on.

* * *

The other shift that stuck around was in my relationships. I made a huge improvement in my friendships, which a few different things contributed to:

-   I felt a lot clearer about what I wanted from close friendships—mainly, a strong felt sense of safety and care—and that this felt like a big missing piece in my life.
-   I also started getting better at feeling those. With a lot of my shame reflex dissolved, I felt generally safer with people overall—my intuitive predictions about how they’d respond to things I did felt more positive. I also got a lot better at noticing and appreciating the different ways people expressed care for me.
-   Because I felt generally safer, I also got more willing to take risks or be vulnerable, which helped build closeness faster. A friend reflected that I seemed more courageous.

This also meant that my “friend type” changed—I cared more about emotional connection and less about intellectual connection. Luckily, even though I live in a really heady milieu, I was still able to find a lot of big-hearted people and gradually build up a set of close friends who are wonderful at both. So far, this is the change I’m the happiest about.

My dating life changed a bunch as well:

-   I started being able to feel much more strongly attracted to people. For example, later that year, I got a crush on someone that was stronger than any crush I’d had in high school.
-   I had a run of anxious-attachment-ish experiences, which I’d never had before, although they got way milder over time.‖‖ I’m not totally sure what was up with this; I think it partly had to do with calibrating to the new level of attraction I could feel.
-   The extra safety and courage I mentioned above were helpful in dating too: I got much more comfortable with communicating tricky things or being in weird situations (e.g. ones outside the standard script of “meet someone on an app, go on some dates, become exclusive”). A friend commented that it seemed like I had more degrees of freedom.

* * *

These changes have been really wonderful overall, but also kind of intense. Becoming much more emotionally aware solved a bunch of my problems, like wanting deeper friendships or not understanding why my relationship was unsatisfying. But it turns out winning that boss fight just made all the enemies level up. My old problems got replaced with horrible new problems, like that sometimes my newly deep friends and I would have conflicts, or that I would be more into the person I was dating than they were into me.

The part of my life where I feel the most mixed about the impact is work. About six months after my retreat, I got a pretty bad case of burnout and left my role managing a big team for a significantly less ambitious IC role. I don’t think the retreat was the main cause of my burnout—the main cause was that I didn’t have enough background in the area I was managing to have the type of impact that I wanted to, with a side helping of some difficult working relationships. But it turns out my new psyche had also gotten worse at repressing or avoiding emotion, and that made everything harder.

I think of repression as the emotional equivalent of [technical debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_debt) or [deferred maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deferred_maintenance): a way of avoiding an immediate cost, in this case feeling uncomfortable feelings, in exchange for paying a higher cost later to clean it up.¶¶ Like tech debt or deferred maintenance, sometimes repression or avoidance can be the right strategy, if you’ll have more resources to deal with the problem later! My role exposed me to a constant stream of low-grade painful things, like people being disappointed or annoyed at me, me being disappointed or annoyed at other people, projects not going well, office snacks being replaced with worse snacks, and so on. With less ability to avoid or repress, I had to feel more of them fully as they came up, and that was really draining. It also required enough time and attention that I had to defer a bunch of maintenance on my personal life.

Even now that I feel like my energy levels have mostly recovered, I’m still not sure whether I want to go back to the center of the vortex. It’s not just that I would have to start deferring maintenance again; I’m also much more aware of a lot of maintenance I’d _already_ deferred and really want to clean up—things like investing in friendships, like I mentioned above.

If I were only working toward my personal growth and happiness, I’d say this change was clearly good. My life feels much richer and more whole, and taking a less ambitious role forced me to release some layers of ego attachment to having a “shiny” job or career, which was deeply freeing. But… I also care a lot about my impact on the world, and I think [the next few years will probably be a critical period](https://ai-2027.com/research/timelines-forecast) where my work could be especially high-leverage. From that perspective, it’s sad to feel resistant to maxing out on work right now.

* * *

I feel pretty unsure about what to take away from all this. Here are some disconnected reflections.

-   I would never have said that I had a problem with self-compassion or self-esteem before doing this retreat, because I was able to say the right self-compassionate words when I directly asked myself things. But from my point of view afterwards, it’s clear that I did have a big self-compassion problem; even though I could say the words, I wasn’t really feeling them or acting like they were true. Having the actual embodied experience and habits of self-compassion is the closest I’ve come in real life to [The Vitamin](https://www.tumblr.com/caracalliope/730830836356775936/moreover-everyone-gathers-around-to-be).✻✻✻✻ “My deepest darkest fantasy is that I collapse on the street and I am rushed to the hospital. They perform a bunch of tests and find out I am severely deficient in some kind of vitamin. Then I start taking the vitamin and I become the happiest cleverest person alive because all my problems were caused by this one deficiency."
-   Jhourney is doing something really cool and is way better than most/all other meditation retreats on several axes (amount of support available, pedagogical excellence). That said, you probably shouldn’t expect to have _this_ life-changing of an experience; I would guess I had something like a 95th-percentile-plus outcome in terms of long-term effects. I still think it’s awesome as an introduction to both meditation and embodied emotional awareness.
-   I feel like I got really lucky on timing: if the retreat had ended one day earlier, I don’t think I would have gotten to the life-changing part. That freaks me out a lot.
-   At first I concluded from this that I should get super into meditation. Then I did two more retreats (another Jhourney retreat and a Dustin DiPerna introduction to mahamudra retreat) and neither one of them had nearly as large of an effect. The second Jhourney retreat was really helpful in reinforcing my daily practice, and I do think a daily meditation practice was helpful in stabilizing the changes that became permanent, but I’m not even totally sure about that.
-   Jhanas are definitely real. Also, it was kind of wild hearing somebody tell me something that sounded totally fake (“achieve bliss on demand using nothing but your brain”) and then back it up with an 80-page engineering manual and meticulous data on success rates. Overall this made me much more likely to believe that various other types of “woo” phenomena are real, at least in the sense that people are accurately describing their experiences. (I still believe the universe runs on physics, but a lot of woo things have physics-compatible explanations, especially if you buy that people’s intuitions can sometimes do spooky things.)
-   I described this as “chang\[ing\] my personality” in the title, but I don’t want to oversell the magnitude. I do have a lot of conversations where someone asks me “so how come you’re so <like X>” and I tell them “I did this weird meditation retreat a couple years ago.” But also, there are a lot of different aspects of personality, and even though a bunch of those completely changed for me, most of them are still the same, so I’m still very similar to my past self in an absolute sense.
-   What flags should tip you off that you should maybe be staring into your emotions more? Here are some things that I think might be clues (a lot of which described me, in retrospect):
    -   You often get stressed and stuck in thought loops
    -   You’re easily annoyed or frustrated
    -   You have too much scarcity mindset—you often worry this is the best job or relationship or whatever that you’ll ever have, in a way that’s not justified by the fundamentals
    -   You seek out a lot of distraction or stimulation; it’s not very enjoyable to just exist in the present
    -   You feel a lot of internal conflict or uncertainty about what you want
    -   There are parts of yourself that you really don’t like or wish were different
    -   You feel some missing depth, richness, or meaning in your life but can’t put your finger exactly on what

I’m also just left with a lot of questions:

-   Was this set of experiences… good?
    -   I’m really confident that it will be good for my own long-term life happiness. But the timing meant that I feel less willing to sign up for intense work during what might be one of the most critical periods for humanity, which feels bad even if the change was positive. I feel kind of unsure how to balance these.
    -   My shoulder [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham) says I should have just kept [playing push-pin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-pin_%5c%28game%5c%29#References_in_philosophy). My shoulder, uh… [deva?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_%5c%28Buddhism%5c%29) says if I follow my heart my impact on the world will be higher, even if it’s less predictable or legible. I don’t really feel like I buy either of these.
-   It seemed like before this I was in a personality [attractor basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor#Basins_of_attraction) with a lot of numbing and repression. How exactly did this happen, and more importantly, how can I/we avoid having it happen to as many people in the future?
    -   I have some sort of fuzzy leads on this (e.g. when I was young it was really easy to make me cry so I got picked on a lot in school), but not a particularly complete picture (e.g.: does this happen to everyone who has that experience? If not, what makes the difference?)
-   Mechanistically, what did the elephant meditation do? Why did it work so suddenly and create such a lasting shift? (Was that in fact what was responsible for most of the lasting shift? How much of it was instead about what I learned at a cognitive level?)
-   Is the glowy, “easier to be the essence of Ben” state that I ended up in right afterwards something that can be stabilized (or have aspects stabilized)? If so, how?
-   More generally—where do I go from here? I’m convinced that doing the right type of introspection or meditation type thing for a week can unlock amazing things, and it feels like there are more amazing things out there to unlock, but I wish I had a better way of navigating towards them than semi-randomly trying things.

* * *

A draft reader asked what I would tell my past self, or someone like me, about this arc. Ultimately, I’d boil it down to:

-   There are probably a bunch of feelings that you’re refusing to welcome into your experience, and this is probably involved in a bunch of the things that are unsatisfying about your life and relationships.

-   You can improve this by gradually, in a safe context, paying loving attention to the uncomfortable parts, from a place of wanting to know and care for yourself rather than to fix yourself.

-   When that’s hard, remember that it’s not your fault. You’re just the rider; elephants gonna elephant. Let it go where it needs to and keep loving it as best you can.
