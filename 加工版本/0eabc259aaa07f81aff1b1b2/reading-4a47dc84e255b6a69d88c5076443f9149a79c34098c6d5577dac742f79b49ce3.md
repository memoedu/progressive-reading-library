## 正文

_（这是_[_第 16 篇文章_](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation)_的续篇，我建议先读那一篇）_

那么，在[昨天的文章](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation)之后，我们知道 prioritisation _极其_重要，也已经找到了一些 goals。这篇文章将讨论如何着手 choosing actions，以便 best achieve 我们的 goals。这是一个_极其_困难的 problem——世界是 complex 且 uncertain 的，而你的 goals 也是 complex 的。这里没有能找到正确答案的 magic formula。而且，即使你知道那些 decisions 是正确的，真正_作出_它们在情感上也很困难——好的 prioritisation 将需要痛苦的 trade-offs，因为世界上精彩的事情实在太多，你不可能全都做。这是让我_非常_挣扎的一件事。不过，我希望能概述一些 tools、mindsets 和常见的 pitfalls，帮助大家起步。

### 做好 prioritise 的准备

第一个 bottleneck，是进入一种你_能够_思考 prioritisation，并真正 act upon 自己的 decisions 的 mental state。而这非常、非常困难！思考 trade-offs，意识到自己不可能做所有事情，意识到世界 fundamentally uncertain，这些都很困难。而你的 intuitions 不会仅仅因为你意识到它们在拖你的后腿，就自行 dissolve。几乎每个人都不擅长 prioritisation，这是有_原因_的。

我将概述一些自己常遇到的 emotional problems，以及我应对它们最有效的 tools——这些非常个人化，不会完美地 transfer！我鼓励你尝试它们，舍弃无效的，保留有效的，最终找到适合自己的 toolkit。

-   放弃一些事情_真的_很难。

    -   例如，我很容易落入这样一个陷阱：把自己的 self-image 定为一个勤奋的学生。这意味着我选了太多课程，有些过度劳累，而且在某种程度上知道自己把精力摊得太薄。但放弃一些事情就是_很难_。这让人感觉像是在认输，像是自己不够好。

        -   但对我来说，这显然是正确的 decision。每门课程都会消耗_大量_时间，使我错过本可以用这些时间做的其他事情，也无法更专注于其他课程。

        -   背后的 problem 是存在 [opportunity costs](https://www.econlib.org/library/Topics/College/opportunitycost.html)——为了多上一门课程，我失去了“这段时间里我本可以做的最好的事情”。但那门课程_让人觉得_ salient，而那个“best possible alternative”却让人觉得 abstract。而 abstract 的事情在情感上没那么有吸引力

            -   这就是 [illusion of doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing)

    -   但“abstract”与“concrete”的区别存在于[我的 mind 中，而不是世界中](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)。所以，对此的 solution 是**让那个“best possible alternative”变得 concrete**

        -   在这个例子中，我可以 track 自己的 time，弄清这门课程花了我多长时间，然后_确切地_ plan out 我将用这些时间做什么。我可以推进的 Projects，可以见的朋友，可以用来放松的时间，可以用来 consolidate 其他课程的时间。这就 dissolves 了这种 asymmetry，让 choice 显得清楚得多！

            -   对于 time tracking，我强烈推荐使用 [Toggl](https://toggl.com/)

-   你不可能做所有事情

    -   一个相关的 problem 是：intuitively，所有事情似乎都应该_很容易_。我不觉得自己_需要_放弃一个 project，因为放弃很痛苦！而且，我肯定可以把所有事情都做完吧？

        -   注意：这个 problem _可能_更偏向我个人的情况——我在别人身上较少见到它

        -   当一个 deadline 即将到来时，我就会注意到这一点——我为此感到 stressed，但这很少 translates into actions，因为我总是_觉得_自己能直接把事情做完

    -   这显然是 empirically false。因此，solution 就是让我的 intuitions 相信这一点

        -   对此，我找到的最佳 solution，是练习为自己作出 accurate time estimates

            -   目前，我会 estimate 自己在每个 [pomodoro](https://francescocirillo.com/pages/pomodoro-technique)、每天以及每周能完成什么，并在每个时段结束时，根据完成得如何进行 calibrate

                -   这_不是_一个 accountability device，它只是为了 track 我的 progress + 更好地 calibrate 我的 intuitions——我经常觉得自己度过了一段非常 focused 的 session，却只 achieved 了 goal 的一半——这说明那个 goal 不好。

        -   我现在依然很不擅长这件事。但由此带来的 tight feedback loops、查看 base rates 的能力以及 calibration，让我的 intuitions 好了_很多_，也让我觉得这件事 salient 得多。

    -   这就是我的 perfectionism 背后的根本缺陷——让工作处于 unfinished 和 unpolished 的状态，会让我觉得很 _salient_，而我花在 polishing 上的时间，却并不让人觉得真的有 cost。

        -   对博客文章进行 Tracking time 在这方面有所帮助——我花了 > 6 hours 编辑自己的[第一篇博客文章](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GLPaZamxqkx7XJbXv/the-skill-of-noticing-emotions)，当时觉得没什么问题。但我现在知道，那段时间里我_本可以_从头写出 3-4 篇粗糙一些的文章——opportunity costs 是真实存在的！

-   放弃一些事情

    -   prioritisation 的一项 key skill，是在获得新的 information 时 **changing my mind**——愿意 change 我的 actions，并在先前的事情不再是我的 highest priority 时放弃它们。而这很难！

    -   这经常表现为 [sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost#Fallacy_effect)——当你_已经_向一个 option invested 大量 time and effort 时，你会对它产生 attachment。即使那个 task 不应再是你的 highest priority，放弃它还是会让你感到_痛苦_。

        -   对我而言，背后的 error 是，已经 invested 的 time and effort _目前_并不让人觉得是 cost，但如果我放弃这个 task，就会有这种感觉——这纯粹是 mental accounting 的一个事实，却让放弃的 decision _感觉_具有比实际高得多的 cost。

    -   注意，你不应该 ignore sunk costs！如果你已经向一个 project invested 很多，那么 finish 它所需的 additional effort 就少得多。而问题是：“given 我现在知道的事情，finishing 这件事的 additional benefit，是否 worth 它所需的 additional effort？”

        -   此外还有 switching costs——你_应该_对过于轻易地放弃保持一定的 biased away。我只是认为，大多数人（包括我！）都在这个方向上偏得太远了/

    -   对我而言，这经常表现为 **completionism**——让某件事感觉 _finished_，有一种强大的吸引力。然而，我做一个 task 往往是为了从中 learn，而不是为了 results。我写文章的原因，至少有一半是为了 clarify 我的 ideas。我做 problem sheets，是为了更好地 understand 那些 concepts。而且，我往往在 process 的开始，而不是结束时，就获得了其中的大部分 benefit，但我仍然对 completion 这个 idea 有强烈的 attachment

    -   对此，我主要的 solution 是那个略显傻气的“teleporting alien” thought experiment。让自己与当前的处境拉开距离，想象自己是一个刚刚 teleported 进你身体的 alien mind。它拥有你的全部 resources 和全部 goals，却对你先前的 actions 没有 attachment——毕竟，那些事是别人做的，所以你不能责怪这个外星人！现在，什么才像是正确的 decision？

-   为它留出时间

    -   Prioritisation 从根本上说，就是用 time 换取 _information_——关于你的 goals、未来的 actions 和 priorities 的 information。这很难，因为 information 是 abstract 的

    -   这使得 prioritise prioritisation 很困难——专门为它花时间让人觉得不对！

        -   注意，这显然大错特错——能力极强又忙碌的人，依然会为 prioritisation 留出时间。它_确实_有价值。

        -   Undervaluing abstract 的事物，是一种_极其_常见的 human bias；在这个领域，你的 intuitions 并不是好的指引。

    -   如果你_已经_感到忙碌且 overwhelmed，这就尤其隐蔽——如果你正忙于重要的 tasks，zoom out 并妥善 plan 和 prioritise，可能_更加_重要，但它却_让人觉得_没有必要，而且不对。

    -   我发现，自己很难在情感层面克服这一点。我的主要 solution，是在 schedule 中划出固定时间，用来 reviewing 我的一周并进行 prioritising，同时确保这段时间让自己觉得**神圣不可侵犯**——无论短期内偏离这一 routine 显得多么像个好主意，我都不会这样做。

        -   让这件事不再感觉像是一个 decision，就没那么痛苦了。而且，在 meta level 上，我确信这是正确的 decision。

-   [兴奋起来吧！](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)

    -   谈了这么多 prioritisation 的困难之后，很容易落入 guilt 和 obligation 的陷阱。Prioritisation 让人觉得像是一件你_必须_做的事情。一件令人不快的苦差事，妨碍你真正享受生活。

    -   然而，prioritisation 从根本上说，是为了 achieving 你的 core values。在你可能遇到的所有事情中，它与你的 external obligation 相距_最远_。Prioritisation 是找出自己身上最拖后腿的部分，并给予自己 overcome 它们的力量

    -   而且，如果你能真正 internalise：prioritisation _棒极了_，那会比试图直接处理任何一种 resistance 都有效得多

    -   我觉得有用的一个小技巧：我很容易把人生中的_大量_时间花在那些感觉 urgent、却不能让我更接近 long term goals 的事情上。回想一下你的上一周，或上个月。你做过多少当时觉得重要、现在却已经不太在乎的事情？花了多少时间回复邮件，做不动脑子的 busywork，或毫无意义的行政杂务？对于一年前做过的事情，现在还有多少件让你感到兴奋？

        -   对我而言——而且我想，对大多数人也一样——这个数字远低于它本可以达到的水平。而**这简直令人愤慨**。我想尽可能多地花时间，去做那些让我感到快乐而充实的事情。而 internalising prioritisation 的 ideas，就是朝这个方向迈出的第一步。

### 如何 prioritise

所以，即使你现在已经对 prioritisation 充满干劲，心中也有了自己的 goals，究竟该如何开始仍然很不清楚。而且 prioritisation 是 _hard_ 的。没有什么神奇的 formula，也没有哪个单一的诀窍能 solve 这个 problem。我们生活在一个根本上 uncertain 的世界里，需要学会应对这一点。而且不同的情况需要不同的 tools。不过，以下是我发现最有用的 tools：

-   保持 grounded

    -   在 prioritisation 中，最容易犯的 mistakes 之一，就是没能 grounded 于你的 terminal goals。至于具体_如何_做到这一点，有许多细微之处，但第一步是问自己：“我正在做的事，是否在 bringing me closer to 我的 true goals？”

    -   如果没有，那这就是一件**大事**。要 achieving 你的 goals，第一步就是去_尝试_。

    -   一个有用的 experiment：在接下来的一周进行_大量的_ time tracking——使用像 [Toggl](https://toggl.com/) 这样的 tool，记录你实际上如何花费时间。到一周结束时，根据每段时间帮助你 work towards 的 high-level goal（如果有的话），为所有这些时间加上 label。那个 number 有你希望的那么 high 吗？

    -   Back-chaining 是做好这件事，并保持 grounded 于你的 true goals 的一个关键 tool

-   关注 scale

    -   Prioritisation 是 hard 的，但有些 decisions 是 obvious 的，即使感觉并非如此。如果你注意到生活中某件自己考虑得不够的事，就做一个 rough estimate，看看 fixing 它可能有多 valuable

        -   如果你以前从未尝试过，[Fermi Estimates](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PsEppdvgRisz5xAHG/fermi-estimates) 是一项值得练习的 skill

    -   例如，我最近一直在尝试营造一个良好的 home office environment——一把好椅子、standing desk 等等。因为我做了一个 rough estimate，估计自己愿意花多少钱来 avoid 日后的 lower back pain，以及 developing 它的 probability，而这_显然_是我应该 prioritise 的事

        -   类似的 reasoning 也 applies 于 exercise、sleep、nutrition，以及对 friendships 和 relationships 的 investing 等等

    -   同样，我应该花_大量_时间 optimising 那些自己会长期做的事，例如 career choice。想想那个 decision 会 impact 你一生中的多少小时

    -   Humans 不擅长 long-term thinking，而且我们缺乏[对 large numbers 的良好 intuition](https://conceptually.org/concepts/scope-insensitivity)。但如果你理解这一点，就能学会何时 overrule 自己的 intuitions，从而做得更好

    -   同样，也别为小事操心！Prioritisation 需要时间和精力，它是实实在在的 cognitive labour。如果你做了 estimate，发现一件事的重要程度很小，那它就不值得你花时间。

-   Hamming Problems（汉明问题）

    -   另一种思考 scale 的方式，是寻找 **Hamming Problems**——你生活中最大的 problems，或 bottlenecks。

        -   这是一份[有用的 worksheet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ZKxHmcb6FUm_2mf1EYEgQoiH6r0AXXMbF82hsvOrAI/edit?usp=sharing)，可以帮助你想清楚自己的 Hamming problems

    -   通常，只要 identifying 它们，就能_明显_看出它们应该成为 priorities，因为它们 underlie 其他一切

-   开始行动

    -   人很容易不堪重负，并因 uncertainty 和 indecision 而感到 paralysed。这是可以理解的，但往往会让你极大地 _over_estimate 其 difficulty，还没开始就放弃了

    -   我发现，设一个 5 分钟的 timer，要求自己花满这 5 分钟来 thinking through 这个 problem，可能很有用——有时我在这段时间里就能 solve 它，而且几乎总能开始行动，感觉也没那么困惑。即使毫无进展，我也只损失了 5 分钟。但先迈出一小步，真正_开始_，是判断 problem 究竟有多 hard 的关键。

    -   通常，先花 5 分钟 brainstorming 可能的 options 是个好办法——我们_实在_太容易 limit 自己的 thinking，漏掉重要的 options，甚至根本不去想有哪些 options！

-   对事情进行系统的思考，做到 Think things through systematically

    -   Prioritisation 是 hard 的，其中很大一部分最终要依靠你的 intuitions。我们很容易没有好好 think through 一个 problem，从而漏掉许多重要 details

    -   一个 solution，是故意做一份很烂的 cost-benefit analysis：

        -   制作一个 table，为每个 option 设置一个 row，为每个重要的 factor 设置一个 column。

        -   逐一 fill out 每个 cell，例如以 5 为满分进行 rating。

        -   然后，根据每个 factor 的重要程度，为它赋予一个 coefficient，取 total，再 sort！

        -   现在，既然这个 process 已经给了我一个 answer，就把这一切全部扔掉，听从我_新的_ gut feel

    -   我很喜欢这种 approach——它给了你具体可做的事，从而 resolves 令人 paralysing 的 uncertainty。而在结束时，我已经被迫 systematically 地 think through 不同的 examples，以及所有重要的 factors。这通常会指出我之前遗漏的东西，也意味着我的 intuition 现在已经得到了好得多的 calibrated。

-   寻求建议

    -   Prioritisation 是 _hard_ 的。而 hard problems 的一个 efficient solution，就是把部分 cognitive work outsource 给别人！

    -   寻找 mentors，以及生活经验更丰富的人，问问他们的想法

        -   也要问问常见的 mistakes，以及 prioritise 的_失败_经历！

        -   尝试[获得他们的 _models_](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations)，而不仅仅是他们的 preferences 和 opinions。问题是_我_应该做什么，而不是_他们_会做什么。mentors 也很容易犯 [Typical Mind Fallacy](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense)

    -   仅仅和朋友聊聊，就可能非常有用——让你的 thoughts 变得 concrete，并 weighing up 其中的 pros and cons，就已经完成了 prioritisation 中的许多工作。让他们帮你做 sanity check，并指出 blind spots。

        -   有些人对此会感到很大的 social resistance——我发现，只要你明确地说“我正为一个 decision 犯难，你能帮我 clarify 我的 thoughts 吗”，大多数朋友都会乐于帮忙。如果你也能为他们提供这样的帮助，那双方都能受益！

-   把 thoughts 从脑海中拿出来

    -   人很容易陷入模糊的 over-thinking 漩涡，却始终毫无进展。把 thoughts 从脑海中拿出来是 valuable 的，而且有助于显著 clarify 事情。和朋友一起把事情聊清楚，很大一部分 value 就在这里！

        -   只需把你的 thoughts 写在纸上，或对着一只 [rubber duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_duck_debugging) 说话，通常也能 replicated 这种效果

    -   一个主要的 failure mode，是没能做到 concrete。挑出具体的 options，_确切地_ explore 你认为它们会是什么样，以及会有什么 consequences，可能很有帮助。在多得令人意外的情况下，这会 highlight 关键的 uncertainties，或让你发现某个 option _显然_很愚蠢／很棒。

-   进行 Explore，探索各种可能

    -   通常，最好的 prioritise 方式就是去尝试！走出去，gain information，做 experiments

    -   不要只是盲目地这样做——弄清楚你的关键 uncertainties，并把 exploration 集中在它们上面

    -   Exploration 需要时间和精力——寻求建议，了解做哪些事最能为你带来 value。和做过类似事情的人聊聊，问问他们从中学到了什么

### 常见 Mistakes

Prioritisation 说起来远比做起来容易，因此下面是我见过的一些最常见的 mistakes，以及如何 overcome 它们的一些想法：

-   没有 making progress

    -   你很容易_以为_自己在 prioritising，实际上却只是在原地打转。花很长时间，为同样的 information 和同样的 choices 反复苦恼。花几个小时 researching，实际上却没有获得任何 clarity。

    -   这就是 [pressing the Try Harder button](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-6-stop-pressing-the-try-harder-button)——prioritisation 的 goal 是**获得更多关于你的 final decision 应该是什么的 information**，而不是走过场、为此 invest effort。如果 investing effort 没有让你获得更多 clarity，那么这就没有在 achieving 你的 goals

    -   当然，要把这种情况与 problem 本身确实 difficult、需要 time 和 effort 的情况 distinguish 开来，也很难！

    -   对此，我主要的 solution 是确保你始终 grounded，identify 你的 key uncertainties，并 focus 在这些 uncertainties 上

        -   你很容易钻进牛角尖，却没有意识到。对此，一个不错的 solution 是安排定期的 check in times，在这些时候 zoom out，summarise 你刚刚做过的事情，并问问自己，这是否正在让你更接近自己的 goals。

    -   定期写下你当前 thoughts 的 state，以及你对正确 action 的 best guess。如果你写下的东西不再 changing，那么你很可能已经不再 making progress 了！

-   对 difficulty 的 Under-estimating

    -   Prioritisation 是 _hard_。它是真正的 cognitive labour。你很容易灰心，觉得自己是个笨蛋，或者漏掉了什么显而易见的东西。而这_极其_无益

        -   我发现，记住 insecurities [是在 map 中，而“我能不能做得更好”是关于 territory 的 question](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)，会很有帮助

    -   但 humans 会 systematically under-estimate information 的 value，以及想到一个 idea 曾经有多 difficult，因此在这件事上，你的 intuitions 并不是可靠的指引。

-   对 alternate options 的 considering 不够充分

    -   你_极其_容易对特定的 options 和 approaches 产生 attachment，而 think 得不够 widely。或者，凡是超出一个很小的 options 的 set 的东西，都会让你觉得_奇怪_，轻易就被 dismissed。

    -   这常常 manifests 为对自己 self-image 的 attachment。

        -   例如，identifying 自己是一个 mathematician，_只能_做 pure maths，而且必须成为 academic。于是便不 considering 这以外的 paths，即使它们可能让你快乐得多

        -   例如，identifying 自己是“不擅长结识新朋友的人”，于是便不 considering 那些会让你走出 comfort zone 的 options，尽管这是可以 learned 的 skill

    -   同样，你很容易拥有 cached thoughts。比如，你在 18 岁时就决定要读 PhD，那时却完全不知道 academia 实际上是什么样子。随后，即使 evidence to the contrary 随着时间慢慢 accumulated，你仍然对那些 cached thoughts 产生 attachment，而不 reconsidering 它们。

-   将 prioritising 与 doing 进行 Decouple

    -   另一个陷阱是不断地_在当下_陷入 paralysed 的状态。我非常渴望 certainty 和 confidence。但这基本上不可能 achieve。

    -   如果我在当下不断为自己的 actions 是否 optimal 而苦恼，那我就永远什么也做不成。而_这_就成了最阻碍我的事情。

    -   solution 是认识到，**prioritising** 和 **doing** 属于我 mind 的不同 parts，需要不同的 modes。当你痛苦地意识到 uncertainty，又为这是否是自己的 best option 而苦恼时，就很难去 _do_ 事情。而且，你很容易 neglect switching costs，不断在不同事情之间来回跳转

    -   实际中，我会对自己 prioritising 和 thinking 的时段进行 time-box，而在生活中的其余时间

        -   我发现，goals 足够 intricate，因此按 layers of abstraction 来 think 很有帮助（下面的 process 对我很有效，对你而言效果可能不同）：

            -   在 weekly review 中，我可以 think 自己的 high-level goals，以及哪些 projects 会让我更接近它。在这些 review 之间，我想做的只是坚持自己想出的 project ideas。（我在考虑把它改为每月一次，或每季度一次——确切的 time periods 很难确定）

                -   这很难，因为如果出现了一些真正新的 information，而且它们_应该_ change 我的 priorities，我也希望对此作出回应！身处其中时，很难 balance，也很难 tell the difference。Nate Soares 对这一点的表述[比我好得多](http://mindingourway.com/deliberate-once/)

            -   每天开始时，我都会 plan 当天的安排。我_知道_自己正在做哪些 projects，所以当下我 thinking through 的是如何 implement 它们，以及如何 allocate 我的 time，而不会 questioning 这些 projects 是否是我的 best projects。如果没有出现新的 information，那么我此时的 thoughts 就会 strictly worse 于那些 prioritisation 时段里的 thoughts

                -   有时候，我_应该_加入一个新的 project——这需要细致的 balance，也需要 practice，我在这方面还远远称不上完美

            -   然后到了当下，我就只是尽量 follow 这个 plan

    -   根本的要点是，在当下 prioritising 时，很容易无法 make progress。你需要对它进行 time-box，在这些时段里把它做_好_，而在生活中的其余时间只需 follow 这个 plan

-   以为自己能做所有事情

    -   prioritisation 的一项 _key_ skill，是认识到你不可能做所有事情。Tasks、projects 和 opportunities 的 accumulate 速度，远远超过你能够完成它们的速度。但放弃事情很痛苦，你很容易以为自己能做所有事情。而且，如果你_反正_都要做这项 task，那现在不妨继续做这项有趣又诱人的 task！

    -   一个有用的 reframing：你_不可能_做所有事情，必须放弃一些事情。Implicitly，你会放弃那些自己没能抽出时间去做的 task。这等于把 prioritisation 的_全部_ power 都交给 default algorithm 和 random chance。不存在一个你什么都不用放弃的 world，你只是在 choice 究竟放弃_什么_

-   Short-term bias（短期偏向）

    -   你极其容易陷入毫无意义的 busywork 的循环，做各种各样最终在 long-term 不会重要的事情。也就是那些感觉 urgent、却并不 important 的 tasks。

    -   Humans 有显著的 short-term bias，因此 by default，你确实会在 short-term 的事情上花太多 time，而在自己的 long-term goals 上花的 time 不够

    -   实际中，这 manifests 为对放弃任何事情都非常犹豫。但当你回顾过去一个月完成的事情时，却没有多少让你真正感到满意的。

    -   这相当难 solve，而且很大程度上取决于你的个人处境，以及你拥有多少 autonomy。但如果你能 identify 那些让你觉得 unimportant、却在 consume 你的 time 的 tasks，就值得真正花些 effort，thinking 如何 reduce 这种情况。

    -   一个有用的 mindset 是：“从现在起 5 年后，我会为自己完成了哪些 tasks 而感到高兴？”找到它们，然后去做。

        -   一个不错的起点是，每周对一段 time 进行 time-box，这段时间_只能_用于朝 long-term goals 努力

-   没有 prioritising 自己如何 prioritise

    -   一个容易掉入的陷阱，是对生活的_每一个_部分都 over-optimise。对任何一点点 wasted motion 都耿耿于怀，为生活的每个细节都陷入焦虑。

    -   这里的 key lesson 是，**humans 是 boundedly rational agents**。Thinking 和 prioritising 需要 time 和 effort，只有当这笔 time 和 effort 的 expenditure 能随着时间充分 increase 你的 efficiency，足以 outweigh 它本身时，这样做才值得。

    -   这就是为什么 consider problem 的 _scale_ 很重要——focus 在_大_事情上，不要为小事劳神。

    -   我 conjecture，喜欢我博客的这类人，_尤其_容易掉入这个陷阱。

### 结语（Conclusion）

希望我已经让你相信，prioritisation 是_极其_重要的，也给了你一些不错的起点！这是一项 difficult 而 fuzzy 的 skill，需要 balance 的事情很多。但我认为，它是你能学到的最重要的 life skills 之一，而且你可以通过 practice 来 improve。

要在 prioritisation 上做到 perfect 很难，但这里也有一些 low-hanging fruit。有些事情_显然_值得 prioritising，但我们很少做得足够。对于大多数人来说，以下是一些最明显、也_确实_重要的事情：

-   睡眠（Sleep）

-   运动（Exercise）

-   营养（Nutrition）

-   心理健康（Mental health）- 足够的 rest、relaxation、therapy、emotionally supportive environments，以及 minimising stress

-   身体健康（Physical health）- 良好的 work environment、following medical advice，以及对有疑问的身体状况进行检查

-   家庭（Family）

-   友谊（Friendships）

-   事业（Careers）

-   休息与放松（Rest and relaxation）

-   恋爱（Romance）

-   学习（Learning）

-   个人发展（Personal development），以及元技能（meta-skills）

-   兴趣爱好（Hobbies）/你如何度过闲暇时间

对于这份清单上的每一项，你都满意自己生活中的现状吗？如果不满意，那个 gap 对你来说是否像是一项 priority？而今后，你具体能怎样更好地 prioritise 它？

## 原文

_(This is a continuation of_ [_post 16_](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation)_, I recommend reading that one first)_

So, after [yesterday’s post](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation), we know that prioritisation is _incredibly_ important, and we’ve found some goals. This post is going to be about how to approach choosing actions that best achieve our goals. This is an _incredibly_ hard problem - the world is complex and uncertain, and your goals are complex. There is no magic formula for finding the right answer here. And it’s emotionally hard to _make_ the decisions, even if you know they’re the right call - good prioritisation will require painful trade-offs, because the world is full of far too many awesome things and you can’t do all of them. This is something I _majorly_ struggle with. But I hope to be able to outline some tools, mindsets and common pitfalls to help get started.

### Being prepared to prioritise

The first bottleneck is getting into the mental state where you _can_ think about prioritisation and genuinely act upon your decisions. And this is super, super hard! Thinking about trade-offs, realising that you can’t do everything, realising that the world is fundamentally uncertain, are all difficult. And your intuitions don’t dissolve just because you realise they’re holding you back. There’s a _reason_ that almost everyone is terrible at prioritisation.

I’m going to outline some of the common emotional problems I run into, and my best tools for dealing with them - this is highly personal, and will not perfectly transfer! I encourage you to try these, discard what doesn’t work and keep what does, and ultimately find a toolkit that works for you.

-   It’s _really_ hard to give up on things.

    -   For example, I easily fall into the trap of having a self-image of a dedicated student. This means I take too many courses, and am a bit over-worked, and know on some level that I’m stretching myself thin. But it’s _hard_ to drop something. It feels like giving up, not being good enough.

        -   But this is clearly the right decision for me. Each course consumes a _lot_ of time, and I miss out on other things I could be doing with that time, and on the ability to focus more on other courses.

        -   The underlying problem is that there are [opportunity costs](https://www.econlib.org/library/Topics/College/opportunitycost.html) - I am losing the “best possible thing I could be doing with this time” in exchange for an extra course. But the course _feels_ salient, while the “best possible alternative” feels abstract. And abstract things are less emotionally compelling

            -   This is the [illusion of doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing)

    -   But “abstract” vs “concrete” lives in [my mind, not in the world](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory). So the solution to this is to **make the “best possible alternative” concrete**

        -   In this example, I can track my time to figure out how long the course is taking me, and then plan out _exactly_ what I’ll do with that time. Projects I can pursue, friends I can see, time I can take to relax, time to consolidate other courses. This dissolves the asymmetry, and makes the choice feel far more obvious!

            -   For time tracking, I highly recommend using [Toggl](https://toggl.com/)

-   You can’t do everything

    -   A related problem - intuitively, everything feels like it should be _easy_. It doesn’t feel like I _need_ to give up a project, because giving up is painful! And surely I can just do everything?

        -   Note: This problem _may_ be more specific to me - I see this less often in others

        -   I notice this when I have an upcoming deadline - I feel stressed about it, but this rarely translates into actions, because it always _feels_ like I can just do things

    -   This is, clearly, empirically false. And so the solution is to convince my intuitions of this

        -   The best solution I’ve found to this is to practice making accurate time estimates for myself

            -   I currently estimate what I can get done in each [pomodoro](https://francescocirillo.com/pages/pomodoro-technique), each day, and each week, and calibrate at the end of each how well it went

                -   This is _not_ an accountability device, it’s solely to track my progress + calibrate my intuitions better - I often feel like I had a super focused session, but only achieved half of my goal - this shows the goal was bad.

        -   I’m still pretty awful at this. But the resulting tight feedback loops, ability to look at base rates and calibration have given me _much_ better intuitions, and made this feel much more salient to me.

    -   This is the underlying fault beneath my perfectionism - it feels _salient_ to leave work unfinished and unpolished, and the time I spend on that polishing doesn’t feel like it really has cost.

        -   Tracking time for blog posts has helped for this - I spent > 6 hours editing my [first blog post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GLPaZamxqkx7XJbXv/the-skill-of-noticing-emotions), and this felt fine at the time. But I now know that I _could_ have written 3-4 rougher posts from scratch in that time - opportunity costs are real!

-   Giving up on things

    -   A key skill of prioritisation is **changing my mind** when I get new information - being willing to change my actions, and give up on previous things if they stop being my highest priority. And this is hard!

    -   This often manifests as the [sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost#Fallacy_effect) - when you’ve _already_ invested a bunch of time and effort into an option, you feel attached to that. Even if that task should no longer be your highest priority, it feels _painful_ to give up on it.

        -   The underlying error, for me, is that the invested time and effort doesn’t _currently_ feel like a cost, but will if I give up on the task - this is purely a fact of mental accounting, but makes the decision to give up _feel_ much higher cost than it is.

    -   Note that you shouldn’t ignore sunk costs! If you’re invested a lot into a project, it takes much less additional effort to finish it. And the question is, “is the additional benefit of finishing this worth the additional effort it’ll take, given what I now know?”

        -   There are also switching costs - you _should_ be biased away from giving up too easily. I just think most people (including me!) err too far this way/

    -   For me, this often manifests as **completionism** - there’s a strong draw to having something feel _finished_. Yet I’m often doing a task in order to learn from it, not for the results. At least half of why I write posts is to clarify my ideas. I do problem sheets to understand the concepts better. And I often get most of this benefit at the start of the process, rather than the end, but I feel a strong attachment to the idea of completion

    -   The main solution I have to this is the slightly silly “teleporting alien” thought experiment. Distance yourself from your current situation, and imagine that you’re an alien mind, that’s just teleported into your body. It has all of your resources, and all of your goals, but no attachment to your previous actions - after all, somebody else did those, so you can’t blame the alien! What seems like the right decision now?

-   Making time for it

    -   Prioritisation is, fundamentally, about trading time for _information_ - information about your goals, future actions and priorities. This is hard, because information is abstract

    -   This makes it difficult to prioritise prioritisation - it feels wrong to take time for it!

        -   Note that this is very obviously wrong - extremely competent and busy people still make time for prioritisation. It _is_ valuable.

        -   Undervaluing abstract things is a _super_ common human bias, this is not an area where your intuitions are a good guide.

    -   This is especially insidious if you _already_ feel busy and overwhelmed - it might be _more_ important to zoom out and properly plan and prioritise, if you’re busy with important tasks, but it _feels_ unnecessary and wrong.

    -   I find it hard to get over this on an emotional level. My main solution is to carve out a regular time in my schedule for reviewing my week and prioritising, and ensuring this feels **sacrosanct** - I will not deviate from this routine, no matter how much of a good idea this seems in the short term.

        -   By making this no longer feel like a decision, it’s less painful. And on a meta level, I’m confident this is the right decision.

-   [Be excited!](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)

    -   After all this talk about the difficulty of prioritisation, it’s easy to fall into the trap of guilt and obligation. Prioritisation feels like a thing you _need_ to do. And unpleasant chore that holds you back from really enjoying life.

    -   Yet, prioritisation is fundamentally about achieving your core values. It is the thing _furthest_ from an external obligation that you could ever encounter. Prioritisation is about finding the parts of yourself that most hold you back, and giving yourself the strength to overcome them

    -   And if you can really internalise that prioritisation is _awesome_, that will go far further than trying to tackle any of the resistances directly

    -   A trick I find useful: It’s easy to spend a _lot_ of my life doing things that feel urgent, but don’t bring me closer to my long term goals. Think back on your last week, or month. How many things did you do that felt important, but you no longer really care about? How much time responding to emails, doing mindless busywork, or pointless admin? How many of the things you did a year ago do you now feel hyped about?

        -   For me, and I think most people, this number is a lot lower than it could be. And **this is outrageous**. I want to spend as much time as I possibly can doing things that make me feel happy and fulfilled. And internalising the ideas of prioritisation is the first step in this direction.

### How to prioritise

So, even if you’re now fully pumped about prioritisation, and have your goals in mind, it’s pretty unclear how to get started. And prioritisation is _hard_. There’s no magic formula, or single trick that will solve the problem. We live in a fundamentally uncertain world, and need to learn to deal with this. And different situations will require different tools. But here are the tools I’ve found most useful:

-   Be grounded

    -   One of the easiest mistakes to make with prioritisation is to fail to be grounded in your terminal goals. There is a lot of nuance with _how_ to do this, but the first step is to ask yourself “is what I’m doing bringing me closer to my true goals?”

    -   And if it isn’t, this is a **big deal**. The first step to achieving your goals is to _try_.

    -   A useful experiment: Do _heavy_ time tracking for the next week - use a tool like [Toggl](https://toggl.com/) and keep track of how you actually spend your time. And at the end of the week, label all of that time according to which high-level goal (if any) that time was helping you work towards. Is that number as high as you want it to be?

    -   Back-chaining is a key tool for doing this well, and remaining grounded in your true goals

-   Look at scale

    -   Prioritisation is hard, but some decisions are obvious, even if they don’t feel that way. If you notice something in your life that you don’t think about enough, do a rough estimate of how valuable fixing that could be

        -   If you’ve never tried before, [Fermi Estimates](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PsEppdvgRisz5xAHG/fermi-estimates) are a valuable skill to practice

    -   For example - I’ve recently been experimenting with having a good home office environment - a good chair, standing desk, etc. Because I did a rough estimate of how much I’d pay to avoid lower back pain later in life, and the probability of developing it, and this was _obviously_ a thing I should prioritise

        -   Similar reasoning applies to exercise, sleep, nutrition, investing in friendships and relationships, etc

    -   Similarly, I should spend a _lot_ of time optimising things I’ll do for a long time, like career choice. Think about how many hours of your life that decision will impact

    -   Humans are bad at long-term thinking, and we lack a [good intuition for large numbers](https://conceptually.org/concepts/scope-insensitivity). But if you understand that, you can learn when to overrule your intuitions and do better

    -   Equally, don’t sweat the small stuff! Prioritisation takes time and effort, it’s genuine cognitive labour. If you do an estimate of how big a deal it is, and it’s pretty small, it’s not worth your time.

-   Hamming Problems

    -   Another way to think about scale is by searching for **Hamming Problems** - the biggest problems, or bottlenecks in your life.

        -   This is a [useful worksheet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ZKxHmcb6FUm_2mf1EYEgQoiH6r0AXXMbF82hsvOrAI/edit?usp=sharing) for thinking through your Hamming problems

    -   Often identifying these can make it _obvious_ that they should be priorities, because they underlie everything else

-   Getting started

    -   It’s easy to be overwhelmed, and feel paralysed by uncertainty and indecision. This is reasonable, but can often mean you wildly _over_estimate the difficulty, and give up before you’ve begun

    -   I find it can be useful to set a 5 minute timer, and to make myself spend the full 5 minutes thinking through the problem - sometimes I solve it in this time, I almost always get started and feel less confused. And even if I get nowhere, I’ve only lost 5 minutes. But taking a small step to get _started_ is key to telling how hard the problem really is.

    -   It’s often good to start by brainstorming possible options for 5 minutes - it’s _so_ easy to limit your thinking, and miss out on important options, or not think of options at all!

-   Think things through systematically

    -   Prioritisation is hard, and a lot of it will come down to your intuitions. It’s easy to not think through a problem properly, and miss a lot of important details

    -   One solution to this is to do an intentionally shitty cost-benefit analysis:

        -   Make a table with a row for each option, and a column for each important factor.

        -   Go through and fill out each cell, eg rating it out of 5.

        -   Then give each factor a coefficient, according to how important it is, take the total, and sort!

        -   And, now the process has given me an answer, throw it all away and go with my _new_ gut feel

    -   I am a big fan of this approach - it resolves paralysing uncertainty by giving something concrete to do. And at the end of it, I’ve been forced to think through the different examples systematically, and through all the important factors. This often points to things I was missing before, and means my intuition is now far better calibrated.

-   Seek advice

    -   Prioritisation is _hard_. And one efficient solution to hard problems, is to outsource some of the cognitive work to other people!

    -   Seek out mentors, and people with more life experience, and ask their thoughts

        -   Ask for common mistakes, and _failures_ to prioritise too!

        -   Try to [gain their _models_](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations), not just their preferences and opinions. The question is what should _I_ do, not what _they_ would do. It’s pretty easy for mentors to commit the [Typical Mind Fallacy](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense) too

    -   Just talking to friends can be incredibly useful - making your thoughts concrete, and weighing up the pros and cons does a lot of the work of prioritisation. Getting them to sanity check you, and point out blind spots.

        -   Some people feel a lot of social resistance to this - I find most friends will be happy to help if you explicitly say “I’m struggling with a decision, can you help me clarify my thoughts”. If this is a service you can also give them, both people win!

-   Getting thoughts out of your head

    -   It’s easy to get caught up in fuzzy spirals of over-thinking, which never go anywhere. Getting thoughts out of your head is valuable, and helps to significantly clarify things. This is a lot of the value of talking things through with a friend!

        -   This can often be replicated by just putting your thoughts on paper, or talking to a [rubber duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_duck_debugging)

    -   A major failure mode is to fail to be concrete. It can be good to pick out specific options, and explore _exactly_ what you think they’d be like, and what the consequences would be. A surprising amount of the time this will highlight key uncertainties, or that an option is _obviously_ dumb/amazing.

-   Explore

    -   Often the best way to prioritise is to try things! Go out into the world, gain information, do experiments

    -   Don’t just do this blindly - figure out your key uncertainties, and focus your exploration on those

    -   Exploration takes time and effort - seek advice on what things to do that could best give you value. Talk to people who’ve done similar things, and ask them what they learned from it

### Mistakes

Prioritisation is far easier said than done, so here are some of the most common mistakes I see, and thoughts on how to overcome them:

-   Not making progress

    -   It’s easy to _think_ your prioritising, but really just go in circles. Spend ages agonising over the same information and same choices. Spend hours researching, but really getting no clarity.

    -   This is [pressing the Try Harder button](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-6-stop-pressing-the-try-harder-button) - the goal of prioritisation is to **get more information on what your final decision should be**, not to go through the motions and invest effort in this. If investing effort gets you no further clarity, this is not achieving your goals

    -   Of course, it’s hard to distinguish this from the problem just being difficult and taking time and effort!

    -   My main solution to this is to ensure you remain grounded, to identify your key uncertainties, and to focus on these

        -   It’s easy to fall down rabbit holes and not realise this. A good solution to this is to have regular check in times, where you zoom out, summarise what you’ve just done, and ask whether it’s bringing you closer to your goals.

    -   Regularly write down the current state of your thoughts, and your best guess for the right action. If what you write down stops changing, then you’ve probably stopped making progress!

-   Under-estimating difficulty

    -   Prioritisation is _hard_. It is genuine cognitive labour. It’s easy to get disheartened, and think you’re being an idiot or missing something obvious. And this is _super_ unhelpful

        -   I find it useful to remember that insecurities [are in the map, and that “could I be doing better” is a question of the territory](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)

    -   But humans systematically under-estimate the value of information, and how difficult it was to think of an idea, so your intuitions are a poor guide for this.

-   Not considering alternate options hard enough

    -   It’s _super_ easy to get attached to specific options and approaches, and not to think widely enough. Or for anything outside a very small set of options to feel _weird_, and easily dismissed.

    -   A common way this manifests is an attachment to your self-image.

        -   Eg, identifying as a mathematician, who can _only_ do pure maths, and must become an academic. And not considering paths beyond this, even if they might make you much happier

        -   Eg, identifying as “bad at meeting new people”, and so not considering options that take you outside of your comfort zone, even though this is a skill which can be learned

    -   Similarly, it’s easy to have cached thoughts. Like, deciding you were going to do a PhD when you were 18 and knew nothing about what academia would actually be like. And being attached to those cached thoughts, and not reconsidering them, even as evidence to the contrary has slowly accumulated over time.

-   Decouple prioritising and doing

    -   Another trap is to be constantly paralysed _in the moment_. I have a deep desire for certainty and confidence. But this is essentially impossible to achieve.

    -   If I constantly agonise in the moment about whether my actions are optimal, then I never get anything done. And _this_ becomes the biggest thing holding me back.

    -   The solution is to realise that **prioritising** and **doing** are different parts of my mind, that require different modes. It’s hard to _do_ things while painfully aware of uncertainty, and agonising over whether this is your best option. And it’s easy to neglect switching costs, and to perpetually flit between things

    -   In practice, I time-box the periods when I’m prioritising and thinking, and in the rest of my life

        -   I find that goals are intricate enough that it’s useful to think in layers of abstraction (the following process works well for me, your mileage may vary):

            -   In a weekly review, I can think about my high-level goals, and which projects will bring me closer to it. Between those, I want to just stick to the project ideas I came up with. (I’m considering switching this to monthly, or quarterly - exact time periods are hard)

                -   This is hard, because if some genuinely new information comes up that _should_ change my priorities, I want to respond to this! It’s difficult to balance and tell the difference from the inside. Nate Soares phrases this point [far better than I can](http://mindingourway.com/deliberate-once/)

            -   At the start of each day, I plan my day. I _know_ the projects I’m working on, so in the moment I’m thinking through how to implement those, and how to allocate my time, but not questioning whether those projects are my best projects. If no new information has come up, then my thoughts will be strictly worse than during those prioritisation periods

                -   Sometimes I _should_ add in a new project - this is a delicate balance, and takes practice, I’m far from perfect at it

            -   And then in the moment, I just try to follow this plan

    -   The underlying point is that it’s easy to fail to make progress when prioritising in the moment. You want to time-box it, do it _well_ in those times, and just follow the plan in the rest of your life

-   Thinking you can do everything

    -   A _key_ skill of prioritisation is realising that you cannot do everything. Tasks, projects and opportunities accumulate far faster than you will ever be able to do them. But giving things up is painful, and it’s easy to think you can do everything. And if you’re going to do this task _anyway_, you may as well continue the fun & shiny task now!

    -   A useful reframing: You _cannot_ do everything, and must give something up. Implicitly, you will give up whatever task you don’t get round to. This is giving over _all_ of the power of prioritisation to the default algorithm, and to random chance. There is no world where you don’t give something up, you’re just exerting choice over _what_ that thing is

-   Short-term bias

    -   It’s super easy to fall into a spiral of meaningless busywork, doing a range of things that ultimately won’t matter in the long-term. The tasks that feel urgent but aren’t important.

    -   Humans have a significant short-term bias, so by default it will be true that you spend too much time on short-term stuff, and not enough time on your long-term goals

    -   In practice, this manifests as a strong hesitation to give anything up. But, when you look back at what you’ve got done over the last month, there isn’t much that you feel truly satisfied about.

    -   This is pretty hard to solve, and will depend a lot on your personal situation and how much autonomy you have. But if you can identify tasks that feel unimportant that consume your time, it’s worth spending some genuine effort thinking about ways you might reduce that.

    -   One useful mindset is “what tasks will I be happy to have done, 5 years from now?”. Find those, and do those.

        -   A good first step can be to time-box some time every week that can _only_ be spent working towards long-term goals

-   Not prioritising how you prioritise

    -   An easy trap is to over-optimise _every_ part of your life. To obsess about any tiny shred of wasted motion, to get into an anxious state about every detail of your life.

    -   The key lesson here is that **humans are boundedly rational agents**. Thinking and prioritising takes time and effort, and it is only worth it if that expenditure of time and effort can increase your efficiency enough over time to outweigh it.

    -   This is why it’s important to consider the _scale_ of the problem - focus on the _big_ things, and don’t sweat the small stuff.

    -   I conjecture that the kind of person who enjoys my blog is _especially_ likely to fall into this trap.

### Conclusion

Hopefully I’ve convinced you that prioritisation is _incredibly_ important, and given some good starting points! This is a difficult and fuzzy skill, and there are a lot of things to balance. But I think it’s one of the most important life skills you can ever learn, and one that you improve at with practice.

Getting perfect at prioritisation is hard, but there’s some low-hanging fruit here. Things that are _obviously_ worth prioritising, but which we rarely do enough. Some of the most obvious things that _really_ matter (for most people):

-   Sleep

-   Exercise

-   Nutrition

-   Mental health - enough rest, relaxation, therapy, emotionally supportive environments, minimising stress

-   Physical health - good work environment, following medical advice, getting questionable things checked up

-   Family

-   Friendships

-   Careers

-   Rest and relaxation

-   Romance

-   Learning

-   Personal development, and meta-skills

-   Hobbies/how you spend your spare time

Are you happy with the state of your life for everything on that list? And if not, does that gap feel like a priority to you? And how, concretely, can you prioritise it better in future?
