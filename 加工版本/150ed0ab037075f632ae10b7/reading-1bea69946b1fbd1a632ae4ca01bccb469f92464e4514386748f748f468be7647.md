# 决策有多好？在易于评估的领域中评估决策质量｜How good are decisions? Evaluating decision quality in domains where evaluation is easy

---

## 正文

我在 tech-utopian 圈子里经常听到的一种说法是，某种看似存在的 inefficiency 实际上不可能是 inefficient，因为 market 是 efficient，而且 inefficiencies 很快就会被 eliminated。一个有争议的例子是[声称 companies 不可能在 discriminating，因为 market 太 competitive，无法容忍 discrimination](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)。一个争议较小的例子是，当你看到一家大 company 做着看起来匪夷所思地 inefficient 的事情时，也许它并不是 inefficient，只是你缺少理解这个 decision 为什么是 efficient 所需的 information。这类说法往往还伴随着关于“incentives matter”的说法，或者说 CEO 有“skin in the game”，而评论者却没有。

遗憾的是，这类 arguments 很难有定论，因为即使事后回看，通常也无法获得足够的 information 来 determine 一个 decision 的 precise “value”。即使这个 decision 带来了明确的 success 或 failure，导致这一 result 的 factors 也如此之多，以至于很难弄清楚事情究竟为什么会发生。

在这篇文章中，我们将考察两类 examples，从中可以看到人们的 decisions 有多好，以及当容易获得的 data 表明人们正在做出 bad decisions 时，他们会如何回应。这两类 examples 都来自这样的 domains：做出或讨论 decision 的人似乎非常在意这个 decision，而 data 清楚地表明，这些 decisions 非常 poor。

第一类 example 来自 sports，第二类来自 board games。sports 的一个好处是，它们往往有 detailed play-by-play data 和 well-defined win criteria，让我们能够知道一个 decision 的 expected value 在 on average 的意义上是多少。在这篇文章中，我们将考察一种 sport 中 bad decision making 的 cost，然后简要讨论为什么 sports 中的 decision quality 可能与其他 fields 中的 decision quality 相同，甚至更好。Sports 是一片沃土，因为直到不久以前，decision making 仍然是 non-data driven，而且通常糟糕透顶，因此我们拥有 major U.S. sports 超过一个世纪的 information；在这段时期的相当一部分时间里，fans 会撰写 analyses，讨论 decision making 有多 poor，以及它给 teams 造成了多大的 cost，但 teams 会置之不理（此后情况发生了变化，如今基本上每个 team 都有一批 stats-PhDs 或具备同等能力的人员研究 data）。

#### Baseball（棒球）

在[另一篇文章中，我们考察了 sports 中的“hiring” decisions 如何完全是胡扯](https://danluu.com/talent/)。在这篇文章中，只因为“rationality community”中一位顶尖思想领袖给出了一个常见借口，说 coaches 的 in-game baseball decision making 并没有那么大的 cost（“bad in-game decisions 会 cost games 吗？当然会。但并不会 cost _那么多_ games。也许每年 162 场中，它们会让你 lose 4 场。”；整篇文章都在暗示这没什么大不了，白白丢掉 4 场 games 也无妨），我们就来看看 bad decision making 的 cost 究竟有多大，以及 teams 会花多少钱，通过其他方式买到 equivalent number 的 wins。不过，你也可以对 football、hockey、basketball 等做同类 analysis；据我了解，在所有这些情形中，你都会得到大致相似的 result。

我们将把 baseball 进行 model，视为一个 state machine，既因为这样容易理解特定 decisions 的 expected value，也因为这样可以让我们讨论 decisions 的 value，而不必逐一介绍 baseball 的大部分 rules。

我们可以将每场 baseball game 视为一个 independent event。在每场 game 中，两个 teams 相互 play against，scores 更多 runs（points）的 team 就会 wins。每场 game 被 split 为 9 个“innings”，每个 inning 中，每个 team 都会获得一组 offense 的机会。在每个 inning 中，每个 team 都会一直 play，直到得到 3 个“outs”。任何一次 play 都可能 result in 一个 out，也可能不会。

我们的 state machine 中，一部分 state 是 outs 的 number 和 inning。我们将 track 的其他部分 state 是谁处于“on base”，以及哪个 player 处于“at bat”。每个 teams 都会为其 active players defines 某种 batters 的 order，每个 player 都 bats 一次之后，这一过程就在 loop 中 repeats，直到 team collects 3 个 outs，这个 inning 随之结束。谁处于 at bat 的 state 会在 innings 之间被 saved。举个例子，你可能看到 batters 1-5 在第一个 inning 中 bat，6-9 然后再次轮到 1 在第二个 inning 中 bat，接着是 2- ……等等。

当一个 player 处于 at bat 时，根据发生的情况，这个 player 可能 advance 到一个 base，而处于 on base 的 players 也可能 advance。当一个 player advances 4 个 bases（也就是经过 1B、2B、3B，到达本来可以叫作 4B、但实际上并不这么叫的位置）时，就会有一个 run 被 scored，而这个 player 会从 base 上被 removed。如上所述，各种 events 都可能导致一个 player 处于 out，在这种情况下，他们也不再处于 on base。

我们的 state machine 中，一个 example state 是：

```
{1B, 3B; 2 outs}
```

这表示 1B 上有一个 player，3B 上有一个 player，并且有两个 outs。注意，这与 score、实际是哪些人在 playing，以及 inning 都是 independent 的。

另一个 state 是：

```
{--; 0 outs}
```

有了这样的 model，如果想 determine 上述 state 的 expected value，我们只需查找一个 season 中所有已进行的 innings 的 runs 的 total number，再 divided by innings 的 number，就能得到上述 state 的 expected number of runs（这里 ignoring 第 9 个 inning，因为 baseball rules 中的一个特殊之处会 distorts 第 9 个 inning 的 statistics）。这样做之后，我们会发现，从上述 state 出发，一个 team 将会 score .555 runs in expectation。

然后，我们可以 compute 所有其他 states 的 expected number of runs：

如上所述，我们看到，开始 inning 时，我们有 `.555` runs in expectation。如果一次 play 让某人到了 1B，而没有产生 out，那么我们现在就有 `.953` runs in expectation，也就是说，在没有 out 的情况下让某人到达 first，worth `.953 - .555 = .398` runs。

这立刻给出了某些 decisions 的 value，例如在没有 outs、且有人处于 first 时尝试“steal” `2B`。如果我们考察 batter 的 state 不发生变化的情形，一次 successful steal 会将我们 moves 到 `{2B, 0 outs}` state，也就是说，它会给我们带来 `1.189 - .953 = .236` runs。一次 failed steal 会将我们 moves 到 `{--, 1 out}` state，也就是说，它会给我们带来 `.953 - .297 = -.656` runs。要 break even，我们 succeed 的频率需要比 fail 高 `.656 / .236 = 2.78x`，也就是说，我们需要 `.735` 的 success rate 才能 break even。如果想 compute 一个 stolen base 的 average value，我们可以 compute 所有 states 上的 weighted sum，但现在，我们只需知道这是可以做到的，而且你需要大约 `.735` 的 success rate，stolen bases 才有意义。

然后，我们可以考察 teams 的 stolen base success rate，看到在任意一个 season 中，也许只有 5-10 个 teams 的表现好于 breakeven，剩下的 20-25 个 teams 都处于 breakeven 或以下（多数低于它）。如果我们考察那个时代一个 bad、但并非 historically bad 的 stolen-base team，其 success rate 可能是 .6。那个时代的一个 team 做出 100 到 200 次 attempts，并不罕见。为了能够 compute 一个 approximation，如果我们 assume 它们全都是从 {1B, 0 outs} state 出发的 attempts，那么每次 attempt 的 average run value 就会是 .4 \* (-.656) + .6 \* .236 = -0.12 runs。另一个 first-order approximation 是，10 runs 的 delta worth 1 win，因此在 100 次 attempts 时，我们有 -1.2 wins，在 200 次 attempts 时，我们有 -2.4 wins。

如果我们针对 actual states 来 run the math，而不是使用 first order approximation，就会看到 average stolen base worth -.467 runs，而 average successful steal worth .175 runs。在这种情况下，一个 success rate 为 .6 的 steal attempt，worth .4 \* (-.467) + .6 \* .175 = -0.082 runs。用这个新的 approximation，对于一个 steals 表现差、而且经常 steals 的 team，我们 estimate，按“平常那样”stealing，与制定一条“不准 stealing”的 rule 相比，其 approximate cost 以 wins 计是每 season .82 到 1.64 wins。注意，这 underestimates 了 stealing 的 cost，因为进入能够 steal 的 position 会 increases successful “pickoff”的 odds，而我们还没有 accounted for 这一点。从我们的 state-machine 角度看，pickoff 几乎 equivalent to 一次 failed steal，但要 compute pickoff probability 的 difference 所需的 analysis，超出了本文的 scope。

我们也可以对 coaches 能够促成（或阻止）的其他 plays 做同样的事。对于“intentional walk”，我们看到，一次 intentional walk 对 opposing team 而言似乎 worth .102 runs。在 2002 年，一个 issued“很多”intentional walks 的 team 可能 issued 了 50 次，给 opposing team 带来 50 \* .102 runs，造成大约 5 runs 或 .5 wins 的 loss。

如果我们乐观地 assume 一次“sac bunt”从不 fails，那么每次 attempt 的 sac bunt cost 就是 .027 runs。如果考察 pitchers 不 bat 的 league，一个大量使用 sac bunts 的 team 可能做了 49 次 sac bunts（我们这样做是为了避开“pitcher” bunts，它们会给 approximation 增加 complexity），造成 total 为 49 \* .027 = 1.32 runs 或 .132 wins 的 cost。

coach 做出的另一个 decision 是 setting the batting order。Players 按 order 进行 bat（轮到自己进行一次），1-9，mod 9。也就是说，当轮到第 10 个“player”时，我们实际上会绕回去，让第 1 个 player bats。game 会在某个时刻结束，所以 team 中并非每个人最终都有相同 number 的“at bats”。

有一个 just-so story，认为应当把 fastest player 放在第一位，把“batting average” high 的人放在第二位，把相当不错的人放在第三位，把你最好的 batter 放在第四位，等等。这个 story，或者类似的说法，成为 standard 已经超过 100 年。

我不打算逐步讲解 computing 一个更好的 batting order 所用的 math，因为我不认为存在一个简短、容易描述的 approximation。结果是，如果我们 compute 一个“optimal” order 与前一段那个 story 所支持的“typical” order 之间的 difference，使用 optimal order 似乎每 season worth 1 到 2 wins。

这些 approximations 都遗漏了重要的 information。在四种 cases 中的三种里，我们始终 assumed 一个 average player，而没有看谁在 at bat。上面的 information 实际上在一定程度上考虑到了这一点，但并不充分。它与更好的 approximation 究竟有何 differs，说来话长，而且对于一篇用 baseball 来讨论 baseball 之外的 decisions 的文章而言，细节可能太多了，所以我们就说，我们有一个相当不错、但并非出色的 approximation：在一个 baseball season 中，一名遵循 conventional wisdom、做出正常范围内的 bad decisions 的 coach，相比于遵循“绝不做那些 on average 具有 negative value 的 actions”的 decision rule，仅这三项 decisions 就可能让自己的 team 损失大约 1 + 1.2 + .5 + .132 = 2.83 wins。如果我们拿更好的 decision rule 来 compare，例如“当这些 actions 具有 positive value 时就做，而当它们具有 negative value 时就不做”，或者拿一名通常会做出 good decisions 的 manager 来 compare，那么我们 conservatively estimate，这或许值 3 wins。

我们已经看过四项 decisions（sac bunt、steal、intentional walk 和 batting order）。但还有很多其他 decisions！我们不妨 arbitrarily 地说，如果我们考察所有 decisions，而不只是这四项 decisions，那么为所有 decisions 采用更好的 heuristic，或许每个 season 能值 4 或 5 wins。

每个 season 的 4 或 5 wins 究竟意味着什么？一种看法是，baseball teams 要 play 162 games，因此一个“average”的 team 会 wins 81 games。如果我们看所涵盖的 seasons，那些 made the playoffs 的 teams 所取得的 wins 数为 {103, 94, 103, 99, 101, 97, 98, 95, 95, 91, 116, 102, 88, 93, 93, 92, 95, 97, 95, 94, 87, 91, 91, 95, 103, 100, 97, 97, 98, 95, 97, 94}。由于这个 system 的 structure，我们无法为某个 season 指定单一的 number，然后说 N wins 是 make the playoffs 所 necessary 的，而少于 N wins 的 teams 就不会 make the playoffs；但我们可以说，95 wins 能让一个 team 有不错的 odds 去 making the playoffs。95 - 81 = 14。5 wins 超过了一个 average team 与一个 makes the playoffs 的 team 之间 difference 的三分之一。无论从声望还是 direct economic value 来说，这都非常重要。

如果我们想从 at the margin 而非 on average 的角度来看，每个 league 中 made the playoffs 的 teams 与没有 made the playoffs 的 teams 之间，wins 的 smallest delta 为 {1, 7, 8, 1, 6, 2, 6, 3}。对于处在边缘的 teams，5 wins 的 delta 并不总能构成 successful season（making playoffs）与 unsuccessful season（不 making playoffs）之间的 difference，但在大多数 seasons 中，都有 teams 距离 making the playoffs 只有 5 win 以内的 delta。如果我们真的在经营一个 baseball team，我们会希望使用细致得多的 model；但作为 first approximation，我们可以说，in-game decisions 是 team performance 的一个 significant factor，而且通过某种 computation，我们能够 determine non-optimal decisions 的 expected cost。

另一种看待 5 wins 价值的方法，是看获得一名不是 pitcher、却有 5 wins above average (WAA) 的 player 需要多少 cost（我们考察 non-pitchers，是因为 non-pitchers 通常每场 game 都会 play，而 pitchers 通常只在部分 games 的部分时段 play，这使 pitchers 与 non-pitchers 之间的 comparison 更 complicated）。对于 8 个 non-pitcher positions（我们考察 non-pitcher positions，是因为这样能让 comparisons 更简单），有 30 个 teams，因此共有 240 个 team-positions pairs。在 2002，这 240 个 team-position pairs 中，[有两个是 >= 5 WAA](https://www.baseball-reference.com/leagues/team_compare.cgi?request=1&year=2002&lg=MLB&stat=WAA)，Texas-SS（Alex Rodriguez，获付 $22m) and SF-LF (Barry Bonds, paid $15m）。如果我们看所考察日期范围内的其他 seasons，每个 season 都有 2 或 3 个 team-position pairs 能让一个 team 获得 >= 5 WAA。这些并不能跨 seasons 保持 stable，因为 player performance 是 volatile 的，所以事情并不像找到一个出色的人、付给他 $15m. For example, in 2002, there were 7 non-pitchers paid $14m 或更多那么简单，而且他们之中只有两个人值 5 WAA 或更多。作为参考，2002 的 average total team payroll（每个 team 有 26 名 players）为 $67m, with a minimum of $34m，而给 manager 最高 $126m. At the time a $1m 的 salary 就会被认为很慷慨，因此一名 5 WAA manager 会是一笔极其划算的交易。

5 WAA assumes typical decision making 与 events 以一种不利、但不是 worst-case 的方式相遇。更 typical 的 case 可能是，一名 manager 让一个 team 损失 3 wins。在这种 case 下，2002 的 240 个 team-position pairs 中，有 25 个能由 single player 弥补按 conventional wisdom 进行 management 所造成的 loss。能够提供这么多 value、又没有因为 player transfers 的 mechanics 而被 artificially cheap deals 绑定在特定 teams 的 Players，仍然比 managers 贵得多。

如果我们看 teams 如何采用 data analysis 来 improve in-game decision making 和 team-composition decisions，这一直是一个缓慢、历时数十年的过程。[Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=391fa6566a95327d7f06f533fb3e2daf) 描述了这一转变的一部分：从使用 intuition 和 observation 来 select players，转向将 statistics 纳入这个过程。至少从 [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabermetrics) 起，Stats nerds 就在讨论如何做到这一点，但直到 90s 才真正有 team 认真对待，而这些 ideas 直到 mid 2000s、一本畅销书出版之后，才真正成为主流。

如果我们考察 teams 在这里讨论的 in-game decisions 上 improved 了多少，这个过程甚至更慢。直到今天，statistics-driven decisions 仍然不是主流。情况正在好转；如果我们看这里提到的 non-optimal decisions 的 aggregate cost，会发现过去二十来年里，随着 intuition-driven decisions 缓慢地 converge、越来越接近 stats nerds 几十年来一直在说的内容，aggregate cost 一直在降低。例如，如果我们看从 1999 到现在所有 teams 所记录的 sac bunts 的 total number，就会看到：

“显然”non-optimal 的 decisions 能持续如此之久，一个具体的 lower-level 原因是，team results 中有大量 noise。你有时会看到，一名 manager 做出一些激进的 decisions（不一定是 statistics-driven），随后出现一些 poor results，导致 management 解雇这名 manager。volatility 大到你实际上无法根据 small samples 来评判 players 或 managers，但这并没有阻止人们这么做。volatility 与对激进 ideas 的怀疑相结合，强烈地 disincentivizes 违背 conventional wisdom。

这种 noise 的众多后果之一，是“world series”（baseball championship）的 winner 在很大程度上由 randomness 决定。一个 team 是否 makes the playoffs，要通过 162 games 来 determine；这不足以消除所有 randomness，却足以让 result 不至于主要由 randomness 决定。playoffs 则并非如此，它太短，以至于 outcome 无法主要由 teams 的 quality 之间的 difference 来 determine。一旦一个 team wins the world series，人们就会编出各种 just-so stories，来论证这个 team 为什么理应 won；但如果我们考察所有 games，就会发现这些故事只是故事。这或许与听别人告诉你他们的 startup 为什么 successful，并没有太大不同。

我们可以使用一些 metrics，它们能更好地充当未来 wins 和 losses 的 predictors（也就是说，它们比 wins 和 losses 更不 volatile）；但直到不久前，说服人们相信这些 metrics 是 meaningful 的，也还是一个激进的 idea。

#### Board games（桌游）

以上就是 baseball 的例子。现在来看 board game 的例子。在这个例子中，我们会看看那些评论“现代”board game strategy 的人，我指的是他们评论 Catan、Puerto Rico、Ark Nova 等 games 的 strategy。

人们经常激烈争论什么 works、什么不 works。如今，这类 online discussions 大多发生在 boardgamegeek (BGG) 上；它是一个 forum，而且是讨论 board games 的最大 forum，远远超过其他 forum。这些讨论的一个特别之处在于，人们在 BGG 上使用的 username 往往与在 boardgamearena (BGA) 上使用的相同；后者是一个 online boardgame site，会 tracked 人们的 ratings (Elos)，而且你可以看到他们的 Elo ratings。

所以，在这些讨论中，你会看到有人说 strategy X 是 dominant。接着另一个人会过来说，不，strategy Y beats strategy X，当别人采用 strategy X 时，我用 strategy Y 总是能 win，等等。如果你理解这个 game，你就会发现主张 X 的人是 correct，而主张 Y 的人是 wrong；然后你去查这些人的 Elos，就会发现 X-player 是一个 high-ranked player，而 Y-player 是一个 low-ranked player。

这里奇怪的是，为什么这些 low-ranked players 能如此自信地争辩，说自己的 position 是 correct？他们不但会得到 per-game information，表明他们是 wrong（因为他们经常 lose），而且还有一个 rating，它 aggregates 他们所有的 gameplay，并大致告诉他们自己的水平如何。尽管这个 rating 告诉他们，他们并不知道自己在 game 中做的是什么，他们却坚信自己是 strong players，正在 playing well；他们不但拥有 good strategies，而且这些 strategies 好到足以让他们就如何 play 向 much higher rated players 提供建议。

当别人纠正这些人时，他们常常会感到冒犯，因为他们确信自己很 good，还会说出这样的话：“我是一个 good \[game name\] player。我 win 了很多 games”，随后便愤愤不平，抱怨自己的建议没有受到重视，和/或气呼呼地评论，说那些认为 strategy X works 的人全都在进行 group think。然而，这些人明明是在同一个 competitive online players 的 pool 中 playing；在那里，如果 strategy X players 确实是在进行 incorrect group think，那么 strategy Y players 就会 beat 他们，并拥有 higher ratings。而且，正如我们[在考察 video game skill 时](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/)指出的，players 经常会因为 losing、没能在 game 中表现得更好而表达强烈的沮丧和愤怒，所以很明显，他们希望表现得更好并 win。但即便有一个能相当 accurately 概括你的 skill 的 rating，始终显示在你的 screen 上，似乎也不足以让人意识到：他们 on average 正在 making poor decisions，而且只要听取 higher-rated players 的建议，而不是坚持认为自己的 losing strategies works，就能轻易 make better decisions。

在考察 video game Overwatch 时，我们指出，人们常常 overestimated 自己的 skill，并将 losses 归咎于 teammates。但在这类 boardgames 中，人们通常不是以 teams 的形式 playing，所以没有其他人可以责怪。不仅没有 teammate 可以责怪，而且在大多数 games 中，最认真的 rated game format 是 1v1，而不是某种 multi-player FFA，所以你甚至不能责怪一个不在自己 team 中的随机的人。一般而言，一个人在 1v1 game 中的 rating，几乎已经是你在任何 domain 中能够得到的、衡量某人 domain-specific decision making skill 的最 accurate 的 metric 了。

然而，尽管自己的 ratings 很 low，人们依然对自己的 skills 极其自信。如果你看看如今的 board game strategy commentary，几乎全都是 wrong；而当你查阅这些人的 ratings 时，会发现这些 commentary 几乎全都来自这样的人：他们在自己玩的每个 game 中都是 low rated，而且似乎不理解如何把任何一个 game play 好。当然，如果一个人享受的就是 playing a game poorly，那本身没有什么不对。这里的不协调之处在于，人们 playing poorly，拥有一个 well-defined rating，显示他们正在 playing poorly，却坚信自己正在 playing well，并在别人指出他们所倡导的 strategies 不 works 时感到冒犯。

#### games 之外的生活

在现实世界中，我们很少能得到像 sports 和 board games 中那样清晰的 evidence，来说明我们的 decision making 的 quality。在 making an engineering decision 时，你几乎不可能拥有像 baseball 中那样 clean 的 data，也不会有一个 Elo rating，能基本 accurately 地概括你过去的 decision making 有多好。这使得在 sports 和 board games 中根据 feedback 作出调整并 make good decisions 要容易得多；然而，我们可以观察到，sport 和 board games 中的大多数 decision making 都是 poor。在 sports 中，尽管涉及巨额金钱，情况基本上一直如此；在 board games 中，尽管人们为之情绪激动、似乎非常在意，情况也同样如此。

如果我们思考一下之前考察的 baseball decision 的 general version，就会发现，实际情况是 decisions 具有 probabilistic payoffs。actual outcomes（wins 和 losses）具有非常高的 variance，所以完全可能 make good decisions，却在很长时间内看不到它们的直接 effect。即使有一些 metrics 能让我们更好地理解一个 decision 的“true” value，如果你所处的环境中，management 并不相信那些 metrics，而你又想做某种 radical 的事情，其 value 只能通过某个听起来晦涩的 metric 来 demonstrated，那么除非他们愿意在你身上冒险一两年，否则你很难保住工作（或者一开始就找到工作）。哪些 metrics 能被接受，已经发生过一些重大的 phase changes，但这些变化都花了几十年。

如果我们考察 business 或 engineering decisions，情况就混乱得多。如果把 product 或 infrastructure 的 success 看作一次“win”，那么一个 team 能否获得一次“win”，其中似乎有多得多的 noise。而且，与 baseball 不同，那种能让人 analyze “wins”和“losses”、以 determine underlying cause 的 play-by-play 乃至 game data，并没有被 recorded，所以不可能 determine decisions 的 true value。即使这些 data 可以获得，决定某件事是否算作一次“win”的 factors 也多得多，因此，即便有 data，我们是否能够 determine decisions 的 expected value，也并不清楚。

我们已经看到，在一个人们可以坐下来 determine decisions 的 expected value 的 field 中，这类 analysis 也可能要花几十年才能 influence 一些重要 decisions。如果我们考察那些更难 determine decisions 的 true value 的 fields，应该预期“good” decision making 要过多久才会显现出来？看起来会需要相当长的时间，或许永远也不会，除非 baseball 和其他 sports 的 structure 中存在某种因素，使得移除一个 poor decision maker 并换上一个 better decision maker 尤其困难。

有人可能会说，baseball 不同，因为 teams 的 number 是 fixed，而且一个新 team enter the market 相当罕见；但如果你看看 public clouds、operating systems、search engines、car manufacturers 等，情况似乎并没有那么不同。真要说有什么区别，接管一个 baseball team 并 replace management 似乎还便宜得多（有时你会看到 baseball teams 以大约十亿美元的价格出售），而且 baseball teams 的数量比我们刚才讨论的那些 markets 中 competitive products 的数量还多，至少在 U.S. 是如此。也有人可能会说，如果考察 baseball teams 的 structure，就会很清楚，positions 通常并不是根据 decision-making merit 来分配的，而且[其他 factors 往往 tend to dominate](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/10/29/leaders-of-men/)；但这一点在 baseball 中似乎并不明显比在 engineering fields 中更为成立。

这并不是说，我们预期到处都会出现明显的 bad decisions。如果你在 [Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=3ec395b394d2f8caaa30df2b2fc15d5d) 出版之前（以及出版之后相当长一段时间里）经常逛 baseball stats nerd forums，可能会有这种想法；但如果你在差不多同一时期看看 formula 1 (F1)，就会看到 teams 雇用了精通 economics 和 game theory 的 PhDs，以确保自己正在 making reasonable decisions。这并不意味着 F1 teams 总是 make perfect decisions，但他们至少避免了几十年来一直 making 那些有兴趣的 amateurs 都能 identify 为 inefficient 的 decisions。有些 fields 中 competition 极其残酷，你必须做 rigorous analysis 才能生存；另一些 fields 中的 competition 则较为平缓。在仍有人记得的年代，[曾有一段时期，为 sports 进行 training 被认为有失绅士风度](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/bitstream/handle/2152/17760/bournen.pdf?%852)，而任何使用哪怕近似现代 training techniques 来 trained 的人，都会拥有巨大的 advantage。在过去十年左右，我们正在看到同样的转变，只不过这次是 baseball 中的 statistical techniques，而不是各种 sports 中的 training。

如果我们想考察 decision making 的 quality，仅仅因为一个 firm 面对 markets、making good decisions 具有 economic value，而且 firm 内的人如果 make good decisions 很可能得到丰厚回报，就说我们预期这个 firm 会 make good decisions，这未免过于简单。你甚至无法通过询问人们是否正在 making rigorous、data-driven 的 decisions，来判断这种情况是否正在发生。如果你问 baseball 中的人是否在自己的 decisions 中使用 data，那么在整个 70s 和 80s，他们都会回答是。Baseball 长期以来一直以这样一种 sport 而闻名：人们 track 各种 numbers，然后使用这些 numbers。只不过，人们并没有 backtest 自己的 predictions，更不用说用 holdouts 来 backtest 自己的 predictions 了。

在过去几十年里，有效使用 data 来 drive decisions 的这种 [paradigm shift](https://www.uky.edu/~eushe2/Pajares/Kuhn.html)，一直以不同 rates 影响着不同 fields，既包括 sports 内部，也包括 sports 之外。之所以这种变化在 F1 中早于 baseball 发生，是由于 F1 teams 与 baseball teams 之间 incentive structure 的 difference，以及 [institutional culture](https://danluu.com/wat/) 的 difference 共同造成的。我们或许会在未来的一篇文章中考察这一点，但事实证明，这是一个相当复杂、需要多得多 background 的问题。

纵观全局，我们可以把这杯水看成是半空的（哇，人们在 making 那些他们认为非常重要的 easy decisions 时都如此糟糕，那么他们在 making non-easy decisions 时一定会糟糕透顶），也可以把它看成是半满的（哇，你可以在许多地方找到很好的 improvement opportunities，甚至在那些人们声称必然很难的领域也是如此；诸如“他们一定正在 making the right call，因为他们是 highly incentivized”这样的 [econ 101 reasoning](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/)，可能会误导人，让人以为并不存在容易把握的 opportunities）。

### 附录：我们的 baseball analysis 中的 non-idealities

为了让这篇文章是一篇短博文，而不是一本书，我们讨论的 approximation 中有很多 simplifications。一个主要的 simplification 是认为所有 runs 都是 equivalent。这与真实情况足够接近，因此是一个还不错的 approximation。但在某些 situations 中，这个 approximation 并不很好，例如到了第 9 个 inning，而 game 处于 tied。在这种情况下，一个 increases scoring 1 run 的 probability、但 decreases scoring multiple runs 的 probability 的 decision，实际上才是正确选择。

这经常被用来作为在相对 late-game 时进行 sac bunt 的 justification。但如果我们看看 successful sac bunt 的 probability，就会发现它在较后的 innings 中会下降。我们没有讨论 defense 是如何 set up 的，但 defenses 可以采用某些方式 set up，来 reduce successful sac bunt 的 probability，同时 increase non-bunts 的 success 的 probability，反之亦然。在最后一个 inning 之前，这实际上会让 sac bunt 在 game 后期变得更差，而不是更好！如果我们在 tie game 的最后一个 inning 中把所有这些都考虑进去，那么 sac bunt 是个好主意的 probability 就取决于另一个我们尚未讨论的因素，也就是 batter at the plate。

在我们的 simplified model 中，我们 computed 了所有 batters 的、以 runs 计的 expected value。但在任何时候，都是某个特定的 player 在 batting。一次 successful sac bunt 会 advances runners，并将 outs 的 number increases 一。另一种选择是让 batter “swing away”，这会 result in 某种 random outcome。batter 越好，出现比 sac bunt 的 outcome 更好的 outcome 的 probability 就越高。要 determine optimal decision，我们不仅需要知道当前 batter 有多好，还需要知道后续 batters 有多好。为 sac bunt 提供的一种常见 justification 是，pitchers 是很差的 hitters，而且他们并不拙于 sac bunting，因为他们有大量这方面的练习（因为他们是很差的 hitters），但事实证明，pitchers 也是 below average sac bunters，而且如果我们详细查看 data，就会发现，因为 pitchers 是差劲的 hitters，所以我们应该预期他们 sac 的 argument 并不成立。

另一个 sac bunt（或者一般而言，bunt）的理由是，偶尔这样做的 tendency 会 induces defense 的 changes，使 non-bunt plays 更有效。

一次 full computation 还应该考虑当前 batter 的 balls 和 strikes 的 number，这是我们完全没有讨论过的一部分 state；还要考虑 batter 和 players on base 的 speed、game 所在的特定 stadium、opposing pitcher，以及他们的 defense 的 quality。所有这些都可以做到，甚至用一台 laptop 就行 -- 对 computers 而言，这些全都是“small data”，但即便只详细讲解某一个特定 decision 的 analysis，篇幅也会远远超过这篇文章包括这段免责声明在内的全部内容。将所有这些 non-idealities 都考虑进去并不会推翻 general result，这或许有点令人意外，但事实确实如此（它发现，在许多 situations 中，sac bunts 具有 positive expected value，但 sac bunts 几十年来仍然被 heavily overused）。

intentional walks 也有类似情况，我们的 analysis 中的 non-idealities 似乎支持 issuing intentional walks。具体来说，为 intentional walk 提供的两个主要 conventional justifications 是

1.  通过 walking 当前 batter，我们可以 set up 一个“force”或一个“double play”（increase 在一次 play 中 getting one out 或 two outs 的 probability）。如果 game 在最后一个 inning 中处于 tied，那么 putting 另一个 player on base 几乎没有 downside，而 upside 则是 increasing allowing zero runs 并 continuing the tie 的 probability。
2.  通过 walking 当前 batter，我们可以轮到下一个、更差的 batter。

人们应用 (1) 中的 justification 的一个 situation 示例，就是处于 `{1B, 3B; 2 out}` state。如果位于 `3B` 的 player advances one base，那么 on defense 的 team 就会 lose。其 reasoning 是，如果当前 batter “puts the ball into play”，walking 一个 player 并将 state changing 为 `{1B, 2B, 3B; 2 out}`，并不会 increase 位于 `3B` 的 player 会 score 并 end the game 的 probability；而 putting 另一个 player on base 则 increases defense 能够 get an out 的 probability。

这个 reasoning 的漏洞在于，batter 不一定会 put the ball into play。在 state 变成 `{1B, 2B, 3B; 2 out}` 之后，pitcher 可能 issue 一次 unintentional walk，导致每个 runner 都 advance，并 losing the game。事实证明，处于这个 state 并不会很大程度地影响 unintentional walk 的 probability。pitcher 会非常努力地 avoid a walk，但与此同时，batter 也会非常努力地 induce a walk！

关于 (2)，这一 justification 通常应用于两种 situations：当前 player at bat 很好或者极好，或者当前 player 正好在 pitcher 之前 batting。让我们分别看看这两种情况。

Barry Bonds 在 2001、2002 和 2004 的 seasons，是有史以来 statistically best seasons 中的一部分，也是 modern baseball 中能找到的最 extreme 的 case。如果我们进行同样的 analysis，并考虑在 Bonds 之后 batting 的 players 的 quality，就会发现，opposing team 有时 intentionally walk Bonds 确实是 correct decision，但大多数 situations 仍然不足以 warrant an intentional walk，而且 Bonds 经常在并不 warrant an intentional walk 的 situation 中被 intentionally walked。如果一个 batter 并没有打出现代 baseball 有记录以来 statistically best seasons 中的一个，那么 intentional walks 就更加不好。

在 pitcher batting 的情况下，进行与上面相同的 analysis 也表明，确实存在 intentional walk 是 appropriate 的 situations（not-late game，{1B, 2B; 2 out}，且该 pitcher 并不是一个相对于 pitcher 而言 significantly above average 的 batter）。尽管 issue an intentional walk 并非总是 wrong decision，intentional walk 仍然被 grossly overused。

有人可能会辩称，我们的 simple analysis 存在所有这些本可能使 analysis invalidated 的 non-idealities，这说明 baseball 中的 decision making 毕竟没有那么差，但我认为这种说法并不成立。一个人花一两个小时就能完成的 first-order approximation 会发现，on average，decision making 似乎相当糟糕。如果一个 team 有兴趣查看 data，这就应该促使他们进行更 detailed analysis，将针对这个显而易见的一小时 analysis、基于 conventional-wisdom 的 critiques 考虑进去。但看来他们并没有这样做，至少几十年间都没有。

问题在于，在人们开始 running the data 之前，我们所能依据的只有故事。有人会说：“在有 2 outs 时，你应该 walk 排在 pitcher 之前的 batter，以便轮到 pitcher \[在某些 situations 中\]，以便轮到 pitcher，并 get the guaranteed out。”另一个人可能会回应：“我们显然不应该在 late game 这样做，因为 pitcher 会被 subbed out，换上一个 pinch hitter；而在 early game，我们也不应该这样做，因为即使它奏效，我们 get the easy out，它也会让另一个 team 得以在下一个 inning 用他们的 #1 hitter lead off，而不是一个 easy out。”这些故事中哪一个才是正确的，事实证明是一个 empirical question。我觉得最遗憾的是，在人们开始 running the numbers、争论变成故事与 data 之争以后，人们仍然几十年坚持采用 story-based argument。我们在 business 和 engineering 中也看到同样的现象，但在那里或许更情有可原，因为那些领域的 decisions 往往更难 quantify。即使你能将某件事 reduce 为一个 simple engineering equation，也总会有人辩称，engineering decision 并不是真正重要的，另一个难以 quantify 的 business concern 才是最重要的。

### 附录：possession

我觉得有意思的一点是，football、baseball 和 basketball 中的 statistical analysis 发现，几十年来，teams 一直严重 undervalued possessions。Baseball 本身没有 possession 这个 concept，但如果把处于 on offense 看作“having possession”，把 getting 3 outs 看作“losing possession”，那么两者就相当类似。

在 football 中，我们看到，[maintaining possession](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/JPE_April06.pdf) 如此重要，以至于在 4th down 时 punt 通常是一个 error，但这并没有阻止 teams 几乎一直默认采用 punting。而在 basketball 中，那些 shoot 很多但 shooting percentage 很低的 players 过去是 overrated 的（而且可以说现在仍然如此）。

我不认为这是什么 fundamental 的性质——possessions 之所以如此 valuable，是由每种 game 的 rules 决定的。这是 arbitrary 的。不过，我仍然觉得这很有意思。

### 附录：management decisions 的其他 analysis

[Bloom et al., Does management matter? Evidence from India](https://people.stanford.edu/nbloom/sites/default/files/dmm.pdf) 考察了 management interventions 的 impact，以及对 productivity 的 effect。

[Bloom 的其他研究](https://people.stanford.edu/nbloom/research)。

[DellaVigna et al., Uniform pricing in US retail chains](http://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/uniform-pricing.pdf) 据称发现，retail chains 放着相当多的钱没有赚到（profits 的百分之七），并探讨了为什么可能出现这种情况，以及会产生哪些 impacts。

这类研究相较于 sports 研究的优点，是它尝试 quantify 人为设计的 game 之外的事物所产生的 impact。缺点是，这些 studies 所研究的事物相当复杂混乱，很难判断 study 究竟说明了什么。举个例子，如果你看关于 innovation 的 studies，economists 经常使用 patents 作为 innovation 的 proxy，然后根据某个 variable 与 number of patents 的关系得出某种 conclusion。但如果你熟悉 engineering patents，就会知道 number of patents 是 innovation 的一个极其糟糕的 proxy。在 hardware 领域，IBM 以大量炮制 useless patents 而闻名（既是指它们对于 innovation 而言 useless，也是在更狭窄的意义上，指它们作为 patent lawsuits 中的 counter-attack 而言 useless），而有些 companies 通过 filing 少得多的 patents，却能从中获得大得多的效益。

据我所知，我们在这里的选择，要么是对一个可以说完全不相关的 context 中的 decisions 了解很多，要么是掌握 ambiguous information，而且对一个看起来与现实世界相关的 context 可能知之甚少。我很想听说这两类中更多的 studies（更好的是，不存在这两种问题的 studies）。

## 原文

A statement I commonly hear in tech-utopian circles is that some seeming inefficiency can’t actually be inefficient because the market is efficient and inefficiencies will quickly be eliminated. A contentious example of this is [the claim that companies can’t be discriminating because the market is too competitive to tolerate discrimination](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/). A less contentious example is that when you see a big company doing something that seems bizarrely inefficient, maybe it’s not inefficient and you just lack the information necessary to understand why the decision was efficient. These kinds of statements are often accompanied by statements about how "incentives matter" or the CEO has "skin in the game" whereas the commentator does not.

Unfortunately, arguments like this are difficult to settle because, even in retrospect, it’s usually not possible to get enough information to determine the precise “value” of a decision. Even in cases where the decision led to an unambiguous success or failure, there are so many factors that led to the result that it’s difficult to figure out precisely why something happened.

In those post, we'll look at two classes of examples where we can see how good people's decisions are and how they respond to easy to obtain data showing that people are making bad decisions. Both classes of examples are from domains where the people making or discussing the decision seem to care a lot about the decision and the data clearly show that the decisions are very poor.

The first class of example comes from sports and the second comes from board games. One nice thing about sports is that they often have detailed play-by-play data and well-defined win criteria which lets us tell, on average, what the expected value of a decision is. In this post, we’ll look at the cost of bad decision making in one sport and then briefly discuss why decision quality in sports might be the same or better as decision quality in other fields. Sports are fertile ground because decision making was non-data driven and generally terrible until fairly recently, so we have over a century of information for major U.S. sports and, for a decent fraction of that time period, fans would write analyses about how poor decision making was and now much it cost teams, which teams would ignore (this has since changed and basically every team has a staff of stats-PhDs or the equivalent looking at data).

#### Baseball

In [another post, we looked at how "hiring" decisions in sports were total nonsense](https://danluu.com/talent/). In this post, just because one of the top "rationality community" thought leaders gave the common excuse that that in-game baseball decision making by coaches isn't that costly ("Do bad in-game decisions cost games? Absolutely. But not _that many_ games. Maybe they lose you 4 a year out of 162."; the entire post implies this isn't a big deal and it's fine to throw away 4 games), we'll look at how costly bad decision making is and how much teams spend to buy an equivalent number of wins in other ways. However, you could do the same kind of analysis for football, hockey, basketball, etc., and my understanding is that you’d get a roughly similar result in all of those cases.

We’re going to model baseball as a state machine, both because that makes it easy to understand the expected value of particular decisions and because this lets us talk about the value of decisions without having to go over most of the rules of baseball.

We can treat each baseball game as an independent event. In each game, two teams play against each other and the team that scores more runs (points) wins. Each game is split into 9 “innings” and in each inning each team will get one set of chances on offense. In each inning, each team will play until it gets 3 “outs”. Any given play may or may not result in an out.

One chunk of state in our state machine is the number of outs and the inning. The other chunks of state we’re going to track are who’s “on base” and which player is “at bat”. Each teams defines some order of batters for their active players and after each player bats once this repeats in a loop until the team collects 3 outs and the inning is over. The state of who is at bat is saved between innings. Just for example, you might see batters 1-5 bat in the first inning, 6-9 and then 1 again in the second inning, 2- … etc.

When a player is at bat, the player may advance to a base and players who are on base may also advance, depending on what happens. When a player advances 4 bases (that is, through 1B, 2B, 3B, to what would be 4B except that it isn’t called that) a run is scored and the player is removed from the base. As mentioned above, various events may cause a player to be out, in which case they also stop being on base.

An example state from our state machine is:

```
{1B, 3B; 2 outs}
```

This says that there’s a player on 1B, a player on 3B, there are two outs. Note that this is independent of the score, who’s actually playing, and the inning.

Another state is:

```
{--; 0 outs}
```

With a model like this, if we want to determine the expected value of the above state, we just need to look up the total number of runs across all innings played in a season divided by the number of innings to find the expected number of runs from the state above (ignoring the 9th inning because a quirk of baseball rules distorts statistics from the 9th inning). If we do this, we find that, from the above state, a team will score .555 runs in expectation.

We can then compute the expected number of runs for all of the other states:

The state we’re tracking for an inning here is who’s on base and the number of outs. Innings start with nobody on base and no outs.

As above, we see that we start the inning with `.555` runs in expectation. If a play puts someone on 1B without getting an out, we now have `.953` runs in expectation, i.e., putting someone on first without an out is worth `.953 - .555 = .398` runs.

This immediately gives us the value of some decisions, e.g., trying to “steal” `2B` with no outs and someone on first. If we look at cases where the batter’s state doesn’t change, a successful steal moves us to the `{2B, 0 outs}` state, i.e., it gives us `1.189 - .953 = .236` runs. A failed steal moves us to the `{--, 1 out}` state, i.e., it gives us `.953 - .297 = -.656` runs. To break even, we need to succeed `.656 / .236 = 2.78x` more often than we fail, i.e., we need a `.735` success rate to break even. If we want to compute the average value of a stolen base, we can compute the weighted sum over all states, but for now, let’s just say that it’s possible to do so and that you need something like a `.735` success rate for stolen bases to make sense.

We can then look at the stolen base success rate of teams to see that, in any given season, maybe 5-10 teams are doing better than breakeven, leaving 20-25 teams at breakeven or below (mostly below). If we look at a bad but not historically bad stolen-base team of that era, they might have a .6 success rate. It wouldn’t be unusual for a team from that era to make between 100 and 200 attempts. Just so we can compute an approximation, if we assume they were all attempts from the {1B, 0 outs} state, the average run value per attempt would be .4 \* (-.656) + .6 \* .236 = -0.12 runs per attempt. Another first-order approximation is that a delta of 10 runs is worth 1 win, so at 100 attempts we have -1.2 wins and at 200 attempts we have -2.4 wins.

If we run the math across actual states instead of using the first order approximation, we see that the average stolen base is worth -.467 runs and the average successful steal is worth .175 runs. In that case, a steal attempt with a .6 success rate is worth .4 \* (-.467) + .6 \* .175 = -0.082 runs. With this new approximation, our estimate for the approximate cost in wins of stealing “as normal” vs. having a “no stealing” rule for a team that steals badly and often is .82 to 1.64 wins per season. Note that this underestimates the cost of stealing since getting into position to steal increases the odds of a successful “pickoff”, which we haven’t accounted for. From our state-machine standpoint, a pickoff is almost equivalent to a failed steal, but the analysis necessary to compute the difference in pickoff probability is beyond the scope of this post.

We can also do this for other plays coaches can cause (or prevent). For the “intentional walk”, we see that an intentional walk appears to be worth .102 runs for the opposing team. In 2002, a team that issued “a lot” of intentional walks might have issued 50, resulting in 50 \* .102 runs for the opposing team, giving a loss of roughly 5 runs or .5 wins.

If we optimistically assume a “sac bunt” never fails, the cost of a sac bunt is .027 runs per attempt. If we look at the league where pitchers don’t bat, a team that was heavy on sac bunts might’ve done 49 sac bunts (we do this to avoid “pitcher” bunts, which add complexity to the approximation), costing a total of 49 \* .027 = 1.32 runs or .132 wins.

Another decision that’s made by a coach is setting the batting order. Players bat (take a turn) in order, 1-9, mod 9. That is, when the 10th “player” is up, we actually go back around and the 1st player bats. At some point the game ends, so not everyone on the team ends up with the same number of “at bats”.

There’s a just-so story that justifies putting the fastest player first, someone with a high “batting average” second, someone pretty good third, your best batter fourth, etc. This story, or something like it, has been standard for over 100 years.

I’m not going to walk through the math for computing a better batting order because I don’t think there’s a short, easy to describe, approximation. It turns out that if we compute the difference between an “optimal” order and a “typical” order justified by the story in the previous paragraph, using an optimal order appears to be worth between 1 and 2 wins per season.

These approximations all leave out important information. In three out of the four cases, we assumed an average player at all times and didn’t look at who was at bat. The information above actually takes this into account to some extent, but not fully. How exactly this differs from a better approximation is a long story and probably too much detail for a post that’s using baseball to talk about decisions outside of baseball, so let’s just say that we have pretty decent but not amazing approximation that says that a coach who makes bad decisions following conventional wisdom that are in the normal range of bad decisions during a baseball season might be able cost their team something like 1 + 1.2 + .5 + .132 = 2.83 wins on these three decisions alone vs. a decision rule that says “never do these actions that, on average, have negative value”. If we compare to a better decision rule such as “do these actions when they have positive value and not when they have negative value” or a manager that generally makes good decisions, let’s conservatively estimate that’s maybe worth 3 wins.

We’ve looked at four decisions (sac bunt, steal, intentional walk, and batting order). But there are a lot of other decisions! Let’s arbitrarily say that if we look at all decisions and not just these four decisions, having a better heuristic for all decisions might be worth 4 or 5 wins per season.

What does 4 or 5 wins per season really mean? One way to look at it is that baseball teams play 162 games, so an “average” team wins 81 games. If we look at the seasons covered, the number of wins that teams that made the playoffs had was {103, 94, 103, 99, 101, 97, 98, 95, 95, 91, 116, 102, 88, 93, 93, 92, 95, 97, 95, 94, 87, 91, 91, 95, 103, 100, 97, 97, 98, 95, 97, 94}. Because of the structure of the system, we can’t name a single number for a season and say that N wins are necessary to make the playoffs and that teams with fewer than N wins won’t make the playoffs, but we can say that 95 wins gives a team decent odds of making the playoffs. 95 - 81 = 14. 5 wins is more than a third of the difference between an average team and a team that makes the playoffs. This a huge deal both in terms of prestige and also direct economic value.

If we want to look at it at the margin instead of on average, the smallest delta in wins between teams that made the playoffs and teams that didn’t in each league was {1, 7, 8, 1, 6, 2, 6, 3}. For teams that are on the edge, a delta of 5 wins wouldn’t always be the difference between a successful season (making playoffs) and an unsuccessful season (not making playoffs), but there are teams within a 5 win delta of making the playoffs in most seasons. If we were actually running a baseball team, we’d want to use a much more fine-grained model, but as a first approximation we can say that in-game decisions are a significant factor in team performance and that, using some kind of computation, we can determine the expected cost of non-optimal decisions.

Another way to look at what 5 wins is worth is to look at what it costs to get a player who’s not a pitcher that’s 5 wins above average (WAA) (we look at non-pitchers because non-pitchers tend to play in every game and pitchers tend to play in parts of some games, making a comparison between pitchers and non-pitchers more complicated). Of the 8 non-pitcher positions (we look at non-pitcher positions because it makes comparisons simpler), there are 30 teams, so we have 240 team-positions pairs. In 2002, of these 240 team-position pairs, [there are two that were >= 5 WAA](https://www.baseball-reference.com/leagues/team_compare.cgi?request=1&year=2002&lg=MLB&stat=WAA), Texas-SS (Alex Rodriguez, paid $22m) and SF-LF (Barry Bonds, paid $15m). If we look at the other seasons in the range of dates we’re looking at, there are either 2 or 3 team-position pairs where a team is able to get >= 5 WAA in a season These aren’t stable across seasons because player performance is volatile, so it’s not as easy as finding someone great and paying them $15m. For example, in 2002, there were 7 non-pitchers paid $14m or more and only two of them we worth 5 WAA or more. For reference, the average total team payroll (teams have 26 players per) in 2002 was $67m, with a minimum of $34m and a max of $126m. At the time a $1m salary for a manager would’ve been considered generous, making a 5 WAA manager an incredible deal.

5 WAA assumes typical decision making lining up with events in a bad, but not worst-case way. A more typical case might be that a manager costs a team 3 wins. In that case, in 2002, there were 25 team-position pairs out of 240 where a single player could make up for the loss caused from management by conventional wisdom. Players who provide that much value and who aren’t locked up in artificially cheap deals with particular teams due to the mechanics of player transfers are still much more expensive than managers.

If we look at how teams have adopted data analysis in order to improve both in-game decision making and team-composition decisions, it’s been a slow, multi-decade, process. [Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=391fa6566a95327d7f06f533fb3e2daf) describes part of the shift from using intuition and observation to select players to incorporating statistics into the process. Stats nerds were talking about how you could do this at least since [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabermetrics) and no team really took it seriously until the 90s and the ideas didn’t really become mainstream until the mid 2000s, after a bestseller had been published.

If we examine how much teams have improved at the in-game decisions we looked at here, the process has been even slower. It’s still true today that statistics-driven decisions aren’t mainstream. Things are getting better, and if we look at the aggregate cost of the non-optimal decisions mentioned here, the aggregate cost has been getting lower over the past couple decades as intuition-driven decisions slowly converge to more closely match what stats nerds have been saying for decades. For example, if we look at the total number of sac bunts recorded across all teams from 1999 until now, we see:

Despite decades of statistical evidence that sac bunts are overused, we didn’t really see a decline across all teams until 2012 or so. Why this is varies on a team-by-team and case-by-case basis, but the fundamental story that’s been repeated over and over again both for statistically-driven team composition and statistically driven in-game decisions is that the people who have the power to make decisions often stick to conventional wisdom instead of using “radical” statistically-driven ideas. There are a number of reasons as to why this happens. One high-level reason is that the change we’re talking about was a cultural change and cultural change is slow. Even as this change was happening and teams that were more data-driven were outperforming relative to their budgets, people anti-data folks ridiculed anyone who was using data. If you were one of the early data folks, you'd have to be [willing to tolerate a lot of the biggest names in the game calling you stupid, as well as fans, friends, etc.](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/). It doesn’t surprise people when it takes a generation for scientific consensus to shift in the face of this kind of opposition, so why should be baseball be any different?

One specific lower-level reason “obviously” non-optimal decisions can persist for so long is that there’s a lot of noise in team results. You sometimes see a manager make some radical decisions (not necessarily statistics-driven), followed by some poor results, causing management to fire the manager. There’s so much volatility that you can’t really judge players or managers based on small samples, but this doesn’t stop people from doing so. The combination of volatility and skepticism of radical ideas heavily disincentivizes going against conventional wisdom.

Among the many consequences of this noise is the fact that the winner of the "world series" (the baseball championship) is heavily determined by randomness. Whether or not a team makes the playoffs is determined over 162 games, which isn't enough to remove all randomness, but is enough that the result isn't mostly determined by randomness. This isn't true of the playoffs, which are too short for the outcome to be primarily determined by the difference in the quality of teams. Once a team wins the world series, people come up with all kinds of just-so stories to justify why the team should've won, but if we look across all games, we can see that the stories are just stories. This is, perhaps, not so different to listening to people tell you why their startup was successful.

There are metrics we can use that are better predictors of future wins and losses (i.e., are less volatile than wins and losses), but, until recently, convincing people that those metrics were meaningful was also a radical idea.

#### Board games

That's the baseball example. Now on to the board game example. In this example, we'll look at people who make comments on "modern" board game strategy, by which I mean they comment on strategy for games like Catan, Puerto Rico, Ark Nova, etc.

People often vehemently disagree about what works and what doesn't work. Today, most online discussions of this sort happen on boardgamegeek (BGG), a forum that is, by far, the largest forum for discussing board games. A quirk of these discussions is that people often use the same username on BGG as on boardgamearena (BGA), a online boardgame site where people's ratings (Elos) are tracked and you can see people's Elo ratings.

So, in these discussions, you'll see someone saying that strategy X is dominant. Then someone else will come in and say, no, strategy Y beats strategy X, I win with strategy Y all the time when people do strategy X, etc. If you understand the game, you'll see that the person arguing for X is correct and the person arguing for Y is wrong, and then you'll look up these people's Elos and find that the X-player is a high-ranked player and the Y-player is a low-ranked player.

The thing that's odd about this is, how come the low-ranked players so confidently argue that their position is correct? Not only do they get per-game information indicating that they're wrong (because they often lose), they have a rating that aggregates all of their gameplay and tells them, roughly, how good they are. Despite this rating telling them that they don't know what they're doing in the game, they're utterly convinced that they're strong players who are playing well and that they not only have good strategies, their strategies are good enough that they should be advising much higher rated players on how to play.

When people correct these folks, they often get offended because they're sure that they're good and they'll say things like "I'm a good \[game name\] player. I win a lot of games", followed by some indignation that their advice isn't taken seriously and/or huffy comments about how people who think strategy X works are all engaging in group think even when these people are playing in the same pool of competitive online players where, if it were true that strategy X players were engaging in incorrect group think, strategy Y players would beat them and have higher ratings. And, as we noted [when we looked at video game skill](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/), players often express great frustration and anger at losing and not being better at the game, so it's clear that they want to do better and win. But even having a rating that pretty accurately sums on your skill displayed on your screen at all times doesn't seem to be enough to get people to realize that they're, on average, making poor decisions and could easily make better decisions by taking advice from higher-rated players instead of insisting that their losing strategies work.

When looking at the video game Overwatch, we noted that often overestimated their own skill and blamed teammates for losses. But in these kinds of boardgames, people are generally not playing on teams, so there's no one else to blame. And not only is there no teammate to blame, in most games, the most serious rated game format is 1v1 and not some kind of multi-player FFA, so you can't even blame a random person who's not on your team. In general, someone's rating in a 1v1 game is about as accurate as metric as you're going to get for someone's domain-specific decision making skill in any domain.

And yet, people are extremely confident about their own skills despite their low ratings. If you look at board game strategy commentary today, almost all of it is wrong and, when you look up people's ratings, almost all of it comes from people who are low rated in every game they play, who don't appear to understand how to play any game well. Of course there's nothing inherently wrong with playing a game poorly if that's what someone enjoys. The incongruity here comes from people playing poorly, having a well-defined rating that shows that they're playing poorly, be convinced that they're playing well and taking offence when people note that the strategies they advocate for don't work.

#### Life outside of games

In the world, it's rare to get evidence of the quality of our decision making that's as clear as we see in sports and board games. When making an engineering decision, you almost never have data that's as clean as you do in baseball, nor do you ever have an Elo rating that can basically accurately sum up how good your past decision making is. This makes it much easier to adjust to feedback and make good decisions in sports and board games and yet, we can observe that most decision making in sport and board games in poor. This was true basically forever in sports despite a huge amount of money being on the line, and is true in board games despite people getting quite worked up over them and seeming to care a lot.

If we think about the general version of the baseball decision we examined, what’s happening is that decisions have probabilistic payoffs. There’s very high variance in actual outcomes (wins and losses), so it’s possible to make good decisions and not see the direct effect of them for a long time. Even if there are metrics that give us a better idea of what the “true” value of a decision is, if you’re operating in an environment where your management doesn’t believe in those metrics, you’re going to have a hard time keeping your job (or getting a job in the first place) if you want to do something radical whose value is only demonstrated by some obscure-sounding metric unless they take a chance on you for a year or two. There have been some major phase changes in what metrics are accepted, but they’ve taken decades.

If we look at business or engineering decisions, the situation is much messier. If we look at product or infrastructure success as a “win”, there seems to be much more noise in whether or not a team gets a “win”. Moreover, unlike in baseball, the sort of play-by-play or even game data that would let someone analyze “wins” and “losses” to determine the underlying cause isn’t recorded, so it’s impossible to determine the true value of decisions. And even if the data were available, there are so many more factors that determine whether or not something is a “win” that it’s not clear if we’d be able to determine the expected value of decisions even if we had the data.

We’ve seen that in a field where one can sit down and determine the expected value of decisions, it can take decades for this kind of analysis to influence some important decisions. If we look at fields where it’s more difficult to determine the true value of decisions, how long should we expect it to take for “good” decision making to surface? It seems like it would be a while, perhaps forever, unless there’s something about the structure of baseball and other sports that makes it particularly difficult to remove a poor decision maker and insert a better decision maker.

One might argue that baseball is different because there are a fixed number of teams and it’s quite unusual for a new team to enter the market, but if you look at things like public clouds, operating systems, search engines, car manufacturers, etc., the situation doesn’t look that different. If anything, it appears to be much cheaper to take over a baseball team and replace management (you sometimes see baseball teams sell for roughly a billion dollars) and there are more baseball teams than there are competitive products in the markets we just discussed, at least in the U.S. One might also argue that, if you look at the structure of baseball teams, it’s clear that positions are typically not handed out based on decision-making merit and that [other factors tend to dominate](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/10/29/leaders-of-men/), but this doesn’t seem obviously more true in baseball than in engineering fields.

This isn’t to say that we expect obviously bad decisions everywhere. You might get that idea if you hung out on baseball stats nerd forums before [Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=3ec395b394d2f8caaa30df2b2fc15d5d) was published (and for quite some time after), but if you looked at formula 1 (F1) around the same time, you’d see teams employing PhDs who are experts in economics and game theory to make sure they were making reasonable decisions. This doesn’t mean that F1 teams always make perfect decisions, but they at least avoided making decisions that interested amateurs could identify as inefficient for decades. There are some fields where competition is cutthroat and you have to do rigorous analysis to survive and there are some fields where competition is more sedate. In living memory, [there was a time when training for sports was considered ungentlemanly](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/bitstream/handle/2152/17760/bournen.pdf?%852) and someone who trained with anything resembling modern training techniques would’ve had a huge advantage. Over the past decade or so, we’re seeing the same kind of shift but for statistical techniques in baseball instead of training in various sports.

If we want to look at the quality of decision making, it's too simplistic to say that we expect a firm to make good decisions because they're exposed to markets and there's economic value in making good decisions and people within the firm will probably be rewarded greatly if they make good decisions. You can't even tell if this is happening by asking people if they're making rigorous, data-driven, decisions. If you'd ask people in baseball they were using data in their decisions, they would've said yes throughout the 70s and 80s. Baseball has long been known as a sport where people track all kinds of numbers and then use those numbers. It's just that people didn't backtest their predictions, let alone backtest their predictions with holdouts.

The [paradigm shift](https://www.uky.edu/~eushe2/Pajares/Kuhn.html) of using data effectively to drive decisions has been hitting different fields at different rates over the past few decades, both inside and outside of sports. Why this change happened in F1 before it happened in baseball is due to a combination of the difference in incentive structure in F1 teams vs. baseball teams and the difference in [institutional culture](https://danluu.com/wat/). We may take a look at this in a future post, but this turns out to be a fairly complicated issue that requires a lot more background.

Looking at the overall picture, we could view this glass as being half empty (wow, people suck at making easy decisions that they consider very important, so they must be absolutely horrible at making non-easy decisions) or the glass as being half full (wow, you can find good opportunities for improvement in many places, even in areas people claim must be hard due to [econ 101 reasoning](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/) like "they must be making the right call because they're highly incentivized" could trick one into thinking that there aren't easy opportunities available).

### Appendix: non-idealities in our baseball analysis

In order to make this a short blog post and not a book, there are a lot of simplifications the approximation we discussed. One major simplification is the idea that all runs are equivalent. This is close enough to true that this is a decent approximation. But there are situations where the approximation isn’t very good, such as when it’s the 9th inning and the game is tied. In that case, a decision that increases the probability of scoring 1 run but decreases the probability of scoring multiple runs is actually the right choice.

This is often given as a justification for a relatively late-game sac bunt. But if we look at the probability of a successful sac bunt, we see that it goes down in later innings. We didn’t talk about how the defense is set up, but defenses can set up in ways that reduce the probability of a successful sac bunt but increase the probability of success of non-bunts and vice versa. Before the last inning, this actually makes sac bunt worse late in the game and not better! If we take all of that into account in the last inning of a tie game, the probability that a sac bunt is a good idea then depends on something else we haven’t discussed, the batter at the plate.

In our simplified model, we computed the expected value in runs across all batters. But at any given time, a particular player is batting. A successful sac bunt advances runners and increases the number of outs by one. The alternative is to let the batter “swing away”, which will result in some random outcome. The better the batter, the higher the probability of an outcome that’s better than the outcome of a sac bunt. To determine the optimal decision, we not only need to know how good the current batter is but how good the subsequent batters are. One common justification for the sac bunt is that pitchers are terrible hitters and they’re not bad at sac bunting because they have so much practice doing it (because they’re terrible hitters), but it turns out that pitchers are also below average sac bunters and that the argument that we should expect pitchers to sac because they’re bad hitters doesn’t hold up if we look at the data in detail.

Another reason to sac bunt (or bunt in general) is that the tendency to sometimes do this induces changes in defense which make non-bunt plays work better.

A full computation should also take into account the number of balls and strikes a current batter has, which is a piece of state we haven’t discussed at all as well as the speed of the batter and the players on base as well as the particular stadium the game is being played in and the opposing pitcher as well as the quality of their defense. All of this can be done, even on a laptop -- this is all “small data” as far as computers are concerned, but walking through the analysis even for one particular decision would be substantially longer than everything in this post combined including this disclaimer. It’s perhaps a little surprising that taking all of these non-idealities into account doesn’t overturn the general result, but it turns out that it doesn’t (it finds that there are many situations in which sac bunts have positive expected value, but that sac bunts were still heavily overused for decades).

There’s a similar situation for intentional walks, where the non-idealities in our analysis appear to support issuing intentional walks. In particular, the two main conventional justifications for an intentional walk are

1.  By walking the current batter, we can set up a “force” or a “double play” (increase the probability of getting one out or two outs in one play). If the game is tied in the last inning, putting another player on base has little downside and has the upside of increasing the probability of allowing zero runs and continuing the tie.
2.  By walking the current batter, we can get to the next, worse batter.

An example situation where people apply the justification in (1) is in the `{1B, 3B; 2 out}` state. The team that’s on defense will lose if the player at `3B` advances one base. The reasoning goes, walking a player and changing the state to `{1B, 2B, 3B; 2 out}` won’t increase the probability that the player at `3B` will score and end the game if the current batter “puts the ball into play”, and putting another player on base increases the probability that the defense will be able to get an out.

The hole in this reasoning is that the batter won’t necessarily put the ball into play. After the state is `{1B, 2B, 3B; 2 out}`, the pitcher may issue an unintentional walk, causing each runner to advance and losing the game. It turns out that being in this state doesn’t affect the the probability of an unintentional walk very much. The pitcher tries very hard to avoid a walk but, at the same time, the batter tries very hard to induce a walk!

On (2), the two situations where the justification tend to be applied are when the current player at bat is good or great, or the current player is batting just before the pitcher. Let’s look at these two separately.

Barry Bonds’s seasons from 2001, 2002, and 2004 were some of the statistically best seasons of all time and are as extreme a case as one can find in modern baseball. If we run our same analysis and account for the quality of the players batting after Bonds, we find that it’s sometimes the correct decision for the opposing team to intentionally walk Bonds, but it was still the case that most situations do not warrant an intentional walk and that Bonds was often intentionally walked in a situation that didn’t warrant an intentional walk. In the case of a batter who is not having one of the statistically best seasons on record in modern baseball, intentional walks are even less good.

In the case of the pitcher batting, doing the same kind of analysis as above also reveals that there are situations where an intentional walk are appropriate (not-late game, {1B, 2B; 2 out}, when the pitcher is not a significantly above average batter for a pitcher). Even though it’s not always the wrong decision to issue an intentional walk, the intentional walk is still grossly overused.

One might argue the fact that our simple analysis has all of these non-idealities that could have invalidated the analysis is a sign that decision making in baseball wasn’t so bad after all, but I don’t think that holds. A first-order approximation that someone could do in an hour or two finds that decision making seems quite bad, on average. If a team was interested in looking at data, that ought to lead them into doing a more detailed analysis that takes into account the conventional-wisdom based critiques of the obvious one-hour analysis. It appears that this wasn’t done, at least not for decades.

The problem is that before people started running the data, all we had to go by were stories. Someone would say "with 2 outs, you should walk the batter before the pitcher to get to the pitcher \[in some situations\] to get to the pitcher and get the guaranteed out". Someone else might respond "we obviously shouldn't do that late game because the pitcher will get subbed out for a pinch hitter and early game, we shouldn't do it because even if it works and we get the easy out, it sets the other team up to lead off the next inning with their #1 hitter instead of an easy out". Which of these stories is the right story turns out to be an empirical question. The thing that I find most unfortunate is that, after started people running the numbers and the argument became one of stories vs. data, people persisted in sticking with the story-based argument for decades. We see the same thing in business and engineering, but it's arguably more excusable there because decisions in those areas tend to be harder to quantify. Even if you can reduce something to a simple engineering equation, someone can always argue that the engineering decision isn't what really matters and this other business concern that's hard to quantify is the most important thing.

### Appendix: possession

Something I find interesting is that statistical analysis in football, baseball, and basketball has found that teams have overwhelmingly undervalued possessions for decades. Baseball doesn't have the concept of possession per se, but if you look at being on offense as "having possession" and getting 3 outs as "losing possession", it's quite similar.

In football, we see that [maintaining possession](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/JPE_April06.pdf) is such a big deal that it is usually an error to punt on 4th down, but this hasn't stopped teams from punting by default basically forever. And in basketball, players who shoot a lot with a low shooting percentage were (and arguably still are) overrated.

I don't think this is fundamental -- that possessions are as valuable as they are comes out of the rules of each game. It's arbitrary. I still find it interesting, though.

### Appendix: other analysis of management decisions

[Bloom et al., Does management matter? Evidence from India](https://people.stanford.edu/nbloom/sites/default/files/dmm.pdf) looks at the impact of management interventions and the effect on productivity.

[Other work by Bloom](https://people.stanford.edu/nbloom/research).

[DellaVigna et al., Uniform pricing in US retail chains](http://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/uniform-pricing.pdf) allegedly finds a significant amount of money left on the table by retail chains (seven percent of profits) and explores why that might happen and what the impacts are.

The upside of work like this vs. sports work is that it attempts to quantify the impact of things outside of a contrived game. The downside is that the studies are on things that are quite messy and it's hard to tell what the study actually means. Just for example, if you look at studies on innovation, economists often use patents as a proxy for innovation and then come to some conclusion based on some variable vs. number of patents. But if you're familiar with engineering patents, you'll know that number of patents is an incredibly poor proxy for innovation. In the hardware world, IBM is known for cranking out a very large number of useless patents (both in the sense of useless for innovation and also in the narrow sense of being useless as a counter-attack in patent lawsuits) and there are some companies that get much more mileage out of filing many fewer patents.

AFAICT, our options here are to know a lot about decisions in a context that's arguably completely irrelevant, or to have ambiguous information and probably know very little about a context that seems relevant to the real world. I'd love to hear about more studies in either camp (or even better, studies that don't have either problem).
