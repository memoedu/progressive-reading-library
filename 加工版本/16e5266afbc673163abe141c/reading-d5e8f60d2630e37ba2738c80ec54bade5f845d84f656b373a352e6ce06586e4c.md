_关于 Tanya M. Luhrmann 的书《How God Becomes Real: Kindling the Presence of Invisible Others》的工作笔记。我写这些笔记，是为一个大型 creative project 准备背景材料，因此主要供自己使用。我把它们公开分享出来，让其他人如果愿意，也可以在一旁看看。请注意：这些笔记仍是 exploratory、incomplete 而且 underdeveloped 的。非常欢迎深思熟虑的评论——请在文末评论区留言。_

Alan Watts 在《The Wisdom of Insecurity》一书中指出，许多人 _believe_，_believing in God_ 是有好处的，但这与真正 believing in God 远不是一回事。仅仅理解某种 belief 会对我们有益，并不会自动使我们 sincerely believe 它。远非如此！正如 Watts 所说：

> 人或许需要一个 myth，但他无法 self-consciously prescribe 一个……人无法长久地 knowingly and intentionally“欺骗”自己。即使那些……最有力的、主张以某种形式 return to orthodoxy 的 arguments，也不过是展示 belief in God 在 social 和 moral 方面的 advantages。但这并不 prove God 是一种 reality。这至多只能 proves，believing in God 是 useful 的……如果公众对他的 exist 有任何怀疑，这种 invention 就是徒劳的。

也就是说：belief 并不只是一个 choice，不是看起来 beneficial 时就可以 adopted 的东西。相反，它与一种 genuine、sincere、durable 的 felt conviction 相联系。事实上，即使这种 conviction 带来不便，甚至 disadvantageous，人也可能仍然 held 它。如果面对这些挑战就无法 hold，那么可以说，它原本就不是一种 belief。在 choice 或 rejection 上都缺乏 volition，有助于让 beliefs 成为 binding communities 的 durable glue。

那么，人们是如何开始 believe 的？Tanya M. Luhrmann 写了一本优美的书来探索这个问题：《How God Becomes Real: Kindling the Presence of Invisible Others》。这本书探讨了这样一个想法：religious practice 的 purpose，很大一部分是帮助 practitioners believe。这颠倒了 conventional wisdom；Luhrmann 认真对待这样一种可能性：有时，人们不是因为 believe 才 worshipping，而是因为 worship 才 believing。更一般地说：Luhrmann 提出了有说服力的论证，说明 belief 与 religious practice 之间的 relationship，远比你可能 naively suppose 的更加 complex，并探索了其中一部分 relationship。

让我回顾一下 Luhrmann 在展开这项研究时采取的几个步骤。她令人信服地指出，religious belief 通常_并不是_容易 attained 的东西，尽管许多 theories of religion 都“presume，belief 是 direct 而且 unproblematic 的——在大多数 cultures 中，人们只是认为 spirit 和 the supernatural 就在那里。这说不通。Gods 和 spirits 无法被看见。你不能与他们握手、看着他们的眼睛，也无法在他们说话时听见他们的声音。假定人们就这样理所当然地认为他们是 present 的，似乎很奇怪。”

因此，Luhrmann 并不假定 religious 的人很容易 believe，而是问：“人们投入 faith 的努力，是否帮助他们 feel 自己的 gods 和 spirits 是 real 的”，“那些 practices 本身会如何 affect 参与其中的人”，以及“这些 practices 本身是否有助于 make the beliefs compelling”？从这个角度看，belief 开始显得更像一种 skillful practice，而不是一种 state。她论证道：

> 一旦我们允许自己问，各地的人们是否都需要 persuade 自己，他们的 gods 是 real 的，religious rituals 就突然更说得通了。毕竟，如果人们 believed，spirits 是 unproblematically present 的——就在那里，responsive 而且 available——为什么还需要一场通宵的 drumming ceremony 来 call them forth？如果 God 始终是 present 而且 aware 的，为什么还会有人需要 pray？
> 
> 我在这里 argue，religion 的 puzzle 并不是 false belief 的 problem，而是 gods 和 spirits 如何对人们 become 并 remain real，以及这种 real-making 对人类起什么作用。这不是在 claim，gods 并不 real，或者 religious 的人会 feel doubt。许多有 faith 的人从不 express doubt；他们说起话来，仿佛自己的 gods 是 real 的这件事显而易见。然而，他们在 worship 上不遗余力。他们耗费大量人力、时间和金钱，建造宏伟的 cathedrals。他们花上数天，甚至数周，为 rituals 做准备，筹集食物、搭建 ritual sites、召集 participants。他们在 sacred spaces 中创造 theatrical effects——temples 里昏暗的灯光、evangelical megachurches 中精心设计的 staging——以 enhance 一种 sense of otherness，而 sacred texts 并未要求这样做。他们 fast。他们穿特殊的衣服。他们一连 chant 数小时。他们力求 pray without ceasing。
> 
> 当然，有人可能会说：他们 believe，所以才建造 cathedrals。我所问的是，如果我们转移关注点，能学到什么：如果我们不再 presuming 人们因为 believe 才 worship，而是转而问，人们是否因为 worship 才 believe。我 suggest，prayer、ritual 和 worship 帮助人们从在 abstract 层面 knowing invisible other 是 real 的，转向 feeling gods 和 spirits 就在当下 present、aware 而且愿意 respond。我将把这称为“real-making”，而且我认为，这个 process 带来的 satisfactions，部分 explain 了 faiths 为何能够 endure。
> 
> 我所说的“real-making”，是指一个有 faith 的人的 task，不只是 believe gods 和 spirits 像 dark energy 一样，以某种 abstract 的方式在那里，而是 believe 这些 gods 和 spirits 在此时此地 matter。我指的不只是你 know 他们是 real 的，就像你 know 地板是 real 的那样（或者说，如果你停下来想一想，就会 know），而是他们 feel real，就像母亲的爱 feels real 那样。我指的是，有 faith 的人开始 inwardly and intimately 地 feel，gods 或 spirits 是 involved with 他们的。

在这个背景下，Luhrmann 总结了她的 basic claim：

> 必须为人们将 god 或 spirit——也就是 invisible other——made real，而这种 real-making 会 changes 做这件事的人。当我审视围绕着我们所称的 religion 的 social practices 时，我看到的是一组 behaviors，它们 change 了 practitioner 对什么是 real 的 felt sense。这些 behaviors 既让 unseen 的东西 feel more present，也 alter 了 performs 它们的人。

对这个 claim 的一种过于简单的解读，是认为它 contradicts 我开头引用的 Watts 那段话。但更好的理解是，它是在 elaboration 和 extension 那段话。Watts 的 fundamental point 是 correct 的：我们无法像在超市里选择买哪种奶酪那样，简单地 choose 自己的 beliefs。相反，我们的 beliefs 如何 form，是一个更加 complex 的 process。但究竟哪些 beliefs 会 form，可以受到我们自己以及他人的 actions 的 influenced，而且这个 process 是可以理解的。也就是说，belief 不只是一种 state of being，一个 noun。它是某种 enacted 的东西：在某种意义上，belief 是一个 verb。作为 state 的 Belief，是作为 action 的 belief 的 consequence[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn1)。这并不是说，我们 believe 的东西与世界的 realities 是 divorced 的，也不是说所有 possible beliefs 都 on an equal footing。远非如此。例如，今天的人 believe 地球是 near-sphere，远比 believe 它是 flat 的容易。Reality 和 evidence 确实 matter。但它们也不是全部，这就促使我们问：我们开始 believe 某件事的 process 是什么？尤其是像 God 这样 intangible 的东西？belief 可能具有哪些不同的 depths 和 complexities？

在这些笔记中，我将通过 Luhrmann 这本书的视角探索这些问题，同时也会联系到她的范围之外的问题。God 远不是我们 believe in 的唯一 intangible phenomenon——想想 microbes、atoms、我们自己未来的 death、cosmic microwave background 或 GDP。对我们大多数人而言，这些东西同样需要 real-making；在某种重要意义上，它们不像椅子、山和足球这些日常物品那样显然是 real 的。我们不会担心椅子、山和足球的 reality，而是理所当然地认为这里不存在 real-making 的 problem[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn2)。因此，我也对我们将 intangible phenomena 进行 make real（或者未能 make real）的更广泛 process 感兴趣。写这些笔记时，我总在想象某个脾气古怪的 atheist 说：“噢，这全是胡扯，是 religious apologetics，是在给 religion 涂上一层迷人的粉饰。”但 Luhrmann 关于 real-making，以及我们如何开始 believe 的想法，与人类对 cosmos 的所有理解都有关，而不仅仅与 religion 有关。它们关乎我们如何开始 believe in 什么_存在_、什么_不存在_，这或许是所有问题中最 fundamental 的一个。

当 implicit premise 是有 plausible grounds 去 believe 某个东西并不 exist 时，Real-making 最显然令人感兴趣，God 就是如此。相比之下，像“microbes 是 real 的吗？”这样的问题，已有一个 well-attested、几乎 universally accepted 的答案，因此“这是否 real？”这个问题似乎就不那么显然值得关注。然而，Luhrmann 对 felt-real 与否的 distinction 仍然存在：此时此地，X 对我来说是否强烈地 feel real，以至于 shapes 我的 think、feel 和 live 的方式？而对我们大多数人而言，microbes、atoms 等 phenomena _并不是_强烈地 felt real 的。我们如何让它们更加 felt real？Luhrmann 的研究有助于阐明这个 problem。

## 一个简单的 model：将 belief-making process 与 truth-determining process 分开

《How God Becomes Real》清楚地表明，人类如何开始 believe 某些事物，与我们如何将 propositions establish 为 true，二者非常不同。我发现，用一个简单的 model 来思考很有帮助：在其中，我们分别考察自己的 _belief-making process_ 和 _truth-determining process_。当然，这样做是 oversimplified：对于两者，我们都没有单一而 stable 的 processes。而且，我把“truth”用作一个粗略的 shorthand，意思类似于“关于世界的 good、useful model，同时我们对这个 model 的 limits 有一定认识”。我并不是指某种强得多的、首字母大写的 Truth 的 notion！不过，带着这些 caveats，我发现，将其划分为 belief-making process 和 truth-determining process，对我自己的思考很有帮助。和许多 scientists 一样，我花了许多年尝试 understand 和 develop truth-determining processes；但用于尝试 understand belief-making processes 的时间却少得多。两者都很重要。

我们很容易认为，belief-making 和 truth-determining processes 应当是相同的。但 Luhrmann 的书清楚地表明，它们是 intrinsically different processes。我们最好的 truth-determining processes 是 culture 中宏大而 non-obvious 的成就——包括有关 experimentally probing nature、reasoning under uncertainty、reproducibility、logical argument 等等的 ideas。这些 ideas 已经由我们的 institutions 进行（imperfectly）reified，在学校中传授，并且需要缓慢而艰苦地 mastered。我们的 belief-making processes 似乎更 deeply-rooted 于 human psychology。无论 truth 如何，belief-making 都可能受到以下因素的强烈影响，且不限于这些：repetition；我们对 social acceptance 和 belonging 的 desire；speaker 的 confidence 或 fluency；music、dance、rhythm 和 ritual 的使用；由 authority 来传达。所有这些都是我们的 truth-determining process 需要 guard against 的因素。这两个 processes 并不相同，而且确实不应相同[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn3)。但它们如何 related，以及如何在两者之间实现 healthy relationship，仍值得 exploring。

就我个人而言，Luhrmann 的书让我清楚地意识到，我有多么低估 belief-making process，尤其是 real-making 的重要性。你可以 know 某件事是 true，却并不 deeply believing 它，而这个 gap 很重要。尤其在 science 中，人们非常重视 truth-determining，却低估 belief-making，往往认为它主要是服务于 discovery 的 process 的工具，其 intrinsic interest 较少。这种低估是我的一个错误，而这些笔记开始了纠正这一错误的过程。belief-making process 极其重要。

## 我是 religious 的吗？究竟什么是 religion？

我几年前第一次读了 Luhrmann 的书。此后，它便经常浮现在我的脑海中。这种强烈反响，很大程度上源于我自己与 religion 的关系。我受洗成为 Catholic。在我 5 岁时，父母送给我一本 Bible stories 和一本儿童百科全书。我读了这两本书，也都很喜欢，但百科全书似乎可靠得多。于是到了 7 岁，我很自然地转向了 atheism，并一直保持至今，只是偶尔会出于咬文嚼字的兴趣而涉足 agnosticism[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn4)。

然而，在此后的几十年间，我逐渐理解到，belief in God 并不总是在 religion 中处于 central 地位。那种想法似乎是 Abrahamic religions 的一种 parochial feature，体现在 Moses 的 first commandment 中：“我是你的 Lord、你的 God……在我之前，你不可有其他 Gods”；也体现在 Jesus 的 great commandment 中：“你应当用全部的 heart、全部的 soul 和全部的 mind 来 love 你的 Lord、你的 God”；还体现在 Islam 的 shahada 中，这是该 faith 的首要且最 fundamental 的 requirement：“除了 Allah 之外，没有 god，而 Muhammad 是 Allah 的 messenger。”

Abrahamic religions 将其 God 置于中心，而在其他 religions 中，一个 god 或多个 gods 所起的作用没那么 central，有时甚至远非 central。在 Buddhism 中，关于 cosmos 的整体 nature，有 14 个 unanswerable questions：它是如何开始的、有多古老，等等。Buddha 告诉我们，不必为这些整体性问题烦恼，重要的是 addressing suffering。为了说明这一点，他讲了一个 parable：一个人被毒箭射中，却拒绝让人拔箭，非要先知道是谁射的、那是什么样的箭，等等。他所 advocating 的是一种 internal、personal view，着眼于 ending suffering，并最终 reaching enlightenment，而不需要 third-party god。因此，Buddhism 的某些 variants 与 atheism 是 compatible 的，也就不足为奇了。

当然，究竟什么使一个人是 religious 的，或者不是，这是一个 contentious 的问题。读了 Joe Carlsmith 关于他所称的 _deep atheism_ 的论述后，我的理解发生了变化[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn5)。在 Carlsmith 的叙述中，deep atheist 是这样的人：他们 believes cosmos 是 fundamentally untrustworthy 的，而我们必须 control nature，才能保护自己免受危险。例如，许多担忧 artificial superintelligence 带来 existential risk 的人都是 deep atheists，他们 believing cosmos 的 nature 使得 humanity 很可能 create 一种会 causing human extinction 的 technology。Carlsmith 将其中一位——Eliezer Yudkowsky——列为 prototypical deep atheist[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn6)。

相比之下，不是 deep atheist 的人基本上 trusts cosmos，相信“一切都会好起来，一切都会好起来，各种各样的事情都会好起来”。Carlsmith 以 Jesuit mystic 和 visionary Teilhard de Chardin 为例；他如此强烈地 believed humanity 注定会 achieve 一个 ultimate Omega Point，以至于这种 destiny obviates extinction threats。这是一种非常强烈的 faith：无需过多 control，事情也会有好的结果[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn7)。

“deep atheist”这个 term 是个 misnomer，因为这种 quality 并不取决于一个人是否 believes 一个 god 或多个 gods exist——它关乎 cosmos 的 nature，以及我们与它的 relationship。但它促使我思考，究竟什么使一个人是 religious 的，或者不是。Deep atheism 指出了我们 orient toward 世界的方式中的一条很深的 axis——我们对这个问题的 stance，往往是一种非常强烈的 default psychological orientation。而一个人是否是 deep atheist，并不是一个 scientific question——尽管我们可能受到 evidence 或 scientific explanations 的影响，但它 fundamentally 不是我们据此决定的事情。而且，我们也不能退回 neutrality。无论你愿不愿意，你对这个问题都有一个 stance。从这个意义上说，deep atheism 这个问题在我看来是 religious 的，尽管它并不涉及 God。

就我自己而言，我现在 believe，理解 religion 的最好方式，是把它看作关乎某一组这类面向 cosmos 的 basic stances。Deep atheism 就是其中之一：你觉得 cosmos 有多 trustworthy（或不 trustworthy），以及你觉得需要多少 control。但在我看来，还有一些其他 stances 在 nature 上也是 religious 的。对于什么构成这样的 stance，我没有一个好的 principled criterion，但有一份清单，虽然并不完整，却让我直觉上觉得恰当，其中包括：你对 cosmos 有什么 obligations；你在多大程度上 believe cosmos 可以被 understood；你如何 relate to mystery 和 creative possibility；你认为什么是 success；哪些 principles 是你愿意为某些事物 make personal sacrifices 的基础。这样看来，每个人都是 religious 的，因为我们对这些问题都有 stances，即使它们不是 explicit 的。没有这些 stances，是一种 extreme apathy；religious 的反面并不是成为 atheist，而是处于 depressed 状态。从这个意义上说，我是一个 deeply religious atheist。而且，正如前面的 Buddhism 例子所说明的，你是否 believe in 一个 god 或多个 gods，只是一个人可以成为 religious 的许多 axes 之一[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn8)。

我知道，这一切都很含糊。我们已经从 religion 的一个简单 litmus test——“你 believe in 一个 god 或多个 gods 吗？”——转向了一组未明确说明的“basic stances”，无论那究竟是什么意思。这些 stances 是什么？是什么使它们 particular to religion，而不是更广泛的 psychology 事实？是否由此可得，big-5 personality traits 之类的东西也是“religious”的？那似乎很荒谬。

我暂且把这条思路搁置在这里，不过很希望听到其他人深思熟虑的评论。我想说的是，它与 Luhrmann 的几项 observations 相当吻合。她将成为 religious 描述为“experience 世界是 responsive 且 alive 的”。她还描述了 religious impulse 的核心：“imagine 一个超越眼前世界的世界的 capacity”。而 participation in religion 的核心“是一个 responsive world，一种觉得世界是 alive、aware、intelligent、interested 的感受……\[那些 spiritual senses\]：那种对穿过大地而脉动着的 aliveness 的 awareness，是用 mind 的 senses felt 到的”[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn9)。最后，是我最喜欢的书中的一句话：它似乎有些 specific to Christianity，尽管许多 religions 中都有类似 experiences。一个男人这样描述他的 conversion experience：“我连续几周都去了 \[church\]，听了 Bible，而它在 addressing 我，在亲自对我 speaking……我逐渐 realizing，这是一部 love story，而且是写给我的。”我不是 Christian，但第一次读到这里时，我哭了。那是一个极其迷失、极其孤独的男人，开始感受到自己被爱着。

如果我们以这样的方式 conceive of religion，那么谁不想 cultivate religion 的 skill 呢？而且，如果 religious impulse 被 understood 为一种可以 developed 的 skilful practice，那么谁不想 develop 那种 skill 呢？我猜，作家 Julian Barnes 那句挑衅性的话指向的正是这一点：“我不 believe in God，但我想念他。”当然，这并不能 solve 应当选择 _哪一种_ religion 的 problem，也不能 solve insincere belief 根本不算 belief 的 problem。许多 religions 是 dogmatic 的，深陷于我们知道是 false 的 beliefs。它们往往是 authoritarian 的，或者有强烈的 centralizing impulses。它们的 change 往往极其缓慢[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn10)。不过，这与其说是 religions 所 intrinsic 的，不如说是它们起源时代的 consequence。数千年来，我们人类已经越来越善于 developing 那些 decentralized、non-authoritarian，并且能够迅速 change 和 improve 的 institutions[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn11)。不过，我们的主要 religions 并不是围绕这些 ideas 建立的。对包括我自己在内的许多人来说，这构成了 participation 的巨大 barrier[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn12)。

## 让其他 intangible entities 成为 real

我对 "How God Becomes Real" 感兴趣，部分原因是，我们大多数人所“know”的许多事物，都只是隐约有所感受。我们都 know atoms exist——但我们大多数人无法 prove 这一点，对于为什么、这意味着什么，或我们如何 know，至多也只有非常 indirect understanding。我们“know” atoms 是 real，却并不 deeply believe。我们隐约 understand 一些 consequences，也察觉还有更多，却并未真正领会它们。我们还没有 made atoms real。或者，稍微更准确地说：我们大多数人只是让它们有了一点 felt real。而 atoms 并不是唯一如此的事物。其他许多重要却 intangible[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn13) 的 phenomena 也具有这种 quality。

我们 real-make intangibles 的一种方式，是通过 communities。例如，在某种意义上，microbiologists 为彼此 make microbes real，远比它们对 laypeople 而言更加 real。当然，所有受过教育的人都“believe” microbes。也许我们曾在 microscope 下见过它们（却不 understand 它的 operation），或者在纪录片中见过（但我们怎么 know 那不只是 computer graphics？）。而且，我们“know”池塘中的 orange colour，或孩子的 ear infection，都是 microbes 造成的。但我们当中有多少人能从头 prove microbes 是 real？有多少人真正 understand antibiotics 如何 work、bacteria 与 archaea 有什么 difference，以及 endosymbiosis 的 evidence 是什么？通过这些以及无数其他方式，microbes 对 microbiologists[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn14) 而言，都远比对我们其余大多数人更加 real。他们举办 workshops，不停地谈论 microbes，understand 许多 detailed properties，并将这些 properties 相互 relate。最重要的是：他们不断 make use of microbes，有时用于日常活动——比如制作奶酪或酸奶——有时则用于更加专门的活动，比如[对 E. coli 进行 engineering，使其在 detect 饮用水中的 arsenic 时 change colour](https://2006.igem.org/wiki/index.php/Edinburgh_summary_page)。这是一个围绕 microbes 令人惊讶的 properties 和 affordances 而形成的 community。它们是 _useful_ 的！而 microbiologists 一生都沉浸在这种 use 之中。

当然，microbes 与 God 之间存在一个重要 difference：任何人都可以使用合适的 equipment，reliably detect microbes 并 work with microbes。在许多 religious communities 中，与 God 的 direct interaction 仅限于少数人，或者至少不是常规活动，更难 repeat 和 verify，而且往往会引来 community members（以及 outsiders）更多的 skepticism[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn15)。当有人说自己一直在 Petri dish 中 working with E. coli 时，他们很容易给出 evidence，证明自己确实如此；当一位 Priest 说 God 希望你给他买一辆 Porsche 时，即使最 devout 的人也可能会有些 skeptical。不过，microbiology 这样的 specialist communities 中的 real-making processes，与 religious communities 中的 real-making processes，仍有许多共同之处。其中包括 repetition、variation、socialization[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn16)、status-making、ritual、developing multiple representations，以及 developing affordances 等 practices。Luhrmann 书中的许多内容，都可以看作对 religious communities 如何赋予 God affordances 的讨论[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn17)。

在另一个领域，我见过的最好的 real-making examples 之一，来自 IBM 的 Don Eigler 的 scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM) lab；他以在 1989 年用 16 个 xenon atoms 拼出“IBM”而闻名。几年后，Eigler 的 postdoc Hari Manoharan（如今是 Stanford 的 professor）给我看了 STM lab bench 上方天花板中的一个巨大 speaker。他解释说，STM labs 的惯常做法是在 monitor 上 display STM current，但这意味着，他必须不断把目光从双手正在进行的操作移向 monitor，再移回来。改为将 current signal 作为 sound 播放，他就能随时“hear” surface。在 surface 上移动时，他能 hear lattice defects、distortions，以及许多其他 phenomena。而随着他投入数千个小时，他 hear surface 的能力越来越好，就像 conductor 训练自己聆听 orchestra 的 music 一样。他正在让它对自己而言越来越 felt-real。确实：他能够 hear 非凡的 detail，几乎不可能用任何其他方式加以描述，在双手与双耳之间形成了一个美妙的 loop。

（顺带一提，许多 intangible entities 的起源故事都很引人注目。money、gods 和 GDP 之类的事物 arise 于 intersubjective social reality，是我们彼此达成的 agreements 的 consequence。它们具有 real consequences，但其 fundaments 在 nature 上主要是 social 的[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn18)。Microbes 则以不同的方式 arise：cosmos 以出乎意料的方式对我们作出回应，往往首先通过某个 smoking gun experiment 被鲜明地 revealed。我觉得很有意思的一点是，从外部看来，这些 experiments 往往似乎塞满了枯燥的细节。例如，physics 中有一种 effect，叫作 quantum Hall effect。如果我描述它，大多数人的脑子很快就会被各种细节弄得麻木：MOSFET、2-dimensional electron gas、apply a transverse voltage、quantized resistance，诸如此类，没完没了。整件事似乎都是为某个特定目的量身定做的，充满无趣的细节。谁会对此感兴趣呢？但这种量身定做之感是一种 illusion。quantum Hall effect 实际上 reveals 的是：_electrons 并不如它们看起来的那样_。它迫使我们对 electrons 究竟是什么形成深刻得多、深刻得多的 understanding。experiment 中那些看似枯燥的细节，只是 creating 某种 setting 时的附带之物，而这种 setting 鲜明地 reveals：我们对于 electrons（以及 cosmos）是什么的 understanding，需要发生 profound change。而这种 change 的 implications _无处不在_，并非只有在你 create 恰到好处的 2-dimensional electron gas _等等等等_ 时才存在。这很常见：experiments 往往看起来是一大堆毫无意义的细节，却会 change 我们对 reality 的 conception，revealing 新的 intangible entities，从而 reorganize 我们对世界的 conception。你需要越过那些细节，才能看到 conception 的这种 change。）

（与此相关的是，我们认为是 real 的许多事物其实并不是，这让整个讨论变得 complicated。一个 human being 看起来非常 solid、非常 material，似乎再 real 不过了。然而，我们是 metastable patterns，我们的 atoms 在不断 turning over。事实上，在 quantum field theory 中，连 atoms 的 identity 这一 notion 都是 complicated 的。一个人身上 real 的并不是 material，而是使 durable continuity 成为可能的 underlying complex patterns。这并不 obvious，也很难 make precise，但尽管如此，我们所 believe 的关于 human 的 folk conception 几乎是一种 illusion，这一点仍然是 true 的。那个“real”的 human 要难以确定得多。）

回到主线：microbe 的 example 让人觉得，我似乎把“making real”当作 understanding more deeply 的 synonym——让 intangible entities 更 vivid，understanding 它们如何 relate to 其他 entities，赋予它们更多 affordances，等等。但这并不完全准确：我们可能完全确信一座山是 real，却仍然可以 understand 它更 deeply。不过，对于更加 intangible 的 entities，understanding 似乎是我们 make things real 的方式中的重要部分，而 communities 往往发挥着很大作用。我们拥有 specialist real-making communities，帮助 make real black holes、radio waves、Neptune、gravity 对 tides 的 effect、complex numbers、GDP、chemical bond、DNA、tree of life、immune system，以及许多其他 phenomena。从这个意义上说，real-making 似乎与 God 没有多大关系。事实上，Luhrmann 的 argument 中有许多部分是 quasi-secular 的——God 的 exact qualities 往往几乎与她的 argument 无关。“God”只是一个 abstract word。虽然并非总是如此，但具体是哪一位 God，其细节往往除了 superficial 层面之外并不怎么重要，这种情况之常见令人惊讶。从这个意义上说，我不认为 God 与其他 intangible entities 之间存在任何 clear demarcation：它们都不同程度地经历 real-making 的 process。

一个居于两者之间、可以说明这种模糊性的 example 是：money 并不是“real”的；它是一套 ritualized social agreements 和 behaviours，旨在 solve 一组围绕 scarcity 和 allocation of resources 的 problems。我们通过这些 behaviours，让 god Mammon 成为 real。在许多方面，这些 rituals 比许多其他 religions 中的 rituals 都要 detailed、decentralized 和 contingent 得多。不过，我们对待 money 的方式与 religion 中的许多内容，仍有许多 similarities。我知道这样说或许显得粗俗，但我并无此意：我是在指出一种 genuine similarity。

这就提出了一个 puzzle。如果 God 是纯粹的 fiction、纯粹的 invention，那么 believe 的能力似乎就值得 study。但 money 同样是纯粹的 invention，却似乎并不存在如何 believe 它的真正 problem。的确，从使用某种特定 currency 的 community 外部来看，money 显得奇怪；但从内部来看，它的 power 和 consistency 如此强大，以至于它显得非常 real。也许 God 只是一种类似的 fiction？虽然我认为这种 comparison 是 useful 的，但两者存在很大 differences。money 被宣称具有的 qualities——作为 medium of exchange 等等——很容易被 tested。只要不交房租或不付一笔 mortgage payment，money 很快就会对你变得相当 real。而 God 经常被宣称具有的 qualities——作为 cosmos 的 creator 等等——似乎更难 reliably test。从这个意义上说，money 更像 microbes，而不是 God：belief 与 consequences 之间存在更 direct connection，而 religious belief 往往并非如此。这让 God 的 real-making 更加困难。

所有这些，都让 religion 成为一个 useful 的 real-making 的 extreme example。通过 studying 它，我们可以希望更广泛地 improve 我们对 real-making 的 understanding，或许还可以 improve 我们对于如何 expand human capacity to believe intangible 事物的 understanding。（我尤其感兴趣的是 science 中 intangibles 的情况：并没有任何 _a priori_ reason，认为我们的 minds 会 well adapted to understand 世界中 non-evolutionarily relevant 的部分。）prayer、chanting、meditation 等 Techniques，或许能为其他类型的 real-making 提供途径。考虑这件事多少有些 taboo，或许是因为人们有时感觉 sacred 与 profane 之间存在 barrier[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn19)。

写这些笔记时，我一直困惑于自己_为什么_会对 Luhrmann 有如此强烈的反应。部分原因是，我意识到自己有兴趣弄清并 deepening 自己的 religious beliefs。我甚至还不太清楚那些 beliefs 是什么，真的不清楚！我从未明确地 articulated 它们。哪些 stances 对我很重要，而我又希望哪些得到 deepened？我怀疑，healthy religion 会使一组特别 healthy 的 principles 和 concerns 变得 real，使 practitioners 能够与自身、彼此以及 outsiders 都相处得好。它帮助人们与 cosmos 的其余部分达成某种 coming into sync，使 self 与 unself 达成 healthy union。因此，Religions 起着 counterprogramming 的作用，针对那些否则会从日常生活的漩涡中产生的 defaults。世界上的 religions 有一大优点：其中许多都把相聚并 reflect on forgiveness、kindness、courage、death、suffering、obligation 等事情放在中心。因此，我也在思考，自己希望生活中的哪些事情变得更 real，以及为什么。这份清单包括：death（我自己的、亲人的，以及 unknown other 的）；creative possibility；wise optimism；healthy 的 success notion；healthy 的 family、community 和 other notion；“the big here and the long now”；眼前的 here and now；cosmos 的 fundamental knowability；处于“doubt and uncertainty and not knowing”之中；wise materialism。我很喜欢 Iris Murdoch 的这句话：“love 是极其困难的 realization，即 oneself 之外的某个事物是 real”，而从某种意义上说，这也是所有这些事情的一个 standard。这里涉及的是 making other people real[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn20)、making the cosmos real，以及 developing 面向二者的 healthy stances。

我做这件事的部分灵感来自 Albert Einstein。Einstein 经常成为 theists 与 atheists 争夺的对象，双方都希望把他算作自己人（他曾就自己对 God 的 belief 或 non-belief 发表过一些含糊的言论[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn21)）。但读他写给同事的信时，你能感受到一种异常强烈的 belief：cosmos 之下存在 fundamental order，而 human mind 有能力 comprehend 那种 order。这让我很感动，仿佛自己正面对着一位 religious belief 有着惊人 depth 的人，尽管他所沿着的 axis 与通常被认为是 religious 的东西非常不同。因此，我也在思索如何 understanding 并 deepening 自己的 religious beliefs。

我们的生活质量取决于我们选择把 attention focus 在什么上面。而这在很大程度上由我们所属的 communities 所 determined 和 mediated；这些 communities 以不同方式 focus 我们的 attention，使不同的 intangible entities 变得 salient 或 powerful，无论那是 microbes、money、afterlife，还是完全不同的其他东西。从这个意义上说，对我们每个人而言，不同事物有着十分不同的 reality levels。division of labour 导致了对 reality 的 felt belief 也出现了影响深远的 division。healthy religion 似乎会以 healthy 的方式 grounds attention，使恰当的 intangible 事物变得 real。Human experience 本质上是穿过 vast 而 complex 的 cosmos 的一个 tiny、linear、narrative cut；美好的生活与那个 cosmos 处于 healthy communion，而我认为，这需要在恰当的 intangibles 中获得 deep collective grounding。

## 摘录与杂记

现在让我摘出书中的一些想法。我会偶尔引用，但也会按照自己的理解重写，或稍稍岔开话题自由发挥。在试图更好地理解这本书时，如有任何误述，我向 Luhrmann 教授致歉。开始列举之前，我应该先说：这本书打动我的方式，并没有被这些笔记呈现出来。它以一种美好的宽厚，进入他人的 beliefs。我想起了 William James 的“The Varieties of Religious Experience”，那本书也有类似的宽厚精神。你可以只是沉浸在人们置身其 belief 的 experiences 中，而我从中获得了极大的乐趣。

-   **关于 prayer：**我很喜欢 Luhrmann 引用的 Kierkegaard 的话：“prayer 或许不会 change God \[或世界\]，但它会 changes 那个 prays 的人。”这部分 explains 了为什么即使 unanswered prayers 也可能令人满足。Luhrmann 将 prayer 描述为一种 metacognitive practice，她的看法似乎与 Simone Weil 相近：“Attention 达到其最高 degree 时，就是 prayer。它 presupposes faith 和 love……如果我们把 mind 转向 good，那么整个 soul 就不可能不逐渐地、不由自主地被它 attracted。”按照这种看法，praying well 似乎就像学习 pay attention well；这是一种 trainable skill，显然有价值，也很值得弄清如何 improve。原本可能显得颇为模糊的东西，变得极其切实可行。
-   **打消我对“quaint folk belief”的 illusion：**虽然我个人并不 believe in 其中任何一种，但我当然明白，有许多 sincerely devout Christians、Muslims、Buddhists 等等。然而，我一直难以相信，竟然曾有人 sincerely believed in Sumerian Enuma Elish 或 Egyptian Book of the Dead 等等。它们似乎是一种 quaint folk belief，而我不知怎的总觉得：“哦，从来没有人_真的_ believed 那个。”现在，我认为那是错的。人们很可能确实 genuinely believe，其中一些人的 belief 非常深，这源于与 Luhrmann 记述的那些 processes 相似的 processes。实际上，我们能够判断这一点的一个依据是，据说像 Babylonian Berossus 这样有学识的 priests 曾说过[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn22)，mythic 的 creation account 应当被 taken allegorically；除非有些人可能会 believe it literally，否则不会加上这样的 qualifier。有趣的是，从这个意义上说，Luhrmann 使 real-making 对我而言变得更加 real 了。
-   **Mystical experiences 似乎部分地从 attentional feedback loops 中 develop：**Luhrmann 描述了许多 mystical experiences 如何似乎 grounded in 相对常见的 phenomena（例如 sleep paralysis），却随后被赋予新的 meaning，并获得更多 attention；这又可能随着时间推移 transform 人们对该 phenomenon 的 experience，与我们 develop 其他 sensory skill（例如品酒的鉴赏力）的方式并无太大不同。我觉得这一点极有说服力。它使 mystical experience 成为一种 grounded in 日常（但常被忽略的）physiological experiences 的 skilled practice，一种可以 sincerely developed 的 practice。这与敌视 religion 的人经常作出的 fakery presumption 非常不同。虽然我确信确实存在一些 fakery，但在我看来，它作为 universal explanation 的可能性始终极低。相反，human psychology 只是远比我们可能 _a priori_ 设想的丰富。
-   **我自己的恐惧：**对于许多 religious experiences，我有一种奇怪的退缩。我参与过其中一些，但有时仍保持着 analytical 的距离。我怀疑，这在很大程度上只是害怕被一种自己并不完全认可的 belief 占据。这使我无法投入 absorption 和 experience，也遗憾地无法享受 sensory delight。Analysis 和 description 是安全的；participation 则是_被 designed_ 来放松你的 control 的。在讲述 witchcraft 及相关 pagan religions 的优美著作“Drawing Down the Moon”中，Margot Adler 描述了几个例子：一些人原本为了好玩或纯粹的 theater 而开始举行 ceremonies，却惊讶地发现自己开始 believe 了。他们无意间通过 real-making 使自己进入了 belief。我害怕这个 process，而且我认为，这种害怕有些充分的理由。但我也怨恨这种恐惧。
-   **religion 中的 Contradictions 可能 stimulate real-making：**我过去认为，这些是 belief-making 的 bug。当然，小时候意识到 Christian Bible 充满 contradictions，曾令我大为反感。但现在我倾向于认为，对 devout 而言，contradictions 是一种 feature，因为它们要求极其 active engagement，才能 resolve the contradiction。
-   **Repetition（重复）：**Repetition 是 Luhrmann 偶尔提及的一种 real-making technique。让我记下一位熟人讲述的关于 repetition 的故事，我觉得它很有感染力。一位 atheist 熟人从小被作为 Muslim 养育，但后来仍偶尔 participate in Muslim ritual。他去参加了一次 desert retreat，并参与了凌晨 4am 的 Dhikr session，房间里的人将“Allah”repeated 了 400 次。他说，房间里许多人都开始哭泣，包括他自己。“这很难形容，仿佛某堵墙被打破了，而你正在向某种比自己更伟大的东西屈服。”
-   **Einstein 谈 Jesus 的 reality：**有一篇精彩的报纸访谈，Einstein 被问及是否接受 Jesus 的 historicity。他回答：“毫无疑问。任何人读 Gospels，都不可能不感受到 Jesus 的 actual presence。他的 personality 在每个字里 pulsates。没有任何 myth 充满这样的 life。例如，我们从 Theseus 这样的古代 legendary heroes 的叙述中获得的印象，是多么不同。Theseus 以及其他同类 heroes 缺少 Jesus 的 authentic vitality。”
-   **其他留待日后 explore 的 connections：**与 mimesis 的 connections；与优秀 storytellers、actors 和 directors 所做之事的 connections。_参见_那些一时忘记自己是 actors 的演员的讲述（例如，_1917_ 的一位主演说过，在某些 takes 中有过这种情况）。将 belief equate 为 strong、persistent attentional loop 是否有帮助？寻找 love 事物的新方式。将 Belief 视作一种 practice，一种可以学会并 develop 的 skill。

## 个人的 creative real-making

Luhrmann 的书有一个令我惊讶的方面：它与我从事 solo creative work 的经历产生了联系。当你从事 abstract solo creative work 时，即使对你自己而言，也可能很难觉得这个 project 是 real。一篇 research paper、essay 或一本 book，最初都是一张白纸；一幅 painting，最初则是一块空白画布。你必须在自己的 imagination 中，一遍又一遍地 make it real，而这正是你用来在世界中 realize 它的燃料。向恐惧或倦怠屈服要容易得多。“如果我的 project 根本不好呢？”“如果我做不到呢？”恐惧是 absorption 的敌人。我怀疑，这也是许多 writers 和 artists 容易陷入 ennui（或 alcoholism、depression）的部分原因。在 collaboration 中，或者有 social mirror 时，开展工作要容易得多。如果你缺少这些，就必须以其他方式 real-make。

有一种流传甚广却 unhealthy 的 idea，认为 creativity 是从一种驱动 creator 的 vision 中 emerges 的。“她对自己的 book 有一个 vision”之类。这有时或许是真的，但我怀疑大部分都是胡扯。我怀疑，大多数 books——当然也包括大多数优秀的 creative books——起初都只有许多 vague hunches 和 false starts，只是逐渐才有了某种清晰的轮廓。Books 是通过 exploration 被 discovered 的，而不是作为某个 vision 的 realizations 被写下来的。这就是 creative exploration 的含义。而这并不总是那么容易。人很容易逐渐疏离，不再 believing in 这个 project。你需要找到一些方式，making 这个 creative possibility real。你_并不_知道那里有什么，但你需要 convince 自己，那里有不可思议的宝藏，值得深入 explore。

因此，你要尽可能做好这种针对 creative possibility 的 real-making。你为自己 create rituals。你将它 socialize。你出门旅行，为这个 project interview 人们，create posters 来 explain 它。只要能 real-make，能为你的 project 带来更多 aliveness，让它不那么 abstract，什么办法都可以。而理想情况下，你会以 healthy 的方式 real-make。Imagining 未来的荣耀、bestsellers、money 和赞美，是一种诱人的 real-making imagination，但它有许多弊端。当然，我希望摆脱 efficiency 和 fame 这些 drivers。（还在努力！）更好的做法是，努力为你的 creative projects developing 更 healthier 的 real-making approaches，找到一些方式，gradually deepening absorption，不断把 attention 带回眼前的 possibility。我最喜欢的 examples 之一，是这张 Stewart Brand 待在 shipping container 里的照片；他就是在那里写出了那本精彩的书 "How Buildings Learn"：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/shipping.jpg)

这是极好的 real-making，是一种让 creative possibility 变得非常 vivid 的方式。正如我在关于 ["Developing Creative Identity"](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html) 的 essay 中所写的：“这个男人在_认真_对待自己的 creative work。这个空间的一切都传达着一个信息：这个 project 很重要。这是一个供他借以 think 的空间，帮助他 organize 自己的 thinking，并让他在情感上与自己的 project connect。它是 adaptable 的，能够随着他正在做的事情而 evolve；但与此同时，它也是 organized 的，很可能 reflecting 并 supporting 着这本 book 的 organization。”

另一个佐证来自 designer Jony Ive 为 Steve Jobs 所作的悼词：“Steve 过去常对我说，而且他说过很多次：‘嘿，Jony，我有个傻乎乎的 idea。’有时那些 idea 确实如此。真的很傻。有时简直糟透了。但有时，它们会让房间里的空气仿佛凝固，让我们两个人都彻底沉默。那些 ideas 大胆、疯狂、壮丽。或者是安静而 simple 的 ideas，却在 subtlety 和 detail 上无比 profound。正如 Steve 热爱 ideas，也热爱 making stuff，他对待 creativity 的 process，怀着一种罕见而美妙的敬畏。你看，我认为他比任何人都更明白：尽管 ideas 最终可能如此 powerful，但它们起初只是 fragile、barely formed 的 thoughts，那么容易被错过，那么容易被损害，那么容易就被压扁。”

在我最引以为傲的作品中，许多都属于 real-making。这是 [discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html) 尤其擅长的事情，或许是因为它涉及从许多 angles 对一个 subject 进行 exploration，从 fundamentals 开始，反复而逐渐地 elaborating，伴随着巨大的 cumulative imagination。最终的成果是送给 reader 的礼物，切实 expansion 了他们对世界的 felt sense。因此，或许我作出贡献的一种独特方式，就是 real-making。

## 结语

我在文学中最喜欢的一句话，出自 Yann Martel 的书 "Life of Pi"。据作者说，他遇到过一位老人，老人对他说：“我有一个 story，会让你 believe in God。”这是一个令人惊叹的 real-making 承诺，我曾为此反复思索。作为一个虔诚的 atheist，我自然而然地想到：有没有可能存在一个 story，让听众 believe in the cosmos？我甚至不太知道那会是什么意思。这几乎像是一个 category error。但我把这句话说给了朋友 Hannu Rajaniemi 听，他是一位思想深刻的 writer；他回答说，这听起来或许是他听过的最好的 artistic mission statement。因此，我一直在琢磨它。从某种意义上说，我们没有谁真正 believe in the cosmos——并没有真正如此。我们的 experience，只是对 vast 而 complex 的 cosmos 的一道 tiny、linear、narrative cut；good life 是与这个 cosmos 处于 healthy communion 的生活，而我认为，这需要 grounding 于恰当的 intangibles。这就是 religion 之所以重要：它提供了一种让我们 grounding 的方式，一种 healthy 的 believing in the cosmos 的方式。

May 8, 2026

_Working notes on Tanya M. Luhrmann's book "How God Becomes Real: Kindling the Presence of Invisible Others". I wrote these as background for a large creative project, and as such the notes are intended primarily for my own use. I'm sharing them publicly so others can, if they wish, read over my shoulder. Be warned: they are exploratory, incomplete, and underdeveloped. Thoughtful comments are most welcome -- please add in the comments section at the end._

In Alan Watts' book "The Wisdom of Insecurity" he points out that many people _believe_ there are benefits to _believing in God_, but that is not nearly the same as actually believing in God. The mere fact we understand a belief would be beneficial to us doesn't automatically make us sincerely believe it. Far from it! As Watts says:

> It may be necessary for man to have a myth, but he cannot self-consciously prescribe one… man cannot for long knowingly and intentionally “kid” himself. Even the… most forceful arguments for some sort of return to orthodoxy are those which show the social and moral advantages of belief in God. But this does not prove that God is a reality. It proves, at most, that believing in God is useful… if the public has any suspicion that he does not exist, the invention is in vain.

That is: a belief isn't a mere choice, something which can be adopted when it seems beneficial. Rather, it is something associated to a genuine, sincere, durable felt conviction. Indeed, that conviction may even be held despite it being inconvenient or even disadvantageous. If it is not possible to hold in the face of such challenges, then arguably it was not a belief. This lack of volition in either choice or rejection helps beliefs serve as durable glue binding communities.

So how do people come to believe? Tanya M. Luhrmann has written a beautiful book exploring this question, "How God Becomes Real: Kindling the Presence of Invisible Others". The book explores the idea that much of the purpose of religious practice is to help practitioners believe. This inverts conventional wisdom, with Luhrmann taking seriously the possibility that sometimes people aren't worshipping because they believe, but rather believing because they worship. More generally: Luhrmann makes a compelling case that there is a much more complex relationship between belief and religious practice than you might naively suppose, and she explores some of that relationship.

Let me review a few of the moves Luhrmann makes in setting up her project. She points out, convincingly, that religious belief usually _isn't_ something easily attained, despite the fact that many theories of religion "presume that belief is direct and unproblematic – that in most cultures, people simply take spirit and the supernatural to be there. That doesn't make sense. Gods and spirits cannot be seen. You cannot shake their hands, look them in the eye, or hear their voice when they speak. It seems odd to assume that people just take for granted that they are present."

So, rather than assume religious people believe easily, Luhrmann asks whether "the effort people invest in their faith helps them to feel that their gods and spirits are real", "how those practices themselves might affect those who engage in them", and "whether the practices themselves help to make the beliefs compelling"? It's a viewpoint in which belief comes to seem more like a skillful practice than a state. She argues:

> And once we allow ourselves to ask whether people everywhere need to persuade themselves that their gods are real, religious rituals suddenly make more sense. After all, if spirits are believed to be unproblematically present—simply there, responsive and available—why do you need an all-night drumming ceremony to call them forth? If God is always present and aware, why does anyone need to pray?
> 
> I argue here that the puzzle of religion is not the problem of false belief, but the question of how gods and spirits become and remain real to people and what this real-making does for humans. This is not a claim that gods are not real or that people who are religious feel doubt. Many people of faith never express doubt; they talk as if it were obvious that their gods are real. Yet they go to great lengths in their worship. They build grand cathedrals at vast cost in labor, time, and money. They spend days, even weeks, preparing for rituals, assembling food, building ritual sites, and gathering participants. They create theatrical effects in sacred spaces—the dim lighting in temples, the elaborate staging in evangelical megachurches—that enhance a sense of otherness but are not commanded in the sacred texts. They fast. They wear special clothes. They chant for hours. They set out to pray without ceasing.
> 
> Of course, one might say: they believe, and so they build the cathedrals. I am asking what we might learn if we shift our focus: if, rather than presuming that people worship because they believe, we ask instead whether people believe because they worship. I suggest that prayer and ritual and worship help people to shift from knowing in the abstract that the invisible other is real to feeling that gods and spirits are present in the moment, aware and willing to respond. I will call this “real-making,” and I think that the satisfactions of its process explain—in part—why faiths endure.
> 
> By “real-making,” I mean that the task for a person of faith is to believe not just that gods and spirits are there in some abstract way, like dark energy, but that these gods and spirits matter in the here and now. I mean not just that you know that they are real, the way you know that the floor is real (or would, if you paused to think about it), but that they feel real the way your mother’s love feels real. I mean that people of faith come to feel inwardly and intimately that gods or spirits are involved with them.

With that context, Luhrmann sums up her basic claim:

> god or spirit—the invisible other—must be made real for people, and that this real-making changes those who do it. When I look at the social practices that surround what we call religion, I see a set of behaviors that change a practitioner's felt sense of what is real. These behaviors both enable what is unseen to feel more present and alter the person who performs them.

One over-simple reading of the claim is that it contradicts the Watts quote I opened with. But it's better viewed as an elaboration and extension. Watts' fundamental point is correct: we can't simply choose our beliefs, in the way we choose which type of cheese to buy from a supermarket. Rather, the way our beliefs form is a much more complex process. But which beliefs form can be influenced, by our own actions and those of others, and it's possible to understand that process. That is, belief isn't just a state of being, a noun. It's something enacted: in some sense belief is a verb. Belief, the state, is a consequence of belief, the action[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn1). That's not to say what we believe is divorced from the realities of the world, or that all possible beliefs are on an equal footing. Far from it. It is, for example, much easier for someone today to believe that the earth is a near-sphere than that it is flat. Reality and evidence do matter. But they are not the whole story either, and that suggests asking: what is the process by which we come to believe something? Especially something so intangible as God? What different depths and complexities of belief are possible?

In these notes I will explore these questions through the lens of Luhrmann's book, but also connecting to questions outside her ambit. God is far from the only intangible phenomenon we believe in – consider things like microbes, atoms, our own future death, the cosmic microwave background, or GDP. For most of us, those things also require real-making; they are in some important sense less obviously real than everyday objects such as chairs, mountains, and soccer balls. We don't worry about the reality of chairs, mountains, and soccer balls, we take it for granted there is no problem of real-making[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn2). And so I'm also interested in the broader process by which we make real (or fail to make real) intangible phenomena. As I wrote these notes, I kept imagining some crusty atheist saying "oh, this is all just bullshit, religious apologetics, putting a charming gloss on religion." But Luhrmann's ideas about real-making and how we come to believe are relevant to all human understanding of the cosmos, not just religion. They are about how we come to believe in what _is_ and _is not_, perhaps the most fundamental question of all.

Real-making is most obviously interesting when, as with God, the implicit premise is that there are plausible grounds to believe the thing doesn't exist. By contrast, a question like "are microbes real?" has a well-attested and near-universally accepted answer, and so the question "is this real?" seems less obviously of interest. However, there remains Luhrmann's distinction between felt-real and not: does X feel strongly real to me, here, now, in a way that shapes how I think and feel and live? And for most of us, phenomena like microbes and atoms and so on are _not_ strongly felt real. How can we make them more felt real? Luhrmann's project helps illuminate that problem.

## A simple model: separating the belief-making process and the truth-determining process

"How God Becomes Real" makes clear that the way humans come to believe things is very different from the way we establish propositions as true. I've found it useful to think in terms of a simple model in which we separately consider our _belief-making process_ and our _truth-determining process_. Of course, this is oversimplified: we don't have single stable processes for either. And I'm using "truth" as a rough shorthand to mean something like "good, useful model of the world, where we have some sense of the limits of the model". I don't mean some much stronger notion of capital-T truth! Still, with these caveats, I've found this division into a belief-making process and a truth-determining process helpful in my own thinking. Like many scientists, I've spent many years trying to understand and develop truth-determining processes; but much less time trying to understand belief-making processes. Both are important.

It's tempting to think the belief-making and truth-determining processes should be the same. But Luhrmann's book makes clear that they are intrinsically different processes. Our best truth-determining processes are grand, non-obvious achievements of culture – ideas about experimentally probing nature, reasoning under uncertainty, reproducibility, logical argument, and so on. These ideas have been (imperfectly) reified by our institutions, taught in schools, and are mastered slowly and arduously. Our belief-making processes seem more deeply-rooted in human psychology. Regardless of truth, belief-making may be strongly affected by, among other things: repetition; our desire for social acceptance and belonging; the confidence or fluency of the speaker; the use of music, dance, rhythm, and ritual; being delivered by authority. All these are things which our truth-determining process need guard against. The two processes are not the same, and indeed ought not be the same[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn3). But it's worth exploring how they are related, and how to achieve a healthy relationship between the two.

Personally, Luhrmann's book crystallized how much I've underestimated the importance of the belief-making process, and especially real-making. You can know something is true without deeply believing it, and this gap is important. In science, especially, there is a lot of emphasis on truth-determining, and belief-making is underrated, often considered largely instrumental to the process of discovery, and of less intrinsic interest. This underestimation has been a mistake on my part, and one these notes begin the process of rectifying. The belief-making process is tremendously important.

## Am I religious? What is a religion, anyway?

I first read Luhrmann's book several years ago. It's come to mind often ever since. Much of that strong response is grounded in my own relationship to religion. I was baptized Catholic. At age 5, my parents gave me a book of Bible stories and a children's encyclopedia. I read and loved both, but the encyclopedia seemed far more reliable. It was a short step at age 7 to an atheism which has lasted ever since, with occasional pedantic dalliance with agnosticism[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn4).

However, in the decades since I've come to understand that belief in God is not always central to religion. That idea seems to be a parochial feature of the Abrahamic religions, reflected in Moses' first commandment: "I am the Lord your God… you shall have no other Gods before me"; in Jesus' great commandment: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind"; and in the shahada of Islam, the first and most fundamental requirement of the faith: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah."

While the Abrahamic religions center their God, in other religions a god or gods play a less central role, sometimes a much less central role. In Buddhism, there are 14 unanswerable questions about the big-picture nature of the cosmos: how did it start, how old it is, and so on. The Buddha tells us not to worry about these big picture questions, that what matters is addressing suffering. To illustrate, he tells a parable in which a man struck by a poisoned arrow refuses to have it removed, until he knows who shot it, what kind of arrow it was, and so on. He is advocating an internal, personal view, one focused on ending suffering, and eventually reaching enlightenment, without the need for a third-party god. Unsurprisingly, some variants of Buddhism are compatible with atheism.

Of course, the question of what makes a person religious or not is contentious. My understanding was changed by reading Joe Carlsmith on what he calls _deep atheism_[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn5). In Carlsmith's account a deep atheist is a person who believes the cosmos is fundamentally untrustworthy, and we must control nature to protect ourselves from danger. For instance, many people concerned about existential risk from artificial superintelligence are deep atheists, believing the nature of the cosmos is such that humanity is likely to create a technology causing human extinction. Carlsmith names one such person, Eliezer Yudkowsky, as a prototypical deep atheist[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn6).

By contrast, someone who is not a deep atheist essentially trusts the cosmos, that "all will be well, all will be well, and all manner of things will be well". Carlsmith uses the Jesuit mystic and visionary Teilhard de Chardin as an example, someone who believed so strongly that humanity is destined to achieve an ultimate Omega Point that this destiny obviates extinction threats. It's a very strong faith that things will turn out well, without needing too much control[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn7).

The term "deep atheist" is a misnomer, since the quality does not depend on whether one believes a god or gods exist – it's about the nature of the cosmos and our relationship to it. But it got me thinking about what makes someone religious or not. Deep atheism identifies a very deep axis in how we orient toward the world – our stance on this question is often a very strong default psychological orientation. And whether one is a deep atheist is not a scientific question – while we may be influenced by evidence or scientific explanations, it's not fundamentally something we decide on that basis. And we don't get to retreat into neutrality, either. You have a stance on the question, like it or not. In this sense, the question of deep atheism seems to me religious, despite not involving God.

For myself, I now believe religion is best understood as being about some such set of basic stances toward the cosmos. Deep atheism is one: how trustworthy (or not) you feel the cosmos is, and how much control you feel is necessary. But there are other stances which seem to me religious in nature too. I don't have a good principled criterion for what makes such a stance, but a laundry list that seems instinctively right, albeit incomplete, includes: what obligations you have to the cosmos; the extent to which you believe the cosmos may be understood; how you relate to mystery and creative possibility; what you deem success is; what principles underlie what you are willing to make personal sacrifices for. Viewed this way, everyone is religious in that we all have stances on such issues, even if they're not explicit. Not having such stances is a type of extreme apathy; the opposite of religious isn't being an atheist, but rather being depressed. In this sense I'm a deeply religious atheist. And, as the earlier Buddhism example illustrated, whether you believe in a god or gods is merely one of many axes along which one may be religious[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn8).

I realize this is all very wishy-washy. We've gone from a simple litmus test for religion – "Do you believe in a god or gods?" – to an unspecified set of "basic stances", whatever that means. What are those stances? What makes them particular to religion, as opposed to broader facts of psychology? Does it follow that things like the big-5 personality traits are "religious"? That would seem ridiculous.

I'll put a pin in that line of thought for now, though I'd appreciate hearing thoughtful comments from others. I will say that it comports well with several observations Luhrmann makes. She describes what it is to be religious as "to experience the world as responsive and alive". And she describes the heart of the religious impulse: "the capacity to imagine a world beyond the one we have before us". And the heart of participation in religion "is a responsive world, a sense that the world is alive, aware, intelligent, interested… \[the spiritual senses\]: that sense of awareness of the aliveness pulsing through the earth, felt with the senses of the mind"[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn9). And, finally, and seemingly somewhat specific to Christianity, though there are similar experiences in many religions, my favourite line in the book, one man describing his experience of conversion: "I went \[to church\] for several weeks in a row, and I heard the Bible and it was addressing me and speaking to me personally… I was realizing that it is a love story, and it's written to me." I am not a Christian, but I wept the first time I read that. It was a very lost and very alone man, coming to feel loved.

If we conceive of religion in such terms, then who wouldn't want to cultivate the skill of religion? And, insofar as the religious impulse is understood as a skilful practice that can be developed, who wouldn't want to develop that skill? I suspect this is what the author Julian Barnes is pointing at with his provocative line: "I don't believe in God, but I miss him". Of course, this does not solve the problem of _which_ religion, nor the problem that insincere belief is no belief at all. Many religions are dogmatic and mired in beliefs we know to be false. They're often authoritarian, or have strong centralizing impulses. They're often extremely slow to change[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn10). This is not intrinsic to religions, though, so much as a consequence of the time they originated. We humans have over the millennia gotten much better at developing institutions[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn11) which are decentralized, non-authoritarian, and change and improve rapidly. Still, our major religions are not built around such ideas. It's an enormous barrier to participation for many, myself included[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn12).

## Making other intangible entities real

Part of the reason I'm interested in "How God Becomes Real" is because most of us "know" many things we only dimly feel. We all know atoms exist – but most of us could not prove it, and have at best some very indirect understanding of why, or what it means, or how we know. We "know" atoms are real, but we don't deeply believe. We dimly understand a few consequences, and apprehend that there are many more, but don't really appreciate them. We haven't made atoms real. Or, slightly more accurately: most of us have only made them a little felt real. And atoms are not alone. Many other important but intangible[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn13) phenomena have this quality.

One way we real-make intangibles is through communities. For example, there's a sense in which microbiologists make microbes real for one another, far more real than they are for laypeople. Of course, all educated people "believe" in microbes. Maybe we saw them once under a microscope (whose operation we didn't understand), or in a documentary (but how do we know it wasn't just computer graphics?) And we "know" microbes are responsible for the orange colour on the pond, or for our child's ear infection. But how many of us could prove microbes are real from scratch? How many of us really understand how antibiotics work, what is the difference between bacteria and archaea, what the evidence for endosymbiosis is? In these and myriad more ways, microbes are far more real for microbiologists[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn14) than most of the rest of us. They run workshops, talk incessantly about microbes, understand many detailed properties, relate those properties to one another. Most of all: they make use of microbes incessantly, sometimes for everyday activities – like making cheese or yoghurt – and sometimes for more esoteric activities, like [engineering E. coli to change colour when they detect arsenic in drinking water](https://2006.igem.org/wiki/index.php/Edinburgh_summary_page). This is a community oriented around the surprising properties and affordances of microbes. They are _useful_! And microbiologists spend their lives immersed in that use.

Of course, there is an important difference between microbes and God: anyone can reliably detect and work with microbes using suitable equipment. In many religious communities direct interaction with God is limited to a few people, or at least is not routine, is harder to repeat and verify, and often attracts more skepticism from community members (and outsiders)[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn15). When someone says they've been working with E. coli in a Petri dish, it's easy for them to give evidence they really have been; when a Priest says God wants you to buy them a Porsche, even the most devout may be a little skeptical. Still, there is much in common between the real-making processes in specialist communities like microbiology and the real-making processes in religious communities. Among them are practices like repetition, variation, socialization[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn16), status-making, ritual, developing multiple representations, and developing affordances. Much of Luhrmann's book may be read as a discussion of the ways religious communities give God affordances[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn17).

In another realm, one of the best examples of real-making I've ever seen was in the scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM) lab of Don Eigler at IBM, famous for spelling out "IBM" with 16 xenon atoms in 1989. A few years later, Eigler's postdoc Hari Manoharan (now a professor at Stanford) showed me an enormous speaker in the ceiling above the STM lab bench. He explained that it was conventional in STM labs to display the STM current on a monitor, but that it meant he had to keep moving his gaze back and forth from what he was doing with his hands to the monitor. By playing the current signal instead as sound, he could "hear" the surface at all times. He could hear lattice defects, distortions and many other phenomena as he moved across the surface. And as he spent thousands of hours his ability to hear the surface got better and better and better, like a conductor training to hear an orchestra's music. He was making it more and more felt-real to himself. Indeed: he could hear remarkable detail in a way almost impossible to describe in any other way, a beautiful loop between his hands and his ears.

(Tangentially, the origin stories of many intangible entities are striking. Things like money and gods and GDP arise in intersubjective social reality, as a consequence of agreements we make with each other. They have real consequences, but the fundaments are primarily social in nature[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn18). Microbes arise in a different way, the cosmos pushing back on us in unexpected ways, often first sharply revealed through some smoking gun experiment. Something I find fascinating is that from the outside those experiments often seem overfull of dull detail. As an example, there is an effect in physics called the quantum Hall effect. If I describe it, most people's minds quickly go numb with details: MOSFET, 2-dimensional electron gas, apply a transverse voltage, quantized resistance, blah blah blah blah blah blah. The whole thing seems hopelessly bespoke, filled with uninteresting detail. Who could possibly be interested? But that bespokeness is an illusion. What the quantum Hall effect actually reveals is that _electrons are not as they seem_. It forced a much, much deeper understanding of what electrons are. The seemingly dull details of the experiment are incidental to creating a setting which sharply reveals the need for a profound change in what we understand electrons (and the cosmos) to be. And that change has implications _everywhere_, not merely if you create exactly the right 2-dimensional electron gas _etc etc_. This is common: experiments often look like a lot of mindless detail, but change our conception of reality, revealing new intangible entities which reorganize our conception of the world. You need to look past the detail to see that change in conception.)

(In a related vein, this entire discussion is complicated by the fact that much of what we think of as real isn't. A human being seems very solid and material, as real as can be. And yet we are metastable patterns, with our atoms constantly turning over. Indeed, even the notion of identity for atoms is complicated in quantum field theory. What is real about a person is not the material, but the underlying complex patterns which enable durable continuity. This is not obvious, and hard to make precise, but it is nonetheless true that the the folk conception of a human which we believe in is very nearly an illusion. The "real" human is much harder to pin down.)

Back to the main line: the microbe example makes it seem as though I'm using "making real" as a synonym for understanding more deeply – making intangible entities more vivid, understanding how they relate to other entities, giving them more affordances, and so on. But that isn't quite right: we may be fully convinced a mountain is real, but we can still understand it more deeply. Still, for more intangible entities, understanding seems to be an important part of the way we make things real, and communities often play a large role. We have specialist real-making communities which help make real black holes, radio waves, Neptune, the effect of gravity on the tides, complex numbers, GDP, the chemical bond, DNA, the tree of life, the immune system, and many other phenomena. In this sense, it seems as though real-making has nothing much to do with God. Indeed, many parts of Luhrmann's argument are quasi-secular – the exact qualities of God are often almost irrelevant to her argument. "God" is just an abstract word. Not always, but it's surprising how often the details of which God don't much matter except in a superficial way. In that sense, I don't think there's any clear demarcation between God and other intangible entities: all go through a process of real-making, to differing extents.

An intermediate example which illustrates the blurriness: money isn't "real": it's a set of ritualized social agreements and behaviours, intended to solve a set of problems around scarcity and allocation of resources. We make the god Mammon real through those behaviours. The rituals are, in many respects, much more detailed and decentralized and contingent than in many other religions. Still, there are many similarities between the way we treat money and much of religion. I realize this perhaps sounds crass, but that is not my intent: I am pointing at a genuine similarity.

This sets up a puzzle. If God is pure fiction, pure invention, then that makes the ability to believe seem worthy of study. But money is also pure invention, and yet there seems to be no real problem about how to believe in it. It's true that from outside a community which uses a particular currency, money looks peculiar; from inside, the power and consistency is so strong that it seems very real. Perhaps God is merely a similar kind of fiction? While I think the comparison is useful, there are large differences. The claimed qualities of money – as a medium of exchange and so on – can easily be tested. Skip rent or a mortgage payment and money will quickly be made quite real to you. Whereas God's oft-claimed qualities – as creator of the cosmos and so on – seem harder to reliably test. In that sense, money is more like microbes than God: there's a more direct connection between belief and consequences than is often the case in religious belief. That makes real-making harder for God.

All this makes religion useful as an extreme example of real-making. By studying it we can hope to improve our understanding of real-making more generally, and perhaps of how we expand human capacity to believe in the intangible. (I am especially interested in the case of intangibles in science: there is no _a priori_ reason for our minds to be well adapted to understand the non-evolutionarily relevant parts of the world.) Techniques such as prayer, chanting, meditation, and so on perhaps offer avenues for other types of real-making. There is some taboo on considering this, perhaps a consequence of the barrier sometimes felt between sacred and profane[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn19).

As I write these notes I've puzzled about _why_ I respond so strongly to Luhrmann. Part of it is realizing that I'm interested in figuring out and deepening my own religious beliefs. I don't even quite know what those beliefs are, not really! I've never sharply articulated them. What stances are important to me, and would I like deepened? I suspect a healthy religion is one which makes real a particularly healthy set of principles and concerns, one that enables practitioners to live well in themselves, with one another, and with outsiders. It helps enable a kind of coming into sync with the rest of the cosmos, bringing self into healthy union with unself. Religions thus work as counterprogramming for defaults that would otherwise arise out of the maelstrom of everyday life. One of the great things about the world's religions is the way many have centered coming together to reflect on things like forgiveness, kindness, courage, death, suffering, obligation, and so on. And so I'm reflecting on some of the things I'd like to make more real in my own life, and why. That list includes: death (my own, that of loved ones, and of the unknown other); creative possibility; wise optimism; a healthy notion of success; a healthy notion of family and community and of the other; "the big here and the long now"; the immediate here and now; the fundamental knowability of the cosmos; in "doubt and uncertainty and not knowing"; a wise materialism. I love Iris Murdoch's line that "love is the extremely difficult realization that something other than oneself is real", and in some sense this is a standard for all of this. It's about making other people real[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn20), making the cosmos real, and developing healthy stances toward both.

Part of my inspiration for this endeavour is Albert Einstein. Einstein is often at the center of a tug-of-war between theists and atheists, each of whom would like to claim him for their own (he made somewhat ambiguous statements about his own belief or non-belief in God[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn21)). But in reading his letters to colleagues you sense an extraordinary strength of belief in a fundamental order underlying the cosmos, and in the human mind's capacity to comprehend that order. I find it very moving, feeling myself to be in the presence of a person with an incredible depth of religious belief, albeit along an axis very different from what is usually considered religious. And so I wonder about understanding and deepening my own religious beliefs.

The quality of our lives depends on what we choose to focus attention on. And much of that is determined and mediated by the communities we are part of, which focus our attention in different ways, and make different intangible entities salient or powerful, whether it's microbes, money, the afterlife, or something else entirely. In this sense, for each of us, different things have quite different levels of reality. The division of labour results in a consequential division of felt belief in reality. A healthy religion seems like one that grounds attention in healthy ways, making the right intangible things real. Human experience is intrinsically a tiny, linear, narrative cut through a vast and complex cosmos; a good life is in healthy communion with that cosmos, and that, I think, requires deep collective grounding in the right intangibles.

## Excerpts, miscellanea

Let me now excerpt a bundle of ideas from the book. I'll quote occasionally, but will also rewrite in terms of my own understanding, or riff on slight tangents. My apologies to Professor Luhrmann for any misrepresentation as I try to understand the book better. I should say before getting to my list: I found the book moving in a way not captured in these notes. It inhabits other people's beliefs with a lovely generosity. I was reminded of William James's "The Varieties of Religious Experience", which has a similar generosity of spirit. You can simply dwell in the experiences of people in their belief, and I get tremendous enjoyment out of that.

-   **On prayer:** I enjoyed Luhrmann quoting Kierkegaard: "prayer may not change God \[or the world\], but it changes the person who prays". This partly explains why even unanswered prayers may be fulfilling. Luhrmann describes prayer as a metacognitive practice, and her view seems similar to that of Simone Weil: "Attention, taken to its highest degree, is the same thing as prayer. It presupposes faith and love… If we turn our mind toward the good, it is impossible that little by little the whole soul will not be attracted thereto in spite of itself." In this view, praying well seems like learning to pay attention well, which is a trainable skill, obviously of value, and a promising target to figure out how to improve. What might seem rather nebulous becomes eminently practical.
-   **Disabusing me of the "quaint folk belief" illusion:** While I don't personally believe in any of them, I certainly understand that there are many sincerely devout Christians, Muslims, Buddhists, and so on. But I've always had trouble believing anyone ever sincerely believed in the Sumerian Enuma Elish, or the Egyptian Book of the Dead, and so on. They seemed like a kind of quaint folk belief, and I somehow felt "oh, no-one ever _really_ believed that". And now I think that was wrong. People likely did genuinely believe, some of them very deeply, due to processes similar to those Luhrmann documents. Indeed, one way in which we can tell is that sophisticated priests such as the Babylonian Berossus supposedly said[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/luhrmann/index.html#fn22) that the mythic account of creation was to be taken allegorically; one does not add such a qualifier unless some are likely to believe it literally. Amusingly, in this sense, Luhrmann has made real-making much more real to me.
-   **Mystical experiences seem to develop in part out of attentional feedback loops:** Luhrmann describes how many mystical experiences seem to be grounded in relatively common phenomena (e.g., sleep paralysis), but then are given a new meaning and receive much more attention, which in turn over time may transform people's experience of the phenomenon, not unlike the way we develop any other sensory skill (e.g., a taste for wines). I found this an extremely compelling point. It makes mystical experience into a skilled practice grounded in routine (but often ignored) physiological experiences, a practice which may be sincerely developed. This is quite distinct from the presumption of fakery often made by people hostile to religion. While I'm sure there is some fakery, it has always seemed to me extremely unlikely as a universal explanation. Rather, human psychology is simply much richer than we might _a priori_ suppose.
-   **My own fear:** I'm oddly reticent about many religious experiences. I've participated in some, but I sometimes remain at an analytical remove. I suspect much of this is just fear of being overtaken by a belief I don't entirely approve of. This holds me back from absorption and experience and, sadly, sensory delight. Analysis and description are safe; participation is _designed_ to loosen your control. In her beautiful book "Drawing Down the Moon", about witchcraft and related pagan religions, Margot Adler describes several instances where groups of people began doing ceremonies for fun or pure theater, and discovered to their surprise that they were beginning to believe. They were accidentally real-making themselves into belief. I fear this process with, I think, some good reason. But I also resent that fear.
-   **Contradictions in religion may stimulate real-making:** I used to think of these as a bug for belief-making. Certainly, as a child realizing the Christian Bible is full of contradictions was a big turn off. But I'm now inclined to think that contradictions are a feature for the devout, since they demand extremely active engagement in order to resolve the contradiction.
-   **Repetition:** Repetition is a real-making technique which Luhrmann occasionally mentions. Let me record a story about repetition from an acquaintance which I found powerful. An atheist acquaintance was raised Muslim, but continued occasionally to participate in Muslim ritual. He went on a desert retreat, and participated in a 4am Dhikr session, where people in the room repeated "Allah" 400 times. He reports that many in the room began crying, including himself. "It is hard to describe, it was as if some wall was broken and you were succumbing to something greater than yourself."
-   **Einstein on Jesus's reality:** There's a marvellous newspaper interview where Einstein is asked if he accepts the historicity of Jesus. He replies: "Unquestionably. No one can read the Gospels without feeling the actual presence of Jesus. His personality pulsates in every word. No myth is filled with such life. How different, for instance, is the impression which we receive from an account of legendary heroes of antiquity like Theseus. Theseus and other heroes of his type lack the authentic vitality of Jesus."
-   **Other connections to eventually explore:** to mimesis; to what good storytellers, actors, and directors do. _Cf._ reports from actors who momentarily forget they are actors (e.g., one of the leads in _1917_ reported this on some takes). Is it helpful to equate belief with a strong, persistent attentional loop? Finding new ways to love things. Belief as a practice, a skill to pick up and develop.

## Personal creative real-making

A surprising aspect of Luhrmann's book was making a connection to my experience of solo creative work. When you do abstract solo creative work, it can be difficult for the project to seem real even to you. A research paper or essay or book starts as a blank page; a painting as a blank canvas. It's up to you to make it real, over and over and over, in your own imagination, and that's the fuel you use to realize it in the world. It's easier to give into fear or lassitude. "What if my project isn't any good?" "What if I can't do this?" Fear is the enemy of absorption. I suspect this is part of why many writers and artists are subject to ennui (or alcoholism or depression). It's much, much easier to do work in a collaboration or when there's a social mirror. If you lack those things, you must real-make in other ways.

One widespread-but-unhealthy idea is that creativity emerges out of a vision that the creator is driven by. "She had a vision for her book" _etc_. This may sometimes be true, but I suspect it's mostly bullshit. I suspect most books – certainly, most good creative books – start out with many vague hunches and false starts, and only gradually arrive at any kind of sharpness. Books are discovered through exploration, not set down as realizations of a vision. That's what creative exploration means. And it's not always so easy. It's easy to drift away, to stop believing in the project. You need to find ways of making the creative possibility real. You _don't_ know what is there, but you need to convince yourself there are incredible riches, to go explore in depth.

And so you do this real-making of creative possibility as well as possible. You create rituals for yourself. You socialize it. You go on trips, interview people for the project, create posters to explain it. Anything to real-make, to bring more aliveness to your project, to make it less abstract. And, ideally, you real-make in a healthy way. Imagining future glory and bestsellers and money and adulation is a tempting type of real-making imagination, but it's got many drawbacks. Certainly, I want to free myself of efficiency and fame as drivers. (Working on it!) Better to work hard on developing healthier approaches to real-making for your creative projects, ways of gradually deepening absorption, continually bringing attention back to the possibility before you. One of my favourite examples is this image of Stewart Brand inside the shipping container where he wrote his marvellous book "How Buildings Learn":

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/shipping.jpg)

This is excellent real-making, a way of making the creative possibility very vivid. As I wrote in my essay on ["Developing Creative Identity"](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html): "This is a man taking his creative work _seriously_. Everything about this space conveys the message: this project matters. It is a space for him to think with, to help him organize his thinking, and to connect him emotionally to his project. It is adaptable, and able to evolve in response to what he is doing; but at the same time also organized, likely reflecting and supporting the organization of the book."

Another attestation came from the designer Jony Ive in his eulogy for Steve Jobs: "Steve used to say to me, and he used to say this a lot, 'Hey Jony, here's a dopey idea.' And sometimes they were. Really dopey. Sometimes they were truly dreadful. But sometimes they took the air from the room and they left us both completely silent. Bold, crazy, magnificent ideas. Or quiet simple ones, which in their subtlety, their detail, they were utterly profound. And just as Steve loved ideas, and loved making stuff, he treated the process of creativity with a rare and a wonderful reverence. You see, I think he better than anyone understood that while ideas ultimately can be so powerful, they begin as fragile, barely formed thoughts, so easily missed, so easily compromised, so easily just squished."

Many of the pieces of work that I'm proudest of are real-making. It's something that [discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html) especially is good at, perhaps because it involves exploration of a subject from so many angles, beginning with fundamentals, and gradually elaborating, over and over, with tremendous cumulative imagination. The result is a gift to the reader, a real expansion of their felt sense of the world. And so maybe one of my own special ways of contributing is real-making.

## Conclusion

One of my favourite lines in literature comes from Yann Martel's book "Life of Pi". According to the author, he met an elderly man who claimed to him that "I have a story that will make you believe in God". It's an astounding promise of real-making, and one I've reflected on a lot. As a devout atheist, I naturally wondered at the possibility of a story which would make the listener believe in the cosmos. I didn't even quite know what that could mean. It seemed almost a category error. But I ran the sentence past my friend Hannu Rajaniemi, a deeply thoughtful writer, who replied that it sounded like perhaps the best artistic mission statement he'd heard. And so I've kept pondering it. There's a sense in which none of us really believe in the cosmos – not really. Our experience is of a tiny, linear, narrative cut through a vast and complex cosmos; a good life is one which is in healthy communion with that cosmos, and that, I think, requires grounding in the right intangibles. This is why religion matters: it provides a way of grounding us, a healthy way of believing in the cosmos.
