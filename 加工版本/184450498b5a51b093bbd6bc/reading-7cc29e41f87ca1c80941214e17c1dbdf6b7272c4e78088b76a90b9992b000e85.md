# Science — The Ending and Endless Frontier(s)

---

## 正文

早在 1945 年，Vannevar Bush 就写下了 _[Science The Endless Frontier](https://www.nsf.gov/od/lpa/nsf50/vbush1945.htm)_。这部 monograph 本身旨在为后来成为 NSF 的机构的 establishment 提供 justify（虽然这并不是第一次有人提出这样的设想），Bush 甚至 proposes 了它应有的确切 structure 和 budget。

但是，尽管有这个标题，这篇 essay 完全没有 implicitly 或 explicitly 地谈论 science 是 endless frontier。文中确实谈到 science 是一个适合 exploration 的 open frontier，但也仅此而已。

如果 scientific knowledge 可以被 accreted ad infinitum，那么 Science 就会是 endless frontier。culture 就是如此：尽管 culture 的 building blocks 构成了一个 [bounded set](https://tvtropes.org/)，但 recombine plots、characters 或 media 的方式确实如此之多，以至于未来数千年我们仍会不断迎来新的 novels、films 和 animes。尽管其中许多与过去的作品有些相似，我们仍会继续欣赏它们。因此，culture 是真正的 endless frontier。

对于 Science，可以提出这样一种论证，[其中](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/diminishing-returns-science/575665/)

> science 是 endless frontier，其中总有新的 phenomena 有待 discovered，也总有重大的新 questions 有待 answered。endless frontier 的 possibility 是一个被称为 emergence 的 idea 所带来的 consequence。比如，考虑 water。拥有 describing 单个 water 的 molecule 如何 behaves 的 equations 是一回事。理解 rainbows 为什么会在天空中 form，或 ocean waves 的 crashing，或太空中那些我们称为 comets 的脏雪球的 origins，则完全是另一回事。这些全都是“water”，但处于不同的 levels of complexity。每一种都 emerges out of 那些 describing water 的 basic equations，但谁能从这些 equations 中预想到 rainbow 或 waves 的 crashing 这样 intricate 的事物呢？$$..$$ 因此，乐观的看法是，science 是 endless frontier，我们会继续 discover，甚至 create 全新的 fields，它们各自有自己的 fundamental questions。如果我们今天看到了 slowing，那是因为 science 仍然过于 focused 于已有的 fields，而在这些 fields 中，make progress 正变得越来越困难。我们希望未来能看到新 fields 更 rapid 的 proliferation，从而 giving rise to 重大的新 questions。这是 science 得以 accelerate 的机会。

而不是

> 把 science——对 nature 的 exploration——看作类似于对新大陆的探索。起初，已知的东西很少。探索者出发，轻而易举地发现重大的新特征。但渐渐地，他们补全了对这片新大陆的 knowledge。为了 make significant discoveries，探索者必须前往越来越偏远的地区，面对越来越艰难的条件。Exploration 变得更困难。在这种看法中，science 是 limited frontier，需要付出越来越多的努力来“补全地图”。总有一天，地图会接近完整，science 也将大体 exhausted。在这种看法中，discovery 的 difficulty 的任何 increase，都是 scientific knowledge 本身的 structure 所 intrinsic 的。

我在 Nintil 这里曾[说过](https://nintil.com/on-the-constancy-of-the-rate-of-gdp-growth/)

> 在 bounded useful（即 productivity enhancing）knowledge 这一 assumption 下，useful knowledge 就像一座矿。它的 amount 会是 fixed 的，exploiting 其中一部分起初会 leads to 更快的 returns，但随着时间推移，矿逐渐耗尽，人们就必须越来越努力，才能从中获得更多 ideas。如果一开始你没有 learning，或者 learning 得不多，那么 initial growth rate 就是 zero。随着你的 knowledge grows，ideas beget 新的 ideas，growth rate 也 increases。在这之后，growth rate 可能继续 increasing、stabilise，或以与之前上升类似的方式 decrease。在前两种 cases 中，growth 会 indefinitely 地继续，但因为我们已经 assumed bounded knowledge，所以必然是后一种。这自然就 leads to sigmoid-like models。$$...$$
> 
> 如果取任意时点的一项 technology，并且假定当前的 techniques 将来仍会被使用，那么就会受到这些 techniques 的 limitations 的 constrained：这相当于把矿中 gold 的 amount 设定为当前 technology 所 allowed 的 amount。但 technology evolves。几年前，US oil production 中也发生了类似的 phenomenon。oil fields 的 production curve 往往也是 logistic，因此每年的 rate of extraction 大体上是一条 [Hubbert's curve](http://archive.economonitor.com/blog/2014/07/302667/)。人们 predicted 即将到来的 Peak Oil，认为它会带来 catastrophic consequences，等等。但随后 shale oil 出现了。不过，归根结底，底层的 prediction 仍然是 correct 的。如果从 curve 中去掉 shale oil，仍然会[看到 decline。](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=35052)这个 peak 之所以突然凭空出现，是因为一座“新矿”开放了：使 economically profitable fracking 成为可能的 technology 已经 matured 到足以被使用，而它也确实被使用了。因此，与其把 TFP 的 rate of growth modeling 为一条巨大的 Hubbert's curve（或类似的 curve），我们可以把它 model 为一连串 curves，每一条 representing 一项新的 technology。Discovering 一项新的 technology，就是向 model 中 adding 一个新的 term，一个此前并无意义的 term。

因此，有两个 questions 需要 addressed：一个是 scientific advance 如何 structured。我想，在这一点上大家大概会达成一致：一个 _给定的_ field 或 paradigm 会 yield 一个 sigmoid-like pattern，并最终 exhausted。新的 fields 随后可以接续，保持 improvement 的 pace，因此 sigmoids 的 stacking 可以 sustain continuous progress。

\[1\]. 也有一些 fields 似乎继续以不错的 pace accreting knowledge，但我并不认为这些 knowledge 与其他 fields 的 knowledge 一样 useful。例如，astronomical equipment 的 Advances 已经 enabled 对越来越多 exoplanets 的 detection，但很难为其 usefulness 提出有力理由，尤其是 relative to alternative investments 时

第二个 question 是 science 总体上是什么样的。或者，总体而言也许并不是恰当的 framing。正如 Patrick 和 Michael 在他们的 essay 前面所指出的，一些 fields 似乎比另一些更 inexhaustible，重要的是，我关心的 inexhaustibility 是 useful knowledge 的 inexhaustibility；当然，人们可以永远不断 proving mathematical theorems，但这本身并不能 stop aging[1](https://nintil.com/science-ending-frontier/#sidenote-1) 。他们提到的一个例子是 Computer Science，过去几十年里，我们在其中取得了大量 progress，且没有停止的迹象。另一方面，我们还有 particle physics，它在凭借 Higgs Boson 补全 Standard Model 的拼图之后，看起来并不像一个 healthy field。

正确的看法是，它是一个 spectrum。似乎 influence 一个 field 有多 inexhaustible 的两个 factors，是 object of study 的 simplicity，以及人们与它相隔多远[2](https://nintil.com/science-ending-frontier/#sidenote-2) 。越 simpler、越 closer，它就越 constrained。比如，在 biology 中，人们可以 zoom in on 一个 protein。更近距离的观察会 reveals 构成这个 protein 的 atoms，以及它们如何 connected together，或者它能够 adopt 哪些不同的 conformations。一旦我们知道了几个 facts，那个“field”就 closed 且 exhausted 了：对于那个 in isolation 的 protein，我们已经知道了所有可知的东西。但随后，我们 add protein-protein interactions、water 和 transcription pathways，便突然有了 possibilities 的 combinatorial explosion。再 Add 多个 tissues 和 organs，就有足够的材料让我们忙上一阵子。

\[2\]. 可以把这理解为 complexity 的 density 和 volume

这两种 scientific progress 的 models 之间的 difference，主要在于 _心态_：总体上还剩下多少 progress？是多到在实践中 endless，还是虽多，但我们已开始触及它的 limits？我承认自己倾向于 bounded 的看法，因为前一种听起来 _过于_ 乐观。

一旦承认它更像是一个 spectrum，question 就转向了不同 fields 在这个 spectrum 上的位置。我过去曾说过 [physics 已经结束了](https://nintil.com/new-physics/)，但这是一个 broad claim（我实际上特别指的是某些 subfields）。能否使这个 argument 更 rigorous？这对 allocate scientific funding 会有意义。

早在 1998 年，Bill Clinton 就[说过](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/library/politics/010398clinton-budget.html?mcubz=0)

> “我确实相信，从 scientific 的角度看，过去 50 年将被视为 physics 的时代和 space exploration 的时代，”Clinton 说。“我认为，未来 50 年极有可能主要被描述为 biology 和 human organism 的 exploration 的时代，尤其是随着 human genome project 的 completion，我认为这确实会使我们关于如何应对 health issues 的 knowledge 爆发式增长。”

HGP 当时也许被过度炒作了，但我们从以 physics 为 progress 的 core engine 的时代，转入以 biology 为 core engine 的时代，这个判断似乎极有先见之明。我确实认为，如果我们当时不是把 NIH 自身的 funding 翻倍，而是设立一个 National Institute for Particle Physics 来占用这笔 funding，outcome 会更糟。但其他 cases 就没有这么明确了。如果是 National Institutes for Energy and Materials Research（budget 相同）呢？也许仔细考察后会发现这并不合理，但至少需要一些 numbers，才能开始考虑这个 decision。

要 quantify 一个 field 距离 ending 有多近，或者反过来说，它有多 promising，我能想到一些 indicators，但每一个都有各自的问题：

| Metric | Pro | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of publications | Easy to measure | Volume doesn't make for relevance |
| Number of researchers in the field | Easy to measure | Just because it's popular doesn't mean it matters |
| Funding the field gets | Easy to measure, a measure of how much funding bodies think the field is worth it | Science funding has inertia and is enmeshed in politics, so it is not directly connected to expectations |
| Subjective sense among researchers that progress is being made or not | Easy to measure | Biased responses |
| Increase in the time between breakthroughs in the field | Directly related to relevant knowledge production and how hard it is to get | Hard to determine what is a breakthrough; a field can make steady progress without key step changes |
| Patents citing less papers from that field | Direct measure of how relevant knowledge is to inventions | Harder to use for basic science |
| Rhetoric shift in calls for funding from "Fund us because it is useful" to "Fund us because knowledge is intrinsically valuable" or "Fund us because we did great in the past" | Indirect but useful metric of researchers' true beliefs about their discipline | Not always a good heuristic (See Deep Learning). Hard to sample. |
| Number of research paths being explored | Direct measure of field exhaustion, especially if restricted to experimentally testable paths | Hard to measure and compare, there may be hundreds of promising drugs to cure cancer, but only 6 ways to to brain computer interfaces |

好，假设我们设法为两个 arbitrarily defined fields 汇集了上述内容，然后呢？也许可以直接把这些 metrics 作为一个 function 的 input，让它像 softmax 那样输出以 100% 为总量的 percentages，或者只取所关注的 metric 占 total 的 proportion。

整个 approach 的一个问题是，必须 define 什么是 fields，以及 discoveries、funding 或 researchers 应归入哪里。要 mapped 的东西是 [nebulous](https://meaningness.com/eggplant/rationalism-definitions) 的，而这些问题大概有许多合理的答案。再次 overcome 这一问题的一种方式，是对一项 discovery 的 contributions 做类似 exponential weighted average 的处理。因此，如果 discovery X 发生在 genomics 中，但它是由 computer science 中的 Y 所 enabled 的，那么 X 就会被 imputed 给 genomics 和 CS 两者，并随着与这项 discovery 的距离增加而采用某种 decay ratio。

看看这篇很酷的 paper：[通过 partial reprogramming 对 Age-associated hallmarks 进行 In vivo amelioration（Ocampo et al., 2016）](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674\(16\)31664-6)，它来自 Salk Institute 的 Izpisua-Belmonte lab。这篇 paper 的要点是采用 genetically engineered mice，它们在被 dosed with doxycycline（一种 antibitiotic）时会 express OSKM transcription factors，进而显示出各种 metrics 上的 rejuvenation。为了达到这一 development，这篇 paper 本身向我们指出了以下 enabling discoveries

1.  Induced pluripotent stem cells 与 OSKM/Yamanaka factors
2.  关于 epigenetic disregulation 涉及 aging 的 Knowledge
3.  Tetracycline-controlled [transcriptional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetracycline-controlled_transcriptional_activation) activation 的 development
4.  过去关于 mice 中 OSKM 的 work，不幸 led to tumorigenesis
5.  过去关于 _in vitro_ 的 OSKM 的 work

这些是我想到的，不过也可以进一步把每一条 citation 都变成这个 list 中的一项，或许这就是一种办法：沿着 citation tree 进行 crawl，深入 N 个 levels，并 assigning scores。Fields 也许不容易 identifiable，但 departments 和 scientists 则可以。这是一个有趣的 research project，我现在可以利用 semantic scholar dataset（以及一个合适的 graph database）来做：对于一个给定的 paper，estimate 那些在 tree 中 N 个 levels of depth 内作出 contributed 的所有人的 scores。这样，对于一组已达成共识的 breakthroughs，就可以对它们全部 run 这个 algorithm，按 author、department 或 field 进行 group，并得到一份 contributions 的 ranked list。接着，这就可以用来 distribute funding。我想知道，这是否会与我们关于哪些应该获得 funded 的 priors 相吻合。

关于这个 problem，应该已经有 literature 了，不过在深入探讨之前，我想先关注另一个 topic。

## 原文

Back in 1945, Vannevar Bush wrote _[Science The Endless Frontier](https://www.nsf.gov/od/lpa/nsf50/vbush1945.htm)_. The monograph itself aims to justify the establishment of what would later become the NSF (altough it was not the first time such a thing had been proposed), and Bush even proposes the exact structure and budget it should have.

But, despite the title, the essay does not talk at all, implicitly or explicitly as science being an endless frontier. There is talk of science being an open frontier ripe for exploration, but that's as close as it gets.

Science would be an endless frontier if scientific knowledge can be accreted ad infinitum. This is the case for culture: Despite there being a [bounded set](https://tvtropes.org/) of building blocks of culture, there is indeed such a great number to recombine plots, characters or media that we will have new novels, films, and animes coming for millennia to come. And despite many of those bearing some resemblance to past work, we will continue enjoying them. Thus culture is a true endless frontier.

For Science one could make a case, [where](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/diminishing-returns-science/575665/)

> science is an endless frontier, where there are always new phenomena to be discovered, and major new questions to be answered. The possibility of an endless frontier is a consequence of an idea known as emergence. Consider, for example, water. It’s one thing to have equations describing the way a single molecule of water behaves. It’s quite another to understand why rainbows form in the sky, or the crashing of ocean waves, or the origins of the dirty snowballs in space that we call comets. All these are “water,” but at different levels of complexity. Each emerges out of the basic equations describing water, but who would ever have suspected from those equations something so intricate as a rainbow or the crashing of waves? \[..\] So the optimistic view is that science is an endless frontier, and we will continue to discover and even create entirely new fields, with their own fundamental questions. If we see a slowing today, it is because science has remained too focused on established fields, where it’s becoming ever harder to make progress. We hope the future will see a more rapid proliferation of new fields, giving rise to major new questions. This is an opportunity for science to accelerate.

rather than

> science—the exploration of nature—as similar to the exploration of a new continent. In the early days, little is known. Explorers set out and discover major new features with ease. But gradually they fill in knowledge of the new continent. To make significant discoveries explorers must go to ever-more-remote areas, under ever-more-difficult conditions. Exploration gets harder. In this view, science is a limited frontier, requiring ever more effort to “fill in the map.” One day the map will be near complete, and science will largely be exhausted. In this view, any increase in the difficulty of discovery is intrinsic to the structure of scientific knowledge itself.

Here at Nintil I [said](https://nintil.com/on-the-constancy-of-the-rate-of-gdp-growth/) that

> Under the assumption of bounded useful (i.e. productivity enhancing) knowledge, useful knowledge would be like a mine. There would be a fixed amount, and exploiting some of it would initially leads to faster returns, but then over time, as the mine gets depleted, one has to work harder and harder to get more ideas out of it. If at the beginning you are not learning or you are not learning much, the initial growth rate is zero. As you knowledge grows, ideas beget new ideas and the growth rate increases. After this point, the growth rate could either continue increasing, stabilise, or decrease in the similar way as it went up. In the two first cases, growth will continue indefinitely, but because we have assumed bounded knowledge, it has to be the latter. This naturally then leads to sigmoid-like models. \[...\]
> 
> if one takes a technology at any given point in time, and one assumes that current techniques will be used in the future, then one is constrained by the limitations of those techniques: One would be setting the amount of gold in the mine to be that allowed by current technology. But technology evolves. A similar phenomenon happened a few years ago in US oil production. The production curve of oil fields tend to also be logistic, thus the rate of extraction per year is broadly a a [Hubbert's curve](http://archive.economonitor.com/blog/2014/07/302667/). People predicted an imminent Peak Oil, which would have catastrophic consequences, etc. But then shale oil happened. At the end of the day, however, the underlying prediction is still correct. If one takes away shale oil from the curve, ones still [sees a decline.](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=35052) The why that peak suddenly appears out of nowhere is that a "new mine" opened up: the technology that allows for economically profitable fracking matured enough to be used and used it was. Thus, instead of modeling the rate of growth of TFP as one big Hubbert's curve (or similar), we could model it as a succesion of curves, each representing a new technology. Discovering a new technology is adding a new term to the model, a term that didn't make sense before.

So there are two questions to be addressed: One is how scientific advance is structured. Here I think there will be probably agreement: A _given_ field or paradigm will yield a sigmoid-like pattern and eventually it will be exhausted. New fields can then pick up and keep the pace of improvement, and so a stacking of sigmoids can sustain continuous progress.

\[1\]. There are also fields that seem to continue accreting knowledge at a good pace, yet it is not knowledge I would think is as useful as that of other fields. Advances in astronomical equipment have enabled the detection of, say, an increasing number of exoplanets, but it's hard to make a case for its usefulness, especially relative to alternative investments

The second question is about what science looks like in general. Or perhaps general is not the right framing. As Patrick and Michael note up in their essay, some fields seem more inexhaustible than others, importantly the inexhaustibility I care about is that of useful knowledge; of course one can keep proving mathematical theorems forever but that won't by itself stop aging[1](https://nintil.com/science-ending-frontier/#sidenote-1) . Computer Science is an example they note where we have had lots of progress over the last decades, without signs of it stopping. On the other hand we have particle physics, which after completing the puzzle of the Standard Model with the Higgs Boson does not seem like a healthy field.

The right view is that it is a spectrum. The two factors that seem to influence how inexhaustible a field is the simplicity of the object of study and how removed one is from it[2](https://nintil.com/science-ending-frontier/#sidenote-2) . The simpler and closer one gets, the more constrained it is. In biology for example one can zoom in on a protein. A closer look reveals the atoms that make up the protein and how they are connected together, or what different conformations can it adopt. Once we know a few facts that "field" is closed and exhausted: We know everything there is to know about said protein in isolation. But then we add protein-protein interactions, water, and transcription pathways and suddenly we have a combinatorial explosion of possibilities. Add then multiple tissues and organs and there is enough material to keep us busy for a while.

\[2\]. One could think of this as the density and volume of complexity

The difference between both models of scientific progress is chiefly one of _mood_: How much progress in general remains? So much it's in practice endless, or a lot but we are starting to run against its limits? I admit I lean towards the bounded view because the former sounds _overly_ optimistic.

Once one has admitted that it's more of a spectrum the question shifts to where different fields are across that spectrum. I've said in the past that [physics is over](https://nintil.com/new-physics/), but that's a broad claim (I'm really talking about some subfields in particular). Could this argument be made more rigorous? This would be of interest to allocate scientific funding.

Back in 1998, Bill Clinton [said](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/library/politics/010398clinton-budget.html?mcubz=0)

> "I do believe that in scientific terms, the last 50 years will be seen as an age of physics and an age of space exploration," Clinton said. "I think the next 50 years will very likely be characterized predominantly as an age of biology and the exploration of the human organism, especially with the completion of the human genome project, which I think will literally explode what we know about how to deal with health issues."

Now maybe the HGP was too hyped but the point that we transitioned from the age of physics to the age of biology as core engine of progress seems remarkably prescient. I do think that had we had a National Institute for Particle Physics instead taking up the funding instead of doubling the NIH's own would have been a worse outcome. But other cases are not as clear. What about the National Institutes for Energy and Materials Research (with the same budget)? Maybe it doesn't make sense on a closer examination, but one'd need some numbers to even start considering the decision.

To quantify how close is a field to ending, or conversely how promising it is, some indicators come to mind, each with their own problems:

| Metric | Pro | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of publications | Easy to measure | Volume doesn't make for relevance |
| Number of researchers in the field | Easy to measure | Just because it's popular doesn't mean it matters |
| Funding the field gets | Easy to measure, a measure of how much funding bodies think the field is worth it | Science funding has inertia and is enmeshed in politics, so it is not directly connected to expectations |
| Subjective sense among researchers that progress is being made or not | Easy to measure | Biased responses |
| Increase in the time between breakthroughs in the field | Directly related to relevant knowledge production and how hard it is to get | Hard to determine what is a breakthrough; a field can make steady progress without key step changes |
| Patents citing less papers from that field | Direct measure of how relevant knowledge is to inventions | Harder to use for basic science |
| Rhetoric shift in calls for funding from "Fund us because it is useful" to "Fund us because knowledge is intrinsically valuable" or "Fund us because we did great in the past" | Indirect but useful metric of researchers' true beliefs about their discipline | Not always a good heuristic (See Deep Learning). Hard to sample. |
| Number of research paths being explored | Direct measure of field exhaustion, especially if restricted to experimentally testable paths | Hard to measure and compare, there may be hundreds of promising drugs to cure cancer, but only 6 ways to to brain computer interfaces |

Ok, suppose we somehow manage to compile the above for two arbitrarily defined fields, then what? One could perhaps just take the metrics as input to a function that spits out percentages out of 100% like softmax or just a proportion of the total for the metric of interest.

A problem with the whole approach is that one has to define what fields are and where discoveries, funding, or researchers fall into. What is to be mapped is [nebulous](https://meaningness.com/eggplant/rationalism-definitions) and there are probably many reasonable answers to these problems. One way to again overcome this is to do something like an exponential weighted average of contributions to a discovery. So if discovery X happened in genomics but that was enabled by Y in computer science then X would be imputed to both genomics and CS with some decay ratio a someone gets away from the discovery.

Take this cool paper: [In vivo amelioration of Age-associated hallmarks by partial reprogramming (Ocampo et al., 2016)](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674\(16\)31664-6) from the Izpisua-Belmonte lab at the Salk Institute. The point of the paper is to take genetically engineered mice that express the OSKM transcription factors when dosed with doxycycline (an antibitiotic), in turn this show that rejuvenation across various metrics. To get to this development, the paper itself refers us to the following enabling discoveries

1.  Induced pluripotent stem cells + The OSKM/Yamanaka factors
2.  Knowledge that epigenetic disregulation is involved in aging
3.  The development of Tetracycline-controlled [transcriptional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetracycline-controlled_transcriptional_activation) activation
4.  Past work on OSKM in mice that unfortunately led to tumorigenesis
5.  Past work on OSKM _in vitro_

These are the ones that come to mind, but one could go as far of making every citation into a point in this list, and perhaps that's a way, just crawl N levels through the citation tree assigning scores. Fields may not be easily identifiable, but departments and scientists are. That's an interesting research project that I can now do with the semantic scholar dataset (And a suitable graph database): For a given paper, estimate the scores of everyone who contributed at N levels of depth in the tree. That way for a given set of agreed breakthroughs, one can run this algorithm on all of them, group by author, department or field and get a ranked list of contributions. In turn that can be used to distribute funding. I wonder if this would coincide with our priors about what ought to be funded.

There should be literature on this problem already, but I wanted to focus on a different topic before going into that.
