## 正文

_想创办一个 EA student group 吗？如果你有这个想法，欢迎[联系我](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)！创办 student group 是 improve the world 的好办法，你也会在这个过程中学到很多东西。我一直很乐意尽我所能帮助新的 student groups。_

在 Harvard 的最后两年里，我共同运营了 [Harvard Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/)，把它从只有一名 member 的 group，发展成拥有由十名学生组成的 organizational board、另有数十人经常参加我们 talks 的组织。我希望这些 tips 中的一部分能帮助其他学生创办自己的 groups，因此在这里分享我学到的 lessons。

注意：这里所有的 points 都有几个 caveats。首先，我的 sample size 很 small——我只运营过一个 group！其次，我的 lessons 未必能 generalize：HEA 在 Harvard 遇到的 problems，可能与我们在其他学校会遇到的不同；而我个人遇到的 problems，也与拥有不同 skillset 的人可能遇到的不同。所以，请谨慎理解这些笔记。

## 要有两个人

HEA 能够成功，最重要、远超其他因素的一点，就是我们有两位 co-presidents。

起初，只有我一个人负责，我对自己 plans 的 motivation 和兴奋劲总是一阵一阵的。我会冒出一个关于 project 或 event 的新想法，然后满腔热情地开始 organizing——但很快就会遇到 problems，我开始觉得它注定会失败，变得灰心丧气，之前推动的一切也就停滞了。显然，这样做是在给自己拆台，对我们 club 的 operations 没什么帮助。

幸运的是，在第一年快结束时，[John Sturm](http://jasturm.blogspot.com/) 主动参与进来，也开始 organizing events。有了 John 承担一半的 organizational burden（而且常常不止一半），即使我灰心丧气时他也依然坚持，我就不再觉得负责这些事情那么令人不堪重负了。这消除了我的顾虑：我原本担心，举办更多 events 又会让我陷入一轮 boom-bust motivation cycle。于是，我们能做的事情远比只有我一个人时多得多。

如果你比我更能承受挫折，也更像个 natural leader，那么有一位 co-organizer 对你来说可能没那么重要。但考虑到寻找第二个人也是最常见的 startup advice 之一，这条建议似乎适用于大多数人，即使他们像大多数 startup founders 那样 resilient、那么有 leader-y 的特质。所以，我建议把寻找 co-organizer 放在很高的 priority 上。

当然，这并不容易，因为 co-organizer 需要有很强的 initiative 和 agency——即使你不在意 project 的时候，他们也必须在意。另一方面，如果你在创办 group 之前找不到好的 co-organizers，也别太担心——我在创办 HEA 之前并不认识 John，直到他加入 group、参加了我们的一些 events，我才认识他。所以，你很可能可以从自己的 membership 中 recruit 一位 co-organizer；只要留心寻找合适的人，因为这会让 organizational capacity 得到_巨大_提升。

## 不要不好意思

Harvard Effective Altruism 第一次做 publicity，是在 Harvard 的“student activities fair”上。那时，Harvard 的每一个 student group 都在一排排桌子前摆开阵势，而那些自讨苦吃的学生则闯过重重关卡，徒劳地试图在本科生活中找到意义。

我们当时在举办一个 [Giving Game](http://www.thelifeyoucansave.org/givinggames.aspx)：向大家介绍三家 charities，请他们选择一家来接受一美元。

一次典型的 HEA pitch 是这样的：

> （一名学生走过）
> 
> （又一名学生走过）
> 
> （第三名学生走过）
> 
> （第四名学生走过，尴尬地朝 HEA poster 瞥了一眼）
> 
> **Ben:** 你好？
> 
> **学生：** 呃，你好，Harvard Effective Altruism 是什么？
> 
> **Ben:** _（不好意思地）_ 好吧，我知道这听起来很老套，不过我们确实在努力找出 improve the world 的 best ways，然后去做这些事情。
> 
> **学生：** 啊？
> 
> **Ben:** 比如说，事实证明，有些 charities 比其他 charities 好得多！想玩这个 Giving Game 吗？
> 
> （一头雾水的学生找了个借口离开）

这样做了三个小时的 pitches 之后，回来参加过不止一次 meeting 的人，总共只有一位。

我们的 publicity 之所以糟糕到这种地步，是因为我不擅长与人交谈，具体有几个原因：

1.  我意识到，HEA 的想法听起来很疯狂：“加入我们，一起努力弄清如何 maximize the good we do in the world！”——它把试图拯救世界的陈词滥调，与宣称某些拯救世界的 ways 比其他 ways 更好的禁忌结合在一起。但我的错误在于，_让别人知道_我自己也清楚 HEA 听起来很疯狂。他们一旦发现，连我自己都觉得傻乎乎的，就再也不可能被说服参与进来了。
2.  因为觉得自己在推销一个疯狂的想法，我也担心浪费别人的时间。这实在很傻。如果这些学生不想浪费时间，就不该来闯 activities-fair 这道关。而且，如果他们愿意，完全有能力自行脱身，不再听这场 pitch。我们本该少客气一点。

相比之下，今年我们在食堂做 advertising 时，一次类似的 pitch 是这样的：

> （一名学生走过）
> 
> （Ben 走上前，递出一张一美元钞票）
> 
> （学生下意识地接过钞票）
> 
> **Ben:** 你想把这一美元 donate 给 charity 吗？
> 
> **学生：** 啊？
> 
> **B:** 就是，我们这里有一份 charities 的 list，你可以挑一家，把这一美元给它。
> 
> **S:** 哦，好吧，可以。
> 
> **B:** 太好了！其实不是直接给出这张钞票，而是通过这个 computer form。你能读一读这三段简介，select 你认为能用这一美元 do the most good 的那一家吗？
> 
> **S:** 没问题。
> 
> （S 读完简介）
> 
> **S:** 所以，这到底是怎么回事？
> 
> **B:** 很高兴你问了！就是，你想 improve the world，对吧？_（S：对。）_ 而且，如果可以，你更希望 improving it _more_，对吧？_（S：对。）_ 所以，我们试着弄清如何 improve it the most。居然没什么其他人在做这件事，挺奇怪的，对吧？

到了这个时候，我已经克服了那种觉得自己傻乎乎的感觉，直接坚持讲下去。我常常承认 HEA 是_不常见的_，但不会说它_古怪_。此外，在最初 engaging with students 时，我也更主动了——毕竟，他们来这里就是来听大家推介的。如果别人看起来不感兴趣，我仍然不会给他们来一场 hard sell，但我不再介意主动争取，把他们的注意力从其他同样咄咄逼人的人那里拉过来。结果，学生们的兴趣和参与程度都高了许多。

## 邀请知名 speakers

如果说我在 running HEA 期间有什么值得自豪的事，那就是我们的 programming。去年，我们请到了超过 10 位 speakers 来为 HEA 进行 talks，其中包括 Peter Singer、Jaan Tallinn，以及[许多其他极受欢迎、日程繁忙的人](http://harvardea.org/events/#past)。我们还 raised 了数千美元，用来支付这些 speakers 的 honoraria。怎么做到的？

诀窍就是像我和 John 一样人脉广泛，这样你就认识各种各样的名人和财力雄厚的 university organizations，可以请他们帮忙。如果你还没有这样的人脉，那就走出去 network！

……开个玩笑。实际上，在邀请他们来 speak 之前，我们的大多数 speakers 我都不认识。我只是给他们发了一封 cold email，问他们是否愿意为 HEA 进行一场 talk（有时会通过朋友或我们出色的 faculty adviser [Nir](http://medethics.med.harvard.edu/people/eyal/) 引荐）。许多邮件都石沉大海——我的 archives 里到处都是无人回复的 invitations 的残骸——但我们也收到了许多回复，而我们这么棒的 speakers 阵容，绝对值得多发那些被扔进虚空的 messages。

这里我还有一条重要的小建议。在给潜在的 speakers 发 emailing 时——乃至在日常生活中——我发现 [Boomerang](http://www.boomeranggmail.com/referral_download.html?ref=4sbhr)[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/studentgroup/#fn:1) _完全不可或缺_。我把所有已发送的 emails 都设置为：如果 recipient 没有回复，就在一周后返回我的 inbox；而且往往在我发出第二封 follow-up email 后，就会收到回复。事实上，要和某些 professors 沟通，Boomerang 几乎是必需的；我怀疑他们奉行的原则就是，email 第一次发来时绝不回复……再强调一次——**如果你依靠 email 推动重要的事情，又没有 Boomerang，那就现在开始用 Boomerang。**

从心理上说，发送 cold emails 常常让我灰心，因为单独来看，每封邮件最终促成一场 talk 的机会都相当低——我经常觉得自己的大部分工作都白做了（尽管我们的 response rate 比我预想的高得多）。我试着用_重视付出而非结果_的方式来对抗这种感觉——我会把自己的 goals 表述为“已发送的 high-quality emails”，而不是“已收到的 responses”或“已安排的 talks”。

总的来说，我们 cold-emailed 的人中，大约有 20% 最终在 HEA 进行了一场 talk。我不能保证其他学校的 student groups 也能取得这样的结果——我们无疑受益于 Harvard 的品牌——但即使 response rate 低得多，我们也可以发送更多 emails，或开始邀请没那么忙的人。拥有如此出色的阵容，为我们带来的 credibility 和关注，在我们 recruiting new members 时帮了大忙，所以我真的很庆幸我们做了这件事。

向出色的人发送 Cold-email！这真的管用！

## 应对 bureaucracy

Running 一个 student group 意味着要处理大量 paperwork，并应付许多 red tape。尤其在刚开始时，我很难把所有事情都记清楚，在为 events reserving space 等事情上，经常拖到最后一刻（甚至更晚）。

在太多个下午提心吊胆地等着 room reservation office 给我回复 email 之后，我终于被压力压垮了。难得有一次，我确实留意了 booking deadlines，并提前一周 reserved 了我们的 room——但到了两天前，他们仍然没有 confirmed。我决定，无论如何，我都要在_今天_把 room reserved 好。我摆出自己最拿手的饥肠辘辘、心急如焚、急需帮助的学生表情，想好最有说服力的借口，大步走向 Science Center，讨个说法。

迎接我的并不是我想象中那个强硬、随时准备开战的 bureaucrat，而是一位名叫 Ron、非常和善的先生。Ron 把一些 files 的顺序弄乱了，随后又被大量 requests 淹没，因为当时正值学期开始，所有 student groups 都想要 space。他没意识到我们已经等了这么久，也很高兴我过来询问——尤其是因为我们还得就 logistics 来回沟通几次，当面处理比通过 email 快得多。

作为一名 software developer，我受到的训练让我觉得，如果 Harvard 为学生与 institutions 沟通 specifies 了一个 interface，那么这个 interface 就是一道神圣不可逾越的 Abstraction Barrier。但事实证明，bureaucratic abstractions 的 [leak](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/LeakyAbstractions.html) 比 computational 的 abstractions _还要严重_——那些 specified interfaces 实际上并不怎么好用。再加上 email 这种抹去人情味的 medium，大家很容易变得无礼或干脆不再回复，不愉快的经历就这样产生了。

我一次又一次地有同样的体验——room booking、financial forms、advertising space，以及我们做的几乎所有其他事情，只要我们在困惑或需要处理复杂事情时_亲自到场_，进展就会顺畅得多。能避免时，我仍然尽量避免这样做——这会占用别人的时间，做得太频繁大概也会惹人烦，而且必须当面 resolve issues，本身就是一个警讯，说明我们在 process 的其他环节出了问题。但如果 bureaucracy 开始拖延，亲自到场几乎总能加快进展。

## 外部 support

HEA 能做成一些让人惊讶或看起来很了不起的事情，一个简单的办法是我们[联系了许多 speakers](https://www.benkuhn.net/good-speakers)。另一个办法是，我们从不属于 Harvard 学生这一“core audience”的人那里获得了_大量_ support。Harvard faculty 和其他认同 EA 的人都给我们提供了大量 information、technical assistance 和引荐，甚至表示，如果我们需要，他们愿意提供 funding。

以下是我们曾需要非学生人士帮助的一份不完整清单：

-   我们出色的 faculty adviser [Nir](http://medethics.med.harvard.edu/people/eyal/) 给了我们很多建议，为我们引荐 speakers；在我们遇到 organizational problems 时，他会定期关心进展，给我们 hosting speakers 方面的建议，总之在各个方面都帮了多得惊人的忙。

    事实上，按照 Harvard 的 regulations，HEA 必须有一位 faculty adviser；但即使你的学校没有这个要求，我也强烈建议找一位——你可以邀请一位认同你们的 professor 担任 official adviser，然后与对方见面、通过 email 请教，或者采用任何适合你的方式。

-   其他 Harvard faculty 会在自己的 classes 上提及我们的 activities，advertised 我们的 speakers，向我们的 fundraiser 进行 donated，或者在晚餐时与我们的 members 交流。这些都极有帮助。现在回想起来，我希望当时能尝试与更多 faculty 建立更密切的联系——我想，他们中有很多人都会乐意提供更多帮助。

    如果你不知道去哪里寻找 faculty，可以试试 philosophy（尤其是 ethics）、economics（尤其是 development）、public health（尤其是 medical ethics）和 psychology（尤其是 moral psychology）的 professors。也可以试着[给我发邮件](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)，因为我或许能帮你联系附近的其他人。

-   [Jeff](http://jefftk.com/) 和 [Julia](http://www.givinggladly.com/) 通常会在每学期进行第一场 talk，向 new members 介绍 effective altruism 的 ideas。与 HEA 通常邀请的 speakers 相比，大学生更容易对他们产生亲近感（我们通常邀请的 speakers 中，许多是 professors 或 nonprofit executives），因此他们非常擅长以友善、热情的方式介绍 effective altruism。

-   一位当地的（anonymous）effective altruist 为我们的第一次冬季 fundraiser sponsored 了一项 matching challenge，这让活动更有 credible，也让人们更有动力 donate。无论是 running 这场 fundraiser 的学生，还是 donated 的人，都因此兴奋得多。

    另一位 supporter 表示，如果我们正在申请的 Harvard grants 没有获批，他愿意为我们提供一笔可观的 back-up funds。虽然去年我们最终没有用到这些 funds，但能够向 speakers 明确保证我们可以 reimburse 他们的 travel and lodging expenses，确实非常有帮助。

-   当地 Boston EA group 的其他人会来参加我们的 talks，向朋友 advertised 这些 talks，向我们的 fundraiser 进行 donated，在有有趣的 speakers 路过 Boston 时告诉我们，总之提供了大量各式各样的建议和帮助。

我无法替其他类型的 student groups 下结论，但如果你要创办的恰好是一个 effective altruism student group，那么找到愿意帮忙的人相当容易——只要在 [Facebook group](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/?ref=br_tf) 里发帖，或者[给我发邮件](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)，我就会帮你联系能提供帮助的人！

## 从 philanthropy fellowship 中学到的经验

我们举办的、真正让 membership 发展起来的 event，就是我们所称的“philanthropy fellowship”。虽然内容是针对 HEA 的 mission 的，但我认为其中的想法和经验可以 generalize 到任何类型的 student group。这个 philanthropy fellowship 是这样运作的：

1.  我们[向一群很棒的人 cold-emailed](https://www.benkuhn.net/good-speakers)，请他们为 Harvard Effective Altruism 的 philanthropy fellowship 进行 give talks。

2.  我们还让[一位 anonymous donor](https://www.benkuhn.net/support)承诺，会 match 我们的 philanthropy fellowship 在该学期 raised 的任何 funds。

3.  在年初的所有 events 上，我们都告诉大家，我们正在开展一个“philanthropy fellowship” program；fellows 需要 apply 并被 accepted，之后便可参加一系列 talks、与我们出色的 speakers 共进晚餐，并在最后举办一场 fundraiser。我记得，当时已答应出席的 speakers 名单中包括 Peter Singer 和 Jaan Tallinn，因此引起了相当多的兴趣。

4.  我们发布了一份包含一些基本问题的 application，为它设定了 deadline，并向大量 mailing lists 进行 publicized（既包括与其主题相关的 mailing lists，也包括允许推介各种 clubs/activities 的 dorm-wide mailing lists。

5.  deadline 过后，我们请所有我们认为合适的 applicants 回来，与一位 HEA organizers 进行简短的 one-on-one interview。在 interview 中，我们问了他们几个问题（主要是为了了解他们），并简要介绍 fellowship 将如何开展（schedule、对 attendance 的期望、最后的 fundraiser 等）。

我们对 application 其实完全没有进行很严格的 selective——主要只是用它来 screen out 那些显然只是来 promote 自己的 charities 的人，而且我记得，我们邀请了所有参加 interviewed 的人加入 fellowship。

但它最终还是形成了一个 core group，这些人几乎参加了我们所有的 events，也经常与 HEA 互动。我不确定促成 philanthropy fellowship 成功的所有因素，但我有几个 theories。（请注意，这些_只是 theories_，也就是说，并未经过 empirically verified——因此，若要 generalize，风险自负！）

1.  **加入 fellowship 有很大的 incentive。**虽然 speakers 的 talks 对公众开放，但与他们共进晚餐的机会仅限于 philanthropy fellowship 的 members。我们大力 advertised 这一点，而且我确信，能与 Peter Singer 共进晚餐的机会至少吸引了一个人加入。

2.  **Scarcity 会 creates value。**如果人们觉得你的 group 很难加入——即使实际上并非如此——他们就会更加 value 自己的 membership，也会对你的 events 更加兴奋。这就是我们设置 application process 的原因，尽管这给我们增加了工作，而且我们只 rejected 了几个人。

3.  **我们首先要求他们作出 attend events 的 explicit commitment。**许多 groups 都有这样一个问题：学生在学期初很感兴趣，但随着课业不断增加，他们开始感到应接不暇，参加的 events 也就少了。由于我们在学期初就要求学生 commit to attending 整个 fellowship，他们往往会把它放在更高的 priority。

当然，我们也从 fellowship 中学到了许多其他经验——主要是关于如何能把它办得更好。

## Advertising strategies（宣传策略）

HEA 尝试过许多不同的办法来吸引人们的注意。下面是我们的一些常用办法——这些 strategies 是我们经常使用、而且通常效果不错的：

### Giving Games（捐赠游戏）

_（注：这部分有些 EA-specific，不过总体思路也适用于其他类型的 groups。）_

几乎所有 student groups 都经常做的一件事就是 tabling。我们发现，interactive activities 的效果相当不错——我们经常举办 [Giving Games](http://www.thelifeyoucansave.org/get-involved/giving-game-2)：为每位 participant 提供 $1，由他们根据自己认为哪一家最 effective，决定将这笔钱 donate 给我们提供的 charities 名单中的一家。

Giving Games 很棒的一点是，它们非常容易进行 study scientifically——如果你在 computer 上 display 人们的 charity choices，就能非常容易地 randomize 人们看到的 text 和 options。然后，你可以 measure 在 Giving Game 结束时有多少人留下 emails，以此作为他们觉得这个 game 有多 engaging 的 proxy。HEA 在上学期末做了[这方面的一些 research](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/06/13/research/)，并发现了一些有趣的 results。

首先，加入更多 speculative charities 可能会 decrease 人们的 engagement。HEA 将一组包含 three global poverty charities 的选项，与一组包含 one global poverty charity、one “meta” charity 和 one 致力于 existential risks 的 charity 的选项进行 tested，发现获得后一组选项的人留下 emails 的可能性 significantly less likely。

此外，data 还 suggested（weakly，且 not-quite-significantly），加入有关 charities 有多 effective 的信息，会使人们对 game 的 engagement _decreased_。这可能是一个真实的 effect，但也可能是由 confounding 造成的，因为包含 effectiveness info 的 descriptions 更长，让人们感到厌烦。或者，这也可能仅仅是 statistical fluctuation。要弄清楚，还需要更多 research；但无论如何，这都很好地提醒了我们：Giving Games 要简短有趣。

如果你正在运营一个 EA group，也想举办这样的活动，上面的链接中有更多信息。你也可以联系 The Life You Can Save，他们或许愿意 fund 你的 Giving Game。

### 巨型、醒目的 posters

我说的确实是**巨型**。Harvard 在 Harvard Yard 和 Science Center 之间的入口处放着一组 four sandwich boards，每次可以 reserved 其中的一面。大多数 student organizations 会拿常规的 event posters，用大约 20 张铺满 sandwich board 的整个版面。而为学期的第一场 talk 做准备时，我们决定换个办法，把印着 [Larry Temkin](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Temkin) 面孔的 poster 放大到原来的 20 倍。活动中告诉我们自己是通过这张 poster 得知活动的人多得出乎意料；我觉得，这在很大程度上是因为它比通常的 student-group poster 更醒目。

### Professors（教授）

通常，如果你正在举办一场偏 academic 的 event，related fields 的 professors 会很乐意在他们的 classes 中 promote 它。HEA 曾成功请 philosophy、economics 和 public health 的 professors 来 advertise 我们的各种 events。Professors 通常也是[很好的 allies](https://www.benkuhn.net/support)，而请他们在 class 中介绍你的 event，可以是一个很好的破冰方式。

### Personal appeals（个人邀约）

请朋友来参加你的 events，并不是特别 scalable。但对于更 targeted 的事情，比如申请 [philanthropy fellowship](http://www.benkuhn.net/fellowship)，我们逐一询问朋友是否有兴趣申请，取得了很好的效果。当然，你只应该对那些你 _确实_ 认为会感兴趣的朋友这样做，因为纠缠朋友很不礼貌，而且如果他们最后并不喜欢这个 event，可能会感到厌烦。但是

### Mass communications（群体传播）

HEA 有一个包含几百名学生的 mailing list，我们也向一些 general-interest mailing lists 发送过 emails（例如 dorm-specific mailing lists）。

分别向每个 list 发送 emails，并在 email 中加入一点 list-specific content，看起来会更用心一些。但这很烦琐，因为这意味着你必须把 email 进行 cut-and-paste，发到许多不同的 lists。为了 speed up 这个 process，你可以[在 Gmail 中 set up 一个 mail merge](http://lifehacker.com/5508283/how-to-set-up-a-mail-merge-in-gmail-for-personalized-mass-emails)，并利用 spreadsheet 中的 customizations 来 send a batch of emails。

我们给 philanthropy fellowship 的 applicants 发送邮件时，有时也会使用 mail-merges；你也可能把它用于向 professors 进行 cold-emailing（虽然我们没有试过，而且需要花些心思，让文字足够好、足够 specific，以免 generic parts 听起来很糟糕）。

mail merge 的好处不仅在于 speeding up 那些你本来就要发送的 batch emails，还在于它让发送变得更容易，从而让你发送更多 email。Email 在为 talks 和 fellowships 激发兴趣方面可能相当 effective，因此这是一个很大的助力。

### Facebook event invitations（Facebook 活动邀请）

我们为所有 talks 都 created 了 Facebook events，并向同学们发出 mass-invited。除了让大家知道这些 events，它也帮助我们 gauge attendance，并向所有想参加的人 distribute messages（例如 event location 的 updates，或 slides 的 links）。我们发往 lists 的所有 emails 也都附上了 event RSVP links，尽可能让所有要参加 event 的人都能在一个 centralized place 获取 info。

当然，每次 event 都向所有相关的 Facebook friends 进行 Mass-inviting，是件非常费力的事，因为如果你不想 spam 别人，就必须 manually curate 你要邀请的人的 list；这意味着你要浏览整个 friend list，并针对每场 event 为每位 friend 做一次决定。幸运的是，有一种容易得多的方法：它利用了这样一个事实——如果你在 Facebook 上建立一个“friend list”，它的 algorithms 就会 automatically suggest 你把其他 similar friends add 进去。具体做法如下：

1.  首先，create 一个 Friend List，并在其中 add 大约 50 位你想邀请来参加社团 events 的同学。然后进入这个 list，查看 friend suggestions——Facebook 会 suggest 你把其余同学也 add 进去。想 Add 谁都可以（我建议全部加入，因为 invite spam 很常见；不过，如果你想更仔细地 curate 这个 list，Facebook 在建议把谁放进 list 时也会变得更聪明）。
2.  现在，在你的 browser 中为以下 link add 一个 bookmark：`javascript:elms=document.getElementsByName(“checkableitems[]”);for (i=0;i<elms.length;i++){if (elms[i].type=“checkbox”)elms[i].click()};`
3.  现在，当你想向这些人 mass-invite 参加某个 event 时，进入 event page，然后 click“Invite Friends”。Click 标着“Search by Name”的 dropdown，并 select 你在 step 1 中建立的 list。现在你应该能看到 list 上的所有人都显示在下方。
4.  对这个 list 一直 Scroll 到底（这很重要——后面的 checkboxes 要等你 scroll down 后才会出现，否则你最后就不会邀请到所有人）。
5.  Click 你在 step 2 中建立的 bookmark。现在应该所有人都已 checked off，你就可以邀请他们了！
6.  Facebook 会 limits 你一次能邀请的人数，所以，为了再次确认你已经邀请了所有人，请 repeat steps 3-4，并 verify 所有 checkboxes 都已 greyed-out（这表示该 friend 已经被邀请）。如果没有，就根据需要 repeat steps 3-5。

这比 manually checking 一大堆 boxes 快得多——对我来说大约需要 30 seconds，而不是 5-10 minutes。这也意味着我们可以可靠地让所有 organizers 都邀请他们的 friends，从而使我们的 Facebook events 拥有广得多的 reach。

## Have two people

Far and away the most important factor in HEA’s success is that we had two co-presidents.

At first, it was just me running things, and my motivation and excitement about my plans came in bursts. I would get a new idea for a project or event and start organizing it in a flurry of energy—but soon problems would come up, I’d start to think it was doomed to failure and get demoralized, and everything I’d set in motion would grind to a halt. Obviously this was self-defeating and not very helpful for our club’s operations.

Fortunately, towards the end of the first year, [John Sturm](http://jasturm.blogspot.com/) took the initiative and got involved with organizing events as well. With John to take care of half the organizational burden (and frequently more) and plug away even when I was demoralized, I felt much less overwhelmed about running things. This broke down my aversion that running more events would start another boom-bust motivation cycle for me, and allowed us to do way more things than we could have with just me.

If you’re more resilient to setbacks and more of a natural leader than I am, it may be less important for you to have a co-organizer. But given that finding a second person is also one of the most popular pieces of startup advice, it seems like it applies to most people, even if they’re as resilient and leader-y as most startup founders. So I’d recommend making it a high priority to find a co-organizer.

That’s hard, of course, because a co-organizer needs to have a lot of initiative and agency—they have to care about the project even when you don’t. On the other hand, if you can’t find any good co-organizers before you start your group, don’t worry too much—I didn’t know John before I started HEA, and only met him when he joined the group and came to some of our events. So you can probably recruit a co-organizer from your membership; just keep your eyes peeled for good ones, because it’s a _huge_ boost in organizational capacity.

## Don’t be embarrassed

The first time Harvard Effective Altruism did any sort of publicity was at Harvard’s “student activities fair,” where literally every student group at Harvard lined up at rows of tables, and masochistic students ran the gauntlet in a doomed quest to find meaning in undergraduate life.

We were running a [Giving Game](http://www.thelifeyoucansave.org/givinggames.aspx) in which we presented people with three charities and asked them to choose one to receive a dollar.

Here’s how a typical HEA pitch went:

> (student walks past)
> 
> (another student walks past)
> 
> (third student walks past)
> 
> (fourth student walks past, awkwardly glances towards the HEA poster)
> 
> **Ben:** Hi?
> 
> **Student:** Um, hi, what is Harvard Effective Altruism?
> 
> **Ben:** _(sheepishly)_ Okay, so I know this sounds cheesy, but we actually try to figure out the best ways to improve the world and then do those things.
> 
> **Student:** Huh?
> 
> **Ben:** Like it turns out some charities are WAY better than others! Want to play this Giving Game?
> 
> (bemused student makes excuses and leaves)

After three hours of such pitches we had a grand total of one person who came back for more than one meeting.

The reason our publicity was so disastrously bad is that I was bad at talking to people, for a couple of reasons:

1.  I realized that the idea of HEA seemed crazy: “join us and we’ll try to figure out how to maximize the good we do in the world!”—it combined the tired cliche of trying to save the world with the taboo of declaring that some ways of saving it were better than others. But my mistake was _letting other people know_ that I knew HEA seemed crazy. As soon as they realized that I myself felt goofy, it was game over for convincing them to get involved.
2.  Because I felt like I was pushing a crazy idea, I was also worried about wasting people’s time. This was just silly. If the students didn’t want their time wasted, they shouldn’t have tried to run the activities-fair gauntlet. And anyway, they were perfectly capable of extracting themselves from the pitch if they wanted to. We should have been less polite.

By contrast, here’s a similar pitch from when we were advertising in a dining hall this year:

> (student walks past)
> 
> (Ben walks up to them, holds out a dollar bill)
> 
> (student automatically takes dollar bill)
> 
> **Ben:** Do you want to donate this dollar to charity?
> 
> **Student:** Huh?
> 
> **B:** Like, we have a list of charities, and you can pick one to give this dollar to.
> 
> **S:** Oh, okay, I guess.
> 
> **B:** Awesome! It’s actually not literally that dollar, it’s this computer form. Can you read through these three blurbs and select whichever one you think will do the most good with your dollar?
> 
> **S:** Sure.
> 
> (S reads through blurbs)
> 
> **S:** So what’s the deal with this?
> 
> **B:** I’m glad you asked! So, like, you want to improve the world, right? _(S: Yeah.)_ And you prefer improving it _more_ if you can, right? _(S: Yeah.)_ So we try to figure out how to improve it the most. It’s weird how nobody else is doing that, huh?

By this time, I had gotten over my feeling of goofiness, and just powered through. I often acknowledged that HEA was _uncommon_, but not that it was _weird_. Plus, I was more aggressive about initially engaging with students—they had come to be marketed at, after all. I still didn’t give people a hard sell if they didn’t seem interested, but I was okay with being pushy to get their attention away from all the other pushy people. As a result, students were way more interested and engaged.

## Getting famous speakers

If there’s one thing that I’m proud of about my tenure running HEA, it’s our programming. Last year we had over 10 speakers come to give talks to HEA, including Peter Singer, Jaan Tallinn, and [many other extremely popular and busy people](http://harvardea.org/events/#past). We also raised thousands of dollars to cover honoraria from these speakers. How?

The trick is to be well-connected like me and John so that you know a diverse pool of famous people and deep-pocketed university organizations to lean on for favors. If you don’t have such a pool already, get out there and network!

…just kidding. I didn’t actually know most of our speakers before I invited them to speak. I just sent them a cold email asking if they wanted to give a talk for HEA (sometimes with an introduction from a friend or our excellent faculty adviser, [Nir](http://medethics.med.harvard.edu/people/eyal/)). We got a lot of silence—my archives are littered with the corpses of unanswered invitations—but we also got a lot of responses, and our awesome lineup of speakers was definitely worth the additional messages cast into the void.

I also have an important sub-tip here. For emailing potential speakers—and also for life in general—I found [Boomerang](http://www.boomeranggmail.com/referral_download.html?ref=4sbhr)[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/studentgroup/#fn:1) to be _totally indispensable_. I set any sent emails to return to my inbox in a week if the recipient didn’t respond, and often if I sent them a second follow-up email I’d get a response after that. In fact, Boomerang is practically a requirement to communicate with some professors, who I suspect just have a policy of never responding to email the first time it’s sent… Again—**if you rely on email to make important things happen and you don’t have Boomerang, start using Boomerang NOW.**

Psychologically, I often found it demoralizing to send cold emails, since each one individually had a fairly low chance of turning into a talk further down the road—it often felt like most of my work was wasted (although our response rate was a lot higher than I thought it would be). I tried to fight this by _valuing effort instead of results_—I would phrase my goals in terms of “high-quality emails sent” instead of “responses received” or “talks scheduled.”

Overall, something like 20% of people we cold-emailed ended up giving a talk at HEA. I can’t promise that this will translate to student groups at other schools—we certainly benefited from the Harvard brand—but even if we had gotten a much lower response rate, we could have sent more emails or started asking less-busy people. The credibility and attention that we got from having such a great lineup was a huge boost when we were recruiting new members, so I’m really glad we did it.

Cold-email awesome people! It works!

## Navigating bureaucracy

Running a student group entails doing a lot of paperwork and getting through a lot of red tape. Especially at first, I had a hard time keeping track of everything, and frequently left things until the last minute (or later) when e.g. reserving space for events.

After spending one too many afternoons on tenterhooks waiting for the room reservation office to email me back, I finally cracked under the stress. For once I had actually paid attention to the booking deadlines and reserved our room a week in advance—but they still hadn’t confirmed two days beforehand. Come hell or high water, I decided, I was going to get my room reserved _today_. I put on my best starving-frantic-student-in-need face, thought up my best excuses, and marched over to the Science Center to demand satisfaction.

Instead of the hard-nosed, battle-ready bureaucrat I’d been imagining, I was greeted by a very pleasant gentleman named Ron. Ron had gotten some files out of order and then been swamped by requests, because it was the beginning of the semester and all the student groups wanted space. He hadn’t realized we’d been waiting so long, and was happy I’d stopped by to ask about it—especially since we had to have some back-and-forth about the logistics, which went much faster in person than over email.

As a software developer, I’ve been trained to think that if Harvard specifies an interface for students to communicate with institutions, that interface is a Holy Abstraction Barrier That Shall Not Be Crossed. But it turns out that bureaucratic abstractions [leak](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/LeakyAbstractions.html) _even more_ than computational ones—the specified interfaces don’t really work very well. Combine that with the depersonalizing medium of email, where everyone is tempted to be rude or stop replying, and you have a recipe for unpleasant experiences.

I had the same experience again and again—room booking, financial forms, advertising space, and practically everything else we did went much more smoothly if we _showed up in person_ when we got confused or had something complicated to do. I still try to avoid it when possible—it takes up people’s time and would probably get annoying if we did it excessively, and having to resolve issues in person is a red flag that we’re getting some other part of the process wrong. But if bureaucracy starts to drag on, showing up in person is an almost sure-fire way to speed things along.

## Outside support

One easy way that HEA achieved suprising or impressive-seeming things is that we [reached out to lots of speakers](https://www.benkuhn.net/good-speakers). A second is that we had a _ton_ of support from folks who weren’t in our “core audience” of Harvard students. Both Harvard faculty and other EA-minded people gave us huge amounts of information, technical assistance, introductions, and even offers of funding if we needed it.

An incomplete list of things that we’ve needed non-student help for:

-   Our amazing faculty adviser [Nir](http://medethics.med.harvard.edu/people/eyal/) gave us a lot of suggestions and introductions to speakers, checked in periodically when we were having organizational problems, gave us advice on hosting speakers, and was generally all-around ridiculously helpful.

    HEA was actually required to have a faculty adviser per Harvard’s regulations, but I highly recommend having one even if your school doesn’t require you to—you can invite a sympathetic professor to be your official adviser and then meet with them, ask them advice by email, or whatever works for you.

-   Other Harvard faculty mentioned our activities in their classes, advertised our speakers, donated to our fundraiser, or had discussions over dinner with our members. These were all immensely helpful. In retrospect I wish we had tried to interact with more faculty, and more closely, than we did—I think many of them would have been happy to help even more.

    If you don’t know where to look for faculty, try the professors of philosophy (especially ethics), economics (especially development), public health (especially medical ethics), and psychology (especially moral psychology). Also try [emailing me](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/), as I may be able to connect you with other folks nearby.

-   [Jeff](http://jefftk.com/) and [Julia](http://www.givinggladly.com/) usually give the first talk of every semester, introducing new members to the ideas of effective altruism. Since they’re more relatable to college students than HEA’s typical speakers are (many of whom are professors or nonprofit executives), they’re great at providing a friendly and welcoming introduction to effective altruism.

-   An (anonymous) local effective altruist sponsored a matching challenge for our first winter fundraiser, which made it much more credible and much more motivating for people to donate. This made the fundraiser way more exciting both to the students running it and to the folks who donated.

    Another supporter offered us a substantial amount of money in back-up funds if the Harvard grants that we were applying for didn’t come through. Although we didn’t end up needing the funds last year, it was very helpful to be able to promise speakers with certainty that we’d be able to reimburse their travel and lodging expenses.

-   Other folks from the local Boston EA group came to our talks, advertised them to their friends, donated to our fundraiser, let us know when interesting speakers were passing through Boston, and generally provided a ton of miscellaneous advice and help.

I can’t speak for other kinds of student groups, but if you’re starting an effective altruism student group in particular, finding people who are willing to help is pretty easy—just post in the [Facebook group](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/?ref=br_tf) or [email me](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/) and I’ll put you in touch with folks who can help!

## Lessons from the philanthropy fellowship

The event we ran that really got our membership going was what we called the “philanthropy fellowship.” Although the content was specific to HEA’s mission, I think the idea and lessons generalize to any kind of student group. Here’s how the philanthropy fellowship worked:

1.  We [cold-emailed a bunch of awesome people](https://www.benkuhn.net/good-speakers) asking them to give talks for Harvard Effective Altruism’s philanthropy fellowship.

2.  We also got [an anonymous donor](https://www.benkuhn.net/support) to promise to match any funds our philanthropy fellowship raised for the term.

3.  At all of our events at the beginning of the year, we told everyone that we were running a “philanthropy fellowship” program in which fellows would apply and be accepted to attend a series of talks and dinners with our awesome speakers, and run a fundraiser at the end. At this point I believe our list of promised speakers included Peter Singer and Jaan Tallinn, so this generated a fair amount of interest.

4.  We put up an application with some basic questions, gave it a deadline, and publicized it to a ton of mailing lists (both ones for which it was on-topic, and dorm-wide mailing lists where it was permissible to hawk random clubs/activities.

5.  After the deadline, we called back all the applicants we thought were a good fit for a short one-on-one interview with one of the HEA organizers, where we asked them a couple questions (mostly to get to know them) and gave a little spiel about how the fellowship would go (schedule, expectations for attendance, the fundraiser at the end, etc.).

We weren’t very selective at all with the application—we mainly used it to screen out people who were obviously there just to promote their own charities, and I think we invited everyone who interviewed to do the fellowship.

But it still ended up creating a core group of people who came to nearly all of our events and engaged with HEA a lot. I’m not sure about all the factors that led to the success of the philanthropy fellowship, but I have a couple theories. (Note that these are _only theories_, i.e., not empirically verified—so generalize at your own risk!)

1.  **There was a big incentive for joining the fellowship.** Although our speakers’ talks were open to the public, the dinners with them were restricted to members of the philanthropy fellowship. We advertised this fact heavily and I’m sure the prospect of dinner with Peter Singer got at least one person to join.

2.  **Scarcity creates value.** If people perceive your group as hard to get into—even if it isn’t—then they’ll value their membership in it more, and get more excited about your events. This is why we had an application process, even though it was more work for us and we only rejected a couple people.

3.  **We asked for an explicit commitment to attend events first.** A problem for a lot of groups is that students are really interested at the beginning of the semester, but as classwork piles on they start to feel swamped and stop going to as many events. Since we asked students to commit to attending the entire fellowship at the beginning of the semester, they tended to make it a higher priority.

Of course, there are plenty of other lessons we learned from the fellowship as well—mainly about how we could have run it better.

## Advertising strategies

HEA tried a lot of different ways of getting people’s attention. Here are some of our workhorses—strategies that we use frequently and generally work well:

### Giving Games

_(Note: somewhat EA-specific, though the general spirit applies to other types of groups.)_

One thing almost all student groups do a lot of is tabling. We’ve found that interactive activities work pretty well—we often run [Giving Games](http://www.thelifeyoucansave.org/get-involved/giving-game-2) where we put up $1 for each participant and they decide to donate it to one of a list of charities we provide, based on which one they think will be the most effective.

Giving Games are cool in that they’re very easy to study scientifically—if you display peoples’ charity choices on a computer, you can very easily randomize what text and what options people see. You can then measure how many people give you their emails at the end of the Giving Game, as a proxy for how engaging they found the game. HEA did [some of this research](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/06/13/research/) at the end of last semester and found some interesting results.

First, including more speculative charities may decrease people’s engagement. HEA tested a group of three global poverty charities against a group of one global poverty charity, one “meta” charity, and one charity working on existential risks, and found that people offered the latter choice were significantly less likely to put down their emails.

Also, the data suggested (weakly, not-quite-significantly) that including information about how effective charities were _decreased_ people’s engagement in the game. This might be a real effect, but it’s possible it was due to confounding because the descriptions containing effectiveness info were longer, and people got bored. Or it’s possible that it’s a mere statistical fluctuation. More research is needed to find out, but either way, it’s a good reminder to keep Giving Games short and sweet.

If you’re running an EA group and want to run one, there’s more information at the link above. You can also get in touch with The Life You Can Save and they may be willing to fund your Giving Game.

### Giant eye-catching posters

And I do mean **giant**. Harvard has a set of four sandwich boards at the gate between Harvard Yard and the Science Center, which can be reserved one face at a time. Most student organizations take their normal event posters and tile the entire face of the sandwich board with about 20 of these. For our first talk of the semester, we instead decided to blow up our poster of [Larry Temkin](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Temkin)’s face by a factor of 20. A surprising number of folks at the event told us that the poster was how they found out about it, and I think a lot of this was that it was more eye-catching than the typical student-group poster.

### Professors

Often if you’re holding an academic-ish event, professors in related fields will happily promote it in their classes. HEA has had success asking professors in philosophy, economics, and public health to advertise various of our events. Professors also make [great allies in general](https://www.benkuhn.net/support), and asking them to introduce your event in class can be a great icebreaker.

### Personal appeals

Asking your friends to go to your events isn’t particularly scalable. But for more targeted things like applying to the [philanthropy fellowship](http://www.benkuhn.net/fellowship), we’ve had good success with individually asking friends if they’d be interested in applying. ### Mass communications

HEA has a mailing list of a few hundred students, and we also sent out emails to a number of general-interest mailing lists (e.g. dorm-specific mailing lists).

It looks a bit nicer to send emails to each list individually, and include a little bit of list-specific content in the email. But that’s annoying, because it means you have to cut-and-paste your email to a bunch of different lists. To speed up that process, you can [set up a mail merge in Gmail](http://lifehacker.com/5508283/how-to-set-up-a-mail-merge-in-gmail-for-personalized-mass-emails) and send a batch of emails with customizations from a spreadsheet.

We also used mail-merges sometimes when emailing applicants for the philanthropy fellowship, and it’s possible that you could use it for cold-emailing professors as well (although we haven’t tried it, and it takes some care to make the writing good enough and specific enough that the generic parts don’t sound bad).

The benefits of mail merge come not only from speeding up the batch emails that you were going to send anyway, but causing you to send much more email because it’s easier. Email can be quite effective at drumming up interest for talks and fellowships, so this is a pretty big boost.

### Facebook event invitations

We created Facebook events for all of our talks and mass-invited our classmates to these. In addition to letting people know about the events, but it also helped us gauge attendance and distribute messages (e.g. updates about the event location or links to slides) to everyone who wanted to attend. We included event RSVP links in all of our emails to lists as well, to contribute as much as possible to having one centralized place where everyone going to the event could get info.

Mass-inviting all your relevant Facebook friends to every event is a huge slog, of course, because if you don’t want to spam people you have to manually curate the list of people you invite, which means looking through your entire friend list and making a decision for each friend for each event. Fortunately, there’s a much easier way, which takes advantage of the fact that if you make a “friend list” on Facebook, their algorithms will automatically suggest that you add other similar friends to it. Here’s how it works:

1.  First, create a Friend List and add maybe 50 or so classmates on it that you want to invite to your group’s events. Then go to the list and look at the friend suggestions—Facebook will suggest that you add the rest of your classmates. Add whomever you want (I suggest all of them, since invite spam is normal, but if you want to curate the list more heavily, Facebook will get smarter about who it suggests to be on the list).
2.  Now add a bookmark in your browser to the following link: `javascript:elms=document.getElementsByName(“checkableitems[]”);for (i=0;i<elms.length;i++){if (elms[i].type=“checkbox”)elms[i].click()};`
3.  Now when you want to mass-invite these people to an event, go to the event page and click “Invite Friends”. Click the dropdown that says “Search by Name” and select the list that you made in step 1. You should now see all the people on the list show up below.
4.  Scroll all the way down that list (this is important—the later checkboxes won’t appear until you scroll down, so you won’t end up inviting everybody otherwise).
5.  Click the bookmark you made in step 2. Everyone should be checked off and you can invite them!
6.  Facebook limits the number of people you can invite in one swoop, so to double-check that you invited everyone, repeat steps 3-4 and verify that all the checkboxes are greyed-out (this means the friend has already been invited). If not, repeat steps 3-5 as necessary.

This is way faster than manually checking a bunch of boxes—about 30 seconds instead of 5-10 minutes for me. It also means that we can reliably get all the organizers to invite their friends, which means our Facebook events have a much broader reach.
