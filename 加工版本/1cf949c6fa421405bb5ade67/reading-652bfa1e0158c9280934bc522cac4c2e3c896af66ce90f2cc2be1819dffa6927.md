## 引言

Michael Nielsen 和 Kanjun Qiu (N-Q) 最近发表了一篇关于 [meta science](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html) 的长文（30k 字）。过去一两年里，我也一直在这里发表关于 [meta-scientific](https://nintil.com/categories/science-funding/) 话题的文章，总计约 80k 字（到这个地步，这些与其说是文章，不如说是书了！），深入探讨各种 foundational papers 和 talking points 的细节。我在这些文章中提出的观点，有些很少见诸其他文字，尽管 metascience community 经常在线下讨论它们（大家普遍同意，[Azoulay 2011](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/) 这篇 paper 不会改变谁的想法，正如我在例如[这里](https://nintil.com/new-science-nih)提到的，但若不是我把这一点写出来，你就不会知道这已经是一个 established point！）。

花了这么多时间阅读和思考这个话题，又参加 meta-science conferences 并在会上发言之后，我得出了什么结论？接下来呢？

N-Q 的愿景是，我们把 _metascience develop 成一个 engine，driving improvement 于 humanity understands the world 的方式_。这是一个乐观的愿景，提出 _exploring 一个庞大的 design space，用于 social processes_，从而带来 _一组 structurally diverse 得多、用于 doing science 的 environments；_ 并最终使 _在现有 environments 内 difficult 或 impossible 的 crucial types of work_ 成为可能。

然而，从另一个角度看，我们所做的一切虽有趣，却终究只是无益的 theorizing，不会带来任何有意义的 change。

N-Q 收到的一条反馈体现了这种看法，他们也在文章中提及了它：“唯一重要的是 fund 那些 doing good work 的 good people”；言下之意是，试图深入思考 science works 的方式只会带来 minor improvements，让所有这些努力都成了浪费时间。只需找到 good scientists，给他们钱和时间，其他一切都是这条 science funding 的 central dogma 的脚注。

我也听过这种观点，而且我承认，我很认同它。在考虑是否写这篇文章时，我发现自己在想，我写过的单篇关于 [US immigration](https://nintil.com/us-immigration) 的文章，很可能比我花在 metascience 上的所有时间都更有帮助；例外是我关于一般意义上的 _bottleneck analysis_，尤其是 aging 的工作，因为它促成了一个具体的 project：[Rejuvenome](https://www.buckinstitute.org/news/astera-institute-and-buck-institute-announce-70-million-collaboration-to-redefine-the-field-of-research-on-aging/)。写这些其他文章所花的时间，比阅读不计其数的 metascience 书籍、papers 和会议录像所花的时间还少；后者就像试图榨一颗干柠檬，结果却榨不出多少汁。因此，这让我开始思考：花时间做 meta-scientific work 的 returns 是什么？如果一个人在 meta-science 上花费 1000 小时，与其他选择相比，这算善用时间吗？更广泛地说，**一个以 accelerating science 为 high level mission 的人应该做什么**？

在这篇文章中，我想探讨这个 negative case：possible institutions 的 space 并不像表面上那么广阔；我们能获得的 benefits 往往是 marginal，尽管并非总是如此；而这些 benefits 又很难 measure，使我们甚至难以知道如何 improve 这个 system，或形成一套 general prescriptions。不过，metascience 可以向 ''metaentrepreneurship" 学习。这是一套 heuristic、基于 case-study 的 body of knowledge，在另一个迄今仍难以进行有用的 scientific theorizing 的 domain——entrepreneurship——的日常实践中，经常得到运用。

由于本文是对 N-Q 那篇文章的评论，你应该先读那篇！阅读我此前关于这个话题的文章也会有所帮助。

这里，我讨论 N-Q 文章中提到的一些 examples，借此提出关于 deriving scientifically grounded prescriptions 以 improving science 之困难的各种观点。

## Example 1：replication crisis 作为 metascience 的成功案例

replication crisis 是 N-Q 文章讨论的主要成功 example。还有更多类似的 metascientific victories 等待着我们吗？这个 example 是 progress 的 existence proof：如果有人认为_什么也做不了_，总可以指出这个可信的 counterexample。

psychology 历史上的这段插曲，始于 researchers 在数十年间逐渐注意到，该 field 遵循的 statistical methods 和 publication practices 正在导致很可能并非 true 的 results。最终，由 Brian Nosek 和 Open Science Collaboration 引领的 reform movement，以明确的 replication 尝试为基础，促成了 research practices 的 field-wide change。

这是 meta-scientific work 的一个好 example，因为其中的工作未必涉及具有有趣 properties 的、新的、更 powerful 的 statistical methods（那会是 _scientific_ work），而是涉及：

> deep 的新 ideas，例如 Registered Reports，以及使它们能够运作所必需的 toolbuilding 和 infrastructure。这需要与 journals 及其他 organizations 建立 partnerships，让 Registered Reports 有机会被 widely adopted。这需要 branding、marketing 和 narrative-crafting，以取得 widespread adoption，并开始改变 scientists 的 internalized values。这还需要 building 更多 tools 和 infrastructure，以 store code、data 和 materials，让 replication 更容易。而且，正如我们将看到的，这需要 institution-building。

重要的是，事后_每个人都认为这是成功的_。没有人说：“嗯，要是没有 replication crisis，谁知道事情是不是照样会改变！”papers fail to replicate 这件事，已经被 repeatedly 且 unquestionably 地 shown。

这种事能在其他地方发生吗？这段插曲之所以可能发生，是因为可以推动大家广泛同意：当时使用的 methods 是 weak，而且没能 replicate；也因为可以通过实际尝试 replicate 多项 studies 来 show 这一点，同时开展大量 pedagogical work，并提出以 statistics 为基础的 theoretical arguments。

例如，假设从来没有人尝试开展 large scale replication effort。那么就不会有 replication crisis，也不会有多少改变。这里真正重要的是一个 experiment（一次 large scale replication attempt），它向一个 field 表明，_他们的 methods_ 并不好。事实上，正如 N-Q 指出的，早在数十年前，Meehl 等人就已经提出相同的 theoretical arguments，但直到实际开展 experiments，这个 field 才有了较大改变。

那么，例如在 physics 的某些 areas，experiments 目前是 unfeasible，而且有人呼吁用 [post-empirical methods of theory](https://backreaction.blogspot.com/2014/07/post-empirical-science-is-oxymoron.html) confirmation，这种事会是什么样子？对 string theory 而言，replication crisis 的 equivalent 会是什么样子？这真的可能，还是仅仅是异想天开的 speculation？Lee Smolin 可以继续[写文章](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trouble_with_Physics)，说 string theory 是一个 cult，但我预测，这对该 field 的 effect 不会像 replication crisis 对 psychology 的 effect 那么强。这并不是说 string theory 是 right 或 wrong，也不是说它是一个 cultish 或 openminded 的 field，而是说，在缺乏 clear experiments 依托的 domains 中，寻求 sociological 或其他方面的 change 很难。

目前，那里的情形看起来像 pre-replication crisis era：数十年间，scientists 一直争论其 methods 和 approaches 的 merits（以 physics 为例，[beauty](https://www.amazon.com/Lost-Math-Beauty-Physics-Astray/dp/0465094252) 是通向 truth 的好 guide 吗？），但我怀疑这些 arguments 能否动摇那些深信不疑的人：我们需要有 clear answers 的 experiments。

相比之下，science 的其他 areas 已经适合发生这种 disruption，例如 life sciences；清理这些 areas，对普通人会更有帮助。在这里，replicate studies 更难，因为哪怕只是开展 experiment，所需的 knowledge 都非常 specialized：一次 replication 的失败，可能只意味着 replicator 的 skilled 程度不够。因此，尽管最近有一个 replicate cancer papers 的 project，其中一些工作仍遭到质疑。引用一篇[较早的](https://nintil.com/links-54) Nintil 文章：

> Center for Open Science 尝试 reproduce 一批 cancer papers，却无法 [reproduce](https://twitter.com/BrianNosek/status/1468203976428605443) [它们](https://www.statnews.com/2021/12/07/cancer-reproducibility-project-research/)中的大多数（59%）。这并不意味着所有 effects 都不是 real！这意味着，他们要么无法获得用来 replicate 它们的 data/materials，要么尝试了却失败了。当然，目前还不清楚，这种 failure to replicate 是因为 CoS 一方的 protocols 是 improper（[tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge)！），还是因为原来的 research 确实是 flawed。话虽如此，这对 scientific practice 来说，看起来确实很糟糕。STAT News 的文章为这件事补充了更多细节：
> 
> “Human biology 很难，而做这件事的我们也是人。我们并不完美，这确实很棘手，”他说。“这些 replications 都不能 invalidate 或 validate 原来的 science。也许原来的 study 是 wrong——一个 false positive 或 false signal。反过来也可能是 true，也就是 replication 是 wrong。更有可能的是，两者都是 true，而我们开展 experiment 的方式中某个平常的细节 causing 了这种 difference。”

这是个好机会！在让 biological experiments 更 reproducible、results 更 solid 方面，我们肯定能有所 improve。这里有一个 clear 的 metric for success（做同样的事情，是否会 result in 同样的 outcome？），而且我们可以针对 scientists 如何接受 trained，或 protocols 如何被 recorded 开展 experiments，看看这会如何 impacts reproducibility。

因此，这可能是 experimental metascience 中最有前景、也可能 highest reward 的 area：Studying studies 为什么 fail to replicate，并 improving fields，直到 reproducibility 成为 norm。从这个 goal 出发，我们可以为 individual scientists（如何 do science）、funders（funding replications）和 entrepreneurs（是否需要 built 某种 product 来帮助实现这个 goal，例如 lab automation？）derive prescriptions。

但 science 的某些 areas _并非_如此：如果我们无法开展 clean experiments 来 measure 不同 approaches 如何 work，而且 N-Q 在一篇较早的 [essay](https://scienceplusplus.org/trouble_with_rcts/index.html) 中认为 funding 就是这种情况，那么该如何取得 _decisive results_（N-Q 的用语）来 drive change？。我们对那些 metrics of success 更简单的 science areas（replication）的乐观态度，不应 transfer 到 scientific institutions 的一般 reform 上。当然，N-Q 也承认，正因如此，advancing metascience 很困难：

> 我们不会 solve 如何 obtain 此类 \[metascientific\] results 这一 general problem：那需要数千人花上数十年。这个 problem 的 difficulty 有一个小小的 indicator：replication crisis 的 decisive results 并不是通过 RCT obtain 的；相反，它们是针对一个特定 problem，以 bespoke 的方式 obtained 的。不过，尽管存在这些挑战，我们仍然乐观地认为，今后的工作将 develop 足够 strong 的 approaches，以 routinely provide decisive metascientific results，completing 这个 metascience learning loop。

我想多听听这种乐观的理由，因为我倾向于相反的看法！

## 例子 2：Empowering 年轻的 scientists

Scientists 正在变老，而 cognitive abilities 往往在晚年 decline。scientific workforce 的 aging 是否构成了一个 problem？从 aggregate 来看，很可能是，正如我在[这里](https://nintil.com/age-and-science) concluded 的那样。这个 result 不只是通过考察 scientific output 得到 backed；它也 grounded in human biology。cognitive abilities 随 age 而 decline，作为一种 regularity，是我们思考 science 时可以利用的 [invariants](https://nintil.com/invariants-metascience) 之一：human cognitive ability declines，无论一个人是 mathematician 还是 biologist，无论他在 XXI 世纪还是 XVIII 世纪工作，也无论他是富有的 independent scientist 还是 R01-holding PI。但这还不足以 drive change。事实仍然是，有些年长的 scientists 依旧非常 productive。此外，当我们考虑 scientists 的 teams，或那些年长 scientist 更像 manager 的 labs 时，情况就更加不明确：一个 mentor-type figure 带着若干 students，很可能比屋里所有人都不到 25 岁更有效。

Institutions 在 theory 上允许年轻人拥有自己的 labs（例如，这要感谢 NIH Director's Early Independence Award），但并没有多少动力或意愿让所有 science 都变成这样。为年长的 academics 恢复 mandatory retirement 是否是好事，完全不明确。

尽管如此，如果有人把我的 _Young Researchers Research Institute_ 作为 institutional experiment 提出来，

> 但如果我们 radically empower 年轻人呢？
> 
> Young Researchers Research Institute (YRRI) 会接收刚刚完成 PhDs 的 students，让他们与其他较年轻的 scientists 一起，在这样的 environment 中 pursue 任何他们想做的 research。考虑到这会是一次古怪的 career move，它可能让一些 researchers 却步；但如果你真正相信自己的 idea，而你的 PI 不想让你去做，那么尝试的意愿可能比加入 YRRI 所涉及的 uncertainties 更强。
> 
> 有人可能反对说，刚出炉的 ~NFTs~ PhDs 还没有准备好 lead 自己的 research。这是无稽之谈。当然，也许其中有些人确实如此，但历史上有许多人很年轻就获得了 professorships，或 published groundbreaking results（例如，Pasteur 在 26 岁时首次成为 professor，Einstein 也在同样的年龄 publishing 了他的 Annus Mirabilis papers）。要让 YRRI 运作起来，我们不需要每个人都在那个年龄准备好进行 independent research，只需要 _some_ scientists 已经准备好，并且愿意 embark on 一个自己 design 的 project。
> 
> 这并不意味着他们要孤军奋战：会有一个由 established scientists 组成的 advisory board，为 YRRI members 提供 coaching 和 scientific advice；而且 YRRI 不会 focused on 某一个特定 topic，在同一屋檐下，可以有人 researching [doubly-special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_special_relativity)、[RNA computation](https://twitter.com/theHessam/status/1296650944302776321)、[gene circuits](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-018-0215-0) 或 [new simulation algorithms for mechanical systems](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/computationalnonlinear/article-abstract/12/1/011020/473624)。

我会非常兴奋！也许，如果我把类似的 proposal 充分展开，就会清楚地发现它行不通；但也许它能挺过那个 planning stage。无论如何，让 YRRI 成为一个实际存在的 institution，会是对这一 hypothesis 更 cleaner 的 test：全面年轻化的 scientific workforce，会比我们目前拥有的带来更多（interesting？Useful？Novel？Different？Riskier？）science。作为一个 cleaner experiment，从中 draw conclusions 会更容易，尽管仍然困难。

这为什么应该有效？Younger scientists on average 比年长的 scientists 知道得少，尤其是在 life sciences 这样的 tacit-knowledge-heavy areas。但他们也可以更努力地工作，工作更长时间。他们是否会从事 'more interesting/riskier' research，并不是 a priori 给定的。可以想象，与 YRRI 同时设立另一个 institute，不按 age 进行 selecting，而是 selects 'weird research'。Don [Braben](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/) 也许已经这样做过：他的 Venture Research [fellows](https://nintil.com/age-and-science#conclusion) 并不是特别年轻。与年长者相比，他较年轻的 fellows 并没有在任何明显意义上更 successful。

到了这一步，我们又回到了 null hypothesis：“fund good people doing good work”，无论他们年长还是年轻。但为此，拥有专门的 institutions 是否更好？例如，在目前的 system 中，隐含的默认要求是，一个人 _必须_ 年纪大了才能 start a lab，除非他能入选每年少量的特殊 fellowships 和 grants。如果相信有很多聪明的 students 本可以 running 自己的 labs、doing great research，却因缺少机会而暂时无法这样做，那么 starting a YRRI 就是合理的。我预计，一个人是否会 start a YRRI，将在很大程度上取决于他对那些自己想要 empower 的具体 young scientists 的亲身了解，而不是被关于这为何可能是个好主意的 abstract arguments 所说服。

## 例子 3：Thiel Fellowship

Thiel Fellowship 虽然不是尝试 improve science 的例子，却很好地展示了 successful institutional entrepreneurship 是什么样子，N-Q 的 essay 也是这样讨论它的。

2011 年推出时，Fellowship 做了一件相当独特的事。基于一种坚定信念，即某些年轻人通过 dropping out 并 starting a company（或写书，或 doing research），可以更好地利用自己的人生，Thiel Foundation 承诺提供 $100k to each Fellow if they dropped out. They were mocked as a "[misdirected piece of philantrophy](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/10/thiel-fellows-program-is-most-misdirected-piece-of-philanthropy-says-larry-summers/)" and made fun of; it was pointed out that some Fellows ended up pursuing [uninspiring](https://www.quora.com/What-were-the-results-of-the-first-Thiel-Fellowship-Class/answers/1293281) goals and that instead of flying cars the Fellows got us caffeine sprays (no [joke](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/20/sprayable-energy/)). Some even went back to school. But the Fellowship also boosted and enabled the careers of people like Laura Deming, Vitalik Buterin, Dylan Field, Boyan Slat, or Austin Russell. 11 years of the Fellowship have costed perhaps 30M$；按 venture standards 来看，这并不是一大笔钱，尤其是与 Fellows 整体取得的成就相比。我们能从中学到什么？假设 Thiel 当时决定把这笔钱转而捐给 Harvard。即便不 running an RCT，我的直觉也是，那不会产生同样的 impact。

事实上，Peter Thiel（以及早期的 Fellowship team，例如 Danielle [Strachman](https://twitter.com/dstrachman) 和 Michael [Gibson](https://twitter.com/William_Blake?ref_src=twsrc%5Egoogle%7Ctwcamp%5Eserp%7Ctwgr%5Eauthor)）在开始之前并没有 run RCTs，他们大概也没有为 design 这个 Fellowship 而 conducted 任何形式的 preliminary scientific research。他们 noticed 一个 market opportunity：被困在 university 里的 Talent，可以用少量的钱和一个 network 来 unlocked。注意到这个 opportunity 后，他们 designed 了一个 program 来 fix 这个问题，而且可以说他们 succeeded 了。

我认为，这应该是 metascientific work 的 general playbook；它更应该是一种 institutional entrepreneurship：先 noticed 一个 concrete issue，再 proposed 一个 fix，并通过恰当的 channels 将其 instantiated（有时是 policy，有时是新的 institutions，或对现有 institutions 的 reform）。重要的是，与 entrepreneurship 一样，这种 fix 非常 context dependent，而且 _不_ 适合进行 scientific（intersubjective、general、crisp、testable）theorizing。

还有其他可以 build 的 Thiel Fellowships 吗？

例如，Lee Smolin argues，只有少数 theoretical physicists 真正在 pushing the field forward，这可以说是一个颇具争议的看法。Entrepreneurship 正是建立在这些大胆看法之上的，所以这是一个 promising 的开始。他 suggests，应该为这少数杰出的 scientists 提供 funded，让他们开展自己的 research。这会非常便宜！而 results 可能是 transformational 的。

> “但说到 theoretical physics，我们谈的根本不是多少钱。假设一个 agency 或 foundation 决定 fully support **所有那些 visionaries**，他们 ignore the mainstream，follow 自己雄心勃勃的 programs，以 solve quantum gravity 和 quantum theory 的 problems。**我们谈的也许是** **two dozen theorists**。Fully Supporting 他们，只需要任何大国 physics budget 的极小一部分。”

我不知道自己会如何 choose 这 two dozen theorists。我无法 start 这个特定的 institution；我缺少所需的 knowledge，也没有足够相信 Smolin 的大胆看法，因而无法真正付诸行动。而要论证为什么偏偏是 _这个_ initiative 能最好地利用 funder 的钱，就更难了。但在我看来，如果我们按 Smolin 所说的去做，就会比不这样做看到更多 interesting physics results；对于一位尤其对 physics 感兴趣的富有 philanthropist 而言，cost 也很小。肯定比 building 更大的 [colliders](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-world-doesnt-need-a-new-gigantic-particle-collider/) 便宜！

## 例 4：Diversifying funding（使资助多样化）

N-Q 的文章没有提到、但他们在多次线下活动中讨论过的，是 NIH 的一项具体 proposal：使用一种叫作 Grant Support Index 的东西来 cap 每个 lab 的 funding；我曾在[这里](https://www.worksinprogress.co/issue/we-dont-know-how-to-fix-science/)和[这里](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/)讨论过。早在 2017 年，就有 research 指出，按照一些基于 citation counts 的 metrics，labs 越 bigger，就越不 efficient。这 implies，与其让一个 lab 拥有 27 项 grants，不如让 9 个 smaller labs 各有 3 项 grants；两者 cost 相同，而后者可能是对资金更好的利用。

应该这样做吗？这就不那么明确了！正如我在自己的文章中所展示的，large labs 更不 productive 这一 conclusion，取决于你如何 measure productivity。我们可能希望拥有 large labs，是有理由的：如果一个 PI 是个优秀的 manager，而且善于 picking 和 managing 优秀的 students，那么为什么不让他们拥有自己的 large lab 呢？我们又回到了 case-by-case 的选择。遗憾的是，metascience work 往往就是如此：actionable insights 淹没在一片“一方面如此、另一方面又那样”的汪洋之中。

N-Q 的文章有一个统一的 conclusion section，他们在其中对 developing metascience，使之成为一种能够“driving improvement 于 humanity understands the world 的方式的 engine”的东西，表现出了一些试探性的乐观。如果说整篇文章有一项反复出现的 recommendation，那就是呼吁更多 structural diversity，因为：

> 这样的 structural diversity 是 science 的一种 core 而 precious 的 resource，一种 enlarging humanity 能够 successfully attack 的 problems 范围的 resource。原因在于，不同的 ambient environments 能够 enable 不同种类的 work。在一种 environment 中 easily soluble 的 Problems，在另一种中可能近乎 insoluble；反过来也一样。事实上，我们往往并不 _a priori_ 知道，什么 environment 最能 enable 对一个 important problem 的 attack。因此，确保有许多非常不同的 environments 可供使用，并使 scientists 能够在它们之间 liquidly move，是很重要的。

然而，一个希望以某种方式 reform science 的人，读过这些之后，仍然可能认为，与其开展更多可能 fail 的 diverse initiatives，不如加大对已奏效做法的投入。他们的文章对这种 reasoning 提出的一项反驳，是 diminishing marginal returns：

> 可以这样想：如果你向 Harvard 或类似的 incumbent donate \$10 million，那么一个合理的 rough model 是，这笔钱会用于 _Harvard 尚未 fund 的事物中最好的那一个_。但 Harvard 拥有庞大的 budget。因此，它会 fund 某个在 Harvard 的 priority list 上排得很靠后的项目：某个 Harvard 认为不够好、因而没有用其最初 several billion dollars 的 spending 来 fund 的项目。相比之下，向新方向开拓的 startup ventures，正在那些方向上尽其所能。Diminishing marginal returns 尚未开始显现。Structural diversity 可能显得混乱、令人困惑，但它也提供了一个机会，让我们摆脱 diminishing marginal returns 的束缚，并 enable 新种类的 creative work。$$...$$ 或许，如果真正重要的是 outlier successes，那么拥有许多 small 但截然不同的 cultures 就足够了。按照这种看法，scaling 可能没有那么重要。事实上，也许 scale 甚至是 _undesirable_ 的。

但每个 institution 应该有多 big 呢？

> 根本 question 是：是什么决定了任何此类 program 的 scale？是风潮、反复无常的观感、politics，以及 **highly legible stories**？还是 scientific contribution，以及这个 program 作为更广泛 ecosystem 的一部分所具有的 value？目前，几乎完全是前者，后者只是偶然起作用。当然，风潮、反复无常的观感和 politics 往往不会以其本来面目出现。它们表现为善意的人们在 _Nature_、_Science_ 和 _The New York Times_ 上发表的热情洋溢的文章。它们表现为新 institutions 那些热情而才华横溢的 founders，带着自己关于 _事情如何能变得更好_ 的心爱想法。这对于 generating 新 social processes 非常出色，却是糟糕的 evaluation 依据。最终结果是一种自然形成的 monoculture，一种 oligarchy，其中某种东西要么多得过头，要么远远不够。这对 science 是一场灾难。

我担心，作出这种 distinction 在实践中可能并没有多大意义。比如，拿刚成立不久、funded 充足的 Arc Institute 来说，它之所以以目前的形式存在，并不是因为它已被证明 successful，而是因为此前的 institutions，例如 Broad Institute 或 HHMI Janelia，存在着关于 success 的 **legible stories**，这些故事帮助塑造了它的 design。我们可以说，有理由相信它会 successful（如果它 attracts 合适的 talent），而这也使它起步时获得的 funding 有了正当依据。但在那之后，如何 evaluating 这个 institution 的 performance 就不那么明确了，原因正是 Nielsen 和 Qiu 在[此前的](https://scienceplusplus.org/trouble_with_rcts/index.html)一篇文章中反复列举的那些。

正如他们（N-Q）不得不借助 science 中关于 struggle 和 success 的 highly legible stories（Kariko 的、Church 的），来传达自己的观点，未来对 Arc 的 performance 进行评估的人，也会同样借助那些获得 Nobel Prize 等 highly legible awards 的 discoveries。事情很难不是如此：science 的 core 是 intersubjectivity，如果我不能以令人信服的方式向你展示，为什么我选择的 institution 或 researcher 是一个好的 funding target，那么我们就只是在争论感觉：尽管 science 的日常工作，以及可以说 entrepreneurial metascience 的 practice，都需要在 illegible gray areas 中开展工作，但作为 science 的 metascience practice，要求最终形成的 metascientific body of work 至少具有某种 legibility。

与我摘录的第一段（支持 approaches 的 diversity）相比，他们在文章其他地方似乎更赞成 flexibility：

> 然而，在 science policy 领域，interventions 往往侧重于现有 power structures 内 practical 的事情。我们关注的将是更 _a priori_ 的原则性 questions，以及 enabling decentralized change，也就是可能发生在现有 power structures 之外的 change。出于所有这些原因，我们认为这篇文章仅仅是 metascience 的一部分。$$...$$
> 
> 我们想要的是：**一个 flourishing ecosystem，其中的人们对新 social processes 有着极富想象力、富有洞察力的 ideas；并让这些 ideas 得到 tested，让最好的 ideas 得到 scaled out**。

也许在他们心中，diversity 和 flexibility 比在我心中更接近。对我而言，可以说更重要的是 flexibility：只有一个主要的 search engine（Google），与 alternatives 相比，它 works 得足够好，但任何人都可以自由挑战它。一个人完全可以同时认为，这个 system 不需要那么多额外的 funding structures experiments，而且应该有更多人被 empowered，就此 make decisions。如果他们恰好作出相同的决定，那也是一种 valid outcome。

拿 Soviet Union 来说，我在[一些](https://nintil.com/the-crumbling-public-private-distinction)[旧文章](https://nintil.com/the-privatepublic-distinction-a-reply-to-stringham-and-powell/)中曾 argued，USSR 以及一般意义上的 central planning 的 problem，主要不在于 private property 被 abolished；你可以想象某个人完全 owning 整个国家，而他们仍然会面临相同的 planning problems。problem 在于，没有对 resources 的 fine-grained 而 fluid 的 control，来让更好的 decision makers 对它们 make decisions（这就是 markets 好的原因）。正如我在那些文章中讨论的，Network effects 可能使这个基本解释复杂化，但乍看之下，这个 theory 似乎是对的：如果我们为 science 增加几个 decision makers，而他们决定应该 fund NIH，而不是一个 _Institute for Traveling Scientists_（原文中的一项 proposal），我并不认为这显然是一个 wrong choice。

话虽如此，总体而言，相较于今天的状况，我赞成在 sciences 中拥有更多 independent 的 funds 和 resources control 来源。也许未来我们会拥有一种 optimal 的 monoculture，但我并不确信 NIH 的那一种就是。

### CRISPR 与 Theory of Everything

也许，可以这样理解 N-Q 与我的出发点：

想象一个巨大的仓库，里面装满了老虎机。每台老虎机都是一个由特定 mechanism 进行 funded 的 field，而这个仓库就是整体的 science。每台机器过一段时间就会停止工作。每拉动一次老虎机的拉杆，都会给你一个 lognormally distributed payoff。任何一台新造机器的 mean 都是从 half-Cauchy distribution 中 drawn 的（它具有 undefined mean，而且有时会因 fat tails 而出现非常大的 outliers）。

你可以选择复制一台现有机器，或在某个位置随机生成一台。你会怎么选？这就相当于在加倍投入某种 funding mechanism 与启动一种新的 funding mechanism 之间作选择。对于上面 toy example 中 parameters 的不同选择，正确的选择可能是前者，也可能是后者。

在一个大部分 science 仍在前方等待我们的世界里，着重 exploring 是合理的；而在一个大多数 useful fields 都已 established 的世界里，转而 exploit 那些 fields 才是合理的。我更倾向于后一种看法，而 N-Q 或许会主张：未必是说 science 真的是 endless frontier，但至少我们无法知道这一点，因此我们应当宁可偏向 maximizing discovery。

说得直白些：你更想要一个更好的 CRISPR，还是下一个 quantum mechanics？这大概就是 crux question。我的回答是，当然给我下一个 CRISPR。与此同时，我也很容易想象，有人抱着不同的 priorities 和 aesthetics，会说请给我下一个 QM。下面这段 Michael 的话表明，比起推动一个已经很“热门”的 field，他在 aesthetics 上更偏好 field-building：

> **DEVON**：competition 在什么时候是 productive 的，又在什么时候更 toxic？
> 
> **MICHAEL**：我想我并不接受这个问题的 premise。Toxic，嗯，好吧。行，让我顺着这个问题说，接受这个问题的 premise。
> 
> 你们大概都能猜到我的看法——不过，听这段话的人也许猜不到。$$...$$
> 
> 如果有一千个人都在 competing 做同一件事，其实其中许多人可以去做别的事。他们可以 invent 其他可玩的 games，其中有些会是 unique games。全世界只有他们在玩的 games；如果他们去做那些别的事，不仅对他们自己更有意义，对他们的家人和朋友也更有意义，对世界也会更好。$$...$$
> 
> 他 \[Hamming\] 是那种更 competitive 的人。他经常想赢。就连他提问时采用的 framing：“你的 field 中最 important 的 problems 是什么，你为什么不 working on 它们？”在我看来，也是一种有点傻的 framing。这是在 accepting 关于 field 是什么的 consensus social reality，而实际上，更好的做法是弄清楚：有哪些 problems，你的 field 中还没有人理解它们是 important 的。**有点像是尝试 invent new problems，甚至可能是 new fields。这在我看来是更令人愉快、最终也更有意义的活动**。（Nielsen，出自《专家们聚在一起谈论 [Richard Hamming](https://www.notion.so/blog/a-roundtable-on-richard-hamming) 的影响》）

与此相反，我觉得 scientific competition 极其有价值。如果相关 labs 之间没有友好（有时也不那么友好）的 competition，我们就不可能像实际那样迅速地走到 gene therapies 这一步。如果他们选择去做一些没人做的其他事情，我们或许会理解得更多，却能做到的更少。我说“或许”，是因为我无法向你 prove：加倍投入某个 field 比开创一个新 field 更好。我只是说，后者并非在所有情况下都 a priori 比前者更好。

这里有一个可以作出的具体 funding decision。以每年 $100k、持续两年的成本，你更愿意

-   为一名 promising student 提供 Fund，让其在 Arc Institute 从事 Hsu 的 lab 关于 [serine](https://twitter.com/pdhsu/status/1457414320300572675) recombinases 的 work 的下一步（否则这名 student 就没有机会从事这项 work）
-   挑选一个给 small lab 的 unfunded grant，其从 reviewers 那里收到的 feedback 是 highly variable 的，并 fund 它。

pro-diversity heuristic 会鼓励我们 fund 后者。Arc Institute 已经 well funded，多一名 student 就会多做一个 unit 的“Arc Institute work”，而第二个选项则会做一些不同寻常的事情。这个 guiding principle 应当占多大权重，又应当在多大程度上 case-by-base 地看待这个具体 decision。从 entrepreneurial 的角度看，并不存在制定 general plans 这回事，只有 entrepreneur 利用手头可用的 means 和 circumstances，在此时此地 acting。

## 作为 platonic form 的餐馆，或者外星人是否会有 PhDs

![image-20221201154634555](https://nintil.com/images/2022-11-21-metascience-limits/image-20221201154634555.png)

来源：https://lexica.art/prompt/632bb078-94d8-42f4-9567-a8e05469626b

在文章开头，N-Q 问，外星人是否也会有 NSF 或 Harvard。人们可能会想，他们当然会同意应当 experiment 并 share results，但他们的 institutions 会像我们的吗？这在他们看来似乎荒谬，在我看来却不然！在我看来，institutional design 的 space of possibilities 比他们所认为的更 constrained。这反过来降低了在 margin 上 experimenting 新 institutional forms 的价值，相比之下，加倍投入我们已知有效的形式更有价值。

可以这样类比：大多数餐馆都很相似。它们有厨房、有座位，有采购食材以及处理剩菜和垃圾的流程。它们会递上账单，也都有菜单。有些可能遵循 [Escoffier's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigade_de_cuisine) brigade system 来组织厨房团队，另一些则不会。有些提供精致美食，另一些提供快餐。

这种向“餐馆”这一 concept 的 convergence，可以用 human physiology 和 customs 的基础 facts（我们需要吃饭、与亲友一起吃饭、喜欢多样的口味），以及 economics（money 与 time 之间的 tradeoff、烹饪的 economies of cooking scale）来 explained。餐馆风格的 Differences 可以用更 context dependent 的 factors 来 explained（需要迎合的不同 preferences 和 purchasing power 意味着 McDonalds 和 French Laundry 的存在）。如果外星人在某些相关方面与我们相似，我们就可以 predict，他们也会有餐馆。

这种 convergence 的原因，并不是只有一家连锁餐馆，恰好以相同的方式做事，压制了 experimentation；全世界有数以百万计的餐馆老板。原因在于，餐馆最适合我们给定的 context。餐馆的 platonic form 从 physics 和 context 中 emerges，正如 calcium-potassium-sodium [neurons](https://www.quantamagazine.org/comb-jelly-neurons-spark-evolution-debate-20150325/) 在 evolution 中一次又一次出现那样。

与这种 convergence 并行的，还有 innovations：外卖、cloud kitchens、菜单长度上更多的 [variance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0qNWzpOc69U)，或者日式的 [payment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iXJgt6etKz4)——服务员不必拿着卡和账单来回折腾，只需在出口交接一次。后一种做法尚未在西方餐馆中普及，这表明 core concept 仍有 improvement 的潜力。

有些餐馆则截然不同：[女仆](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maid_caf%C3%A9)咖啡馆、由你而不是厨师自己烹饪食物的[火锅](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_pot)餐馆、在黑暗中[吃饭](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_dining)的餐馆，或是用电话点餐、食物由小火车送来的[餐馆](https://www.fritzskc.com/)。我在写这一段时，刚想出了一个[新餐馆点子](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1598418195903565824)。挺有趣，但别指望它很快就会风靡。

如果要开一家餐馆，应该开什么样的餐馆？你应该开一家汉堡店，让它为汉堡带来 [Tartine](https://blog.ciachef.edu/elisabeth-prueitt-chad-robertson-bio/) 为面包带来的改变吗？还是应该开一家古怪的餐馆，只用 bread、beans、butter 或 bananas 这类以“B”开头的食物来做菜？

这就是我读 N-Q 文章时的一部分反应：Monoculture 并不 necessarily equal stagnation，向某种 pattern 的 convergence 可能有非常好的理由（正如餐馆那样），而 science 与餐馆的相似程度，比 N-Q 所说的更高。

我的另一部分反应是同意：我们还远未完全弄清 science，而且目前确实存在一些可以使它 improvement 的合理 changes。这在一定程度上是因为，science 中的 decision making 确实非常 centralized，因此 experimentation 比 market 中少。而且，与 markets 不同，并没有一条明显的 transmission chain，把 institutional performance 与 norm dissemination connects 起来。如果 successful institutions 能够 scaled，确实会很有用，但我们如何 scientifically 地知道，什么是 successful scientific institution？

Social institutions 并不由 laws of physics 直接 dictated。[The Soviet Union,](https://nintil.com/the-soviet-union-series/) 曾在一段时间里，对[不同](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy)程度的 [central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet-type_economic_planning) planning 进行 experimented。虽然正如我曾经 [argued](https://nintil.com/the-soviet-series-from-farm-to-factory-stalins-industrial-revolution/) 的那样，它们 underperformed 一个 counterfactual market economy，但 USSR 的持续存在并没有“违背” economics，原因与 market economy 并非由“positive” economics 的 body of work 所 implied 相同。_political economy_ 能告诉我们的是：在给定某些 goals（例如广义理解的 human welfare），以及某些关于人类行为方式的 background facts 后，有些 systems 会比另一些更有可能 achieve 那些 goals。

对 metascientists 来说，Studying legal systems 可能是一项富有成效的活动：与 science 一样，law 是一种存在于每个 modern society 中的 complex social phenomenon。与 science 不同，law 几乎从人类历史开始时就已存在。每个 society 都必须 solve 类似的 problems，学会应对 power 并 foster coordination，也都有机会为这些 problems develop 不同的 solutions。

如今，广泛存在的主要 system 有[两个](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_legal_systems#/media/File:Map_of_the_Legal_systems_of_the_world_\(en\).png)，即 civil 和 common law systems。每个 system 都 addresses 相同的 problems，但它们采用的方式略有不同（一个大量涉及 juries，另一个则不然）。哪个更好？其中一个（或两个）是否 unfair？我们能否拥有更好的 legal systems，不只是对现有 systems 做些微调，而是 radically different 的 systems？

我们今天观察到的 legal systems 是几个世纪 evolution 的结果，但也与它们所在国家特有的 political histories 纠缠在一起。认为我们拥有的 systems 已经足够好，并非不合理；但它们也很可能仍然能够 improvement。

我们可以做的一项 exercise，是尝试从 first principles 来 derive 一个 legal system，看看它与我们现有的相比如何。例如，鉴于我们对人类的了解，让某人在自己涉入的 case 中担任 judge，似乎不是个好主意，因为他们会是 biased。因此，每个 society 都已朝着 third-party adjudication of justice 这一 principle 发展。这方面的 exceptions 涉及由 single individual 统治的 societies（如 medieval kings），这些人不受自己的 laws 约束（不过请注意，这种 exception 只适用于一个人，大部分 justice 仍然是 third-party mediated）。

在 _The Structure of Liberty_ 一书中，Randy Barnett 尝试做的正是这件事：从 high level considerations 来 deriving 一个 ideal legal system，而它最终（剧透一下）与我们现有的相似。他把这个 idealized system 称为“natural law”。虽然我多年前就读过这本书，但他用来引入这一 concept 的 [analogy](https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/1235/)，直接影响了我对 institutions 整体、尤其是 science 的思考方式：

> 当我们想到 engineering 或 architecture 这些 disciplines 时，natural law 的 idea 就不那么神秘了。例如，engineers 会这样 reason：鉴于 gravity 对 building 所 exerts 的 force，如果我们想要一座能让人在其中生活或工作的 building，就需要提供具有一定 strength 的 foundation、walls 和 roof。gravity 这一 physical law 引出了如下针对 human action 的“natural law”训诫：既然 gravity 会使我们 fall rapidly，那么如果我们想活着并且快乐，就最好不要从高楼上跳下去。engineering 的 principles 虽然由人类 formulated，却不是其意志的产物。这些 principles 必须面对人类的 nature 以及人类所生活的世界；无论是否被任何 government 所 recognized 或 enforced，它们都在 operate。而且，尽管它们从未 perfectly precise，总是可能有 incremental improvements，有时甚至会有 breakthroughs，但它们远非 arbitrary；违背它们，风险自负。

除了 Barnett，我过去还读过一些对截然不同的 legal systems 的考察；例如 [Rules](https://nintil.com/comments-on-rules-for-a-flat-world-gillian-hadfield/) for a Flat World、[Invisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invisible_Hook) Hook（讨论 pirate institutions），以及最为全面的 Legal [Systems](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Legal%20Systems/LegalSystemsContents.htm) Very Different from Ours。

其中一个启示是，ideal legal system 有时可能取决于 context。Rules for a Flat World 描述了一个 developing country 尝试 copy 美国 legal system 的 case。结果并不好：它过于 complex，超出了当地 society 所能支持的程度。同样，由 village elders administered、没有 lawyers 参与的 customary justice 仍然存在，是因为一些 societies 在经济上无法证明配备专职 lawyers 或 judges 的合理性。

但 Barnett 会说，如果一个 society 与我们的足够相似，那么他们最终就会拥有一个与我们的相似的 system。

这对 metascience 有什么 implications？Legal systems 随着时间推移趋于 converge，approaches 的 diversity 不断下降；它们在处理 complex social phenomena 时，并不需要 randomized control trials，不过，随着 legal systems 在一个又一个 case 中受到 stress-tested，也积累了大量 learning by doing。我认为，通过 analogy，我们可以对 metascience 作类似的 reason，看看在 producing useful and/or interesting knowledge 这一 objective 下，science 可能是什么样子。这些 goals，以及人类本身的样貌，在多大程度上 constrain scientific institutions？

## 外星人会有 PhDs 吗？

或者，如果我们能将 human history 重新运行到今年，PhD 这一 institution 会以我们今天熟知的形式 re-emerge 吗？

大概会。

我在 Twitter 上问了[这个](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1596298839303913473)问题，灵感来自 N-Q 文章中的一段话，他们在那里问道：

> 想象你是一位 science fiction 作者，正在写一个描绘 alien species 作出 scientific discovery 的故事。在你的故事里，你近距离展示 alien scientists——他们如何工作，如何生活。你会让他们在类似人类 universities 的 institutions 中工作，拥有 **PhD system**、grant agencies、academic journals 等等吗？他们会使用 peer review 和 citation 这样的 social processes 吗？alien scientists 会像人类 scientists 一样，没完没了地争论 h-index、impact factor 以及类似的 measure science 尝试的 merits 吗？
> 
> 几乎可以肯定，我们人类的 scientific social processes 的 design 过于 contingent on 历史的偶然，以至于这些问题不可能全部回答“是”。人类似乎不太可能已经找到了 allocating scarce scientific resources 的最佳可能手段！我们怀疑，即便是 Harvard 或 US National Science Foundation 最热忱的支持者，也不会将它们视为 platonic ideal。h-index 及类似 measures 似乎也不太可能是 scientific merit 的 universal measures。
> 
> 这并不意味着外星人不会与人类共有许多 scientific facts 和 methodological ideas——例如，很可能会使用 mathematics 来 describe universe，或者让 experiment 在 improving 我们的 understanding 中发挥 central role。**但这样的外星人也很可能拥有 radically different social processes 来 support science**。那些 social processes 会是什么？他们是否可能已经发展出 scientific institutions，其相对于我们的 superior 程度，就如同 modern universities 相对于博学的 medieval monasteries？

在我看来，这似乎不太可能！请注意，他们和我一样，都觉得外星人拥有 mathematics 或开展 experiments 是可信的。外星人为什么会这样做？当然是因为，在给定某些 ends 的情况下，这比 alternatives 更有效；philosophers 曾努力思考 universe 如何运作，虽然这让我们走了很远（我会说，Einstein 关于在电梯里下落的奇妙 speculations 就非常 philosophical！），但仅靠这些，并不能让我们抵达 standard model。

但考虑到我过去所见的 legal systems，我更倾向于不认同这些其他 institutions 会有多么 radically different。我更倾向于持有这一看法，也是因为，虽然我不是 historical materialist（[ideas](https://www.amazon.com/Enlightened-Economy-Economic-History-1700-1850/dp/0300124554/ref=sr_1_4?qid=1669742985&refinements=p_27%3AJoel+Mokyr&s=books&sr=1-4) 也很重要！），但也许我对 economic history 的阅读，让我更能体会 background material conditions 对 social institutions 的 development 的 impact。现在我们可以问：modern scientific institutions 比博学的 medieval monasteries 更 superior 吗？

相关的 comparison 是 modern university 与 monastery。前者是后者的 intellectual descendent，有些甚至在数千年后仍占用着同样的建筑，Oxbridge 就是如此。它们的 purposes 不同，因此，主张 modern university 比 monastery 更擅长 science，就像说汽车比一袋土豆更适合交通运输。毫无疑问是对的，但显然也是不公平的 comparison！

例如，medieval monastic schools 有定期 prayer，而 universities 没有。这一 practice 并不是为了帮助 advance science，而是为了荣耀 God，modern universities 则没有这样的使命。Monasteries 及其 schools 更侧重 teaching，而非 doing research。我们甚至可以说，research 是其 core activities 的 byproduct。

PhD 这一 concept 可以一直追溯到 guilds 和 master-apprentice relationships。modern PhD 包括一段 comparatively little pay 的时期、由 mentor figure 引导的 intensive learning，以及 completion 一个 project 或一系列 project，以达到 granting of a degree 的要求。

在一些国家，PhD students 会 teach，而在另一些国家则不会；在 academia 之外的一些 professional fields 中，社会要求从业者拥有 PhDs（如 biotech），而在另一些领域则没有这种要求（software engineering），但其 basic institution 非常相似。

进行 Abstracting away 后，当我们问外星人中是否会有 PhD students 时，可以 assume 外星人很可能也会 age，而且出生时没有多少 knowledge。就像我们一样，某个时候有一个聪明、年轻、精力充沛却缺乏 knowledge 的外星人，后来则有了一位 practicing alien scientist。中间发生了什么？嗯，他们肯定在 learning 某些东西。Learning by doing 是做事的一种 particularly effective 方式，而不是仅仅读相关材料（他们的 brains 得有多么不同，才会让这一点不成立？）。那里也极有可能存在 [Tacit](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) knowledge，因此需要 personal transmission of knowledge。到这一步，我们就这样通过 hand waved，得到了某种类似 PhD system 的事物具有 necessity 的 proof。

我们可以想象 students 拿到更多报酬，那将明显偏离 PhD system 现有的运作方式。为什么 academia 不像 software engineering 那样，让 interns 拿到高得多的报酬？这里可以指出 N-Q 文章中 identified 的一个 bottlenecks：_不存在 driving growth in new institutions 的 natural feedback loop_。如果一个人正在为之准备的 research 与 direct application 脱节，就很难像 companies 那样以 self-sustaining 的方式为其付费，这使得 science 依赖 self-funding（如早期的 gentleman scientists 或 endowments）或 charity（public or philanthropic funding），从而难以支付更多报酬。

Entrepreneurship，尤其是其中 Silicon Valley-style venture-backed startups 这一 subset，与 science 有许多共同点。和 science 一样，它存在于一个 continuum 之中，一端是相对成熟的 business models（开第 nth 家餐馆；scaling 最新的 -omics technology），另一端是 speculative businesses（把此前的 [academic](https://github.com/mit-pdos/noria) database 推向世界；inventing expansion microscopy）。和 science 一样，我们可以指出这一 field 的 high level descriptive trends：例如，我们可以说，on average，年龄稍大的 [entrepreneurs](https://sbecouncil.org/2019/05/01/entrepreneurship-and-success-does-age-matter/) 往往更容易 succeed。我们也可以举出 YC 这样看似 successful 的 institutions（startups 中的 HHMI？）；与 meta-science 一样，也能看到这样的争论：其 startups 的 success 应归功于 accelerator 本身，还是说，它们只是在 picking winners，而这些 winners 无论如何都会 successful。

我们还可以看到一些故事，讲 funds 使用 [algorithms](https://9to5google.com/2018/07/19/alphabet-gv-algorithm-the-machine-gv/) 来尝试 predict 应当 invest 哪些 companies，结果几年后又 [abandon](https://www.axios.com/2022/09/28/google-ventures-shelves-its-algorithm) 了它们。science 中还没有 at scale 尝试过这种做法，但我怀疑，如果有人尝试根据 authors 获得的 citations 数量来 fund 他们，也会很快 abandon 那个 algorithm。

然而，与 science 不同，entrepreneurship 有 profit motive 的助力：存在强大的 economic incentives，推动人们 invest 合适的 companies、start 合适的 companies，并找到合适的 hire、manage 和 fire 方式。与 science 不同，它有一个明确的 reward signal 可供 optimize：company 是否在赚钱？

在 meta-science 中，我们有关于 [metascience](https://www.dashunwang.com/book/the-science-of-science) 的书，在 meta-entrepreneurship 中[也](https://www.amazon.com/Unicorns-Shadow-Combating-Dangerous-Investors/dp/B09RCF9JRJ/ref=sr_1_1?crid=3RAK9TDJDBZ75&keywords=mollick+unicorn&qid=1669830009&sprefix=mollick+unicorn%2Caps%2C168&sr=8-1)有书。我们既有 scientists 的传记，也有 founders 的传记。Meta-entrepreneurship 似乎更进一步，为所涉及的所有 sub-skills 都提供了书籍：如何 fundraise、hire、manage。science 似乎没有数量相当的这类书。

尽管如此，对于诸如_你的 company 是否应该有一位 cofounder_这样的问题，仍没有共识。如果从 [statistical](https://twitter.com/emollick/status/1472631335470747651) 角度看 aggregate 中的 data，那么一个人必须有 cofounder 这一[公认的](http://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html) knowledge 似乎并不 true。我时不时会读到一些 [articles](https://flocrivello.com/co-founding-considered-harmful/)，它们来自 [founders](https://mobile.twitter.com/celinehalioua/status/1246904648083992576?lang=bg)，谈论为什么实际上 solo founding 才是该走的路。对此应该怎么想？也许有些人有一位会做得更好，另一些人没有反而会做得更好。

如果我们连这个问题都无法 solve，还能指望对 meta-science 了解多少呢？我认为，这是反对 meta-science 宏大愿景的主要 argument。好的 founders 会找到自己的路，正如好的 scientists 会找到他们自己的路。

> 起初，我觉得，那些只是节省时间的“marginal improvements”不会是多么值得思考的事情。做同样的事，只是更快或更便宜，似乎并不是获得有趣新想法的方式。但如果这些想法主要是花在思考上的时间的 function，那么购买时间就是获得更多想法的方式：scientists 不仅花大量时间 applying for grants，还要 pipetting、cleaning lab equipment，以及将 samples 移入或移出 instruments。花更多时间 reading papers 或 doing analysis，似乎是更好地利用他们时间的方式。我们能否做到比购买更多时间更好的事情，可以通过 surveys 来弄清楚 specific discoveries 是如何产生的，例如考察 Nobel-winning research，或者直接与 scientists 交谈。([Applied positive meta-science](https://nintil.com/metascience-categories/))

Marginal improvements 在 compounded 时其实非常棒。我的意思是，拿那些我们已经知道如何完成、也知道怎样才算做得好的 tasks，让它们变得更容易或更快。Replicating 已有的 work 就是一个例子。此外，正如我在那篇文章中所建议的，任何能够直接为 scientists 购买更多时间的事情也是如此。我们可以 measure time，而且我们知道 science 需要时间：我们可以通过购买更多时间来购买更多 science（这不是明摆着的吗，对吧？）。

我觉得这大有希望！像 scientists 把 GoPros 绑在额头上、分享他们如何工作这样简单的事情，尽管看起来有趣，却可能对 training 非常有用。我会立即 fund 一个旨在这样做的 project！然后，可以看看获准访问这个 recorded library of examples 的 scientists 有什么想法。也许可以 count 在获得访问权限之前/之后，experiments 因为必须有人前来教授一项新 skill 而 delayed 的天数。我认为有用的其他想法包括 documenting labs 里事情是如何进行的（research questions 是如何 decided 的，会议通常怎样进行，reagents 是如何 managed 的？），在被怀疑状况不佳的 areas 中 replicating research（例如 [cancer](https://nintil.com/malignant-review/) research 或 [Alzheimer's](https://nintil.com/categories/alzheimer-s-disease/) research），在合适的地方 starting FROs，或者也许 funding Smolin fellowship。

关键在于，我认为这一切都应该由具有 entrepreneurial mindset 的同一个人或 team 来完成：走出去，发现哪些 problems 需要 solving，然后围绕该 team 能够 solved 的 problems 来开展 research，接着付诸行动。而不是让一些人 produce research，却不据此采取行动。

Metascience 是一个宽广的领域，容得下 pure academics、para-academics、ex-academics，以及在互联网上写博客的人。其中一部分人已经着手 reform 和 start 新的 institutions，另一部分人则以 [quantitatively](https://www.dashunwang.com/book/the-science-of-science) 的方式 studies science 如何 works。我们还可以指出第三个发展较少、funded 也较少的 area：qualitative meta-science（想想 Latour 或 Harry Collins）。这三个 groups 中，第一个不同于另外两个，因为他们的 aims 包括 change science works 的方式。另外两个旨在 understand 它的某些 aspects。在某种 idealized scenario 中，theory 与 practice 之间存在一种 _strongly bidirectional_ 的 connection。实际上，迄今为止，这样的 connection 并不多。从 Empirically 的角度说，我在 institutional engineering 领域观察到的是，通过 1:1 交流传递的 stylized observations 和个人轶事，帮助 shape reforms 和 founding efforts。迄今为止，这些 efforts 并未伴随着对新 efforts 本身进行 study 的强烈意愿。在 Rejuvenome，我曾在 Astera 博客上发表一些博文，介绍 project 所作的 decisions，但直到今天，Arc Institute、Arcadia 或 Convergent Research 都没有 Arc-hivist。就像我们很少看到 economists 或 historians studying 新 startups 如何 work 一样。

meta-entrepreneurship 尽管拥有更强的 improvement incentives，却未能与 entrepreneurship 建立 deep links，这让我对这些 bidirectional linkages 在 metascience 中出现的可能性持悲观态度。因此，我 predict，metascience 和 metascience entrepreneurship 将继续各走各的路：下一次重大的 NIH reform 或新 institution 的创立，不会受到 academic 或 theoretical metascience 的强烈影响。

在“qualitative metascience”中，关于如何 fund science 或 conduct research，还有丰富的 body of work 有待开发。Startups 有 _Getting Things Done_、_High Output Management_ 或 _The Great CEO within_，以及大量关于 fundraising、managing 或 marketing 的配套随笔和文章。Startups 确实会运用这些通过经验艰难获得的 knowledge。science 有什么可以与之相比的东西呢？

我会说，我们确实有这样的例子。我认为，它们可以帮助 scientists、funders 和 policymakers，就像 startup 书籍能够帮助 founders 一样。这些 works 并不是 scientific，但它们是我们能做到的最好的东西。

-   Lee [Smolin](https://www.amazon.com/Trouble-Physics-String-Theory-Science/dp/061891868X) 和 [Hossenfelder's](https://www.amazon.com/Lost-Math-Beauty-Physics-Astray/dp/1541646762/ref=sr_1_3?crid=3KR5IGWWQ46NC&keywords=hossenfelder&qid=1669933730&s=books&sprefix=hossenfelde%2Cstripbooks%2C154&sr=1-3) 对 modern physics research 的抱怨
-   Ramon y Cajal 给 [young](https://www.amazon.com/Advice-Young-Investigator-Bradford-Book/dp/0262681501/ref=sr_1_1?crid=D05YD5JUNOJ1&keywords=cajal+advice&qid=1669933905&s=books&sprefix=cajal+advic%2Cstripbooks%2C125&sr=1-1) investigator 的 Advice

## 原文

## Introduction

Michael Nielsen and Kanjun Qiu (N-Q) recently published a lengthy essay (30k words) on [meta science](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html). Over the past year or two I have been publishing here on [meta-scientific](https://nintil.com/categories/science-funding/) topics as well, totaling around 80k words (at this point these are more books than essays!), going into the minutiae of various foundational papers and talking points. The points I make throughout my work are sometimes rarely found elsewhere in writing though they are often discussed in person in the metascience community (It's generally agreed that the [Azoulay 2011](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/) paper wouldn't change someones mind as I noted eg. [here](https://nintil.com/new-science-nih), but you wouldn't know this was an established point if not for me writing that!).

After all that time reading and thinking about the topic and attending and presenting at meta-science conferences, what conclusion do I come to? What comes next?

N-Q's vision is one where we _develop metascience into an engine driving improvement in the way humanity understands the world_. It's an optimistic vision, proposing _exploring an enormous design space for social processes_, leading to _a far more structurally diverse set of environments for doing science;_ and ultimately enabling _crucial types of work difficult or impossible within existing environments._

There is, however, another optic from which all we have done is interesting but ultimately unhelpful theorizing that will not lead to any meaningful change.

This perspective is embodied by a response N-Q got as feedback and that they gesture at in their essay: "all that matters is to fund good people doing good work"; implying that trying to think deeply about the way science works will lead to minor improvements, making all that effort a waste of time. Just find good scientists and give them money and time, everything else is a footnote to this central dogma of science funding.

I too have heard this view and, I will admit, I have lots of sympathy for it. When thinking whether to write this very essay, I found myself thinking that it's quite possible that a single post I've written on [US immigration](https://nintil.com/us-immigration) will be more helpful than all the time I've spent on metascience, with the exception of my work on _bottleneck analysis_ in general and of aging in particular, as that led to a concrete project, [Rejuvenome](https://www.buckinstitute.org/news/astera-institute-and-buck-institute-announce-70-million-collaboration-to-redefine-the-field-of-research-on-aging/). The time it took to write these other pieces was less than the time spent on reading uncountable books, papers, conference recordings on metascience, trying to squeeze a dry lemon for not, it ends up being, much juice. So that got me thinking: What are the returns to spending time on meta-scientific work. If someone spends 1000 hours in meta-science, is that a good use of their time, compared to some alternative? Broadly, **what should someone with the high level mission of accelerating science do**?

In this particular essay I want to explore this negative case: that the space of possible institutions is not as vast as it might seem, that the benefits we can gain are often, but not always, marginal, and that these being hard to measure makes it difficult to even know how to improve the system or have a general set of prescriptions. However, metascience can learn from ''metaentrepreneurship", the heuristic, case-study based body of knowledge routinely leveraged in daily practice in another domain that has so far proven resistant to useful scientific theorizing, entrepreneurship.

As this essay is written as commentary on the N-Q essay, you should read that first! It will also be useful to read my previous writing on the topic.

Here I discuss some of the examples mentioned in the N-Q essay, using them to make various points about the difficulty of deriving scientifically grounded prescriptions for improving science.

## Example 1: The replication crisis as a metascience success story

The replication crisis is the main example of success that is discussed in the N-Q essay. Are there more metascientific victories like this one awaiting? This example is an existence proof of progress: If one thinks _nothing_ can be done, one can always point to this as credible counterexample.

This particular episode in the history of psychology started with researchers noticing, over many decades, that the statistical methods and publication practices being followed in the field were leading to results that were probably not true. Eventually, a reform movement spearheaded by Brian Nosek and the Open Science Collaboration, built on top of explicit attempts at replication, induced field-wide change in research practices.

It's a good example of meta-scientific work because the work involved not necessarily new, more powerful, statistical methods with interesting properties (that would be _scientific_ work), but rather:

> deep new ideas, such as Registered Reports, as well as the toolbuilding and infrastructure necessary to make them work. It required partnerships with journals and other organizations, so that Registered Reports had a chance to be widely adopted. It required branding and marketing and narrative-crafting, to get widespread adoption and to begin a change in the internalized values of scientists. It required the building of yet more tools and infrastructure to store code and data and materials, to enable easier replication. And, as we'll see, it required institution-building.

Importantly, post-facto _everyone sees this as a success_. No one says "Well, absent the replication crisis, who knows if things would have changed anyway!" That papers fail to replicate was something shown repeatedly and unquestionably.

Could this happen elsewhere? This particular episode was possible because it was possible to drive broad agreement that the methods that were being used were weak and didn't replicate, and it was possible to show this by actually trying to replicate multiple studies, in parallel with lots of pedagogical work and theoretical arguments grounded in statistics.

Suppose, for instance, that no one had tried to run a large scale replication effort. There wouldn't have been a replication crisis. Not much would have changed. What mattered here was an experiment (a large scale replication attempt) that showed a field that _their methods_ were not good. Indeed, as N-Q point out, one can find the same theoretical arguments being made many decades prior (by Meehl and others) but not much changed in the field until the actual experiments were done.

What would this look like in, for example, some areas of physics where experiments are currently unfeasible and some are calling for [post-empirical methods of theory](https://backreaction.blogspot.com/2014/07/post-empirical-science-is-oxymoron.html) confirmation? What does the equivalent of the replication crisis look like for string theory? Is that even possible or just a fancy speculation? Lee Smolin can keep [writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trouble_with_Physics) about string theory being a cult, that won't have, I predict, an effect on the field as strong as the replication crisis had in psychology. This is not to say that string theory is right or wrong or a cultish or openminded field, just that seeking change, sociological or otherwise, in domains untethered to clear experiments is hard.

Currently, the situation there looks like the pre-replication crisis era where for many decades scientists had debated the merits of their methods and approaches (Is [beauty](https://www.amazon.com/Lost-Math-Beauty-Physics-Astray/dp/0465094252) a good guide to truth, in the case of physics) but I doubt these arguments will sway the thoroughly convinced: we need experiments with clear answers.

Other areas of science are, in contrast, ripe for this sort of disruption, areas the janitoring of which will be more useful to the average person, like the life sciences. Here it is harder to replicate studies because the knowledge required to even do the experiment is very specialized: failing a replication may just mean the replicator was not skilled enough. So while there was a recent project to replicate cancer papers, some of this work was met with skepticism. Quoting from an [earlier](https://nintil.com/links-54) Nintil post:

> The Center for Open Science tries to reproduce a bunch of cancer papers, they are not able to [reproduce](https://twitter.com/BrianNosek/status/1468203976428605443) most (59%) of [them](https://www.statnews.com/2021/12/07/cancer-reproducibility-project-research/). This doesn't mean all the effects were not real! It means they were not able to either obtain data/materials to replicate them or that they attempted it and failed. It's unclear of course if this failure to replicate was improper protocols on CoS's side ([tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge)!) or if the original research was actually flawed. That all said though, it looks really bad for scientific practice. The STAT News articles adds more color to this story:
> 
> “Human biology is very hard, and we’re humans doing it. We’re not perfect, and it’s really tricky,” he said. “None of these replications invalidate or validate the original science. Maybe the original study is wrong — a false positive or false signal. The reverse may be true, too, and the replication is wrong. More than likely, they’re both true, and there’s something mundane about how we did the experiment that’s causing the difference.”

This is a good opportunity! Surely we can improve on trying to make biological experiments more reproducible and results more solid. There is a metric for success (Does doing the same thing result in the same outcome?) which is clear and there are experiments one can do in either how scientists are trained, or protocols recorded, and see how that impacts reproducibility.

Hence this is probably the most promising and probably highest reward area of experimental metascience: Studying why studies fail to replicate and improving fields until reproducibility is the norm. From this goal we can derive prescriptions for individual scientists (How to do science), funders (funding replications), and entrepreneurs (Is there any product that needs to be built to help this goal, like lab automation?).

But science has areas that are _not_ like this: if we can't do clean experiments where we measure how different approaches work, and N-Q argued in an earlier [essay](https://scienceplusplus.org/trouble_with_rcts/index.html) that this is the case for funding, then how _decisive results_ (N-Q's term) are to be attained to drive change? . Our optimism about areas of science with simpler metrics of success (replication) should not transfer to reform of scientific institutions in general. N-Q of course admit to the difficulty of advancing metascience for this reason:

> We won't solve the general problem of how to obtain such \[metascientific\] results: that will take thousands of people many decades. One small indicator of the difficulty of the problem is that the decisive results of the replication crisis weren't obtain by an RCT; rather, they were obtained in a bespoke fashion, in response to a particular problem. Still, despite the challenges, we are optimistic that further work will develop approaches strong enough to routinely provide decisive metascientific results, completing the metascience learning loop.

I'd like to hear more about that optimism because I lean the other way!

## Example 2: Empowering young scientists

Scientists are getting older, and cognitive abilities tend to decline later in life. Does the aging of the scientific workforce constitute a problem? In the aggregate, probably, as I concluded [here](https://nintil.com/age-and-science). This result is not one that is backed only by looking at scientific output; it's one grounded in human biology as well. The fact that cognitive abilities decline with age, as a regularity, is one of the [invariants](https://nintil.com/invariants-metascience) we can use when thinking about science: human cognitive ability declines regardless of whether one is a mathematician or a biologist, regardless of whether one works in the XXI century or the XVIII century, regardless of whether one is a wealthy independent scientist or a R01-holding PI. But this has not been enough to drive change. The truth remains that there are older scientists that remain very productive. Moreover, when considering teams of scientists or labs where the old scientist is more of a manager, it becomes even less clear: It may well be that having one mentor-type figure and a number of students works better than if everyone in the room was under 25.

Institutions in theory allow young individuals to have their own labs (thanks to e.g. the NIH Director's Early Independence Award) but there is not much of an impetus or desire to have all of science to be like this. It's altogether unclear if bringing back mandatory retirement for older academics would be a good thing.

Despite this, if someone proposed as an institutional experiment my _Young Researchers Research Institute_,

> But what if we radically empower the young?
> 
> The Young Researchers Research Institute (YRRI) would take in students that have just finished their PhDs and would put them in an environment with other younger scientists to pursue any kind of research they want. Given that it would be a weird career move, it might dissuade some researchers, but if you truly believe in your idea, and your PI doesn't want you to do that, then the will to try might be stronger than the uncertainties involved in joining the YRRI.
> 
> Freshly minted ~NFTs~ PhDs, it could be objected, are not yet ready to lead research of their own. This is nonsense. Sure, maybe some of them are, but there are many historical figures that achieved professorships or published groundbreaking results very early in life (e.g. Pasteur becoming professor for the first time at the age of 26, or Einstein publishing his Annus Mirabilis papers at the same age). We don't need everyone to be ready for independent research by that age for YRRI to work, we just need _some_ scientists that are, and would like to embark on a project of their own design.
> 
> This wouldn't mean that they would be on their own: There would be an advisory board of established scientists to offer coaching and scientific advice to YRRI members; and YRRI wouldn't be focused on one particular topic, you could have under the same roof someone researching [doubly-special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_special_relativity), [RNA computation](https://twitter.com/theHessam/status/1296650944302776321), [gene circuits](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-018-0215-0) or [new simulation algorithms for mechanical systems](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/computationalnonlinear/article-abstract/12/1/011020/473624).

I would be very excited! Perhaps if I fully fleshed out a proposal for something like this it would become clear that this would not work but perhaps it would survive that planning stage. Regardless, having the YRRI as an actually existing institution would be a cleaner test of the hypothesis that a younger scientific workforce across the board would lead to more (interesting? Useful? Novel? Different? Riskier?) science than what we currently have. As a cleaner experiment, it would be easier, though still hard, to draw conclusions from it.

Why should this work? Younger scientists on average know less than older scientists, especially in tacit-knowledge-heavy areas like the life sciences. But they can also work harder and longer hours. Whether they will work on 'more interesting/riskier' research is not a priori given. One could imagine an alternative institute set up, in parallel to YRRI where instead of selecting for age, one selects for 'weird research'. Don [Braben](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/) might have done this: his Venture Research [fellows](https://nintil.com/age-and-science#conclusion) were not particularly young. His younger fellows were not more successful in any obvious sense compared to the older ones.

At that point we are back to the null hypothesis: "fund good people doing good work" regardless of whether they are old or young. But to this end, is it better to have dedicated institutions? For example, in the current system, implicitly one _has_ to be old to start a lab by default unless one makes the cut for a small number of special fellowships and grants every year. If one believes there are many bright students that could be running their own labs and doing great research and that they are currently not able to do so for lack of opportunity, then starting a YRRI would make sense. Whether a given person will start a YRRI will be heavily conditioned, I expect, by their personal knowledge of specific young scientists they want to empower, as opposed as being swayed by abstract arguments about why it may be a good idea.

## Example 3: The Thiel Fellowship

The Thiel Fellowship, while not an example of an attempt to improve science is a good example of what successful institutional entrepreneurship looks like, and is discussed as such in the N-Q essay.

When they launched back in 2011, the Fellowship did something quite unique. Built on the strong conviction that certain young individuals could be doing something better with their lives by dropping out and starting a company (or writing a book, or doing research), the Thiel Foundation committed to give $100k to each Fellow if they dropped out. They were mocked as a "[misdirected piece of philantrophy](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/10/thiel-fellows-program-is-most-misdirected-piece-of-philanthropy-says-larry-summers/)" and made fun of; it was pointed out that some Fellows ended up pursuing [uninspiring](https://www.quora.com/What-were-the-results-of-the-first-Thiel-Fellowship-Class/answers/1293281) goals and that instead of flying cars the Fellows got us caffeine sprays (no [joke](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/20/sprayable-energy/)). Some even went back to school. But the Fellowship also boosted and enabled the careers of people like Laura Deming, Vitalik Buterin, Dylan Field, Boyan Slat, or Austin Russell. 11 years of the Fellowship have costed perhaps 30M$ which by venture standards is not a large amount, especially when compared to what the Fellows as a whole have accomplished. What can we learn from this? Suppose Thiel had decided instead to donate that money to Harvard. Without running an RCT, my hunch is that it wouldn't have had the same impact.

And indeed Peter Thiel (And the early Fellowship team, people like Danielle [Strachman](https://twitter.com/dstrachman) and Michael [Gibson](https://twitter.com/William_Blake?ref_src=twsrc%5Egoogle%7Ctwcamp%5Eserp%7Ctwgr%5Eauthor)) did not run RCTs before starting this, nor they probably conducted preliminary scientific research of any sort to design the Fellowship. They noticed a market opportunity: Talent locked in in university that could be unlocked with a small amount of money and a network. Having noticed that opportunity, they designed a program to fix that, and arguably they succeeded.

This I think should be the general playbook for metascientific work; it should rather be institutional entrepreneurship where a concrete issue is noticed and then a fix is proposed and instantiated through the right channels (sometimes policy, sometimes new institutions or reform of existing ones). Importantly, this fix is, as with entrepreneurship, very context dependent, and _not_ amenable to scientific (intersubjective, general, crisp, testable) theorizing.

Are there other Thiel Fellowships one could build?

Lee Smolin, for example, argues that only a small number of theoretical physicists are really pushing the field forward, which arguably is a bit of a hot take. Hot takes is what entrepreneurship is built on, so it's a promising start. He suggests that these few remarkable scientists should get funded to do their research. This would be very cheap! And the results could be transformational.

> “But when it comes to theoretical physics, we are not talking about much money at all. Suppose that an agency or foundation decided to fully support **all the visionaries** who ignore the mainstream and follow their own ambitious programs to solve the problems of quantum gravity and quantum theory. **We are talking about perhaps** **two dozen theorists**. Supporting them fully would take a tiny fraction of any large nation’s budget for physics.”

I don't know how I would choose these two dozen theorists. I wouldn't be able to start this institution in particular; I lack the required knowledge and a strong enough belief in Smolin's hot take to actually go ahead and do it. And it's harder to argue why _this_ particular initiative would be the best use of a funder's money, but it seems to me that if we did as Smolin says, we would see more interesting physics results than otherwise, at a small cost to a wealthy philanthropist that's interested in physics in particular. Definitely cheaper than building larger [colliders](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-world-doesnt-need-a-new-gigantic-particle-collider/)!

## Example 4: Diversifying funding

Not present in the N-Q essay but discussed by N-Q at various in-person events is a specific proposal from NIH to cap funding per lab using something called the Grant Support Index, which I discussed [here](https://www.worksinprogress.co/issue/we-dont-know-how-to-fix-science/) and [here](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/). Back in 2017 some research came out pointing to the fact that by some metrics based on citation counts, labs become less efficient as they are bigger. That implies that instead of having a lab with 27 grants, having 9 smaller labs with 3 grants each, which would cost the same, might be a better use of the money.

Should this be done? This is less clear! As I showed in my own post, the conclusion that large labs are less productive depends on how you measure productivity. There are reasons why we may want large labs: if a PI is a good manager and is good at picking and managing good students, then why not let them have their large lab? Once again we are back to picking case-by-case. This is sadly what tends to happen with metascience work: the actionable insights get lots in a sea of on the one hand and on the other hand.

The N-Q essay has a unified conclusion section where they show some tentative optimism about developing metascience into something that can be an 'engine driving improvement in the way humanity understands the world'. If there's one recommendation that recurs throughout the essay is a call for more structural diversity because:

> Such structural diversity is a core, precious resource for science, a resource enlarging the range of problems humanity can successfully attack. The reason is that different ambient environments enable different kinds of work. Problems easily soluble in one environment may be near insoluble in another; and vice versa. Indeed, often we don't _a priori_ know what environment would best enable an attack on an important problem. Thus it's important to ensure many very different environments are available, and to enable scientists to liquidly move between them.

However, someone wanting to reform science in some way could read this and still think that it's better to double down on what works, as opposed to more diverse initiatives that might fail. One counter to this reasoning made in their essay is diminishing marginal returns:

> One way to think about it is this: if you donate $10 million to Harvard or a similar incumbent, then a reasonable rough model is that that money will go to _the best thing Harvard didn't already fund_. But Harvard has an enormous budget. And so it will fund something far down Harvard's priority list: something Harvard thought wasn't good enough to fund with its first several billion dollars in spending. By contrast, startup ventures striking out in new directions are doing the best they can in those directions. Diminishing marginal returns haven't yet set in. Structural diversity may be chaotic and confusing, but it also offers a chance to escape the tyranny of diminishing marginal returns, and to enable new kinds of creative work. \[...\] Perhaps, if outlier successes are what matter, then it's sufficient to have many small but very different cultures. In this view, scaling may not matter so much. Indeed, maybe scale is even _undesirable_.

But how big should each institution be?

> The fundamental question is: what sets the scale for any such program? Is it fashion and fickle perception and politics and **highly legible stories**? Or is it the scientific contribution, and the value of the program as a part of the wider ecosystem? At present, it is almost entirely the former, and only incidentally the latter. Of course, fashion and fickle perception and politics often do not present as such. They present as enthusiastic articles by well-meaning people in _Nature_ and _Science_ and _The New York Times_. They present as enthusiastic and brilliant founders of new institutions, with their pet ideas about _how things could be better_. This is excellent for generating new social processes, but a terrible basis for evaluation. The net result is a natural monoculture, an oligarchy in which there's either far too much or not nearly enough of something. This is a disaster for science.

I worry that making this distinction may not matter much in practice. If one takes, say, the newly launched and well funded Arc Institute, it exists in its current form not because it has shown to be successful, but rather because of the existence of **legible stories** of success of institutions that preceded it like the Broad Institute or HHMI Janelia, which helped shape its design. We can say that there are reasons to believe it will be successful (if it attracts the right talent), and that this justifies the funding it started with. But after that, evaluating the performance of the institution becomes less clear, for reasons that Nielsen and Qiu enumerate throughout a [previous](https://scienceplusplus.org/trouble_with_rcts/index.html) essay.

Just like they (N-Q) had to leverage highly legible stories of struggle and success in science (Kariko's, Church's) to get their points across, so will do future evaluators of Arc's performance do with discoveries awarded highly legible awards like the Nobel Prize. It's hard for it to be otherwise: science has intersubjectivity at its core and if I cannot show to you in a convincing way why my institution or researcher of choice is a good funding target then we're just arguing vibes: As much as the day to day of science, and argueably the practice of entrepreneurial metascience, requires working in illegible gray areas, the practice of metascience qua science requires at least some legibility of the resulting metascientific body of work.

In contrast to the first paragraph I extracted (pro-diversity of approaches), elsewhere in the essay they seem more in favor of flexibility:

> However, in the science policy world interventions are often focused on what is practical within existing power structures. We shall be concerned with more _a priori_ questions of principle, and enabling decentralized change, i.e., change that may occur outside existing power structures. For all these reasons we think of the essay as simply part of metascience. \[...\]
> 
> What we want is **a flourishing ecosystem of people with wildly imaginative and insightful ideas for new social processes; and for those ideas to be tested and the best ideas scaled out**.

Perhaps in their mind diversity and flexibility are closer than in mine. To me arguably what matters more is flexibility: There is only one major search engine (Google) which works well enough compared to the alternatives, but anyone is free to challenge them. One can simultaneously think the system doesn't need that many more experiments in funding structures and that more people should be empowered to make decisions about that. If they happen to decide the same, that's also a valid outcome.

If one takes the Soviet Union, in [some](https://nintil.com/the-crumbling-public-private-distinction) [old posts](https://nintil.com/the-privatepublic-distinction-a-reply-to-stringham-and-powell/) I argued the problem with the USSR and in general central planning is not that chiefly private property is abolished; you could imagine someone owning the country outright and they would still have the same planning problems. The problem is that there is no fine-grained and fluid control over resources to let better decision makers make decisions about them (why markets are good). Network effects, as I discuss in those posts, can complicate this basic story, but at first the theory seems right: if we add a few more decision makers to science and they decide that they should fund the NIH instead of a _Institute for Traveling Scientists_ (a proposal in the original text) I don't think this is an obviously wrong choice.

This all said, on net I favor, relative to what we have today, having more independent sources of control of funds and resources in sciences. Maybe in the future we'll have a monoculture that's optimal, but I am not convinced the NIH's is that.

### CRISPR vs a Theory of Everything

Perhaps a way to understand where N-Q are coming from and where I'm coming from is this:

Picture a massive warehouse full of slot machines. Each slot machine is a field being funded by a specific mechanism and this warehouse is science as a whole. Each stops working after a while. Each pull of the lever in each slot machine gives you a lognormally distributed payoff. The mean of any given machine that is made anew is drawn from a half-Cauchy distribution (which has an undefined mean and sometimes have very large outliers due to fat tails).

You can choose between copying an existing machine or randomly spawning one into place. What do you choose? This is the same as the choice between doubling down on a given funding mechanism or starting a new one. For different choices of parameters in the toy example above the right choice is one or the other.

In a world where there most of science is ahead of us, it makes sense to focus on exploring whereas in a world where most useful fields are already established, it makes sense to exploit those instead. I'm more of this latter opinion whereas N-Q might argue not necessarily that science is truly an endless frontier, but at the very least that we can't know that and thus we should err on the side of maximizing discovery.

Forcefully: Do you rather want a better CRISPR or the next quantum mechanics? This is probably the crux question. I say obviously give me the next CRISPR. At the same time, it's easy for me to imagine someone with different priorities and aesthetics that will say may I have the next QM please. Here's a quote from Michael showing his aesthetic preference for field-building, as opposed as pushing forth an already "hot" field:

> **DEVON**: When is competition productive and when is it more toxic?
> 
> **MICHAEL**: I think I deny the premise of the question. Toxic, well okay. All right. Let me lean into the question, let me accept the premise of the question.
> 
> You can probably all guess — but maybe people listening to this can't — my opinion.\[...\]
> 
> If there's a thousand people all competing to do the same thing, actually a lot of them could go and do other things. They could invent other games to play, some of which would be unique games. Games that only they in the world were playing, and it would both be more meaningful for them, it'd be more meaningful for their family and for their friends, and just better for the world if they were to go and do those other things. \[...\]
> 
> He \[Hamming\] was a more competitive kind of a person. He wanted to win quite often. Even his framing of asking, "What are the most important problems in your field and why are you not working on them?" That seems to me like kind of a silly framing. It's accepting the consensus social reality of what the field is, when in fact it is much better to figure out what are the problems that nobody in your field has understood are important yet. **Sort of trying to invent new problems, and maybe even new fields. That strikes me as a more enjoyable, and ultimately, more meaningful activity**. (Nielsen, in Experts gather to talk about the impact of [Richard Hamming](https://www.notion.so/blog/a-roundtable-on-richard-hamming))

Whereas to the contrary, I find scientific competition extremely valuable. We wouldn't have gotten as fast as we did to gene therapies without friendly (and sometimes not so friendly) competition between the relevant labs. Had they chosen to go do something else that no one was doing we might understand more, but be able to do less. I say might because I can't prove to you that doubling down on a field, as opposed to starting a new one is better. Just that the latter is not a priori better than the former in all cases.

Here's a concrete funding decision one could make. At a cost of $100k per year for two years, would you rather

-   Fund a promising student to work at Arc Institute on the next step of Hsu's lab work on [serine](https://twitter.com/pdhsu/status/1457414320300572675) recombinases (who wouldn't otherwise get to work on that)
-   Pick an unfunded grant to a small lab that received highly variable feedback from reviewers, and fund it.

The pro-diversity heuristic would encourage us to fund the latter. Arc Institute is already well funded, an additional student will do one unit of "Arc Institute work" whereas the second option will do something unusual. How much weight to put on this guiding principle vs looking at this particular decision case-by-base. From an entrepreneurial point of view, there is no making general plans, there is only acting here and now with the means and circumstances available to the entrepreneur.

## Restaurants as a platonic form, or whether aliens would have PhDs

![image-20221201154634555](https://nintil.com/images/2022-11-21-metascience-limits/image-20221201154634555.png)

Source: https://lexica.art/prompt/632bb078-94d8-42f4-9567-a8e05469626b

At the beginning of their essay, N-Q ask whether aliens would also have a NSF or a Harvard. Surely, one may think, they will agree that they should experiment and share results, but would their institutions look like ours? This seems preposterous to them but not so to me! The space of possibilities for institutional design seems more constrained to me than it does to them. In turn, this reduces the value of experimenting with new institutional forms as opposed to doubling down on what we know works, on the margin.

One analogy is this: Most restaurants are very similar. They have kitchens, they have seating, they have processes for procuring groceries and disposing of leftovers and garbage. They hand out checks and they have menus. Some might follow [Escoffier's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigade_de_cuisine) brigade system to structure their kitchen teams, others do not. Some serve gourmet food, others fast food.

The convergence on this concept 'the restaurant' can be explained by underlying facts about human physiology and customs (we need to eat, eating with friends and family, taste for variety), and economics (tradeoff between money and time, economies of cooking scale). Differences in restaurant style can be explained by more context dependent factors (different preferences and purchasing power to be catered imply the existence of McDonalds and the French Laundry) If aliens are in some relevant ways like us, we can predict they too will have restaurants.

The reason for this convergence is not that there is a single restaurant chain and they happen to do things in the same way, squelching experimentation; there are millions of owners of restaurant throughout the entire world. The reason is that restaurants are what best fits our given context. The platonic form of the restaurant emerges out of physics and context just like calcium-potassium-sodium [neurons](https://www.quantamagazine.org/comb-jelly-neurons-spark-evolution-debate-20150325/) do over and over in evolution.

In parallel with this convergence, there have been innovations: food delivery, cloud kitchens, more [variance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0qNWzpOc69U) in menu length , or Japanese-style [payment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iXJgt6etKz4) where there is no dance of waiters carrying cards and bills back and forth, there is a single interaction at the exit. This latter has not yet taken over western restaurants, suggesting potential for improvement of the core concept.

Some restaurants are strikingly different: [maid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maid_caf%C3%A9) cafes, [hot pot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_pot) restaurants where you, rather than a chef, cook your own food, restaurants where one [eats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_dining) in the dark, or [restaurants](https://www.fritzskc.com/) where food is ordered by phone and it arrives in a mini train. I just came up with a [new restaurant idea](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1598418195903565824) as I was writing this paragraph. Fun, but don't expect it to take over anytime soon.

If one were to open a restaurant, what kind of restaurant should one open? Should you open a spot that does for burgers what [Tartine](https://blog.ciachef.edu/elisabeth-prueitt-chad-robertson-bio/) did for bread? Or should you start a quirky restaurant where only foods that start with 'B' like bread, beans, butter, or bananas are used in cooking?

This is part of my reaction when reading the N-Q essay: Monoculture does not necessarily equal stagnation, there can be really good reasons for convergence on a given pattern (as with restaurants) and science is more like restaurants than N-Q suggest.

The second part of my reaction is agreeing with the fact that we are still far from science being fully figured out and that currently there are reasonable changes one could effect to improve it. The reason for this is, to some extent, that decision making in science is indeed very centralized so there's less experimentation than what one would find in a market. And unlike markets, there is no obvious transmission chain that connects institutional performance to norm dissemination. It would indeed be useful if successful institutions could be scaled, but how do we know, scientifically, what a successful scientific institution is?

Social institutions are not directly dictated by the laws of physics. [The Soviet Union,](https://nintil.com/the-soviet-union-series/) for a while, experimented with [various](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy) degrees of [central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet-type_economic_planning) planning. While, as I [argued](https://nintil.com/the-soviet-series-from-farm-to-factory-stalins-industrial-revolution/) , they underperformed a counterfactual market economy, the continued existence of the USSR didn't "defy" economics for the same reason a market economy is not implied by the body of work of "positive" economics. What _political economy_ can tell us is that given some goals (like human welfare broadly construed) and some background facts about the way human beings behave, some systems will be more likely to achieve those goals than others.

Studying legal systems could be a fruitful activity for metascientists: like science, law is a complex social phenomenon that exists in every modern society. Unlike science, law has been present almost since the beginning of human history. Every society had to solve similar problems, learning to deal with power and foster coordination, and had the chance to develop different solutions for them.

Today, there are [two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_legal_systems#/media/File:Map_of_the_Legal_systems_of_the_world_\(en\).png) main system that are widespread, the civil and common law systems. Each system addresses the same problems but they do so in slightly different ways (one heavily involves juries, the other does not). Which is better? Is one (or both) unfair? Are there better legal systems we could have that are not just tweaks on the existing ones but radically different instead?

The legal systems we observe today are the result of centuries of evolution but are also entangled with the particular political histories of the countries where they exist. It's not unreasonable to assume that the systems we have are good enough but also probable capable of improvement.

An exercise one could do is try to derive a legal system from first principles and see how that compares with what we have now. For example, given what we know about human beings, having someone being a judge in a case where they are involved seems like a bad idea because they'll be biased. Because of this, every society has moved towards a principle of third-party adjudication of justice. The exceptions to this have to do with societies ruled by a single individual (like medieval kings) that were exempted from their own laws (though note the exception applied to one person, and most justice was still third-party mediated).

In the book _The Structure of Liberty_, Randy Barnett tries to do exactly that: deriving an ideal legal system from high level considerations, which (spoilers) ends up being similar to what we have now. This idealized system he calls "natural law". Though I read his book many years ago, the [analogy](https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/1235/) he uses to introduce the concept directly influences the way I think about institutions in general and science in particular:

> When we think of the disciplines of engineering or architecture, the idea of a natural law is not so mysterious. For example, engineers reason that, given the force that gravity exerts on a building, ifwe want a building that will enable persons to live or work inside it, then we need to provide a foundation, walls, and roof of a certain strength. The physical law of gravity leads to the following "natural law" injunction for human action: given that gravity will cause us to fall rapidly, if we want to live and be happy, then we had better not jump off tall buildings. The principles of engineering, though formulated by human beings, are not a product of their will. These principles must come to grips with the nature of human beings and the world in which human beings live, and they operate whether or not they are recognized or enforced by any government. And though they are never perfectly precise and always subject to incremental improvements and sometimes even breakthroughs, they are far from arbitrary, and we violate them at our peril.

Besides Barnett I have also read in the past examinations of very different legal systems; some examples here being [Rules](https://nintil.com/comments-on-rules-for-a-flat-world-gillian-hadfield/) for a Flat World, the [Invisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invisible_Hook) Hook (on pirate institutions) or most comprehensively, Legal [Systems](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Legal%20Systems/LegalSystemsContents.htm) Very Different from Ours.

One lesson from that is that the ideal legal system may sometimes depend on context. Rules for a Flat World describes a case where a developing country tried to copy the legal system from the US. That didn't go well, it was too complex for what their society could support. Likewise customary justice administered by village elders, without lawyers, continues to exist because some societies can't economically justify dedicated lawyers or judges.

But if a society is sufficiently like ours, Barnett would say, then they would end up having a system that is like ours.

What are the implications for metascience? Legal systems have tended to converge over time, with diversity of approaches going down, without the need for randomized control trials while dealing with complex social phenomena, though with plenty of learning by doing as legal systems get stress-tested case after case. By analogy, I'd argue, we can reason similarly about metascience and see what science could look like given the objective of producing useful and/or interesting knowledge. How much do those goals, and the way human beings are, constrain scientific institutions?

## Would aliens have PhDs?

Or, if we could re-run human history to the current year, would the institution of the PhD re-emerge as we know it today?

Probably.

I asked Twitter [this](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1596298839303913473) question, inspired by a line in the N-Q essay where they wonder:

> Imagine you're a science fiction author writing a story depicting a scientific discovery made by an alien species. In your story you show the alien scientists up close – how they work, how they live. Would you show them working within institutions resembling human universities, with **the PhD system**, grant agencies, academic journals, and so on? Would they use social processes like peer review and citation? Would the alien scientists have interminable arguments, as human scientists do, about the merits of the h-index and impact factor and similar attempts to measure science?
> 
> Almost certainly, the design of our human scientific social processes has been too contingent on the accidents of history for the answers to those questions to all be "yes". It seems unlikely that humanity has found the best possible means of allocating scarce scientific resources! We doubt even the most fervent proponents of, say, Harvard or the US National Science Foundation would regard them as a platonic ideal. Nor does it seem likely the h-index and similar measures are universal measures of scientific merit.
> 
> This doesn't mean the aliens wouldn't have many scientific facts and methodological ideas in common with humanity – plausibly, for instance, the use of mathematics to describe the universe, or the central role of experiment in improving our understanding. **But it also seems likely such aliens will have radically different social processes to support science**. What would those social processes be? Could they have developed scientific institutions as superior to ours as modern universities are to the learned medieval monasteries?

To me, this does not seem likely! Note that to them, like to me, it seems plausible that they would have mathematics or they would do experiments. Why would aliens do this? Of course, because given certain ends, this works better than the alternatives; philosophers tried hard to think about how the universe works and while that got us far (I'd argue Einstein's fanciful speculations about falling in elevator are very philosophical!), it wouldn't have gotten us to the standard model by themselves.

But on the light of what I've seen for legal systems in the past, I am more inclined to disagree with how radically different these other institutions might be. I am also more inclined to hold this opinion because, while not a historical materialist ([ideas](https://www.amazon.com/Enlightened-Economy-Economic-History-1700-1850/dp/0300124554/ref=sr_1_4?qid=1669742985&refinements=p_27%3AJoel+Mokyr&s=books&sr=1-4) matter too!), perhaps my reading of economic history makes me appreciate the impact of background material conditions in the development of social institutions. Now we can ask: Are modern scientific institutions superior to learned medieval monasteries?

The relevant comparison is the modern university to the monastery. The former is an intellectual descendent of the latter, in some cases occupying the same buildings millennia after as with Oxbridge. The purposes were different, so arguing that the modern university is better at science than a monastery is like saying that a car is better for transportation than a sack of potatoes. Unequivocally true, but also a clearly unfair comparison!

For example, medieval monastic schools had regular prayer and universities don't. This practice wasn't there to help advance science, it was there to glorify God, which modern universities don't have the mandate to do. Monasteries and their schools were more focused on teaching than on doing research. We could even say that research was a byproduct of their core activities.

The concept of the PhD is one that we could trace back all the way to guilds and master-apprentice relationships. The modern PhD involves a period of comparatively little pay, intensive learning guided by a mentor figure, and the completion of a project or series thereof that merit the granting of a degree.

In some countries PhD students teach and in others they do not; in some professional fields outside of academia PhDs are socially required (As in biotech) and in others they are not (software engineering), but the basic institution is very similar.

Abstracting away, when asking whether there would be PhD students among the aliens, we can assume that aliens very likely age and are born without much knowledge. Just like us at some point there is a smart young alien full of energy and devoid of knowledge and at a later point there is a practicing alien scientist. What happens in between? Well surely they are learning something. Learning by doing is a particularly effective way of doing something, as opposed to only reading about it (how different would their brains have to be for this not to be true?). [Tacit](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) knowledge would most likely also be present there, leading to the need for personal transmission of knowledge. At that point we have hand waved our way to a proof of the necessity of something like the PhD system.

We could imagine students being paid more, that would be a marked departure from the way the PhD system works. Why is academia not like software engineering, where interns are paid significantly more? Here one could point to one of the bottlenecks identified in the N-Q essay: there's _no natural feedback loop driving growth in new institutions_. If the research one is preparing for is disconnected from direct application, then it's hard to pay for in a self-sustaining way as companies are able to, which makes science reliant on self-funding (as in the early gentleman scientists or endowments) or charity (public or philanthropic funding), leading to difficulties paying more.

Entrepreneurship, especially the subset of it that is Silicon Valley-style venture-backed startups, has a lot in common with science. As with science, it exists in a continuum between relatively well-established business models (opening the nth restaurant; scaling the latest -omics technology) and speculative businesses (launching a hitherto [academic](https://github.com/mit-pdos/noria) database into the world; inventing expansion microscopy). As with science, one can point to high level descriptive trends about the field: One can say, for example, that on average [entrepreneurs](https://sbecouncil.org/2019/05/01/entrepreneurship-and-success-does-age-matter/) that are a bit older tend to succeed more often. One can also point to seemingly successful institutions like YC (The HHMI of startups?) and as with meta-science one can find debates on whether the accelerator itself is to be credited for the success of its startups or whether on the other hand they are just picking winners that would be successful regardless.

One can also find stories of funds using [algorithms](https://9to5google.com/2018/07/19/alphabet-gv-algorithm-the-machine-gv/) to try to predict what companies they should invest in, and finding that a few years after they [abandon](https://www.axios.com/2022/09/28/google-ventures-shelves-its-algorithm) them. This hasn't been tried at scale in science, but I suspect if someone tried to fund authors based on how many citations they get, they would abandon that algorithm soon enough.

Unlike science, however, entrepreneurship has the profit motive going for it: there are strong economic incentives to invest in the right companies, start the right companies, find the right ways to hire, manage, and fire. Unlike science there is a clear reward signal to optimize for: is the company making money?

In meta-science, we have [metascience](https://www.dashunwang.com/book/the-science-of-science) books, and we have books [too](https://www.amazon.com/Unicorns-Shadow-Combating-Dangerous-Investors/dp/B09RCF9JRJ/ref=sr_1_1?crid=3RAK9TDJDBZ75&keywords=mollick+unicorn&qid=1669830009&sprefix=mollick+unicorn%2Caps%2C168&sr=8-1) in meta-entrepreneurship. We have biographies of both scientists and founders. Meta-entrepreneurship seems more advanced in having books for all the sub-skills involved: how to fundraise, hire, manage. There does not seem to be a similar number of those books for science.

Despite this, there is no agreement on questions like _should you have a cofounder for your company_. If one looks at the data in the aggregate through a [statistical](https://twitter.com/emollick/status/1472631335470747651) sense, the [received](http://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html) knowledge that one has to have a cofounder doesn't seem true. From time to time I read [articles](https://flocrivello.com/co-founding-considered-harmful/) from [founders](https://mobile.twitter.com/celinehalioua/status/1246904648083992576?lang=bg) on why actually solo founding is the way. What should one think here? Maybe some people would do better with one and others without.

If we can't solve this can we hope to learn much about meta-science? This is, I think, the main argument against an expansive vision of meta-science. Good founders find their way just as good scientists find theirs.

> At first, I thought that "marginal improvements" that just save time wouldn't be that interesting to think about. Doing the same thing but faster or cheaper doesn't seem like how one gets to interesting new thoughts. But if those thoughts are a function of primarily time spent thinking, then buying time is how one gets more of thoughts: scientists spend a lot of time not only applying for grants, but also pipetting, cleaning lab equipment, and moving samples in our out of instruments. Spending more time reading papers, or doing analysis seems like a better use of their time. Whether we can do better than buying more time is something that we could figure out by surveys of how specific discoveries came to be, for example taking Nobel-winning research, or by talking to scientists directly. ([Applied positive meta-science](https://nintil.com/metascience-categories/))

Marginal improvements are actually great when compounded. By this I mean taking tasks that we already know how to do, and for which we know what good work looks like, and making that easier or faster. Replicating existing work is one example. But also, as I suggest in that post, anything that will directly buy more time for scientists. We can measure time, and we know science requires time: we can buy more science by buying more time (duh, right?).

I think there is a lot of promise to this! Something as simple as scientists strapping GoPros to their foreheads and sharing how they work, amusing as it may seem, could be very useful for training purposes. I would fund a project aiming to do this immediately! Then one could look at what scientists given access to the recorded library of examples think. One could maybe count the number of days that experiments used to be delayed because someone had to come teach a new skill before/after having access to this. Other ideas I find useful include documenting how stuff happens at labs (How do research questions get decided, how do meetings tend to go, how are reagents managed?), replicating research in areas that are suspected of being in bad shape (like [cancer](https://nintil.com/malignant-review/) research or [Alzheimer's](https://nintil.com/categories/alzheimer-s-disease/) research), starting FROs where they make sense, or maybe funding the Smolin fellowship.

Crucially, I think this should all be done by the same person or team with an entrepreneurial mindset: going out and finding what problems need solving, then doing the research scoped to what problems can be solved by that team, and then doing that. As opposed to there being people that produce research but do not act on it.

Metascience is a broad tent that fits pure academics, para-academics, ex-academics, and people with a blog on the internet. A subset of this group has gone to reform and start new institutions while another subset studies [quantitatively](https://www.dashunwang.com/book/the-science-of-science) how science works. We could also point to a third, lesser developed and funded area of qualitative meta-science (Think Latour or Harry Collins). The first of these three groups is different from the second two in that they include among their aims to change the way science works. The second two aim to understand aspects of it. In some idealized scenario there is a _strongly bidirectional_ connection between theory and practice. In practice there hasn't been much of one so far. Empirically what I have observed in the space of institutional engineering is stylized observations and personal anecdotes conveyed 1:1 helping shape reforms and founding efforts. These so far have not come coupled with a strong will to study the new efforts themselves. At Rejuvenome I published some blogposts in the Astera blog about decisions the project made, but to this date there is no Arc-hivist at Arc Institute, Arcadia, or Convergent Research. Just like one rarely see economists or historians studying how new startups work.

The failure of meta-entrepreneurship to establish deep links with entrepreneurship, given stronger incentives for improvement, makes me be pessimistic about the possibilities of these bidirectional linkages from manifesting in metascience. Hence I predict metascience and metascience entrepreneurship will continue walking separate paths: The next big NIH reform or new institution started will not be strongly influenced by academic or theoretical metascience.

In 'qualitative metascience' there is a rich body of work to be developed about how to fund science or conduct research. Startups have _Getting Things Done_ or _High Output Management_ or _The Great CEO within_ and a plethora of supporting essays and articles about fundraising, managing, or marketing. Startups do make use of this hard-earned knowledge through experience. What does science have that's comparable?

I'd say we have examples of this. These, I think, can be helpful for scientists, funders, and policymakers in the same way the startup books are for founders. These works are not scientific, but they are the best we can do.

-   Warren Weaver's [handbook](https://twitter.com/abiylfoyp/status/1597973119540817921) for Rockefeller foundation officials
-   Lee [Smolin](https://www.amazon.com/Trouble-Physics-String-Theory-Science/dp/061891868X)'s and [Hossenfelder's](https://www.amazon.com/Lost-Math-Beauty-Physics-Astray/dp/1541646762/ref=sr_1_3?crid=3KR5IGWWQ46NC&keywords=hossenfelder&qid=1669933730&s=books&sprefix=hossenfelde%2Cstripbooks%2C154&sr=1-3) complaints about modern physics research
-   Ramon y Cajal's Advice for a [young](https://www.amazon.com/Advice-Young-Investigator-Bradford-Book/dp/0262681501/ref=sr_1_1?crid=D05YD5JUNOJ1&keywords=cajal+advice&qid=1669933905&s=books&sprefix=cajal+advic%2Cstripbooks%2C125&sr=1-1) investigator
-   The [FRO](https://www.dayoneproject.org/ideas/focused-research-organizations-to-accelerate-science-technology-and-medicine) whitepaper
-   Polya's [How to Solve It](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691164076/how-to-solve-it)
-   Root-Bernstein's [Discovering](https://www.amazon.com/Discovering-Robert-Root-Bernstein/dp/0674211766/ref=sr_1_1?crid=1ZTMGARA8HJ7K&keywords=discovering+bernstein&qid=1669935098&s=books&sprefix=discovering+bernstein%2Cstripbooks%2C204&sr=1-1&ufe=app_do%3Aamzn1.fos.006c50ae-5d4c-4777-9bc0-4513d670b6bc)
-   Ben Reinhardt's DARPA and [PARPA](https://benjaminreinhardt.com/parpa) reports
-   [Bottleneck](https://nintil.com/bottlenecks-workshop) analysis
-   And [many others](https://www.reddit.com/r/Professors/comments/hovi9o/book_recommendations_for_new_faculty_that_lacked/), on how to become a professor in the current system.

It is the production of this sort of work where (theoretical) metascience can add the most value to (applied) metascience entrepreneurs.
