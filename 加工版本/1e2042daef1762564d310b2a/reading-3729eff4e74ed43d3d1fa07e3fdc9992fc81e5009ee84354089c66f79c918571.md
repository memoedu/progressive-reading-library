# Energy、Pacing 与 Human Endurance 的 Limits

---

## 正文

Parkinson’s Law 指出：

> “Work 会 expands，从而 fill 可用于其 completion 的 time。”

这个“law”是英国 naval historian [C. Northcote Parkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Northcote_Parkinson) 在 1955 年为 _The Economist_ 撰写的一篇讽刺文章中提出的。如果按字面理解，它显然是 false。仅仅 setting 一个 deadline，并不能让一个 goal 在任意 timeframe 内变得 achievable。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw1.jpg)

大量 academic literature 提出了相反的论点。[planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy) describes 了一个有充分记录的 tendency：complex projects 会出现 cost overruns 和 delays。事实上，真相也许更接近 Hofstadter’s Law；作者 [Douglas Hofstadter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Hofstadter) 曾半开玩笑地说：“所需的 time 总比你预期的更长，即使你已经把 Hofstadter’s Law 考虑在内。”

尽管其 empirical status 明确表明它并不像一条 law，Parkinson’s Law 确实 capture 了一条关于人的重要真相：当我们 loosen time constraints 时，做某件事所需的 time 不知怎么就会 expands，fill 至少一部分空隙。

## Energy、Pacing 与 Human Endurance 的 Limits

我在阅读 [Endure](https://www.amazon.ca/Endure-Curiously-Elastic-Limits-Performance/dp/006249998X) 时，想到了 Parkinson’s Law。这是 Alex Hutchinson 的一本佳作，examining 了 human persistence 的 limits，以及围绕这些 limits 的确切 nature 的一些 scientific controversy。

Hutchinson 自己也是一位出色的 endurance athlete。他讲述了自己参加 track and field 期间，试图在 1500m race 中 beat 自己的 personal best time 的经历。他指出，尽管自己已经付出了能调动的每一分 effort，他的 pace 总会在最后时刻 speed up。他甚至试图“骗”自己 running flat out，却始终发现自己在 races 中段出现同样的 speed 下滑。

如果我们 assume，endurance 主要是被身体的 internal resources——muscle glycogen、oxygen uptake、ATP 等等——所 limited，这就说不通了。如果我们真的已经竭尽全力，那么当 race 进入 final stretch 时，我们怎么还可能 speed up？

不过，有这种特殊表现的并不只有 Hutchinson。事实证明，world-record runs 也呈现同样的 pattern：pace 先下滑，随后在接近 race 结束时出现 slight acceleration。即便是最 well-trained、disciplined 和 motivated 的 runners，也一定有所保留。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw2.jpg)

这类 Phenomena 让一些 academics 认为，endurance 的真正 limits 并不是 physical，而是[在 brain 中](https://www.paulogentil.com/pdf/Challenging%20beliefs%20-%20ex%20Africa%20semper%20aliquid%20novi.pdf)。像 Hutchinson 这样的 Runners 并不是因为 reach 了真正的 physical limits 才 top out，而是因为 brain 会 throttles performance，让他们永远不冒 reach 那些 limits 的风险。这提供了充足的 margin，以 prevent bodily damage；同时，它也会 anticipates 未来对 performance 的 requirements，在 race 离结束还很远时有所保留。

## Physical Fatigue 与 Mental Energy 之间的 Connection

存在一个会 throttles athletic performance 的“central governor”，从 evolutionary 角度看是合理的。如果我们跑得太拼，以至于 tear 我们的 muscles、break blood vessels，或让我们的 brain 缺乏 oxygen，那么刚刚 set 了 personal best 也就无关紧要了。

但同样的 rules 也适用于 mental fatigue 吗？毕竟，没有人的 brain 会因为自己停止 procrastinating 就缺乏 oxygen。

mental 与 physical fatigue 之间的 links 很有意思。先 perform difficult mental tasks、随后在 exercise bike 上做 endurance test 的 Participants，会比那些没有做过 hard mental tasks 的人[更早 give up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131473/)。Mental performance [通常会因 exercise 而 enhanced](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2022.957677/full)，但当我们尝试在 exercising 的同时做 cognitive tasks 时，我们的 performance 通常会 suffers。

一些 scholars 甚至认为，physical 和 mental fatigue 是[同一回事](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/psychology-of-fatigue/026AC1D806D757CC7B4BA2FFAFA4905D)。也就是说，虽然这一 phenomenon 确实有不同 facets（例如 sleepy、muscle weakness 等），但 fatigue 似乎存在一个 general component，同时 encompass physical 和 mental work。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw3.jpg)

mental fatigue 的 function 是什么，目前还不清楚。有可能，mental fatigue 与 physical fatigue 一样，都在 tracking 某种 underlying biological state：energy availability、local sleep debt 或 stress hormones。

 另一种可能是，整体而言的 fatigue，尤其是 mental fatigue，实际上是在 protecting 我们，避免我们 investing 于 [undesirable goals](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2021/05/03/effort-opportunity-cost/)。当我们在一项缺乏 intrinsic value、也不能带来 immediate satisfaction 的 activity 上 work 太久时，fatigue 就开始 build。也许 fatigue 是一种更 general 的 emotion，会 creates pressure，促使我们 change activities——protecting 我们的身体免于 athletics 中的 physical overexertion，也 protecting 我们 limited attention，避免它被看似 uninteresting 或 futile 的 tasks 所 absorbed。

无论是哪种情况，mental fatigue 的 effect 都与 physical fatigue 类似：throttle performance，以 prevent overexertion，既针对当下，也 anticipating 未来的 demands。

## 通过减少 Working 来完成更多事情

我提起这些，是因为 energy management 的一个关键 idea，就是在 effort 和 rest 的 natural rhythms 内 working。如果 Work non-stop，我们就会 exhaust 自己。但如果我们能让一段段 intense focus 配合 complete recovery，那么看似矛盾的是，我们反而能用更少的 time 完成更多事情，同时减少 exhaustion。

我当然不是第一个指出这一点的人；当我们把 working hours 限制在 natural rhythms 内，反而能完成更多事情，这个 idea 早已是 productivity literature 中长期存在的 finding。从早年 H. M. Vernon [发现](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781003291046/industrial-fatigue-efficiency-vernon)，从当时常见的每周 70 到 80 hour workweeks 减少 workloads，并未导致 work output 减少，到如今 Cal Newport 的 [fixed-schedule productivity](https://calnewport.com/fixed-schedule-productivity-how-i-accomplish-a-large-amount-of-work-in-a-small-number-of-work-hours/) 等形式，这个看似矛盾的 finding——强迫自己 work 更少，反而会更 productive——长期以来一直是 self-help 中的常见内容。

这些关于 fatigue 的 ideas，为上述 explanations 增添了一个有趣的转折。如果我们投入 tasks 的 effort 不只是 underlying mental capacity 的 measure，而是我们的 brain 为完成 work 而 implementing 的一种微妙的“pacing”strategy，那么这就解释了为什么严苛的 non-stop schedules 经常会 _lower_ productivity。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw4.jpg)

预料到自己将无法 rest，我们会 unconsciously reduce 自己投入 effort 的意愿。这可能意味着继续坚持 task，但投入更少的 effort，并接受 reduced performance。也可能意味着 procrastinating、偷懒，或只做 work 中那些 effort 更低、也较不重要的琐碎部分。

结果，按给定 standard 完成 work 所需的 time 会 expands，我们便得到了一种类似 Parkinson 在 1955 年文章中所 described 的 effect。

## manage energy 意味着什么？

我认为，这触及了 manage energy 的核心含义。这不只是寻找“balance”，或在 self-care 与工作时间之间作出 trade-offs。相反，它反映了这样一个根本事实：当我们拥有 healthy 的 work 与 recovery 的 rhythms 时，我们才能 work best。

我们的文化太常把两个极端对立起来。你要么是雄心勃勃、一心拼搏的奋斗者，要么是必须受到呵护、不能承受 excess stress 的娇弱兰花。接着，不出所料，人们纷纷站队，谴责一方，支持另一方。

我认为，我一直介绍的 research 有说服力地表明，这是一种 false dichotomy。Meaningful work、work 与 rest 的 natural rhythms，以及培养良好的 sleep、diet 和 exercise 等 healthy lifestyle habits：这些 managing energy 的 practices 不仅是努力工作的关键，也是好好生活的关键。

## 原文

Parkinson’s Law states:

> “Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion.”

This “law” was proposed by the British naval historian [C. Northcote Parkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Northcote_Parkinson) in a satirical 1955 essay for _The Economist_. Taken literally, it’s obviously false. Simply setting a deadline doesn’t make a goal achievable on an arbitrary timeframe.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw1.jpg)

There’s a heap of academic literature making the opposite case. The [planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy) describes the well-documented tendency for complex projects to have cost overruns and delays. Indeed, the truth is perhaps closer to Hofstadter’s Law, where the author [Douglas Hofstadter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Hofstadter) argued tongue-in-cheek that, “It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter’s Law.”

Despite its definitively un-lawlike empirical status, Parkinson’s Law does capture an essential human truth: when we loosen time constraints, the time needed to do something somehow expands to fill at least some of the gap.

## Energy, Pacing and the Limits of Human Endurance

I was thinking about Parkinson’s Law as I was reading [Endure](https://www.amazon.ca/Endure-Curiously-Elastic-Limits-Performance/dp/006249998X), Alex Hutchinson’s excellent book examining the limits of human persistence and some of the scientific controversy as to the exact nature of those limits.

Hutchinson, an impressive endurance athlete himself, retells a story of trying to beat his personal best time in the 1500m race in his days in track and field. He notes that despite giving every bit of effort he could muster, his pace would always speed up right at the end. He even tried to “trick” himself into running flat out, but consistently found the same dip in speed in the middle of his races.

If we assume that endurance is limited primarily by the body’s internal resources—muscle glycogen, oxygen uptake, ATP and whatnot—this makes no sense. If we’re giving it absolutely everything we’ve got, how is it possible that we can speed up as the race reaches its final stretch?

Hutchinson wasn’t alone in his peculiarity, however. It turns out that world-record runs show the same pattern of a dip in pace followed by a slight acceleration toward the end of the race. Even the most well-trained, disciplined and motivated runners must be holding something back.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw2.jpg)

Phenomena like this suggest to some academics that the true limits on endurance are not physical, but [in the brain](https://www.paulogentil.com/pdf/Challenging%20beliefs%20-%20ex%20Africa%20semper%20aliquid%20novi.pdf). Runners like Hutchinson don’t top out because they reach their true physical limits, but because the brain throttles performance so they never risk reaching those limits. This gives a comfortable margin to prevent bodily damage, and it anticipates future requirements for performance, holding back some when the race is still far from over.

## The Connection Between Physical Fatigue and Mental Energy

The presence of a “central governor” that throttles athletic performance makes evolutionary sense. If we run so hard we tear our muscles, break blood vessels or starve our brain of oxygen, it doesn’t matter that we just set a personal best.

But do the same rules work for mental fatigue? After all, nobody’s brain starves of oxygen because they stop procrastinating.

The links between mental and physical fatigue are interesting. Participants who perform difficult mental tasks and then do an endurance test on an exercise bike [give up earlier](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131473/) than those who haven’t done hard mental tasks. Mental performance is [generally enhanced by exercise](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2022.957677/full), but our performance typically suffers when we try to do cognitive tasks while exercising.

Some scholars even argue that physical and mental fatigue are [the same thing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/psychology-of-fatigue/026AC1D806D757CC7B4BA2FFAFA4905D). That, while there are certainly different facets to the phenomenon (e.g., being sleepy, muscle weakness, etc.), there is a general component of fatigue that seems to encompass both physical and mental work.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw3.jpg)

It’s not yet clear what the function of mental fatigue is. It’s possible that, like physical fatigue, mental fatigue is tracking some underlying biological state: energy availability, local sleep debt or stress hormones.

 Another possibility is that fatigue in general, and mental fatigue specifically, is really about protecting us from investing in [undesirable goals](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2021/05/03/effort-opportunity-cost/). When we work too long at an activity that lacks intrinsic value and is not immediately satisfying, fatigue begins to build. Perhaps fatigue is a more general emotion that creates pressure to change activities—protecting our bodies from physical overexertion in athletics and our limited attention from being absorbed by tasks that seem uninteresting or futile.

In either case, the effect of mental fatigue is similar to physical fatigue: throttle performance to prevent overexertion, both in the moment and anticipating future demands.

## Getting More Done by Working Less

I bring all of this up because a key idea in energy management is working within natural rhythms of effort and rest. Work non-stop and we’ll exhaust ourselves. But if we can adopt periods of intense focus with complete recovery, paradoxically, we can get more done in less time with less exhaustion.

I’m certainly not the first to point this out, and the idea that we somehow get more done when we restrict our working hours within natural rhythms is a long-standing finding in productivity literature. From the earliest days when H. M. Vernon [found](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781003291046/industrial-fatigue-efficiency-vernon) that reducing workloads from then-common 70 to 80 hour workweeks did not result in reduced work output, to modern incarnations like Cal Newport’s [fixed-schedule productivity](https://calnewport.com/fixed-schedule-productivity-how-i-accomplish-a-large-amount-of-work-in-a-small-number-of-work-hours/), the paradoxical finding that we’re more productive when we force ourselves to work less has long been a self-help staple.

These ideas on fatigue add an interesting twist to the explanations. If the effort we put into tasks is not simply a measure of our underlying mental capacity, rather a subtle “pacing” strategy our brain is implementing to get through the work, it explains why punishing, non-stop schedules so often _lower_ productivity.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Parkinsonslaw4.jpg)

Anticipating that we’ll be unable to rest, we unconsciously reduce our willingness to put in effort. This can mean sticking to the task but putting in less effort and accepting reduced performance. Alternatively, it can mean procrastinating, slacking off or engaging in trivial aspects of the work that are lower effort and less important.

As a result, the time needed to finish the work to a given standard expands, and we get an effect akin to what Parkinson described in his 1955 essay.

## What Does it Mean to Manage Energy?

This, I think, gets at the heart of what it means to manage energy. It’s not simply about finding “balance” or making trade-offs between self-care and time for work. Instead, it’s reflecting the fundamental reality that we work best when we have healthy rhythms of work and recovery.

Too often, our culture pits extremes against each other. You’re either an ambitious striver committed to the hustle, or you’re a delicate orchid that must be sheltered from excess stress. Then, predictably, people line up to denounce one side and support the other.

I think what the research I’ve been covering shows, convincingly, is that this is a false dichotomy. Meaningful work, natural rhythms of work and rest, and healthy lifestyle habits like nurturing good sleep, diet and exercise: these practices for managing energy aren’t just key to working hard, but to living well.
