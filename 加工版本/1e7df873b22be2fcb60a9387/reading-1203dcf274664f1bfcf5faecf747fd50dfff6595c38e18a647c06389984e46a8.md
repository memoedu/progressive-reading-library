# 清单 email

---

## 正文

我的一位朋友最近在看一些 startups 的 job offers，问我该如何看待他获授的 equity awards。

这在很大程度上取决于一些细节，而 companies 往往不会告诉你，除非你主动问。因此，我最后写出了这样一封 email：面对任何 equity offer，我都会发它来询问所有容易藏有玄机的细节——并附上如何 interpret 回复的说明。

* * *

在询问那些容易藏有玄机的细节之前，有两个问题需要你自己先回答：

-   **你需要这笔钱吗？** 如果需要，就别指望靠 equity 来获得它！你的首要考虑始终应该是有足够的 cash，支付你需要的一切。Equity 通常只有在你接近 risk-neutral 时才是划算的，比如因为[你打算把它全部捐给慈善机构](https://80000hours.org/2012/01/salary-or-startup-how-do-gooders-can-gain-more-from-risky-careers/)。

-   **你信任 founders 吗？** Equity 确实很复杂，基本上不可能确保你不会以某种隐蔽的方式被坑。只有当你相信 founders（以及其他掌握权力的人）不会这么做时，下面的问题才有意义。遗憾的是，除非你能通过某种私下渠道获得关于 founders 的 reference，否则这很难判断。

## 清单 email

如果我有一些 job offers，正在 evaluating 其中的 equity，下面就是我会索取的信息，整理成了方便使用的 email 格式：

> 你好，$NAME，
> 
> 有机会在 $COMPANY 工作，我真的很兴奋！关于 equity offer，我有几个问题，想借此弄清该如何 value 它：
> 
> -   如果我 leave 或被 terminated，options 会在什么时候 expire？
> -   这个 offer 包含多少 shares，company 又有多少 shares 是 outstanding 的？
> -   你们各次 funding rounds 的 investment terms 是什么？（Amount invested、post-money valuation，以及任何 liquidation preferences 或其他 protections）
> -   vesting 是 four years，带有 one-year cliff，还是其他安排？
> -   你们提供 early exercise 吗？
> -   options 的 strike price 是多少？
> -   它们是 ISOs 还是 NSOs？

（尽管复制粘贴，用于你自己的 negotiations！你大概也应该询问 company 的 metrics 和 financial health，不过那留到另一篇文章再谈。）

_附言：如果你对其他问题有建议，或有相关经历或 data points，请在下面留言！如果你希望这些内容保持私密，也可以[给我发邮件](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)。_

## 推理依据（Reasoning）

下面是每项信息背后的 reasoning，以及我会如何 interpret 回答：

-   **Option expiry window（期权到期窗口）。** default 是 options 在你 leave company 后的 90 days expire。你可以通过 exercising 它们来防止它们 expiring，但这可能给你带来[各种可怕的问题](https://www.benkuhn.net/options#golden-handcuffs)。如果 option expiry window 是 short，你基本上就需要一直留在 company，直到它 goes public，才能 cash out。这很糟糕，原因有很多：

    -   如果工作环境变差，或你遇到糟糕的经理，或你的人生重心改变，你都无法 leave（除非 giving up 你已经 earned 的 equity）。

    -   你对 company 没有 negotiating leverage，因为你没有好的 [BATNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_alternative_to_a_negotiated_agreement)。

    -   你无法[利用 vesting schedule 来获得额外的 option value](https://www.benkuhn.net/optopt)。

    这无疑是 stock options 最重要的“陷阱”（不过，你也许可以通过 early exercise 避开它——见下文）。就我个人而言，除非我打算 early exercise，否则我会把带有 90-day expiry 的 option package 看作基本上 worthless。

```
The 90-days provision is the default because of rules in the tax code, so it doesn't mean that a company with 90-day expiry is malicious or evil, just uninformed. (But if they tell you that they want to keep the 90-day window to incentivize people to keep working there, that *does* mean they're at least a little bit evil. There are better ways of doing that!)
```

-   **Fraction of the company（公司所占份额）。** 这显然很重要，因为你要用它与 company 的 value 相 multiply，才能得到你的 stock 的 expected value。遗憾的是，多得令人沮丧的 companies 只会告诉你它们给你的 options 的 number（或者同样糟糕的是，只告诉你“X dollars 的 options”，却不告诉你 X 是如何 computed 的）。

-   **VC investment terms** [（此前的讨论）](https://www.benkuhn.net/terms)。为了弄清如果 company 被 acquired，你能赚到多少钱，你需要知道其他 investors 拿到了什么 terms。看到某个 VC invested 于 \$100 million valuation, and conclude that your 1% of the company is worth \$1 million，很容易让人心动。但如果 VC 的 shares 带有比如说 [2x liquidation preference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidation_preference)，那么 share-for-share 而言，它们就比你的 valuable 得多——取决于 terms，可能高达 3x。

-   **Vesting schedule（归属安排）。** 这一项排得靠后，仅仅是因为大多数 companies 都给你 standard vesting schedule：4 years，带有 1 year cliff。有些的 vesting period 更长，这是以隐蔽的方式 lower 你的 effective（annualized）compensation。另一种常见的 modification 是 Back-weighting options（例如，第 1 年 vesting 10%，到第 4 年 scaling up to 40%）。

    这两种安排都是潜在的 red flags——例如，据说 Amazon 会 back-weight 其 vesting，因为他们让很多人在 1-2 years 内就 burn out，这样能为他们省下很多钱。不过，companies 想要 disincentivize 人们仅仅一两年后就 leaving，也有其合理性。只要 total grant amount 更高，足以弥补更差的 timing，我就不会太担心较慢的 vesting schedule。

-   **Early exercise** [（此前的讨论）](https://www.benkuhn.net/options)。这指的是你提前支付 options 的 strike price，甚至在它们 vested 之前就支付，从而让你 own 的是 stock，而不是 options。如果 options 带有 short expiry window（见上文），你应当强烈倾向于默认这样做。

    如果 options 带有 long expiry window，early exercise 带来的差别就较小：如果 company succeeds，它会让你的 tax situation 更好；如果 company fails，它会让你亏掉更多钱。如果你很在意 optimizing 已经极好的 outcomes，而且有 disposable cash，你应该阅读链接中的讨论，了解细节。

-   **Strike price（行权价格）。** 如果你在考虑 early exercise，strike price 就 determines 这样做需要预先付出多少成本。否则，除非这是一家 late-stage company，strike price 并没有那么重要，因为你最终 exercise options 的 chance 是 low。

-   **ISOs 与 NSOs。** ISOs 是一个非常强的 default，也是大家都在谈论的类型，但如果 company 说是 NSOs，你应该查一查[这会如何影响 tax treatment](https://www.theventurealley.com/2016/10/isos-vs-nsos/)，因为它可能让最终的 expected value 出现很大的差别（30% 或更多）。你也应该问问他们为什么使用 NSOs，因为这是一个奇怪的决定。

## 对 offer 进行 Valuing

如果 company 拒绝向你提供这些 information 中的任何一项，那就是一个巨大的 red flag：他们并不在乎，或者不希望你能够 effectively 地 value 这些 options，很可能是因为这些 options 实际上并没有多大 valuable。

如果他们回答了你的问题，而第一个回答是“90 day expiry window”，你有三种选择：

-   说服他们将其改为 7+ years。这对你来说是最好的 outcome，而且 company 确实_应该_愿意这样做，因为这会让他们的 offers 更有吸引力，而 cost 相对很小。但他们可能不愿意，或者无法足够快地 adjust 他们的 stock option plan。

-   决定进行 early exercise。这会花很多钱（option strike price times number of options），但会让你可以随时离开，而不至于白白烧钱。注意，如果你离开时持有 unvested shares，你要么可以保留这些 unvested shares，要么可以拿回你为它们支付的钱——由 company 选择哪一种。

-   什么也不做。在这种情况下，为了保留 equity 而必须放弃的 career optionality，很可能比这份 equity 更值得。

如果他们有合理的 expiry window，或者你决定进行 early exercise，那么你接下来就必须弄清楚 option package 的实际 expected value。原则上，这是一个 simple formula：

> options expected value = number of shares × (expected value per share − probability of success × per-share strike price)

当然，你根本不知道“expected value per share”或“probability of success”的 values 是多少！

实际操作中，对于 early stage startups，probability of success 和 strike price 通常足够 low，因此你可以 ignore 那个 term。不过，对于 late stage startups，它可能非常重要，所以要小心。

Expected value per share 是更难确定的那一个。最稳妥的估计方式是借助 [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis)：assume 这个 company 的 venture investors 支付了 fair price，并据此 value 你的 share。

棘手之处在于，你只能 assume 那些 VCs 为_他们的_ stock 支付了 fair price，而他们的 stock 附带的 protections 比你的更多。这就是为什么你需要知道实际的 investment terms。我在[之前的讨论](https://www.benkuhn.net/terms)中引用的 study 发现，取决于 terms，common stock 的价值比 VCs 的 stock 低 0% 到 66%。以下是我对 rule of thumb 的猜测：

-   如果 VCs 的 stock 与你的 exactly the same，你完全不需要 discount。

-   如果 company 为其 VCs 提供的 protections 数量正常（基本上是 1x liquidation preference），而且 VC ownership 的数量也正常（对于 early stage companies 是 10-40%），就进行大约 25% 的 discount——这是所引用 paper 中的 median discount。

-   如果 company 的 VC ownership 占 high fraction（超过 40%），或者 investment terms 特别糟糕（超过 1x liquidation preference、uncapped participation 等），那么进行的 discount 应更接近 50%。

## 原文

A friend of mine was looking at some job offers from startups recently and asked me for advice on how to think about the equity awards he was offered.

This depends a lot on details that companies often don’t give you unless you ask. So I ended up writing up the email I’d send asking for all the tricky details on any equity offer—plus instructions on how to interpret the response.

* * *

Before you ask for any tricky details, there are two questions you need to answer on your own:

-   **Do you need the money?** If so, don’t rely on equity to get it! Your first priority should always be to have enough cash to pay for everything you need. Equity is usually only a good deal if you’re close to risk-neutral, for instance because [you plan to donate it all to charity](https://80000hours.org/2012/01/salary-or-startup-how-do-gooders-can-gain-more-from-risky-careers/).

-   **Do you trust the founders?** Equity is really complicated and it’s basically impossible to make sure you won’t get screwed in some tricky way. The below questions only make sense if you trust the founders (and other people with authority) not to do that. Unfortunately, this is hard to assess unless you get some kind of backdoor reference on the founders.

## The checklist email

If I had job offers whose equity I was evaluating, here’s the info I’d ask for, in convenient email format:

> Hi $NAME,
> 
> Really excited about the opportunity to work at $COMPANY! I just have a few questions about the equity offer to help me figure out how to value it:
> 
> -   If I leave or am terminated, when do the options expire?
> -   How many shares is the offer, and how many shares in the company are outstanding?
> -   What were the investment terms of your funding rounds? (Amount invested, post-money valuation, and any liquidation preferences or other protections)
> -   Is vesting four years with a one-year cliff or something else?
> -   Do you offer early exercise?
> -   What’s the strike price of the options?
> -   Are they ISOs or NSOs?

(Feel free to copy and paste it for your own negotiations! And you should probably be also asking about the company’s metrics and financial health, but that’s for another post.)

_PS: If you have suggestions for other questions, or relevant stories or data points, please comment below! If you’d prefer them to stay private you can also [email me](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)._

## Reasoning

Here’s the reasoning behind each piece of info, and how I’d interpret the answer:

-   **Option expiry window.** The default is for options to expire 90 days after you leave the company. You can prevent them from expiring by exercising them, but this can cause you [all kinds of horrible problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/options#golden-handcuffs). If the option expiry window is short, you essentially need to stay at the company until it goes public to cash out. This is horrible for many reasons:

    -   You can’t leave (without giving up the equity you earned) if the work environment becomes bad, or you get a bad manager, or your life priorities change.

    -   You have no negotiating leverage with the company because you don’t have a good [BATNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_alternative_to_a_negotiated_agreement).

    -   You can’t [use the vesting schedule for extra option value](https://www.benkuhn.net/optopt).

    This is by far the most important “gotcha” of stock options (though you may be able to avoid it through early exercise—see below). Personally, I’d treat an option package with 90-day expiry as basically worthless unless I was planning to early exercise.

```
The 90-days provision is the default because of rules in the tax code, so it doesn't mean that a company with 90-day expiry is malicious or evil, just uninformed. (But if they tell you that they want to keep the 90-day window to incentivize people to keep working there, that *does* mean they're at least a little bit evil. There are better ways of doing that!)
```

-   **Fraction of the company.** This is obviously important because it’s what you multiply the company’s value by, to get the expected value of your stock. Unfortunately, a depressing number of companies will only tell you the number of options they’re giving you (or, just as bad, only tell you “X dollars of options” without telling you how X was computed).

-   **VC investment terms** [(previous discussion)](https://www.benkuhn.net/terms). In order to figure out how much money you’ll make if the company is acquired, you need to know what terms their other investors got. It’s very tempting to notice that a VC invested at a $100 million valuation, and conclude that your 1% of the company is worth $1 million. But if the VC’s shares have, say, a [2x liquidation preference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidation_preference), then share-for-share they’re way more valuable than yours—maybe up to 3x depending on the terms.

-   **Vesting schedule.** This is low down only because most companies give you the standard vesting schedule: 4 years with a 1 year cliff. Some have a longer vesting period, which is a sneaky way to lower your effective (annualized) compensation. Back-weighting options (e.g. vesting 10% in year 1 scaling up to 40% in year 4) is the other common modification.

    Both of these are potential red flags—for instance, Amazon is reputed to back-weight their vesting because they burn out so many people within 1-2 years that it saves them a lot of money. But it’s also reasonable for companies to want to disincentivize people from leaving after just a year or two. As long as the total grant amount is higher to make up for the worse timing, I wouldn’t be too worried about a slower vesting schedule.

-   **Early exercise** [(previous discussion)](https://www.benkuhn.net/options). This is where you pay the strike price of your options up front, even before they’ve vested, so that you own the stock instead of the options. If the options have a short expiry window (see above) you should strongly default to doing this.

    If the options have a long expiry window, early exercise makes less of a difference: it will make your tax situation better if the company succeeds, and make you lose more money if it fails. If you care a lot about optimizing already-awesome outcomes and have disposable cash, you should read the linked discussion to learn the details.

-   **Strike price.** If you’re considering early exercise, the strike price determines how much it costs up front to do that. Otherwise, the strike price doesn’t matter that much unless it’s a late-stage company, because the chance that you ever exercise the options is low.

-   **ISOs vs NSOs.** ISOs are a very strong default and what everyone talks about, but if the company says NSOs, you should look up [how that affects the tax treatment](https://www.theventurealley.com/2016/10/isos-vs-nsos/), because it can make a big difference (30% or more) in the eventual expected value. Also you should ask them why they use NSOs because it’s a strange decision.

## Valuing the offer

If the company refuses to give you any of this information, it’s a huge red flag: they don’t care or don’t want you to be able to value the options effectively, probably because the options aren’t actually very valuable.

If they answer your questions, and the first answer is “90 day expiry window,” you have three options:

-   Convince them to change it to 7+ years. This is the best outcome for you, and the company really _should_ want to do it because it makes their offers so much more attractive, at relatively little cost. But they may not want to, or they may not be able to adjust their stock option plan quickly enough.

-   Decide to early exercise. This will cost a lot of money (the option strike price times the number of options), but will give you the option of leaving at any time without lighting money on fire. Note that if you leave with unvested shares, you will either get to keep the unvested shares, or get back the money that you paid for them—the company gets to choose which.

-   Do nothing. In this case the equity is probably not worth the career optionality that you have to give up to keep it.

If they have a reasonable expiry window or you decide to early exercise, you then have to figure out the actual expected value of the option package. In principle, this is a simple formula:

> options expected value = number of shares × (expected value per share − probability of success × per-share strike price)

Of course, you have no idea what the “expected value per share” or “probability of success” values are!

In practice, for early stage startups the probability of success and strike price are usually low enough that you can ignore that term. For late stage startups, though, it can matter a lot, so be careful.

Expected value per share is the harder one. The safest way to guess it is by appealing to the [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis): assume that the company’s venture investors paid a fair price, and value your share based on that.

The tricky part you can only assume the VCs paid a fair price for _their_ stock, which comes with more protections than yours. That’s why you need to know the actual investment terms. The study I cited in my [previous discussion](https://www.benkuhn.net/terms) found that common stock was worth between 0% and 66% less than VCs’ stock, depending on the terms. Here’s my guess at a rule of thumb:

-   If the VCs’ stock is exactly the same as yours, you don’t need to discount at all.

-   If the company has a normal amount of protections for their VCs (basically, 1x liquidation preference) and a normal amount of VC ownership (10-40% for early stage companies), discount by about 25%—that’s the median discount from the cited paper.

-   If the company has a high fraction of VC ownership (over 40%), or especially bad investment terms (more than 1x liquidation preference, uncapped participation, etc.), then discount by more like 50%.
