# 235 Making Startups Powerful

## 阅读前置卡

- 价值判断：高。内容围绕创业公司如何从弱变强，提出可操作的启发式（网络效应、全栈、上游、早期客户、API、慷慨），并配有 PayPal、Stripe、Rippling、YC 等具体案例，对创业者与产品策略有直接指导意义，但属于经验性建议而非可验证的普适理论。
- 信息密度判断：高。全文几乎每段都给出一个独立策略或判断，并附案例或反例，信息点密集；但部分段落是同一主题的展开与脚注补充，存在一定重复，因此未达 very_high。
- 是否继续阅读：4 精细阅读。结构清晰、论点递进，从具体策略到统一约束（必须让客户更好），并给出可反向生成创意的启发式，阅读收益高且节奏紧凑，适合继续深入。

## 核心洞察

一、以“更强大”替代“更赚钱”作为提问框架：把“如何赚更多钱”换成“什么会让这家公司更强大”，能跳出渐进式改进，转向数量级价值提升。强大意味着改变公司在价值链中的位置，而不仅是优化现有收入。
[[#^pr-f94f4fb78d3d|正文跳转]]

二、让资金流经自己：从单纯组件供应商转变为掌握客户关系的一方，或让资金流经自身，是提升公司权力的直接路径。资金流经通常意味着同时掌握客户关系与支付环节，从而在生态中占据枢纽位置。
[[#^pr-ffed3df1b0dc|正文跳转]]

三、用网络效应改造普通产品：主动寻找在看似不可能的地方引入网络效应的办法，常能把服务变成市场。让用户分享数据、比较表现或贡献模型训练，都是诱导网络效应的具体手段，且可能彻底改变产品形态。
[[#^pr-055ad036709c|正文跳转]]

四、泛化创意以制造网络效应：把创意泛化可以加倍增强公司权力。例如让代理之间互相付款，就从工具变成市场；若市场价值足够大，甚至值得让整个公司向该方向倾斜，必要时充当做市商来启动。
[[#^pr-d9f54bf0b8c0|正文跳转]]

五、全栈策略：吞掉客户：不把技术卖给做某事的公司，而是自己用该技术去做那件事并与它们竞争。全栈会拉伸创意直至吞没原客户，并改变外部接触面的形状；其极限是客户只负责跑腿，真正的客户变成客户的客户。
[[#^pr-0b682e095509|正文跳转]]

六、警惕“尾巴摇狗”的信号：当用户把产品用于非预期用途时，可能意味着存在更迫切的需求。PayPal 从安全演示变成支付业务就是典型；应倾听用户误用发出的信息，而不是因误用而恼怒。
[[#^pr-4b3142049084|正文跳转]]

七、帮助用户赚钱是最强增长杠杆：帮助用户赚钱会让他们快速采用并愿意支付高价，收入因此加速增长。最成功的被投公司大多帮助用户赚钱，YC 本身也是如此；这比单纯优化产品功能更能带来权力。
[[#^pr-a5e4e387c89f|正文跳转]]

八、长期博弈与慷慨创造权力：多数竞争者只关注短期，因此十年后才回报的取舍常被低估。慷慨地创造多于捕获的价值，看似理想主义，实则是通往真正富有的路径；开源、可扩展、API 开放都是其表现。
[[#^pr-bc40dfe6eccd|正文跳转]]

九、尽早卖给早期公司：卖给越早期的公司，虽然它们钱少，但若按使用量收费，收入会以创业公司速度增长。Stripe 特意尽早签约，因为支付基础设施一旦安装就不易更换；早期客户决策快，最好的产品直接胜出。
[[#^pr-e07318606dfb|正文跳转]]

十、上游化：资金、关系、阶段与数据：无论是资金流、用户关系、客户阶段还是数据，向上游移动几乎总是有利。Rippling 从入职软件切入，因为那是员工数据的生命起点，从而为成为员工数据操作系统奠定基础。
[[#^pr-6eeba5dff663|正文跳转]]

十一、避开黑手党式市场，从侧面进入：创业公司在公平竞技场最强，在由唱片公司、PBM 等黑手党式企业主导的市场最弱。正面攻击难以取胜，应通过让它们变得无关紧要来从侧面进入，使击败它们成为附带结果。
[[#^pr-94d63bff1dfb|正文跳转]]

十二、识别被其他公司拖住之处：让公司更强大的常见模式是找到被其他公司拖住的地方：组件供应商被困在他人定义的盒子里，卖给官僚大公司则受制于其节奏。把“当前创意在哪些方面被其他公司拖住”当作生成更大创意的启发式。
[[#^pr-9ac98f3ff15d|正文跳转]]

十三、创业公司常被自己拖住：许多创业建议只需加一个“just”：你不需要做 x，只需 y。创意复杂往往源于恐惧，公司无意识地畏缩，做比本可以更不雄心的事。Just y 实际上是“站直”，做到后公司会高得多。
[[#^pr-9fa72b51ec9f|正文跳转]]

十四、所有权力策略的共同约束：让客户更好：添加网络效应、让资金流经自己、全栈等策略都必须让客户变得更好，否则不会有采用。创业公司最初的弱小正是其对世界有益的原因：它们只能通过改善客户生活来获得权力。
[[#^pr-7ef91aedf607|正文跳转]]

十五、反向运行约束来生成创意：从客户角度设想完美世界，如果其中某个组件是创业公司可以把自己转变而成的，那大概就应该这么做。这一约束如此刚性，以至于可以反向用来产生创意，把客户利益作为创意生成的起点。
[[#^pr-a4fd194367bd|正文跳转]]

# 235 Making Startups Powerful

September 2026

我在与 startups 进行 office hours 时最有用的 heuristics 之一就是问：什么会让这家 company 更 powerful？问这家 company 如何能赚更多钱也是一个好的 heuristic，但它往往只会带来 incremental improvements。而思考如何让它更 powerful，有时会让它 valuable 提升 orders of magnitude。

^pr-f94f4fb78d3d

这个问题有很多不同的 variants，取决于 company 的类型。有没有办法把这家 company 从一个单纯的 component supplier 转变为拥有与 customer 的 relationship 的那一方？或者相关的问题：有没有办法让 money flow through it？当 money flows through you 时总是好事。[1](#fn1)

^pr-ffed3df1b0dc

有没有办法创造出类似 app store 的东西，让其他 companies 可以在你的 product 之上 build？那么它们为创造 valuable things 所做的一切努力也会让你更有 valuable。理想情况下，这与 owning the customer relationship 以及 making the money flow through you 相结合。[2](#fn2)

Network effects 让 companies 更 powerful，而我几乎总是思考如何引入它们。我把它当作一种挑战，看看有没有办法在你甚至不会预期拥有它们的事物中获得 network effects。[3](#fn3) 令人惊讶的是，这竟然如此经常能够做到。而当能够做到时，这有时会彻底 transforms the idea；原本是一项 service 的东西现在成了一个 marketplace。豪华版是 full app store，但如果没有更直接的方式做到这一点，你往往可以通过让你的 users 分享某些东西来 induce network effects。例如，如果你 opt in，我们会告诉你与其他 users 相比你做得如何。明显的 AI variant 是让你的 users opt in，用他们与 model 的 interactions 来 training your model。许多人会抗拒这一点，但如果有些人不抗拒，他们得以使用的 model 将 outperform 其他人使用的 vanilla one。

^pr-055ad036709c

你往往可以通过 generalizing the idea 来 induce network effects，这会让 startup 加倍地更 powerful。例如，如果我在与一家正在构建让 agents 为事物付款的方式的 startup 交谈，我会问的第一个问题是这些 agents 是否也能互相付款。如果它们能做到这一点，你就成了一个 marketplace。而成为一个 marketplace 是如此 valuable，以至于如果 agents 能互相付款买什么不是立刻显而易见，那就值得花大量时间努力想出点什么。如果你能想出来，也许值得把整个 company 向那个方向倾斜，必要时甚至成为一个 market maker 来让它运转起来。

^pr-d9f54bf0b8c0

这些对 original idea 的假设性 transformations 并不总是能产生任何有前途的东西。远非如此。但它们总是值得考虑；至少，尝试 transform 一个 idea 能帮助你更好地理解它。

有某些类型的思考，ideas 开始显得几乎像 physical 的一样。大多数 programmers 大概都经历过。Manipulating startup ideas 感觉也是如此。一种感觉特别 physical 的 transformation 是 going full stack 的策略：不是把你的 technology 卖给做 x 的 companies，而是你自己使用这项 technology 去做 x，与它们竞争。你几乎能看到这个 idea 在它 engulf 原本的 customer 时 stretch。而现在你拥有了 outside surface，那的形状大概也会改变。

^pr-0b682e095509

Going full stack 有一种 variant，即你通过替 customer 做所有最难的工作来逐渐 eat your way through the customer。在 limit case 中，你在做所有的 brainwork，而他们只是在为你跑腿。到那时，如同在 full stack case 中一样，真正的 customer 是他们的 customer；最初的 customer 现在只是一种 hand puppet。

我一直在寻找的另一件事是能够 wag the dog 的 tails。startups 的历史充满了这些。Paypal 最初是为 hand-held devices 做 security。他们创建 Paypal 作为他们 security software 的 demo。但随后 eBay sellers 开始用它来 take payments，几个月后 founders 承认这就是他们现在所处的 business，尽管他们本无意如此。所以每当 founders 构建某种 main product 的 peripheral 的东西时，我总是问：这会不会是真正的 product？

^pr-4b3142049084

当你注意到 users “misusing” 你的 product 去做你未曾打算的事情时，这是令人兴奋的。这意味着有某种东西他们如此迫切地想要，以至于他们不仅会使用你提供的任何 solution，甚至会使用那些本不打算作为 solutions 的东西。当你看到类似这样的情况时，不要因为你的 users 用错了你的 product 而恼火；倾听他们发出的 message，因为它可能是有 valuable 的。

Paypal 增长如此之快的原因是它帮助 users make money。很少有东西比这更能让你 powerful。当你帮助 users make money 时，他们 (a) 会迅速 adopt your product，(b) 会为此付很多钱。所以你的 revenues 会加倍地快增长。我们资助过的最成功的 companies 大多帮助它们的 users make money，YC 本身也是如此。

^pr-a5e4e387c89f

Playing the long game 让你更 powerful，因为你遇到的大多数其他人都不会这么做。大多数与你竞争的 startups 将由希望被收购的 opportunists 经营。你打交道的大多数 big companies 将由那些不指望在那里待超过几年、只想着这个季度数字的 executives 经营。所以那些只在 10 年后才有回报的 tradeoffs 通常会被 underpriced。经典的一种是为了 acquire users 而提供 great terms。我一般建议 startups 一开始尽可能便宜地卖；先 get all the users，然后再担心你的 margins。但通常也有更深层的、结构性的方式来 play the long game。[4](#fn4)

Being generous 让你更 powerful。正如 Tim O’Reilly 所说，你应该 create more value than you capture。许多精明的 business types 会把这当作理想主义的 hippy stuff 而不屑一顾，但事实上这是变得真正富有的途径。从 customers 身上榨取每一分钱是一种 distraction。它最多给你带来 2x returns。而发现某种你可以为他们制造的新东西，则很容易给你带来 10x 或 100x returns。它们是两种不同的看待世界的方式，而 O’Reilly 的方式能赚更多，对于那些能做到的人来说。[5](#fn5)

^pr-bc40dfe6eccd

Generosity 带来 power 的经典例子是 companies open source 它们的 software。它们字面上把 product 送出去，但通过把它送出去，它们既使它成为一个 standard，又让 users 更信任它。这使它 spread，最终它们得到的是一个 much, much bigger pie 的一小块。

如果你不想 go full open source，你可以通过让你的 product extensible 来获得一些好处。那个 continuum 的一端是 app store，但如果你不限制 extensions 或对其收费，你最终可能会得到一个更大的 ecosystem。[6](#fn6)

Extensibility 的极致是让你的 product 可以通过 API 被调用。许多 companies 对此退缩，因为它们不喜欢随之而来的 control 的丧失。它们希望自己的 software 只以它们打算的方式被使用。可能有一些 specialized domains 你希望对此 rigid，但我怀疑这通常是个 mistake。尤其是现在 agents 正在取代 human users。谁知道它们会想做什么？所以宁可偏向于拥有 APIs。尤其是当你是一家 larval startup 且没有什么可失去的时候。

奇怪的是，卖给更早期阶段的 companies 让你更 powerful。Founders 常常对此感到惊讶，因为你卖给 startups 越早，它们的钱越少。但如果你在最初就把它们变成 customers，并且你基于 usage 收费，你的 revenues 将以 startup rates 增长。

^pr-e07318606dfb

这就是为什么 Stripe 特意在尽可能早的时刻签下 companies。对于 payments infrastructure，如果它 ain’t broke，你就不 fix it，而既然 Stripe ain’t broke，安装了它的 companies 从不 churn。而卖给 early stage startups 是如此直接。Founders 很 sophisticated 且决定很快。如果你有最好的 product，你就赢。而如果你做的东西要等到 companies 有 500 人才能卖给它们，你的处境就弱得多。现在你在做 enterprise sales，这要花很久，而且众所周知不是一个最好的 product 会赢的 domain。

当我问一家 startup，一个 customer 必须多大才会买它们的 product 时，我总是希望这个数字会很低。如果不是，我总是问有没有办法 tweak the product 以便它们能更早地卖。理想的是某种它们现在就能为它们的 batchmates [_Collison-install_](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) 的东西。[7](#fn7)

更一般地说，拥有决定快的 customers 让你 powerful。不仅仅是因为速度，还因为决定快的 customers 倾向于根据你有多好来做决定。Startups 通常做出最好的东西（如果你既小又平庸，你怎么可能活下来？），而当 customers 决定快时，做出最好的东西直接导致赚最多的钱。而卖给像 hospitals 和 school districts 这样的 customers 就像在泥里走路。每当我遇到一家卖给那样 customers 的 startup，我都会问有没有办法至少从卖给市场中决定更快的一个 subset 开始。[8](#fn8)

有一种策略类似于尽早获得 customers，那就是尽早获得 data。Rippling 出色地使用了这个 technique。他们的目标始终是既成为处理 employee data 的 applications 的 operating system，又成为在其上运行的大多数 applications。他们并不确切知道这会是什么样子；它必须 evolve；所以他们从写 onboarding software 开始，因为那是 employee data 的生命开始的地方。而由于对他们来说利害关系不止是 onboarding software market，他们的 onboarding software 比它需要的要好得多，并迅速 spread。

Upstream 几乎总是好的，无论是关于 money 还是 user relationship 还是 customer stage 还是 data。

^pr-6eeba5dff663

由于 startups 做出最好的东西，它们在 level playing fields 上最强。它们在由你会描述为 mafia 的 companies 主导的 markets 中最弱。Record labels 是 mafia。PBMs 是 mafia。在这些世界里，你不是靠拥有最好的 product 来赢。事实上，你可能只在 mafia 选择允许你的时间内存在。这并不是说它们无法被击败。它们大概可以被击败，但你必须从侧面进入来做到——通过以某种方式让它们变得 irrelevant，而不是通过 frontal attack。那样你就不必依赖击败它们来成功；那将是你在另一个维度获胜的附带好处。[9](#fn9)

^pr-94d63bff1dfb

通常，当你探索让一家 company 更 powerful 的方式时，你所做的是找到不被其他 companies 拖住的方式。如果你是一个 component supplier，不得不生活在另一家 company 创造的（有时是字面意义上的）box 里，如果你能找到一种方式 own the customer relationship，你就能逃脱这一点。如果你卖给的是又大又官僚的 companies，你可以通过在它们更小、决定更快时卖给它们，或者通过你们自己使用你们的 technology 与它们竞争来逃脱这一点。这个 pattern 如此常见，以至于你可以把它当作一个 heuristic 来生成让一个 idea 更大的方式。当前的 idea 在哪些方面被其他 companies 拖住了？

^pr-9ac98f3ff15d

不过，startups 往往是被它们自己拖住的。我给出的 startup 建议中，有令人惊讶的比例含有 “just” 这个词。_You don’t need to x. Just y._ Startups 的 ideas 被扭曲成死结的一种方式是它们从别的东西 evolve 而来；现在有一部分它们不需要，而它们还没意识到。但尤其是对于非常 early stage 的 startups，idea 复杂的原因往往是 fear。这家 company 在无意识地畏缩，做着比它们本可以做的更不 ambitious 的事情。_Just y_ 往往实际上是 _just stand up straight_。而当它们这么做时，它们高得多。

^pr-9fa72b51ec9f

但是所有这些让 startups 更 powerful 的策略都有一个共同点——或者更准确地说，都必须遵守一个 constraint。它们都必须为 customer 把事情变得更好。你不能仅仅因为自己愿意就 add network effects 或 make the money flow through you 或 go full stack。只有当结果对 customer 更好时，你才能做这些事情。否则你就不会有任何 uptake。

^pr-7ef91aedf607

这些是让 startups 在长期变得 powerful 的策略，但执行这些策略的 startups 在这样做的时候通常还没有任何 power。而事实上，startups 这种最初的 weakness 正是它们总体上对世界有益的原因。新成立的 startups 太 weak，无法把任何事情强加给任何人。它们变得 powerful 的唯一方式是让 customers 的生活变得更好。事实上这个 constraint 如此 rigid，以至于你可以把它反过来运行来产生 ideas。从 customer 的角度来看，完美的世界会是什么样子？如果那个世界中有一个 component 是 startup 可以把自己 transform 成的，那它大概就应该这么做。

^pr-a4fd194367bd

[3](#fnref3). YC 本身就是 network effects 出现在你不会预期有它们的东西中的一个实例。我们并非有意如此，但我们很快意识到那正是我们偶然发现的东西。

[4](#fnref4). 甚至有些时候以亏损出售是值得的。但你这样做时要小心，因为如果你送出太多，你就会失去 customers 通过付钱给你所发送的 signal。如果你的 product 是一个 $10 bill that you sell for $5，你的 growth rate 就没有告诉你任何有用的东西。

[5](#fnref5). O’Reilly 看待世界的方式在 founders 中更常见。当 companies 从制造新产品转向从现有产品中榨取更多 profit 时，往往是因为控制权已从 founders 转移到 hired managers。部分原因是只有 founders 往往有创造新事物的倾向或能力。但也是因为 founders 经历过 weakness。Hired CEOS 把他们所经营的 companies 的 power 视为理所当然，而 founders 记得公司曾经如此 weak，以至于必须 delight users 才能生存的那些日子。

[6](#fnref6). 也许这个方面的 flexibility 将是最终取代 Apple 的关键。有一件事你可以确定，那就是他们会对与他们整合的 hardware 和 software 加以限制。

[7](#fnref7). 如果你从问“我们应该 target 什么规模的 customers？”转向“我们应该在他们生命的哪个时点 acquire customers？”，就会清楚，target 更大的 companies 只是更晚地 target 一个给定的 company。只要你确信 customers 不会 churn，为什么不早早把他们 lock in？当你可以直接卖给 early stage startups 然后与它们一起成长时，为什么要做缓慢、脆弱的 enterprise sales？这种情况正是 YC 强调 growth _rate_ 而非绝对数字的原因。如果你的 growth rate 足够好，绝对数字会自己照顾好自己。而获得最快 growth rate 的方式就是卖给那些成长最快、决定最快的 customers。当你 playing the long game 时，这种看待世界的方式会自然而然地出现。如果你以 quarters 思考，potential customers 似乎有固定的规模。如果你以 decades 思考，你就能看到它们有 trajectories。

[8](#fnref8). 在一个正常的行业里，采用新技术缓慢的 customers 对 startups 来说代表着一个机会。他们越慢，你就越有可能通过 going full stack 获胜。向 hospitals 和 school districts 销售之所以如此 grim，是因为你通常做不到——尽管有一些机会可以绕过 schools，直接去 serving students。

[9](#fnref9). 显然 record labels 和 PBMs 的一个共同点是它们充满了 lawyers。所以这大概是一种识别这类 companies 的方式。

# 235 Making Startups Powerful

September 2026

One of the most useful heuristics I have when doing office hours with startups is to ask: what would make this company more powerful? Asking how the company could make more money is a good heuristic too, but it tends to yield incremental improvements. Whereas thinking about how to make it more powerful will sometimes make it orders of magnitude more valuable.

There are a lot of different variants of this question, depending on the type of company. Is there a way to transform the company from a mere component supplier into the one that owns the relationship with the customer? Or the related question: is there a way to make the money flow through it? It’s always good when money flows through you. [1](#fn1)

Is there a way to create something akin to an app store, where other companies can build upon your product? Then all their efforts to create valuable things make you more valuable too. Ideally this is combined with owning the customer relationship and making the money flow through you. [2](#fn2)

Network effects make companies more powerful, and I almost always think about how to introduce them. I treat it as a kind of challenge to see if there’s a way to get network effects even in things you wouldn’t expect to have them. [3](#fn3) It’s surprising how often it can be done. And when it can, this sometimes transforms the idea completely; what had been a service is now a marketplace. The deluxe version is full app store, but if there’s no more direct way to do it, you can often induce network effects by letting your users share something. For example, if you opt in, we’ll tell you how you’re doing compared to other users. The obvious AI variant is to let your users opt in to training your model on their interactions with it. Many will resist that, but if some don’t, the model they get to use will outperform the vanilla one used by the others.

Often you can induce network effects by generalizing the idea, which makes the startup doubly more powerful. For example, if I were talking to a startup building a way for agents to pay for things, the first question I’d ask is whether the agents could also pay one another. If they can do that, you become a marketplace. And being a marketplace is so valuable that if it wasn’t immediately obvious what agents could pay one another for, it would be worth spending a lot of time trying to think of something. If you could, it might be worth tilting the whole company toward that, and if necessary even becoming a market maker to get it rolling.

These hypothetical transformations of the original idea don’t always yield anything promising. Far from it. But they’re always worth considering; if nothing else, trying to transform an idea helps you understand it better.

There are certain kinds of thinking where ideas start to seem almost physical. Most programmers have probably experienced it. Manipulating startup ideas feels this way too. One transformation that feels especially physical is the strategy of going full stack: instead of selling your technology to companies doing x, you use the technology yourself to do x in competition with them. You can almost see the idea stretch as it engulfs what had been the customer. And now that you own the outside surface, the shape of that probably changes too.

There’s a variant of going full stack where you eat your way gradually through the customer by doing all their hardest work for them. In the limit case, you’re doing all the brainwork and they’re just running errands for you. At which point, as in the full stack case, the real customer is their customer; the initial customer is now just a kind of hand puppet.

Another thing I’m always looking for is tails that could wag the dog. The history of startups is full of these. Paypal started out doing security for hand-held devices. They created Paypal as a demo of their security software. But then eBay sellers started using it to take payments, and after a couple months the founders acknowledged that this was the business they were now in, even though they hadn’t meant to be. So whenever founders build something peripheral to the main product I always ask: could this be the real product?

It’s exciting when you notice users “misusing” your product to do something you hadn’t intended. This means there’s something they want so desperately that they’ll not only use any solution you offer, but even use things that aren’t meant to be solutions. When you see something like that, don’t be annoyed that your users are using your product wrong; listen for the message they’re sending, because it could be valuable.

The reason Paypal grew so fast was that it helped users make money. Few things make you more powerful than that. When you help users make money, they’re (a) quick to adopt your product and (b) will pay a lot for it. So your revenues grow doubly fast. Most of the most successful companies we’ve funded help their users to make money, as does YC itself.

Playing the long game makes you more powerful, because most other people you encounter won’t be. Most startups competing with you will be run by opportunists hoping to be acquired. Most big companies you deal with will be run by executives who don’t expect to be there for more than a few years and are only thinking about this quarter’s numbers. So tradeoffs that only pay off in 10 years will usually be underpriced. The classic one is to offer great terms in order to acquire users. I generally advise startups to sell as cheaply as they need to at first; get all the users, then worry about your margins. But there are usually also deeper, structural ways to play the long game. [4](#fn4)

Being generous makes you more powerful. As Tim O’Reilly said, you should create more value than you capture. Many hard-headed business types would write this off as idealistic hippy stuff, but in fact this is the route to becoming really rich. Squeezing every last penny out of customers is a distraction. It gets you 2x returns at most. Whereas discovering some new thing you could make for them could easily get you 10x or 100x returns. They’re two different ways of looking at the world, and the O’Reilly way makes more, for those who can do it. [5](#fn5)

The classic example of generosity leading to power is when companies open source their software. They literally give away the product, but by giving it away they both make it a standard and make users trust it more. That makes it spread, and in the end they end up with a small piece of a much, much bigger pie.

If you don’t want to go full open source, you can get some of the benefits by making your product extensible. One end of that continuum is the app store, but if you don’t restrict or charge for extensions you may ultimately end up with a bigger ecosystem. [6](#fn6)

The ultimate in extensibility is to let your product be called via an API. Many companies shrink from that because they dislike the loss of control that comes with it. They want their software to be used only in the way they intended. There may be some specialized domains where you want to be rigid about this, but I suspect it’s usually a mistake. Especially now that agents are replacing human users. Who knows what they’ll want to do? So err on the side of having APIs. Especially when you’re a larval startup and have nothing to lose.

Strangely enough, selling to earlier stage companies makes you more powerful. Founders are often surprised by this, because the earlier you sell to startups, the less money they have. But if you get them as customers at the very beginning and you charge based on usage, your revenues will grow at startup rates.

This is why Stripe makes a point of signing up companies at the first possible moment. With payments infrastructure, if it ain’t broke, you don’t fix it, and since Stripe ain’t broke, companies that install it never churn. And selling to early stage startups is so straightforward. The founders are sophisticated and decide quickly. If you have the best product, you win. Whereas if you’re making something you can’t sell to companies till they have 500 people, you’re in a much weaker position. Now you’re doing enterprise sales, which takes forever and is notoriously not a domain where the best product wins.

When I ask a startup how big a customer has to be before they’ll buy their product, I always hope the number will be low. And if it’s not, I always ask if there’s some way to tweak the product so they can sell it earlier. The ideal is something they can [_Collison-install_](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) right now for their batchmates. [7](#fn7)

More generally, having customers who decide fast makes you powerful. Not just because of the speed, but because customers who decide fast tend to decide based on how good you are. Startups usually make the best stuff (if you were both small and mediocre, how could you even survive?) and when customers decide fast, making the best stuff leads straight to making the most money. Whereas selling to customers like hospitals and school districts is like walking through mud. Whenever I meet a startup selling to customers like that, I ask if there’s some way they could at least start by selling to a subset of the market that decides faster. [8](#fn8)

There’s a strategy similar to getting the customers early, which is to get the data early. Rippling used this technique brilliantly. Their goal was always to be both the operating system for applications dealing with employee data and most of the applications running on it. They didn’t know exactly what this would look like; it would have to evolve; so they started by writing onboarding software, because that’s where the life of employee data begins. And since there was more at stake for them than just the onboarding software market, their onboarding software was way better than it needed to be, and spread rapidly.

Upstream is almost always good, whether it’s with money or user relationship or customer stage or data.

Since startups make the best stuff, they’re strongest on level playing fields. They’re weakest in markets dominated by companies you’d describe as mafia. Record labels are mafia. PBMs are mafia. In these worlds you don’t win by having the best product. Indeed you may only even exist for as long as the mafia chooses to allow you to. Which is not to say they can’t be defeated. They probably can be, but you’d have to do it by coming in from the side – by somehow making them irrelevant, rather than by frontal attack. Then you wouldn’t depend on beating them to succeed; it would be an ancillary benefit of winning in another dimension. [9](#fn9)

Often what you’re doing when you explore ways to make a company more powerful is finding ways not to be held back by other companies. If you’re a component supplier and have to live in a (sometimes literal) box created by another company, you can escape that if you can find a way to own the customer relationship. If you’re selling to companies that are big and bureaucratic, you can escape that by selling to them when they’re smaller and decide faster, or by using your technology yourselves to compete with them. This pattern is so common that you can use it as a heuristic for generating ways to make an idea bigger. In what ways is the current idea being held back by other companies?

Often as not, though, startups are being held back by themselves. A surprising percentage of the advice I give to startups has the word “just” in it. _You don’t need to x. Just y._ One way startups’ ideas get twisted into knots is by evolving from something else; there’s now a part they don’t need, and they don’t realize it yet. But with very early stage startups especially, the reason the idea is complicated is often fear. The company is unconsciously cowering by doing something less ambitious than they could. _Just y_ is often, in effect, _just stand up straight_. And when they do they’re much taller.

But all these strategies for making startups more powerful have one thing in common – or more precisely, have to obey one constraint. They all have to make things better for the customer. You can’t add network effects or make the money flow through you or go full stack just because you’d like to. You can only do these things when the result is better for the customer. Otherwise you won’t have any uptake.

These are strategies for making startups powerful in the long term, but startups that execute them don’t usually have any power at the point when they do. And indeed this initial weakness of startups is why, on the whole, they’re good for the world. Newly founded startups are too weak to force anything on anyone. The only way they can become powerful is to make customers’ lives better. In fact this constraint is so rigid that you can run it backwards to generate ideas. What would the perfect world look like, from the customer’s point of view? If there’s a component of that world that the startup could transform itself into, it probably should.
