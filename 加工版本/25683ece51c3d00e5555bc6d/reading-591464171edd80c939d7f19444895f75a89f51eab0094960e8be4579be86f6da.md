# 关于 short-termism 的 hypothesis

---

## 正文

firms 会 invest 于 long term R&D 吗？

它们会，对吧？这样的例子并不少见。就在最近，我们看到 Intel 将向 Quantum Computing invest [50 million dollars](http://newsroom.intel.com/community/intel_newsroom/blog/2015/09/03/intel-invests-us50-million-to-advance-quantum-computing)（另见 Intel 的 CEO 所写的这篇[简短说明](http://www.intel.com/newsroom/archive/promise_of_quantum_computing.pdf)）。它们并不指望立即取得 results：

> 今天，Intel Corporation 宣布与 Delft University of Technology 及 TNO，即 Dutch Organisation for Applied Research，建立为期 10-year 的 collaborative relationship，以 accelerate quantum computing 的 advancements。为实现这一目标，Intel 将 invest US\$50 million，并将在 on-site 和 Intel 提供 significant engineering resources，以及 technical support。
> 
> $$...$$
> 
> 尽管已有 significant progress，quantum computing 仍至少需要十几年才能实现。因此，现在就需要 practical and theoretical research，我们也因此将与 industry partners 合作。

那么，有意思的问题就是：哪些种类的 corporations 会 invest 于 R&D，它们又是在怎样的 timescales 上这样做。例如，可以认为，Intel 在 microprocessor 这块蛋糕中占据的 large slice，使它们能够进行这种 long term investment。

在继续之前，我们必须明确 [R and D](http://www.nsf.gov/statistics/randdef/fedgov.cfm) 之间的 distinction：R 是 research，它可以是 applied（Focused 于 solving 某个 particular problem），也可以是 basic（并未考虑任何 particular problem）；D 则是 development（去 design 某种 meets 一些 requirements 的东西）。我想，说 applied research 和 development 并不存在问题，并不会令人意外。corporations 在 R&D 上的 spending 大部分都用于 D。其次有一些 applied research，而 basic research 则 relatively little。因此，下文我们将关注 research，而不是 development。

话虽如此，有些人由于 corporate executives 所面对的 incentives，而怀疑是否存在对 long term basic research activity 的 significant commitment。不过，即便如此，这也不会是一个大问题：幸运的是，corporations 并非 innovation 这场游戏中的唯一参与者。但这仍会令人担忧，因为它们掌握着 large amounts 的 resources，并拥有将自身 research convert 为 products 的 technical means。

## 关于 short-termism 的 hypothesis

反对存在 significant long term investment 的 argument 是，managers 非常在意自己的 short term results，因为 investors 也在意这些，即便 investors 如果更有耐心，company 本可在 long run achieve better results。只有当 rate of return relatively independent of firm investment 时（由于 monopoly），firm 才可能（但不一定会）invest。那些伟大的 corporate laboratories 的衰落，例如著名的 Bell Labs，或许可算是这一 theory 的某种 evidence（这些 Labs 仍然存在，但与其辉煌的过去相比，[已如一道影子](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs)）。我们可以在 Mazzucato 的书中找到类似的说法：

> 最近的一项 MIT multidisciplinary study 考察了 US innovation system 的 strengths 和 weaknesses，以及 America 的 manufacturing 出现 relative decline 的 causes。这项 study 力图理解，为什么 promising innovations 的 development 在 reaching commercial scale 之前就 stalling，或干脆 moving abroad。study 揭示的 reasons 之一是，large R&D centres——如 Bell Labs、Xerox PARC 和 Alcoa Research Lab——在 big corporations 中已成为过去；它们大多已经消失。Long-term basic and applied research 已不再是 ‘Big Business’ 的 strategy 的一部分，因为 corporate R&D 如今 focuses 于 short-term needs。这项 study 认为，‘\[US\] industrial ecosystem 中已经出现 large holes’：在三十年代，像 DuPont 这样的 corporation 不仅会用十年时间 invested 于最终带来 nylon 的 fundamental research，而且一旦 lab 拥有 promising product，DuPont 也有 capital 和 plants 将其投入 production。如今，innovation 更有可能 emerge 于 small spin-offs，或来自 university 或 government labs，那么 scale-up resources 从何而来？在 scale-up 的每个 critical stages：prototyping、pilot production、demonstration and test、early-manufacturing、full-scale commercialization，所需 funding 又有多么 available？当 scale-up 主要通过对 adolescent start-ups 的 merger and acquisition 获得 funded，而 acquiring firms 又是 foreign 时，American economy 如何 benefit？(PIE Commission 2013, 26)

这一 theory 带有 monopoly proviso，因此 Intel 的 quantum computing investment 不能算数。一种回应是，Intel 并非 monopoly，而是 competitive market 的一部分，因为它并不像典型教科书所描述的那样 behave。其他 similar performing products 与 Intel 的 products 相比，也并没有明显 cheap 多少。对此的反驳则是，market inertia 确保 Intel 在 OEMs 不再向其索要更多 microprocessors 之前，有充足时间察觉情况是否出了问题。Google 的 Google X 也会受到同样的 critique。因此，如果我们想继续举例，就需要一家在其 market 蛋糕中并未占据 big share 的 corporation。这里的例子或许可以是 [IBM](http://www.research.ibm.com/) 或 [Toyota](https://www.google.com/search?q=toyota+central+r%26D+labs+basic+science&oq=TOYOTA+CENTRAL+R%26d+LABS+BASIC+SCIENCE&aqs=chrome.0.69i59.5230j0j1&sourceid=chrome&es_sm=122&ie=UTF-8)。[这里](http://www.nature.com/news/basic-science-finds-corporate-refuge-1.15124)还有一些 evidence。

乍看之下，这个 argument 似乎 plausible，而且正如我们将看到的，确实有一些 evidence 可用于 support 它。但令人起疑的是，它 posits 了一种正在进行 long term research、且 corporation 自身从中 benefits 的情况，并将其与假定的当前情况 compares：investors 拥有 suboptimal investment horizon，因而得到 lower long term return，以及更少的 innovation。myopia 的说法由此而来。这第一个 thesis 要么将 investors 描述为 irrational，要么将它们描述为某种 prisoner dilemma 情境中的 rational agents：其中 cooperative solution 是 cooperate（每个人都做 research），而 Nash equilibrium 则是 defect（没有人做 research）。

第一个 thesis 是两者中较不 plausible 的：如果有哪个 market 像教科书中的 markets 那样运作，那就是 stock market。假定 Efficient Market Hypothesis holds（其 strength 的 degree 尚有争议），如果你掌握了一条可用于使自己更富有的 information（你知道，如果你在 R&D 上 invest 得比当前更多，future returns 就会 increase），那么其他人很可能已经想到同样的事，而 price 也早已 corrected。

第二个 thesis 看起来只是通常的 market failure argument（将在今后的某篇文章中讨论）。

## market myopia theory：市场短视理论

那么，让我们关注第一个 thesis。要使它成立，就必须证明：corporate investment 在 research 上一直在 falling；corporations 没有用其他东西填补这一 gap（比如 startups 的 acquisitions）；上述 behaviour 是由 managerial short termism 所 motivated；而后者又是由 investor short termism 所 propelled。

首先，financial markets 是 myopic 的吗？

就像《Soylent Green》一样，financial markets 是由人组成的。而人是各不相同的。在 spectrum 的一端，我们有 day trader（试图 predict short term prices），另一端则有 value investor（试图 predict underlying long term value）。第一类 investors determine market 中的 short term prices，第二类则 determine long run 的 prices。或者，正如 investor Ben Graham 过去常说的：“在 short run 中，market 是一台 _voting_ machine，但在 long run 中，它是一台 _weighing_ machine。”用 control theoretical terms 来说，short term investors 是 [PID controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PID_controller) 中的一个 derivative term，而 long term investors 则是 proportional 和 integral terms。两者都有各自的作用。

因此，概括而言，如果以下情况成立，corporations 就是 myopic 的：

1.  Corporate research spending 出现 decreases
2.  而且上述 spending 是 lower than 一个 optimal amount 的
3.  原因在于
    1.  要么是 market-induced short termism
        1.  Investors 想要 short term rewards
        2.  Managers 为了 satisfy investors 而 pursue short term objectives
        3.  为此，他们 slash R&D
    2.  要么是 market failure
        1.  Corporations 知道自己需要某些 research
        2.  但 research 可以被其他人 appropriated
        3.  所以他们不做 research，或者做的 research 是 less research than an optimal amount
4.  而且没有其他 research spending 的 sources 来 make up for 这一 decrease

## Myopia 或 non-corporate research 的兴起，这两种 explanations

我们接下来要讨论的一篇 paper 提供了支持第一点的 evidence：_Killing the Golden Goose? corporate research 的 changing nature，1980-2007_（[Arora, Belenzon, and Patacconi 2015](https://faculty.fuqua.duke.edu/%7Esb135/bio/Science%201%2091%2015.pdf)）

这篇 article confirms 了 corporate research 的 decrease，并对其 causes 作出推测：也许是 short termism（引用了 Lazonick，他是 short-termist hypothesis 的推动者之一），也可能是 corporations 做 innovations 的方式发生了变化（引用了 The Economist 的一篇 article，谈论 high tech startups 的兴起，以及它们后来被 corporations acquired）。

他们首先关注 company scientists 在 scientific journals 上的 scientific publications，并发现这些 publications 已经 decreased；他们根据一系列考量 infer，companies 对 science 的 value 更低了：它们 publish 得更少，publications 的 stocks 所带来的 market value premium 更小，M&A 中为 publications 支付的 acquisition premium 更小，而且 innovative firms 被 acquired 后，它们的 invest 更少。然而，patenting 已经 increased，patents 的 implied value 则保持 stable。他们说，除 biotechnology 外，这些 finds 出现在所有 industries 中。

他们说，这是一个 long term trend 的一部分：corporations 现在更依赖 external innovations，重视 science 下的金蛋，却不重视那只下金蛋的鹅本身。

有没有可能，它们只是为了 protect 自己的 research 而 publishing 得更少？他们说，不太可能。如果真是这样，applied journals 中的 publication 应当比 basic science journals 中的 publications fallen 得更多，但事实并非如此；而且，前面概述的那些考量也不会因此被 invalidated。

有没有可能，它们在 science 上 invest 得更少，是因为 science 不那么 useful 了？他们认为不是：如果我们用 patent citation 来 measure scientific usefulness，近期的 patents 正在被 cited，而且 high scientific capability firms 的 cited 更多。

会不会是 market failure？他们说，原则上不是：market failure 的 causes 并没有 intensified。而且，他们的 findings 同时出现在 US 和 Europe，因此 legislative changes 不太可能是 cause。

他们说，cause 在于 firms 在 internally develop 新 products 和 processes 方面面临 reduces incentives。他们将其 attribute 于 increased competition，以及这样一种 conjecture：diversified firms 比 narrow scope 的 firms 能更好地 use basic science。narrow 和 broad focused firms 的 publication count，以及 publications 的 stock market value，为此提供了支持。

> 这种从 science 中的 withdrawal，伴随着日益发展的 division of innovative labor；其中 large firms 专注于 development 和 commercialization，而让 universities 和 small firms 去 generate 新 ideas。然而，我们的 results 也 suggest，small science-intensive firms 不能仅仅依靠 generating scientific knowledge，并指望被 acquired。由于 pure scientific capability 所获得的 rewards 已经 diminished，这些 firms 也必须 invest 于寻找其 ideas 的 tangible commercial application。如果 universities 和 small firms 没有足够的能力来 undertake 这种更 applied 的 research，就可能产生 inefficienies。

然而，我们不应排除 universities、venture capitalists 和 corporations 之间形成 strong alliances 的可能性。它们的结合能够做到任何一方单独都做不到的事情。关于 financing innovation 的多种方式，可以在 [Bakker 2013](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004873331300156X) 中找到一篇 review。

这篇关于 lithium-ion rechargeable batteries 的[这篇近期 article](http://mappingignorance.org/2015/09/02/recent-advances-in-lithium-ion-rechargeable-batteries/) 展示了这种 collaborative approach 的一个 example：其中引用的一篇 article 包括来自 universities 和 Toyota 的 researchers，另一篇则由 universities 和 Samsung 的 researchers 撰写。如果 universities 中正在发生有意思的 innovation，corporations 就有 strong incentives 与 academic world 保持联系，以利用这些 innovation。

回到这篇 article，

> **几个 factors 促成了 small firms 日益增长的 importance，尤其是在 science-intensive sectors 中。其中一个是 universities 和其他 research institutions 在 science 的 commercialization 中发挥了更 prominent 的作用。1980 Bayh-Dole Act 鼓励 universities 更积极地 license 和 commercialize 自己的 discoveries，而 scientists 也越来越觉得创办自己的 businesses 很有吸引力。**Genentech 是一家由 biochemist、Nobel Prize winner Herbert Boyer 和 venture capitalist Robert A. Swanson 创办的 biotechnology company；它的成功展示了这种 strategy 所可能带来的巨大 rewards。此外，start-ups 的 high-powered incentives 很难在 large、established firms 中 replicate，因为那里的 bureaucracy、politics 和过去遗留的负担往往会 thwart radical change (Schumpeter, 1942; Leibenstein, 1966; Christensen and Bower, 1996; Sull et al., 1997)。institutional 和 legal environment 的 Changes 对这些 trends 起到了补充作用。Startups 可以从 venture capitalists、SBIR 和其他 government programs 获得 financing (Kortum and Lerner, 2000; Lerner, 1999; Mazzucato, 2013)。从 early 1980s 开始，Intellectual property rights 得到了 significantly strengthened，先是在 U.S.，随后是在其他国家 (Jaffe and Lerner, 2004; Guellec and van Pottelsberghe de la Potterie, 2007)。**这些 developments 推动了一种新的 division of labor：small start-ups specialize in scientific research，而 larger、更 established 的 firms specialize in product development and commercialization** (Arora and Gambardella, 1994)

注意，在这篇 paper 的另一个 section (6.2) 中，他们说了一些似乎与此 contradict 的内容：也就是我们已经提到的，这种 tendency 也存在于 European 的情况。对此最好的 interpretation 可能是，Europe 已经 implemented 了与 US 类似的 policy changes。这些 changes，例如美国的 [Bayh-Dole Act](http://www.ipwatchdog.com/2013/09/15/bayh-dole-a-success-beyond-wildest-dreams/id=45171/)，使 firms 和 researchers 能够对使用 federal funds 完成的 research claim ownership，从而更容易 commercially exploit 这些 research。

在同一个 section 中，这篇 paper rules out 了 market short-termism hypothesis，指出 private（例如 Koch Industries）和 public（例如 Google）firms 的 research funding 的 rate of decline 是 similar 的。

概括这篇 paper：firms 如今的 scope 更 narrower，而 competition 可能 reduce innovation 的 payoff（Schumpeterians 可以欢呼了，尽管他们在另一个 section 中说，这方面的 evidence 是 mixed 的）。但是，他们说，如果 science 是 useful 的，successful firms 就会 invest 于它。因此，对此有两种 explanations：

> 一种可能性是，当 firm profitability declines 时（由于 global competition），markets 和 managers 都会变得更 short-term oriented。另一种可能性是，对 internal science 的 investment 是早已逝去的过去所遗留的 inefficient relic；当时，American 和 European 的 big firms 还负担得起对 resources 的“浪费”。按照这种看法，large firms 是 research 的 inefficient performers，需要受到推动，将 research outsource 给 smaller、更加 nimble 的 partners。**对于 large firms 的 publication output 的 decline，我们发现其他可能的 explanations 得到的支持很少**。

因此，myopic hypothesis 的第一部分，即 reduced corporate research，似乎是真的。但这篇 paper 也对 short-termist _market_ hypothesis 提出了一些 doubt，同时又将其保留为这种 reduced investment 的一种 explanation。因此，如果我们能够 rule out short termist _market_ hypothesis，那么他们提出的另一个 hypothesis（research performance 的 composition 发生 change，转向与 corporations 建立 partnership 的 university 和 startups）或许就是正确的。

## 关于 market myopia 的更多讨论

我们现在来看 _Long-Term Investing：什么 determines Investment Horizon？_（[Warren 2014](http://www.ifswf.org/pst/Long-Term-Investing-Part-1.pdf)）。他 reviews 了关于 investor 的 investment horizon 的 literature，并评论了现有的不同 types 的 investors：

> **investor short-termism 与 corporate myopia 之间的 link 并不 clear cut——虽然有一些 evidence 支持这种 link，但它绝非 compelling**。Laverty (1996) examines 了关于 short-termism 是否存在的 arguments，并指出：(1) 没有关于 flawed short-term oriented management practices 的 clear evidence；(2) 关于 stock market myopia encourages corporate short-termism，只有 mixed evidence，例如他提到，一些 papers 发现了 stock market 对 long-term investment 的 positive reactions；以及 (3) 关于 ‘fluid capital’ 对 corporate behaviour 的所谓 influence，缺乏 empirical support。
> 
> Marston and Craven (1998) 对 company management 所做的 survey 的 Results，也对 institutional investors 在多大程度上以 short-term 为 focus 提出了质疑。虽然他们的 survey 发现，人们认为 sell-side (broking) analysts focused 于 short-term，但 company management 并不认为 buy-side analysts 和 fund managers 也是如此。当被问及 buy-side 是否过于关注 short-term profit opportunities 时，只有 21% 同意，而 53% 不同意。

话虽如此，Warren 确实认为 short-termism 超过了 optimal 的程度，但这为 longer term investors 创造了 opportunity。

另一篇 paper 是 _Institutional Investors 与 Equity Returns：Short-term Institutions 是否 Better Informed？_（[Yan and Zhang 2014](http://cdn1.valuewalk.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/horizon_RFS.pdf)）。他们 discard 了 short-termist hypothesis。他们 analyse 了 short 和 long term horizon 的 institutional investing，并 conclude，short term investing 也会向 market adds information。

> 对我们 results 的一种 alternative explanation 是，short-term institutional investors 向 managers 施压，要求他们以 long-run firm value 为代价 maximize short-run profits \[即 ‘‘short-term pressure’’ hypothesis；例如参见 Porter (1992)、Bushee (1998, 2001)\]。具体而言，Bushee (1998) 发现了这样的 evidence：具有 higher transient institutional ownership 的 firms 更有可能在 R&D 等 long-term、intangible projects 上 underinvest，以 reverse earnings decline。**short-term pressure hypothesis 或许有助于 explain short-term institutional ownership 和 trading 的 short-run predictive ability，但它也 predicts，由 short-term institutions held 或 traded 的 stocks 会出现 long-run price reversal。为了 test 这一 prediction，我们 examine 了 institutional ownership 和 trading 与最长三年内的 future stock returns 之间的 relation。对于由 short-term institutional investors held 或最近 traded 的 stocks，我们没有发现 long-run price reversal 的 evidence，这 suggesting，我们的 results 无法由 short term pressure hypothesis 来 explained**。我们也没有发现 evidence 表明，long-term institutional investors 的 holdings 或 trading 中有任何一项 predicts long-run stock returns。这一 result 与 long-term institutions 对 long-run returns 是 better informed 的 hypothesis 是 inconsistent with。$$...$$
> 
> 本文的 results 对 identifying 和 attracting ‘‘right’’ investors 这一问题具有 implications。近期 studies \[例如 Gaspar, Massa, and Matos (2005)\] suggest，short-term institutional investors 是 weak monitors。此外，short-term institutions 向 managers 施压，使其采取 short-term focus，从而 hurting long-run firm value \[例如 Bushee (1998)\]。综合而言，这些 results suggest，对 firms 来说，target 和 attract long-term institutional investors 可能是 beneficial 的 \[Porter (1992)、Brancato (1997) 和 Bushee (2004)\]。**我们 argue，firms 也应考虑 institutional ownership 的 informational aspect。我们的 results indicate，short-term institutions 在 stock market 中发挥着 significant informational role。**由于 more informative prices 有助于更好的 financing 和 investment decisions，并可能 reduce cost of capital，我们的 results 对一种仅试图 attract long-term investors 的 strategy 的 benefit 提出了质疑。

因此，即使 short-term investors 能够 induce corporate short-termism，这也未必是坏事，因为它可能反映了 underlying information。在某个给定时点，funding short 与 long term investment 之间存在需要作出的 tradeoff，因此在某些时点 reducing long term investment 会是一种 efficient strategy。虽然一些 studies（[Bushee 1998](http://www.jstor.org/stable/248542)）表明，short-termist investors 会 induce R&D 的 cuts，但这些观点已经受到近期 studies 的质疑（参见 [Wahal and McConnell 2000](http://www.krannert.purdue.edu/faculty/mcconnell/publications/PublicationsPDFS/Do...Myopia%20JCF%20Sept2000%20V6%20N3%20307-329.pdf)）。

> 较近的一项 contribution 来自 Bushee 1998，他 examines R&D spending 的 reductions，以 determine，具有 higher institutional ownership 的 firms 是否更有可能为应对 earnings decline 而 cut R&D spending。他特别关注的是，具有 high institutional ownership 的 firms 的 managers，是否更有可能或更不可能通过 cut R&D spending 来 manage short-term earnings。他的 sample 包含在某一年经历 earnings decline、而这一 decline 随后可以通过 R&D expenditures 的 cut 来 reversed 的 firms。他 estimates 了一个 logistic regression，其中，如果 firm cut 了 R&D expenditures，dependent variable 就是 one，否则就是 zero，而 key independent variable 是 institutional investors 所 owned 的 shares 的 fraction。**遗憾的是，对他关于 institutionally induced myopia 的 results 的 interpretation 也存在问题。Bushee 的 analysis 的一个 implicit assumption 是，firm 当前的 R&D expenditures 的 level 是 ‘‘optimal’’ 的**。然而，Suppose causation 从 institutional ownership 指向 R&D spending，使得具有 high institutional ownership 的 firms 有着 low R&D expenditures。如果如此，那么具有 high institutional ownership 的 firms 在应对 earnings decline 时，就会有 ‘‘更少的空间’’ 来 cut expenditures。在这种情况下，Bushee 所 estimated 的那类 logistic regression 将 indicate，具有 higher institutional ownership 的 firms 更不可能 cut R&D expenditures，即便 institutional investors 可能普遍 depress corporate expenditures 中用于 R&D 的部分。**因此，虽然 Bushee 的 tests 能够说明 earnings management 的问题，但它们并未 address institutional ownership 是否 leads to 对 PP&E 和 R&D 等具有 long-term payoffs 的 projects 的 lower spending 这一问题。**

他们 conclude：

> 利用来自 1988 至 1994 年的约 2500 家 firms 的 sample，我们 show，PP&E 和 R&D 的 firm-level expenditures 与这些 firms 中 institutional investors 的 share ownership 的 level 及 trading activity 是 positively related 的。**我们的 results 对这样一种观点提出了质疑：相较于 individual investors 的 direct share ownership 可能 induced 的任何 myopia，institutions 会使 corporate managers behave myopically**。这些 results 与 institutional investors 在 firms 和 less patient individual investors 之间充当 buffer 的 perspective 是 consistent with。这一 buffer 的后果之一，是它使 managers 能够拥有比没有它时更长的 investment horizons。

此外，

> 在 interpreting 我们的 results 时，至少有两点 caveats 需要说明。首先，我们并未 address US firms 相较于世界其他地区的 firms 是否 systematically underinvest 这一问题。不过，Hall and Weinstein 1996 和 Lee 1997 对这一问题进行了仔细的 examined。**这些 authors 没有发现 evidence 表明，US firms 的 managers 相较于 Japanese 和 German firms 的 managers 是 myopic 的**。其次，人们可以批评我们只 examined 了 ‘‘最容易’’ analyze 的 investment categories，即 PP&E 和 R&D，却忽视了那些 underinvestment 问题可能最 severe 的 categories。Froot et al. 1992 argue，由 myopic investors 所 engendered 的那类 underinvestment，更有可能体现于 difficult to observe investments，例如 human resources 的 development，以及 customer loyalty 的 development 过程中产生的 costs。如果这一 argument 是成立的，那么我们就没有抓住要害。**因此，我们只能 claim，我们找不到 institutional investors exacerbate PP&E 和 R&D 的 underinvestment 的 evidence**，而这很可能不同于 institutional investors 不会 exacerbate 具有 long-term payoffs 的 projects 的 underinvestment 这一 conclusion。考虑到这些 caveats，data 并不 indicate institutional investors 会使 managers underinvest。恰恰相反，institutional investors 的 share ownership 似乎使 US corporate managers 能够比 individual investors 的 direct share ownership 情况下，对具有 long-term payoffs 的 projects invest 更多。

## startup 的崛起？

所以，这里似乎有一个问题（对 short-termist hypothesis 而言）：corporate research 确实正在 decreasing，但这显然并非由 induced short-termism 所 driven。如果确实如此，那么 startup-as-innovators 的观点就是 plausible 的。我们再来考察一篇 paper，Hirschey、Skiba 和 Wintoki (2012) 的 _[U.S. firms 从 1976 到 2010 的 corporate R&D spending 的 size、concentration 和 evolution：Evidence 与 implications](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0929119912000260)_。先介绍一些背景：

> 我们的 sample 由从 1976 到 2010 这 thirty-five-year 期间的所有 publicly traded firms 组成。我们的 analysis 揭示，在整个 economy 中，corporate R&D spending 的 size、distribution 和 economic importance 都发生了 significant change。R&D spending 一直在稳步从 government 向 business sector transfer。Corporations 目前 account for U.S. R&D expenditures 的大约 two thirds。Corporate R&D spending 的 size 大约是 print and broadcast media advertising 的 twice；约为 corporate spending 在 property, plant and equipment（capital expenditures）上的 one-half。**Median R&D spending 的 grown 速度几乎是 average spending 的 twice。这意味着，与 largest firms 相比，medium-size firms 对 R&D spending 的 growth 作出了更多 contributed**，尽管 largest spenders 仍然 dominate United States 的 R&D spending。至于 advertising expenditures，trend 则相反。自 1976 以来，large firms 的 Advertising 变得更加 concentrated。R&D spending 在所有 industries 之间远非 uniform。从 aggregate basis 来看，R&D 的 distribution 比 advertising 和 capital expenditures 的 distribution 更加 skewed；少数 high-tech sectors account for R&D activity 中压倒性的 share。不过，我们的 analysis 所揭示的一个重要事实是，R&D spending 的 industry concentration 随时间一直保持 stable。例如在 1976，有 9 个 industries reported zero R&D expenditures。到了 2010，这 9 个中的 4 个仍然 reported 没有 R&D spending，而且这 nine 个之中没有任何一个 reported 的 aggregate R&D spending greater than total sales 的 one-hundredth of one percent。R&D spending 和 R&D intensity（R&D 作为 sales revenue 的 percentage）在 transportation equipment、tobacco products、chemicals and allied products、petroleum refining、industrial machinery and computer equipment、electronic equipment、business services，以及 measuring instruments、photography、watches 等 industry groups 中是 highest 的。在 retailing（general merchandise stores、food stores、building materials、hardware and garden）、health services、bars and restaurants、construction 和 transportation services 等 big sectors 中的 large corporations，几乎不开展或完全不开展 R&D。尽管如此，在存在 significant R&D spending 的 industries 内，report R&D 的 firms 的 number 在 1976 到 2010 之间 increased significantly。在 1976，所有 firms 中的 45% reported R&D，到 2010 这一比例 increased 到了 53%。$$...$$
> 
> 我们的 analysis 也为过去二十年里 managerial myopia 是否对 U.S. 的 aggregate R&D spending 产生了 effect 这一 debate 作出了贡献。在 1990s 初期，一些 academics（例如 Jacobs, 1991; Porter, 1992）依据 business press 中流行的报道和轶事，提出 United States 的 managers 可能会 systematically reduce 对 R&D 的 investments，以 meet short-term earnings benchmarks，而以 value maximization 和 long-term growth 为代价。**尽管关于 managerial myopia 的任何 cross sectional variation 的 evidence 充其量也是 mixed 的（例如 Bange and DeBondt, 1998; Lundstrum, 2002; Wahal and McConnell, 2000），U.S. firms 可能会 systematically under-invest 于 R&D 的看法在 business press 中依然流行（例如参见 Mandel, 2009）。**

也许 startup hypothesis 是成立的？

> 尽管我们没有在 firm level 直接 test managerial myopia hypothesis，但我们提供了与 managerial myopia 这一看法 inconsistent 的 suggestive evidence。例如，在所有 firms 中，aggregate R&D spending 与 corporate earnings 的 ratio 从 1976 的 24.6% increased 到了 2010 的 34.3%。尽管 firms 的 R&D spending 的 ratio 总体上 increased 了，**这一 increase 中的 significant portion 来自 young、R&D-intensive firms 的 R&D spending 的 increase，而这些 firms 的 earnings 很少，甚至是 negative 的**。此外，正如我们已经指出的，我们发现 profitability 对 R&D spending 的 cross-sectional variation 所能 accounts for 的部分极少。综合来看，这些 findings 没有任何一项表明，在 R&D 方面存在任何 systematic economy-wide bias，偏向 managerial myopia。相反，data 表明，large corporations 的 top managers 愿意将 current profits 中很大的 share 投入具有 uncertain payoffs 的 risky long-term R&D investment projects。事实上，我们发现，即使在 2008–2010 financial and economic crises 期间，R&D 与 sales 的 ratio 相比 long-term historical average 也保持 unchanged。
> 
> $$...$$
> 
> 最后，我们发现了 strong evidence，表明在 fundamental innovation 对 survival 至关重要的 industries 中，managers 愿意将 earnings 中 significant 且 growing 的 share commit 于 risky long-term R&D。我们几乎没有发现 evidence 来 support business press 中流行的 managerial myopia 这一看法。即使在 economic recession 期间，Managers 也不会为应对 profitability 的 short declines 而 reduce R&D intensity。

不过，他们 analyse 的是 aggregate R&D，并没有专门讨论 basic research，因此对此必须谨慎看待。关于 young firms 的 finding 可能是支持 substitution（以 startups research 替代 corporate research）hypothesis 的 evidence，但这篇 paper 对此并非完全 conclusive。其 validity 将取决于什么算作 basic，什么算作 applied research。Intel 的 Quantum Computing initiative 听起来像 basic research，而且它 targets 的是遥远未来的某种事物，但其 goal 是 solve 某项给定 tech 的 practical problem，因此从这个意义上说，它算作 applied research。我还需要查一查，哪些东西被算作哪一类，以及 startups 是否正带着相关的 market-ready basic research 成果 entering the market。

## 结论（Conclusion）

总之，鉴于上述内容，market induced myopia theory 可以被 rejected。这个 theory 在一个最接近 textbooklike 的 markets 中 posits 广泛的 irrationality，因此，我们应要求有 strong evidence，才能认为 myopic view 是 true。如果我们接受这一点，那么，按照被 cited 的 papers 中的一篇，我们也需要接受 research 的开展方式已经改变。Corporations 不再像过去那样 investing 于 research，而是已经将其 outsourced。这可能具有这样的 advantage：让 corporations 能够 focus on improving 它们已经在做的事情，并将开展 radical innovation 的任务留给 universities 和 startups，因为后者没有 large firms 所面临的 financial constraints。此外，它们可以帮助 solving R&D market failure problematic。Universities 开展 research 不是为了 sell 它，而是将其视为目的本身，因此不受这种 market failure 的影响。不过，进一步推进 division of knowledge 会在所涉及的不同 agents 之间 generates coordination problem。我尚未对 non-corporate source 的 research funding 所起的 role 做过 in-depth survey，但鉴于上述讨论，我预计它会 growing，而它们与 corporations 的 partnerships 也会 increasing。有关一些初步信息，请参阅[这篇文章](http://qz.com/465152/dont-believe-those-who-tell-you-american-innovation-is-losing-its-way/)。

鉴于 myopia argument 是针对 financialization 以及 capitalism 近期的变化提出的，而不是针对始终存在的 market failures，如果我们将讨论限定于这种 myopia 观点，那么 myopic argument 就应被 rejected。如果我们 expand 这个 thesis，将 market failure 包括在内，那么，也可能存在 myopia，其含义类似于 prisoner dilemma 中的 prisoners 因 defecting 而表现为 myopic。究竟有多少 market failure，又出现在哪里？未来某个时候，这里会刊出对此的 study。

## 原文

Do firms invest in long term R&D?

They do, right? There are examples around. Just recently we saw that Intel will invest [50 million dollars](http://newsroom.intel.com/community/intel_newsroom/blog/2015/09/03/intel-invests-us50-million-to-advance-quantum-computing) in Quantum Computing (Also, this [brief note](http://www.intel.com/newsroom/archive/promise_of_quantum_computing.pdf) by Intel's CEO). They don't expect to have results immediately:

> Today Intel Corporation announced a 10-year collaborative relationship with the Delft University of Technology and TNO, the Dutch Organisation for Applied Research, to accelerate advancements in quantum computing. To achieve this goal, Intel will invest US$50 million and will provide significant engineering resources both on-site and at Intel, as well as technical support.
> 
> \[...\]
> 
> Despite significant progress, quantum computing will take at least a dozen years to fulfill. That’s why practical and theoretical research is needed now, and why we will work with industry partners.

Then, the interesting question would be what kinds of corporations invest in R&D, and at which timescales they do. It could be argued, for example, that Intel's large slice of the microprocessor's pie allows them to do this kind of long term investment.

Before continuing, we have to make clear the distinction between[R and D](http://www.nsf.gov/statistics/randdef/fedgov.cfm): R is research, and it can be applied (Focused on solving a particular problem) or basic (no particular problem in mind), and D is development (To design something that meets some requirements). I think it won't be surprising to say that there is no problem with applied research and development. Most of the spending on R&D corporations do is in D. Then there is some applied research and relatively little basic research. So in what follows, we will focus on research and not development.

That said, there are people who are doubtful that there exist significant commitment to long term basic research activity because of the incentives faced by corporate executives. This, if true, would not be a massive problem, though: fortunately corporations are not the only players in the innovation game, but it would be a worry, given that they command large amounts of resources, and have the technical means to convert their research into products.

## The short-termism hypothesis

The argument against the existence of significant long term investment would be that managers care a lot about their short term results, because in turn investors care about that, even if the company could achieve better results in the long run if the investors were more patient. Only if the rate of return were relatively independent of firm investment (Because of a monopoly), then the firm could (but not necessarily will) invest. Evidence of sorts for the theory would be the fall of the great corporate laboratories like the well-known Bell Labs (The Labs are still around, but [as a shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) of its glorious past). We can find something like this in Mazzucato's book:

> A recent MIT multidisciplinary study has looked at the strengths and weaknesses of the US innovation system and the causes of relative decline of manufacturing in America. The study has strived to understand why the development of promising innovations are stalling or simply moving abroad before reaching commercial scale. One of the reasons unveiled by the study is the fact that large R&D centres – like Bell Labs, Xerox PARC and Alcoa Research Lab – have become a thing of the past in big corporations; they have mostly disappeared. Long-term basic and applied research is not part of the strategy of ‘Big Business’ anymore, as corporate R&D now focuses on short-term needs. The study argues that ‘large holes in the \[US\] industrial ecosystem have appeared’: In the thirties, a corporation like DuPont not only invested for a decade in the fundamental research that led to nylon, but once the lab had a promising product, DuPont had the capital and the plants to bring it into production. Today, when innovation is more likely to emerge in small spin-offs or out of university or government labs, where do the scale-up resources come from? How available is the funding needed at each of the critical stages of scale-up: prototyping, pilot production, demonstration and test, early-manufacturing, full-scale commercialization? When scale-up is funded mainly through merger and acquisition of the adolescent start-ups and when the acquiring firms are foreign, how does the American economy benefit? (PIE Commission 2013, 26)

This theory has a monopoly proviso, so the Intel quantum computing investment would not count. A reply would be that Intel is not a monopoly, but part of a competitive market, as it doesn't behave as a typical textbook. Other similar performing products aren't that much noticeably cheap compared to Intel's. A counter reply to this would be that market inertia makes sure that Intel has plenty of time to notice if things are going wrong before OEMs stop asking them for more microprocessors. Google's Google X would receive the same critique. So if we want to play the examples game, we would need a corporation without a big share of its market pie. The example here could then be [IBM](http://www.research.ibm.com/) or [Toyota](https://www.google.com/search?q=toyota+central+r%26D+labs+basic+science&oq=TOYOTA+CENTRAL+R%26d+LABS+BASIC+SCIENCE&aqs=chrome.0.69i59.5230j0j1&sourceid=chrome&es_sm=122&ie=UTF-8). Some more evidence [here](http://www.nature.com/news/basic-science-finds-corporate-refuge-1.15124).

On its face, the argument seems plausible, and as we will see, there are some pieces of evidence that can be used to support it. But what seems fishy about it it's that it posits a situation where long term research is being done, and the corporation itself benefits it from it and compares that to the putative present situation, where investors have a suboptimal investment horizon, leading them to lower long term return, and less innovation. Hence, the myopia. This first thesis either presents the investors are irrational, or as rational agents in the context of some sort of prisoner dilemma, where the cooperative solution would be to cooperate (Everyone does research), but the Nash equilibrium would be to defect (No one does research).

The first thesis is the less plausible of the two: If there is a market that works like textbook markets do, that is the stock market. Given that the Efficient Market Hypothesis (with a disputable degree of strength) holds, if you had a piece of information that could be used to make you richer (That you know that future returns will increase if you invest more in R&D than is currently being invested), then some other has probably thought the same, and the price is already corrected.

The second thesis seems just the usual market failure argument (And will be discussed in some post to come).

## The market myopia theory

Let's focus then on the first thesis. For it to hold, it would have to be proven that corporate investment in research has been falling, that corporations have not filled that gap with other thing (say, acquisitions of startups), that said behaviour is motivated by managerial short termism, and that, in turn, this is propelled by investor short termism.

First, are financial markets myopic?

Like Soylent Green, financial markets are people. And people are diverse. We have on one side of the spectrum the day trader (tries to predict short term prices), and on the other the value investor (tries to predict the underlying long term value). The first kind of investors determine the short term prices in the market, and the second ones determine the long run ones. Or as investor Ben Graham used to say "In the short run, the market is a _voting_ machine but in the long run, it is a _weighing_ machine.". In control theoretical terms, short term investors are a derivative term in a [PID controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PID_controller), and long term investors would be the proportional and integral terms. They both have a role.

So, to sum up, corporations would be myopic if

1.  Corporate research spending decreases
2.  And said spending is lower than an optimal amount
3.  Because
    1.  Either market-induced short termism
        1.  Investors want short term rewards
        2.  Managers pursue short term objectives to satisfy investors
        3.  To do so, they slash R&D
    2.  Or market failure
        1.  Corporations know they need certain research
        2.  But research can be appropriated by others
        3.  So they don't do research, or do less research than an optimal amount
4.  And no other sources of research spending make up for this decrease

## Myopia or the rise of non-corporate research, the two explanations

There is evidence for point one in a paper we will now discuss:_Killing the Golden Goose? The changing nature of corporate research, 1980-2007_ ([Arora, Belenzon, and Patacconi 2015](https://faculty.fuqua.duke.edu/%7Esb135/bio/Science%201%2091%2015.pdf))

The article confirms the decrease in corporate research, and speculates on its causes: Perhaps it is short termism (citing Lazonick, one of the pushers of the short-termist hypothesis), or maybe it is a change in the ways corporations do innovations (citing an article in The Economist about the rise of high tech startups that are later acquired by corporations).

They focus first on scientific publications in scientific journals by company scientists, and they find out they have decreased, and from a series of considerations they infer that companies value science less: they publish less, stocks of publications command a smaller market value premium, the acquisition premium paid for publications in M&A is smaller, and when innovative firms are acquired, they invest less. However, patenting has increased, and the implied value of patents has remained stable. They say this finds happen across all industries, save for biotechnology.

This is part of a long term trend, they say: corporations now rely more on external innovations, and value the golden eggs of science, but not the golden goose itself.

Could it be that they are just publishing less to protect their research? Unlikely, they say. If it were true, publication in applied journals would have fallen more than publications in basic science journals, but that is not the case, plus the considerations sketched before are not invalidated by this.

Could it be that they invest less in science because science is less useful? They argue that not, if we measure scientific usefulness by patent citation, recent patents are being cited, and more so by high scientific capability firms.

Could it be market failure? Not in principle: the causes of market failure have not intensified, they say. And their findings happen in both the US and Europe, so legislative changes are unlikely to be the cause.

The cause, they say, is that firms face reduces incentives to develop new products and processes internally. They attribute this to increased competition, and the conjecture that diversified firms  can better use basic science than firms with a narrow scope. This is supported by the publication count, and stock market value of publications, of narrow and broad focused firms.

> This withdrawal from science has been accompanied by a growing division of innovative labor in which large firms focus on development and commercialization, leaving universities and small firms to generate new ideas. However, our results also suggest that small science-intensive firms cannot simply rely on generating scientific knowledge and hope to be acquired. Because the rewards for pure scientific capability have diminished, these firms also have to invest in finding tangible commercial application of their ideas. To the extent that universities and small firms are ill-equipped to undertake this more applied research, inefficienies may result.

However, we shouldn't exclude the possibility of strong alliances between universities, venture capitalists, and corporations. Their combination can do what each alone can't. A review of the many ways of financing innovation can be found in [Bakker 2013](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004873331300156X).

One example of this collaborative approach is illustrated in this [this recent article](http://mappingignorance.org/2015/09/02/recent-advances-in-lithium-ion-rechargeable-batteries/) about lithium-ion rechargeable batteries: One of the articles cited include researchers from universities and Toyota, other by universities and Samsung. If interesting innovation is occurring at universities, corporations have strong incentives to keep in touch with the academic world, to take advantage of them.

Back to the article,

> **Several factors contributed to the growing importance of small firms, particularly in science-intensive sectors. One is the more prominent role played by universities and other research institutions in the commercialization of science. The 1980 Bayh-Dole Act encouraged universities to more aggressively license and commercialize their discoveries, and scientists found increasingly attractive to start their own businesses.** The success of Genentech, a biotechnology company founded by biochemist and Nobel Prize winner Herbert Boyer and venture capitalist Robert A. Swanson, showcased the potentially huge rewards associated with such a strategy. Also, start-ups’ high-powered incentives were di¢ cult to replicate in large, established firms, where bureaucracy, politics and the burden of past legacies tend to thwart radical change (Schumpeter, 1942; Leibenstein, 1966; Christensen and Bower, 1996; Sull et al., 1997). Changes in the institutional and legal environment have complemented these trends. Startups can get financing from venture capitalists and SBIR and other government programs (Kortum and Lerner, 2000; Lerner, 1999; Mazzucato, 2013). Intellectual property rights have been significantly strengthened starting from the early 1980s, first in the U.S. and subsequently in other countries (Jaffe and Lerner, 2004; Guellec and van Pottelsberghe de la Potterie, 2007). **These developments have promoted a new division of labor, where small start-ups specialize in scientific research and larger, more established firms specialize in product development and commercialization** (Arora and Gambardella, 1994)

Note that in another section (6.2) of the paper, they say someting that appears to contradict this: What we already mentioned about this tendency being also European. The best interpretation of this could be that Europe has implemented policy changes similar to those in the US. These changes, such as the American [Bayh-Dole Act](http://www.ipwatchdog.com/2013/09/15/bayh-dole-a-success-beyond-wildest-dreams/id=45171/) enabled firms and researchers to claim ownership of research done with federal funds, making it easier to commercially exploit them.

In the same section, the paper rules out the market short-termism hypothesis, saying that the rate of decline of research funding is similar in private (e.g. Koch Industries) and public (e.g. Google) firms.

To sum up the paper: firms are now narrower in scope, and competition may reduce the payoff to innovation (Schumpeterians rejoice, although in other section they say evidence for this is mixed). But, they say, if science were useful, successful firms would invest in it. So there are two explanations for this:

> One possibility is that markets, as well as managers, become more short-term oriented when firm profitability declines (as a result of global competition). Alternatively, it could be that investment in internal science is an inefficient relic of a past long gone, when big American and European firms could afford to ‘waste’resources. In this view, large firms are inefficient performers of research and need to be pushed to outsource research to smaller and more nimble partners. **We find little support for other potential explanations for the decline in publication output by large firms**.

So, it seems that the first part of the myopic hypothesis, reduced corporate research, is true. But this paper also casts some doubt on the short-termist _market_ hypothesis, while at the same time leaving this as one explanation for this reduced investment. So if we could rule out the short termist _market_ hypothesis, the other hypothesis they present (the change in the composition of research performance, towards university and startups in partnership with corporations) perhaps is the correct one.

## More on market myopia

We now move on to _Long-Term Investing: What determines Investment Horizon?_ ([Warren 2014](http://www.ifswf.org/pst/Long-Term-Investing-Part-1.pdf)). He reviews the literature on investor's investment horizon, and comments the different types of investors that exist:

> **The link between investor short-termism and corporate myopia is not clear cut – While there is some evidence in support of such a link, it is by no mean compelling**. Laverty (1996) examines arguments on the existence of short-termism, and points out there is: (1) no clear evidence of flawed short-term oriented management practices; (2) only mixed evidence that stock market myopia encourages corporate short-termism, noting for instance findings of positive stock market reactions to long-term investment by some papers; and, (3) an absence of empirical support for the supposed influence of ‘fluid capital’ on corporate behaviour.
> 
> Results of a survey of company management by Marston and Craven (1998) also question the extent to which institutional investors are short-term in focus. While their survey uncovers a perception that sell-side (broking) analysts are focused on the short-term, company management did not consider this the case for buy-side analysts and fund managers. When asked if the buy-side was too concerned with short-term profit opportunities, only 21% agreed while 53% disagreed.

That said, Warren does think there is more short-termism than would be optimal, but that this creates an opportunity for longer term investors.

Another paper, _Institutional Investors and Equity Returns: Are Short-term Institutions Better Informed?_ ([Yan and Zhang 2014](http://cdn1.valuewalk.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/horizon_RFS.pdf)). They discard the short-termist hypothesis. They analyse short and long term horizon institutional investing, and conclude that short term investing also adds information to the market

> An alternative explanation for our results is that short-term institutional investors pressure managers to maximize short-run profits at the expense of long-run firm value \[the ‘‘short-term pressure’’ hypothesis; see for example, Porter (1992), Bushee (1998, 2001)\]. In particular, Bushee (1998) finds evidence that firms with higher transient institutional ownership are more likely to underinvest in long-term, intangible projects such as R&D to reverse an earnings decline. **The short-term pressure hypothesis might help explain the short-run predictive ability of short-term institutional ownership and trading, but it also predicts long-run price reversal for stocks held or traded by short-term institutions. To test this prediction, we examine the relation between institutional ownership and trading and future stock returns up to three years. We find no evidence of long-run price reversal for stocks held or recently traded by short-term institutional investors, suggesting that our results cannot be explained by the short term pressure hypothesis**. We also find no evidence that either the holdings or trading by long-term institutional investors predicts long-run stock returns. This result is inconsistent with the hypothesis that long-term institutions are better informed about long-run returns. \[...\]
> 
> This article’s results have implications for the issue of identifying and attracting the ‘‘right’’ investors. Recent studies \[e.g., Gaspar, Massa, and Matos (2005)\] suggest that short-term institutional investors are weak monitors. Moreover, short-term institutions pressure managers into a short-term focus, thereby hurting long-run firm value \[e.g., Bushee (1998)\]. Collectively, these results suggest that it might be beneficial for firms to target and attract long-term institutional investors \[Porter (1992), Brancato (1997), and Bushee (2004)\]. **We argue that firms should also consider the informational aspect of institutional ownership. Our results indicate that short-term institutions play a significant informational role in the stock market.** Since more informative prices facilitate better financing and investment decisions and may reduce cost of capital, our results cast doubt on the benefit of a strategy that attempts to attract only long-term investors.

So even if short-term investors can induce corporate short-termism, this would not be a bad thing, as it would be reflecting underlying information. At a given point in time, there is a tradeoff to be made between funding short vs long term investment, so reducing long term investment at some points would be an efficient strategy. While some studies ([Bushee 1998](http://www.jstor.org/stable/248542)) show that short-termist investors induce cuts in R&D, these views have been challenged in recent studies (see [Wahal and McConnell 2000](http://www.krannert.purdue.edu/faculty/mcconnell/publications/PublicationsPDFS/Do...Myopia%20JCF%20Sept2000%20V6%20N3%20307-329.pdf))

> A more recent contribution is by Bushee 1998 , who examines reductions in  R&D spending to determine whether firms with higher institutional ownership are more likely to cut R&D spending in response to an earnings decline. He is specifically interested in whether managers of firms with high institutional ownership are more or less likely to cut R&D spending to manage short-term earnings. His sample encompasses firms that experienced an earnings decline during a year, which could subsequently be reversed by a cut in R&D expenditures. He estimates a logistic regression in which the dependent variable is one if the firm cut R&D expenditures and zero otherwise and the key independent variable is  the fraction of shares owned by institutional investors. **Unfortunately, interpretation of his results regarding institutionally induced myopia is also problematic. An implicit assumption of Bushee’s analysis is that a firm’s current level of R&D expenditures is ‘‘optimal’’**. Suppose, however, that causation runs from institutional ownership to R&D spending such that firms with high institutional ownership have low R&D expenditures. If so, then firms with high institutional ownership will have ‘‘less room’’ to cut expenditures in response to an earnings decline. In that case, a logistic regression of the type estimated by Bushee will indicate that firms with higher institutional ownership are less likely to cut R&D expenditures, even though institutional investors may generally depress corporate expenditures for R&D. **Thus, while Bushee’s tests can speak for the issue of earnings management, they do not address the question of whether institutional ownership leads to lower spending for projects with long-term payoffs, such as PP&E and R&D.**

They conclude that

> With a sample of approximately 2500 firms from 1988 to 1994, we show that firm-level expenditures for PP&E and R&D are positively related to the level of share ownership and trading activity of institutional investors in those firms. **Our results cast doubt on the view that institutions cause corporate managers to behave myopically relative to whatever myopia may be induced by direct share ownership by individual investors**. They are consistent with the perspective that institutional investors act as a buffer between firms and less patient individual investors. One of the consequences of this buffer is that it allows managers to have longer investment horizons than they would otherwise have.

Furthermore,

> At least two caveats are in order when interpreting our results. First, we do not address the question of whether US firms systematically underinvest relative to firms in other parts of the world. That question is, however, examined carefully by Hall and Weinstein 1996 and Lee 1997 . **These authors find no evidence that  managers of US firms are myopic relative to managers of Japanese and German firms**. Second, we can be accused of examining the ‘‘easiest’’ investment categories to analyze, PP&E and R&D, while ignoring those in which the problem of underinvestment is likely to be most severe. Froot et al. 1992 argue that the type of underinvestment that can be engendered by myopic investors is more likely to manifest itself in difficult to observe investments such as development of human resources and the costs incurred in the development of customer loyalty. To the extent that that argument is true, we are missing the target. **We can, therefore, only claim that we can find no evidence that institutional investors exacerbate underinvestment in PP&E and R&D**, which may very well be different from the conclusion that institutional investors do not exacerbate underinvestment in projects with long-term payoffs. With these caveats in mind, the data do not indicate that institutional investors cause managers to underinvest. Rather, to the contrary, share ownership by institutional investors appears to allow US corporate managers to invest more in projects with long-term payoffs than would direct share ownership by individual investors.

## The rise of the startup?

So there seems to be a problem (For the short-termist hypothesis) here: corporate research is in fact decreasing, but apparently this is not driven by induced short-termism. If this is true, the startup-as-innovators view would be plausible. Let's examine one more paper, _[The size, concentration and evolution of corporate R&D spending in U.S. firms from 1976 to 2010: Evidence and implications](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0929119912000260)_, by Hirschey, Skiba and Wintoki (2012). Some introduction:

> Our sample consists of all publicly traded firms over the thirty-five-year period from 1976 to 2010. Our analysis reveals that significant change has occurred in the size, distribution, and economic importance of corporate R&D spending across the economy as a whole. There has been a steady transfer of R&D spending from the government to the business sector. Corporations now account for roughly two thirds of U.S. R&D expenditures. Corporate R&D spending is roughly twice the size of print and broadcast media advertising; it is about one-half the size of corporate spending on property, plant and equipment (capital expenditures). **Median R&D spending has grown almost twice as fast as average spending. This means that medium-size firms have contributed more to the growth in R&D spending than the largest firms**, although the largest spenders still dominate R&D spending in the United States. With advertising expenditures, the trend has been the opposite. Advertising by large firms has become even more concentrated since 1976. R&D spending is far from uniform across all industries. On an aggregate basis, the distribution of R&D is much more skewed than is the distribution of advertising and capital expenditures; a few high-tech sectors account for the overwhelming share of R&D activity. However, one important fact that emerges from our analysis is that the industry concentration of R&D spending has remained stable over time. For example in 1976, there were 9 industries that reported zero R&D expenditures. In 2010, 4 of these 9 still reported no R&D spending and none of the nine reported aggregate R&D spending greater than one-hundredth of one percent of total sales. R&D spending and R&D intensity (R&D as a percentage of sales revenue) are highest in industry groups such as transportation equipment, tobacco products, chemicals and allied products, petroleum refining, industrial machinery and computer equipment, electronic equipment, business services, and measuring instruments, photography, watches. Little to no R&D takes place among large corporations found in such big sectors as retailing (general merchandise stores, food stores, building materials, hardware and garden), health services, bars and restaurants, construction, and transportation services. Nevertheless, within the industries where there is significant R&D spending, the number of firms that report R&D increased significantly between 1976 and 2010. In 1976, 45% of all firms reported R&D and this increased to 53% in 2010. \[...\]
> 
> Our analysis also contributes to the debate on whether or not managerial myopia has had an effect on aggregate R&D spending in the U.S. over the past two decades. In the early 1990s, some academics (e.g. Jacobs, 1991; Porter, 1992), following popular accounts and anecdotes in the business press, suggested that managers in the United States may systematically reduce investments in R&D to meet short-term earnings benchmarks at the expense of value maximization and long-term growth. **While the evidence for any cross sectional variation in managerial myopia is mixed at best (e.g. Bange and DeBondt, 1998; Lundstrum, 2002; Wahal and McConnell, 2000), the idea that U.S. firms may systematically under-invest in R&D remains popular in the business press (see, for example, Mandel, 2009).**

Maybe the startup hypothesis is true?

> While we do not test the managerial myopia hypothesis directly at the firm level, we provide suggestive evidence that is inconsistent with the idea of managerial myopia. For example, the ratio of aggregate R&D spending to corporate earnings increased from 24.6% in 1976 to 34.3% in 2010 across all firms. While the ratio of R&D spending has increased across firms in general,**a significant portion of the increase has come from an increase in R&D spending by young, R&D-intensive firms with little or even negative earnings**. In addition, as we have already noted, we find that profitability accounts for very little of the cross-sectional variation in R&D spending. Taken together, none of these findings suggests any systematic economy-wide bias towards managerial myopia with respect to R&D. On the contrary, the data suggest a willingness among top managers of large corporations to devote a large share of current profits to risky long-term R&D investment projects with uncertain payoffs. Indeed, we find that even during the 2008–2010 financial and economic crises, the ratio of R&D to sales remains unchanged from the long-term historical average.
> 
> \[...\]
> 
> Finally, we find strong evidence that managers in industries where fundamental innovation is essential for survival are willing to commit a significant and growing share of earnings to risky long-term R&D. We find little evidence to support the idea popular in the business press of managerial myopia. Managers do not reduce R&D intensity in response to short declines in profitability even during periods of economic recession.

However, they analyse aggregate R&D, and do not specifically talk about basic research, so this has to be taken with care. The finding about young firms could be evidence in favour of the substitution (of corporate by startups research) hypothesis, but this paper is not totally conclusive on the matter. The validity this would hinge on what counts as basic and what counts as applied research. Intel's Quantum Computing initiative sounds like basic research, and it targets something removed in the future, but the goal is to solve the practical problem of a given piece of tech, so in that sense it counts as applied research. I would have to look up what kind of things are being counted as which, and whether startups are entering the market with relevant pieces of market-ready basic research.

## Conclusion

To conclude, given the above, the market induced myopia theory can be rejected. This theory posits widespread irrationality in one of the most textbooklike markets, so we should require strong evidence for the myopic view to be true. If we accept this, then we would need to accept, as per one of the cited papers, that the way research is done has changed. Corporations are not investing in research as they used to, but they have outsourced it. This could have the advantage of enabling the corporations to focus on improving what they are already doing, and leave universities and startups the task of doing radical innovation, since they are free of the financial constraints large firms have. Moreover, they can help solving the R&D market failure problematic. Universities don't do research to sell it, but as an end in itself, therefore being immune to this market failure. However, furthering the division of knowledge generates coordination problem between the different agents involved. I have not done an in-depth survey of the role of non-corporate source of research funding, but given the above discussion, I expect it to be growing, and their partnerships with corporations, increasing. See [this post](http://qz.com/465152/dont-believe-those-who-tell-you-american-innovation-is-losing-its-way/) for some preliminary information.

Given that the myopia argument is brought forward in relation to financialization, and recent changes in capitalism, and not in relation to always present market failures, if we restrict ourselves to this view of myopia, the myopic argument stands rejected. If we expand the thesis to include market failure, then there can be myopia in the sense of the prisoners of a prisoner dilemma being myopic for defecting. How much market failure and where? A study of that will come here, at some point in the future.
