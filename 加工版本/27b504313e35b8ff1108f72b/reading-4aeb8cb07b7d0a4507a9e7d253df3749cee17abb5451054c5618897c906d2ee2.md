# 阅读引文比大多数人想象的更容易｜Reading citations is easier than most people think

---

## 正文

声称某个 meme 得到了“studies”或“science”支持的说法，实在很常见。但当我查看实际的 studies 时，通常会发现 data 与那个 claim 是 opposed 的。下面是我最近遇到的几个例子。

## Dunning-Kruger：邓宁—克鲁格效应

Dunning-Kruger 的一种 pop-sci 版本，也是我最常看到被 cited 的版本，是一个人对某个 subject 知道得越少，就越觉得自己知道得多。另一种 pop-sci 版本则是，对某件事知道得很少的人会 overestimate 自己的 expertise，因为他们缺乏 knowledge，这使他们误以为自己知道的比实际更多。Dunning 和 Kruger 实际提出的 claim 比第一种 pop-sci claim 要弱得多，而且在我看来，evidence 也比第二种 claim 更弱。原 paper 并不比大多数对它作出 incorrect 解读的 pop-sci 文章长多少，而我们只要看看 paper 中的四幅 figures，就能相当清楚地了解其中的 claims。在下面的 graphs 中，“perceived ability”是 subjective self rating，而“actual ability”是 test 的 result。

![Dunning-Kruger 的图表](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_1.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger 的图表](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_2.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger 的图表](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_3.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger 的图表](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_4.webp)

四种 cases 中有两种在 perceived skill 与 actual skill 之间存在明显的 positive correlation，这与我们讨论过的第一种 Dunning-Kruger 的 pop-sci conception 恰好 opposite。至于第二种，我们可以看到，处于 top end 的人也没有 correctly 地 rate 自己，因此，用那些对某个 subject 所知不多的人来 explanation Dunning-Kruger 的 results（考虑到这项 study 的标题是 Unskilled and Unaware of It: How Difficulties in Recognizing One's Own Incompetence Lead to Inflated Self-Assessments，很容易对它作出这样的 interpretation）是 insufficient 的，因为这并不能 explain 为什么处于 charts 顶端的人对自己 skill level 的 guess，至少在这项 study 的 conditions 下，看起来也是 symmetrically incorrect 的。有人可能会 argue，存在一种完全不同的 effect，只是碰巧在 perceived ability 上 cause 了相同的、roughly linear 的 slope，与那些“unskilled and unaware of it”的人一样。但如果有任何合理而更简单的 explanation，那么在没有额外 evidence 的情况下，那个 explanation 就显得过于复杂了（即使存在这样的 evidence，paper 中也没有提供）[1](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:D)。

对于 perceived skill 为什么会被 compressed，尤其是在 low end，一种合理的 explanation 是，很少有人愿意把自己 rate 为 below average 或 absolute best，这使 scale 被 shrinking，但仍 keeping 了 roughly linear fit。这些 scales 的 crossing point 位于 median 之上，indicating 人们 on average 会 overestimate 自己，不过考虑到 tested 的 population，这并不令人意外（后面会再谈）。在另外两种 cases 中，correlation 非常 close to zero。可能是不同 tasks 的 effect 不同，也可能只是 sample size 很小，不同 tasks 之间的 differences 是 noise。还有一种可能是，这种 effect 来自 sampled 的特定 population（Cornell 的学生，他们在许多方面很可能确实是 above average 的）。如果你在 Wikipedia 上查阅 Dunning-Kruger，它会声称，一项对 East Asians 进行的 Dunning-Kruger replication 显示了 opposite result（perceived skill 比 actual skill 更低，而且 skill 越高，difference 越大），并声称这种 effect 可能只是 American culture 的 artifact，但那个 citation 实际上是指向一篇 editorial 的链接，其中提到了一项关于 East Asian confidence 的 meta analysis，因此这可能又是一个 false citation 的例子。也可能只是链接到了错误的 source。无论如何，这种 effect 肯定不是人们知道得越多，就越觉得自己知道得少。

## Income 与 Happiness

money 不会让人 happy，这已经成了常识。在撰写本文时，用 Google search 搜索 `happiness income`，会返回一张 knowledge card，说赚得超过 $75k/year has no impact on happiness. Other top search results claim the happiness ceiling occurs at $10k/year、$30k/year, $40k/year 和 $75k/year。

![Google 的 knowledge card 说，$75k 对任何人来说都应该足够了](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/google_happiness.webp)

这不仅是错的，而且这种错误[在每一个 studied 的国家中也都是 robust 的](https://web.archive.org/web/20160610202901/http://www.brookings.edu/~/media/research/files/papers/2013/04/subjective%20well%20being%20income/subjective%20well%20being%20income.pdf)。

![income 更多的人更 happier](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/happiness_within_country.webp)

happiness 与 income 是 correlated 的，这并不是通过 cherry picking 某一项 study 得出的。这个 result 在 World Values Survey 的五轮调查（1981-1984、1989-1993、1994-1999、2000-2004 和 2005-2009）、Pew Global Attitudes Survey 的三轮调查（2002、2007、2010）、International Social Survey Program 的五轮调查（1991、1998、2001、2007、2008），以及一项 large scale Gallup survey 中都 holds。

上面的 graph 将 income 放在 log scale 上；如果你选一个国家，并在 linear scale 上 graph 这些 results，就会得到[类似这样的东西](https://gravityandlevity.wordpress.com/2013/05/06/how-we-measure-our-happiness/)。

![happiness vs. income 的 Best fit log，即幸福程度与收入的最佳对数拟合](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/happiness_income_linear.webp)

与所有 log function 的 graphs 一样，这个 graph 看起来像是即将 level off，于是便出现了下面这样的 interpretations：

![Distorted 的 log graph，即失真的对数图](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/bad_log_1.webp)

这确实是一篇声称 income 不会让人 happy 的文章中的 graph。这些隐约像 log-like、随后便 level off 的 graphs 实在很常见。如果你想多看一些，可以试试[对“happiness income”进行 image search](https://duckduckgo.com/?q=happiness+income&iax=1&ia=images)。我最喜欢的那一张，是赚到足够 money 的人真的碰到了 scale 的顶端。显然，存在某个 dollar value，不仅能让你 happy，还能让你达到人类所能达到的最 happy 的程度。

与 Dunning-Kruger 一样，你可以看看 papers 中的 graphs，了解究竟是怎么回事。在这里，人们为什么会传播错误的说法，稍微更容易理解一些，因为当 data 被 plotted 在 linear scale 上时，很容易被 misinterpret；但只要看一眼实际的 studies，仍然很容易弄清楚究竟是怎么回事。

## Hedonic Adaptation & Happiness（享乐适应与幸福感）

人们会从 setbacks（以及 positive events）中 bounce back，并 return 到 fixed level 的 happiness，这一观点在 [Daniel Gilbert 在一本畅销书中写到它](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1400077427/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=1400077427&linkCode=as2&tag=abroaview-20&linkId=3ERCGEMU57L2FDF7)之后进入了大众意识。

但即使不看关于 adverse events 的 adaptation 的 literature，上一节关于 wealth 的内容也应该让人对此产生一些怀疑。如果人们既会从 bad events 中 rebound，也会从 good 中 rebound，那么 making more money 怎么会 causes 人们变得 happier 呢？

事实证明，人们会 adapt 到 negative events 并 return 到先前的 set-point，这一观点是个迷思。尽管具体的 effects 会随 bad event 而 vary，disability[2](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:1)、divorce[3](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:2)、loss of a partner[4](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:3) 和 unemployment[5](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:4) 都对 happiness 有 long-term negative effects。Unemployment 是其中相对容易被 undone 的 event，但即使人们 become reemployed，effects 仍然 persist。我在这里只 citing 了四项 studies，但[对 literature 的一项 meta analysis](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3289759/) shows，这些 results 在已有的 studies 中都是 robust 的。

同样的情况也 applies 于 positive events。虽然 winning the lottery 不会让人们 happier 已是“常识”，但[事实证明，这也不是真的](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/05/27/science/how-to-win-the-lottery-happily.html)。

在这两种情况下，早期的 cross-sectional results indicated，extreme events，比如 winning the lottery 或 becoming disabled，不会对 happiness 有 long term effects，这种看法是 plausible 的。但那些随着 events 发生而 follow individuals、并随时间 measure 同一个人的 happiness 的 longitudinal studies，却 show 了 opposite result -- events 事实上确实会 affect happiness。大体上，这些并不是新的 results（一些最初的 results 比 Daniel Gilbert 的书还早），但那些基于不那么 rigorous 的 studies 的较早 results，仍然比 corrections 传播得更快。

## Chess position memorization（国际象棋局面记忆）

我经常看到一些 citations 声称，虽然 experts 比 non-experts 更能 memorize chess positions，但当 positions 被 randomized 时，这种 advantage 就会完全消失。人们提到某个具体的 citation 时，通常指的是 Chase 和 Simon 在 1973 年发表的 paper Perception in Chess，这篇“经典”在 literature 中的 cited 次数高达 7449 次，其中写道：

> 不过，De Groat 确实在他的 short-term memory experiments 中 find 了 masters 与 weaker players 之间一个有趣的 difference。Masters showed 出了非凡的 ability：只 viewing 一个 chess position 5 sec，便能几乎 perfectly 地 reconstruct 它。对于 below the master level 的 players，这种 ability 出现了 sharp dropoff。这个 result 不能 attributed to masters 的 generally superior memory ability，因为当 chess positions 是通过在 board 上 randomly placing 相同 numbers 的 pieces 来 constructed 时，masters 在 reconstructing 它们时并不比 weaker players 做得更好。因此，masters 似乎与其他所有人一样，受到相同的 severe short-term memory limits 的 constrained（ Miller, 1956），而他们在 "meaningful" positions 上的 superior performance，必然在于他们能够 perceive 这些 positions 中的 structure，并将其 encode 成 chunks。具体而言，如果一位 chess master 能 remember board 上 20 或更多 pieces 的 location，但 short-term memory 中只有大约 five chunks 的 space，那么每个 chunk 就必然由 four 或 five pieces composed，并 organized 成一个 single relational structure。

这篇 paper 随后 runs 了一项 experiment，“proves” master-level players 在 memorizing random mid-game positions 时，实际上比 beginners 做得更差，尽管他们在 memorizing real mid-game positions 时做得好得多（而在 end-game positions 中，当 positions 被 randomized 时，他们做得与 beginners 大致相同）。遗憾的是，这篇 paper 使用的 sample size 小得荒唐：每个 skill level 只有 one chess player。

稍作搜索便能发现，这个 result 在 larger sample sizes 下并不 reproduce。例如，Gobet 和 Simon 在 "Recall of rapidly presented random chess positions is a function of skill" 中说：

> 一个被 widely cited 的 result asserts，当 experts 面对 random material 时，他们在 recalling 自身 domain of expertise 中的 meaningful material 方面相对于 novices 的 superiority 就会 vanishes。然而，对近期以 random positions 作为 control material 的 chess experiments（presentation time 在 3 至 10 sec 之间）的 review shows，即使面对 random positions，strong players 通常仍 maintain 着相对于 weak players 的一定 superiority，尽管 skill levels 之间的 relative difference 远小于面对 game positions 时的情况。文中 discussed 了这一 finding 对 chess 的 expertise 的 implications，并 raised 了其他 domains 中 random material 的 recall 这一 question。

他们 find，这种情况会 scales with skill level。例如，对于 "real" positions，2350+ ELO players 所 memorized 的 correct pieces 的 number，是 1600-2000 ELO players 的 ~2.2x，但对于 random positions，这个 difference 为 ~1.6x（这些 ratios 来自对一张 graph 的目测，可能略有偏差）。1.6x 是 smaller than 2.2x，但它肯定不是所声称的 1.0。

我也见过有人 cite 这个 result，声称它 applies 于其他 fields；但稍作搜索，看看将这个 result applying 于其他 fields 的情况，就会发现 results 要么 show 了类似的情况（randomized positions 上有较小但仍 observable 的 difference），要么并不 reproduce。例如，McKeithen 对 programmers 做了这类研究，并 found，在尝试 memorize programs 时，对于 "normal" program，experts 在 first trial 上比 beginners 好 ~2.5x，到 6th trial 时则好 3x；而对于 "scrambled" program，experts 在 first trial 上好 3x，到 6th trial 时则变成只好 ~1.5x。尽管这个 result 与 Chase 和 Simon contradicting，我却见过有人 cite 这个 result，来声称与 Chase 和 Simon 相同的事情，想必这些人并没有读过 McKeithen 实际写了什么。

## Type Systems（类型系统）

遗憾的是，关于 studies 和 evidence 的 false claims 并不限于 pop-sci memes；它们在 software 和 hardware development 中无处不在。例如，看看一位 Scala/FP“思想领袖”的这条评论：

![这条推文声称，任何对 type systems 是否 helpful 的怀疑，都 equivalent to 成为 anti-vaxxer](https://danluu.com/images/empirical-pl/pl_godwin.webp)

我每周至少会看到一次这样的说法。我选择这个例子，不是因为它特别恶劣，而是因为它很典型。如果你在 twitter 上关注几位知名的 FP 支持者，就会经常看到这样的 claims：有非常 strong empirical evidence 和大量 studies 在 backing up type systems 的 effectiveness。

然而，[对 empirical evidence 的一项 review](https://danluu.com/empirical-pl/) shows，这些 evidence 大多是 incomplete 的，而那些并非 incomplete 的部分也是 equivocal 的。在所有 false memes 中，我觉得这个最难理解。在其他例子中，我能看出一种 plausible mechanism，说明 results 可能如何被 misinterpreted。“Relationship 比预期的 weaker”可以变成“relationship 与预期的 opposite”，log 看起来可能很像 asymptotic function，而使用 inferior methods 得出的 preliminary results，可能比开展得更好的 follow-up studies 传播得更快。但在这个例子中，我不确定 evidence 和 beliefs 之间有什么 connection。

## 这能避免吗？

我能理解，为什么 false memes 可能迅速传播，即使它们直接 contradict reliable sources。读 papers 听起来很费工夫。有时确实如此。但往往并不是。读一篇 pure math paper 通常很费工夫。为了 determine methodology 是否 sound 而读一篇 empirical paper，也可能很费工夫。例如，biostatistics 和 econometrics 的 papers 往往 apply 完全不同的 methods，而要充分熟悉某个 field 所用的一组 methods，从而准确理解它们何时 applicable、有哪些 holes，需要下很大工夫。但如果读 empirical papers 只是为了看看它们提出了什么 claims，通常相当容易。

如果你读了 abstract 和 conclusion，再略读 paper，找找有意思的部分（graphs、tables、methodology 中能说明问题的 flaws 等），在大多数情况下，就足以看出关于这篇 paper 的流行 claims 是否 true。在我理想中的世界里，你只读 abstract 就能得到这些信息，但 papers 在 abstract 中提出的 claims 远比正文中的 claims 更 strong，这种情况并不少见，因此你至少需要略读 paper。

也许我在这里有些天真，但我认为，false memes 背后的一个主要原因，是 checking sources 听起来远比实际上更困难、更令人望而生畏。一个突出的例子是，Quartz 曾发表文章，声称 tech salaries 中并不存在 gender gap，[但它 cited 的多个 sources 却 showed 完全 opposite 的情况](https://danluu.com/gender-gap/)。Twitter 上一片热议，人们纷纷宣称 gender gap 已经消失。当我发表了[一篇仅仅 quote 那些实际被 cited 的 studies 的文章](https://danluu.com/gender-gap/)后，其中许多人又宣称，自己最初的说法有误。他们愿意发推更正[6](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:L)，这很好，但据我所知，实际上并没有人去读 source data，尽管 graphs 和 tables 让人一眼就能看出，原先那篇 Quartz 文章的作者是在推行自己的主张，甚至不是靠 cherry picked citations，而是靠那些 showed 其 thesis 的 opposite 的 citations。

遗憾的是，对那些确实读 studies、却并不利他的人而言，让读 studies 显得困难，最符合他们的利益。例如，我曾与一家广受使用的付费网站的创始人交谈，这家网站 reviews 关于 supplements 和 nutrition 的 evidence。他声称，认为“普通人”能够正确 interpret studies 是荒谬的，大众需要 experts 来阅读和 summarize studies。但他不过是一位连续创业者，意识到可以靠阅读 studies 并 summarizing results 赚很多钱！一个更普遍的例子是，人们有时会声称，你需要某些资历或声望标志，才能真正阅读或 interpret studies，以此维持权威姿态。

确实有些 fields 需要一定背景才能恰当地 interpret 一项 study，但即便如此，一个 degree 所包含的 knowledge 也相当少，任何人都可以学会。例如，除去 lab work（其中没有任何对 interpreting results 至关重要的 knowledge），我在学校每个 credit hour 上所花的时间，与一小时只差一个很小的 constact factor。按这个 conversion rate，我母校的一个 engineering degree 需要略多于 100 小时，而几乎所有 non-engineering degrees 都不到 40 小时，彼此之间还有大量 overlap，因为许多 degrees 会要求修读相同的课程（例如 calculus）。既然一个人花上一周的时间，就能获得一个 degree 提供的 knowledge，那么以是否拥有 degree 之类的资历，来限制谁可以阅读和 interpreting 一项 study，实在荒谬。

### 2021 年更新

回过头看，我认为“type systems”这个例子的谜底很简单：它是一种与其他例子不同的 fake citation。在前三个例子中，一个聪明、反常识、但实际上错误的想法被四处传播。这说得通，因为人们喜欢聪明、反常识的想法，而且并不太在意它们是否错误，所以聪明、反常识的想法，相对于其 correctness 而言，往往更容易广泛传播。

至于 type systems 的例子，事情只不过是，人们经常 fabricate evidence，然后 appeal to authority 来支持自己的立场。在这篇文章中，我感到困惑，因为我无法理解，一个人怎么会看过 evidence 后，还提出 type system 支持者所提出的那些 claims。但在读过数千场人们为自己偏爱的 tool/language/practice 辩护的讨论后，我明白了，以为这些支持者竟会考虑寻找 evidence，而不是根本没找过就假装 evidence 存在，是我太天真了。

## 原文

It's really common to see claims that some meme is backed by “studies” or “science”. But when I look at the actual studies, it usually turns out that the data are opposed to the claim. Here are the last few instances of this that I've run across.

## Dunning-Kruger

A pop-sci version of Dunning-Kruger, the most common one I see cited, is that, the less someone knows about a subject, the more they think they know. Another pop-sci version is that people who know little about something overestimate their expertise because their lack of knowledge fools them into thinking that they know more than they do. The actual claim Dunning and Kruger make is much weaker than the first pop-sci claim and, IMO, the evidence is weaker than the second claim. The original paper isn't much longer than most of the incorrect pop-sci treatments of the paper, and we can get pretty good idea of the claims by looking at the four figures included in the paper. In the graphs below, “perceived ability” is a subjective self rating, and “actual ability” is the result of a test.

![Dunning-Kruger graph](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_1.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger graph](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_2.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger graph](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_3.webp) ![Dunning-Kruger graph](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/dunning_4.webp)

In two of the four cases, there's an obvious positive correlation between perceived skill and actual skill, which is the opposite of the first pop-sci conception of Dunning-Kruger that we discussed. As for the second, we can see that people at the top end also don't rate themselves correctly, so the explanation for Dunning-Kruger's results is that people who don't know much about a subject (an easy interpretation to have of the study, given its title, Unskilled and Unaware of It: How Difficulties in Recognizing One's Own Incompetence Lead to Inflated Self-Assessments) is insufficient because that doesn't explain why people at the top of the charts have what appears to be, at least under the conditions of the study, a symmetrically incorrect guess about their skill level. One could argue that there's a completely different effect that just happens to cause the same, roughly linear, slope in perceived ability that people who are "unskilled and unaware of it" have. But, if there's any plausible simpler explanation, then that explanation seems overly complicated without additional evidence (which, if any exists, is not provided in the paper)[1](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:D).

A plausible explanation of why perceived skill is compressed, especially at the low end, is that few people want to rate themselves as below average or as the absolute best, shrinking the scale but keeping a roughly linear fit. The crossing point of the scales is above the median, indicating that people, on average, overestimate themselves, but that's not surprising given the population tested (more on this later). In the other two cases, the correlation is very close to zero. It could be that the effect is different for different tasks, or it could be just that the sample size is small and that the differences between the different tasks is noise. It could also be that the effect comes from the specific population sampled (students at Cornell, who are probably actually above average in many respects). If you look up Dunning-Kruger on Wikipedia, it claims that a replication of Dunning-Kruger on East Asians shows the opposite result (perceived skill is lower than actual skill, and the greater the skill, the greater the difference), and that the effect is possibly just an artifact of American culture, but the citation is actually a link to an editorial which mentions a meta analysis on East Asian confidence, so that might be another example of a false citation. Or maybe it's just a link to the wrong source. In any case, the effect certainly isn't that the more people know, the less they think they know.

## Income & Happiness

It's become common knowledge that money doesn't make people happy. As of this writing, a Google search for `happiness income` returns a knowledge card that making more than $75k/year has no impact on happiness. Other top search results claim the happiness ceiling occurs at $10k/year, $30k/year, $40k/year and $75k/year.

![Google knowledge card says that $75k should be enough for anyone](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/google_happiness.webp)

Not only is that wrong, the wrongness [is robust across every country studied, too](https://web.archive.org/web/20160610202901/http://www.brookings.edu/~/media/research/files/papers/2013/04/subjective%20well%20being%20income/subjective%20well%20being%20income.pdf).

![People with more income are happier](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/happiness_within_country.webp)

That happiness is correlated with income doesn't come from cherry picking one study. That result holds across five iterations of the World Values Survey (1981-1984, 1989-1993, 1994-1999, 2000-2004, and 2005-2009), three iterations of the Pew Global Attitudes Survey (2002, 2007, 2010), five iterations of the International Social Survey Program (1991, 1998, 2001, 2007, 2008), and a large scale Gallup survey.

The graph above has income on a log scale, if you pick a country and graph the results on a linear scale, you get [something like this](https://gravityandlevity.wordpress.com/2013/05/06/how-we-measure-our-happiness/).

![Best fit log to happiness vs. income](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/happiness_income_linear.webp)

As with all graphs of a log function, it looks like the graph is about to level off, which results in interpretations like the following:

![Distorted log graph](https://danluu.com/images/dunning-kruger/bad_log_1.webp)

That's an actual graph from an article that claims that income doesn't make people happy. These vaguely log-like graphs that level off are really common. If you want to see more of these, try an [image search for “happiness income”](https://duckduckgo.com/?q=happiness+income&iax=1&ia=images). My favorite is the one where people who make enough money literally hit the top of the scale. Apparently, there's a dollar value which not only makes you happy, it makes you as happy as it is possible for humans to be.

As with Dunning-Kruger, you can look at the graphs in the papers to see what's going on. It's a little easier to see why people would pass along the wrong story here, since it's easy to misinterpret the data when it's plotted against a linear scale, but it's still pretty easy to see what's going on by taking a peek at the actual studies.

## Hedonic Adaptation & Happiness

The idea that people bounce back from setbacks (as well as positive events) and return to a fixed level of happiness entered the popular consciousness after [Daniel Gilbert wrote about it in a popular book](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1400077427/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=1400077427&linkCode=as2&tag=abroaview-20&linkId=3ERCGEMU57L2FDF7).

But even without looking at the literature on adaptation to adverse events, the previous section on wealth should cast some doubt on this. If people rebound from both bad events and good, how is it that making more money causes people to be happier?

Turns out, the idea that people adapt to negative events and return to their previous set-point is a myth. Although the exact effects vary depending on the bad event, disability[2](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:1), divorce[3](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:2), loss of a partner[4](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:3), and unemployment[5](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:4) all have long-term negative effects on happiness. Unemployment is the one event that can be undone relatively easily, but the effects persist even after people become reemployed. I'm only citing four studies here, but [a meta analysis of the literature](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3289759/) shows that the results are robust across existing studies.

The same thing applies to positive events. While it's “common knowledge” that winning the lottery doesn't make people happier, [it turns out that isn't true, either](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/05/27/science/how-to-win-the-lottery-happily.html).

In both cases, early cross-sectional results indicated that it's plausible that extreme events, like winning the lottery or becoming disabled, don't have long term effects on happiness. But the longitudinal studies that follow individuals and measure the happiness of the same person over time as events happen show the opposite result -- events do, in fact, affect happiness. For the most part, these aren't new results (some of the initial results predate Daniel Gilbert's book), but the older results based on less rigorous studies continue to propagate faster than the corrections.

## Chess position memorization

I frequently see citations claiming that, while experts can memorize chess positions better than non-experts, the advantage completely goes away when positions are randomized. When people refer to a specific citation, it's generally Chase and Simon's 1973 paper Perception in Chess, a "classic" which has been cited a whopping 7449 times in the literature, which says:

> De Groat did, however, find an intriguing difference between masters and weaker players in his short-term memory experiments. Masters showed a remarkable ability to reconstruct a chess position almost perfectly after viewing it for only 5 sec. There was a sharp dropoff in this ability for players below the master level. This result could not be attributed to the masters’ generally superior memory ability, for when chess positions were constructed by placing the same numbers of pieces randomly on the board, the masters could then do no better in reconstructing them than weaker players, Hence, the masters appear to be constrained by the same severe short-term memory limits as everyone else ( Miller, 1956), and their superior performance with "meaningful" positions must lie in their ability to perceive structure in such positions and encode them in chunks. Specifically, if a chess master can remember the location of 20 or more pieces on the board, but has space for only about five chunks in short-term memory, then each chunk must be composed of four or five pieces, organized in a single relational structure.

The paper then runs an experiment which "proves" that master-level players actually do worse than beginners when memorizing random mid-game positions even though they do much better memorizing real mid-game positions (and, in end-game positions, they do the about the same as beginners when positions are randomized). Unfortunately, the paper used an absurdly small sample size of one chess player at each skill level.

A quick search indicates that this result does not reproduce with larger sample sizes, e.g., Gobet and Simon, in "Recall of rapidly presented random chess positions is a function of skill", say

> A widely cited result asserts that experts’ superiority over novices in recalling meaningful material from their domain of expertise vanishes when they are confronted with random material. A review of recent chess experiments in which random positions served as control material (presentation time between 3 and 10 sec) shows, however, that strong players generally maintain some superiority over weak players even with random positions, although the relative difference between skill levels is much smaller than with game positions. The implications of this finding for expertise in chess are discussed and the question of the recall of random material in other domains is raised.

They find this scales with skill level and, e.g., for "real" positions, 2350+ ELO players memorized ~2.2x the number of correct pieces that 1600-2000 ELO players did, but the difference was ~1.6x for random positions (these ratios are from eyeballing a graph and may be a bit off). 1.6x is smaller than 2.2x, but it's certainly not the claimed 1.0.

I've also seen this result cited to claim that it applies to other fields, but in a quick search of applying this result to other fields, results either show something similar (a smaller but still observable difference on randomized positions) or don't reproduce, e.g., McKeithen did this for programmers and found that, on trying to memorize programs, on "normal" program experts were ~2.5x better than beginners on the first trial and 3x better by the 6th trial, whereas on the "scrambled" program, experts were 3x better on the first trial and progressed to being only ~1.5x better by the 6th trial. Despite this result contradicting Chase and Simon, I've seen people cite this result to claim the same thing as Chase and Simon, presumably from people who didn't read what McKeithen actually wrote.

## Type Systems

Unfortunately, false claims about studies and evidence aren't limited to pop-sci memes; they're everywhere in both software and hardware development. For example, see this comment from a Scala/FP "thought leader":

![Tweet claiming that any doubt that type systems are helpful is equivalent to being an anti-vaxxer](https://danluu.com/images/empirical-pl/pl_godwin.webp)

I see something like this at least once a week. I'm picking this example not because it's particularly egregious, but because it's typical. If you follow a few of the big time FP proponents on twitter, you'll see regularly claims that there's very strong empirical evidence and extensive studies backing up the effectiveness of type systems.

However, [a review of the empirical evidence](https://danluu.com/empirical-pl/) shows that the evidence is mostly incomplete, and that it's equivocal where it's not incomplete. Of all the false memes, I find this one to be the hardest to understand. In the other cases, I can see a plausible mechanism by which results could be misinterpreted. “Relationship is weaker than expected” can turn into “relationship is opposite of expected”, log can look a lot like an asymptotic function, and preliminary results using inferior methods can spread faster than better conducted follow-up studies. But I'm not sure what the connection between the evidence and beliefs are in this case.

## Is this preventable?

I can see why false memes might spread quickly, even when they directly contradict reliable sources. Reading papers sounds like a lot of work. It sometimes is. But it's often not. Reading a pure math paper is usually a lot of work. Reading an empirical paper to determine if the methodology is sound can be a lot of work. For example, biostatistics and econometrics papers tend to apply completely different methods, and it's a lot of work to get familiar enough with the set of methods used in any particular field to understand precisely when they're applicable and what holes they have. But reading empirical papers just to see what claims they make is usually pretty easy.

If you read the abstract and conclusion, and then skim the paper for interesting bits (graphs, tables, telling flaws in the methodology, etc.), that's enough to see if popular claims about the paper are true in most cases. In my ideal world, you could get that out of just reading the abstract, but it's not uncommon for papers to make claims in the abstract that are much stronger than the claims made in the body of the paper, so you need to at least skim the paper.

Maybe I'm being naive here, but I think a major reason behind false memes is that checking sources sounds much harder and more intimidating than it actually is. A striking example of this is when Quartz published its article on how there isn't a gender gap in tech salaries, [which cited multiple sources that showed the exact opposite](https://danluu.com/gender-gap/). Twitter was abuzz with people proclaiming that the gender gap has disappeared. When I published [a post which did nothing but quote the actual cited studies](https://danluu.com/gender-gap/), many of the same people then proclaimed that their original proclamation was mistaken. It's great that they were willing to tweet a correction[6](https://danluu.com/dunning-kruger/#fn:L), but as far as I can tell no one actually went and read the source data, even though the graphs and tables make it immediately obvious that the author of the original Quartz article was pushing an agenda, not even with cherry picked citations, but citations that showed the opposite of their thesis.

Unfortunately, it's in the best interests of non-altruistic people who do read studies to make it seem like reading studies is difficult. For example, when I talked to the founder of a widely used pay-walled site that reviews evidence on supplements and nutrition, he claimed that it was ridiculous to think that "normal people" could interpret studies correctly and that experts are needed to read and summarize studies for the masses. But he's just a serial entrepreneur who realized that you can make a lot of money by reading studies and summarizing the results! A more general example is how people sometimes try to maintain an authoritative air by saying that you need certain credentials or markers of prestige to really read or interpret studies.

There are certainly fields where you need some background to properly interpret a study, but even then, the amount of knowledge that a degree contains is quite small and can be picked up by anyone. For example, excluding lab work (none of which contained critical knowledge for interpreting results), I was within a small constact factor of spending one hour of time per credit hour in school. At the conversion rate, an engineering degree from my alma mater costs a bit more than 100 hours and almost all non-engineering degrees land at less than 40 hours, with a large amount of overlap between them because a lot of degrees will require the same classes (e.g., calculus). Gatekeeping reading and interpreting a study on whether or not someone has a credential like a degree is absurd when someone can spend a week's worth of time gaining the knowledge that a degree offers.

### 2021 update

In retrospect, I think the mystery of the "type systems" example is simple: it's a different kind of fake citation than the others. In the first three examples, a clever, contrarian, but actually wrong idea got passed around. This makes sense because people love clever, contrarian, ideas and don't care very much if they're wrong, so clever, contarian, relatively frequently become viral relative to their correctness.

For the type systems example, it's just that people commonly fabricate evidence and then appeal to authority to support their position. In the post, I was confused because I couldn't see how anyone could look at the evidence and then make the claims that type system advocates do but, after reading thousands of discussions from people advocating for their pet tool/language/practice, I can see that it was naive of me to think that these advocates would even consider looking for evidence as opposed to just pretending that evidence exists without ever having looked.
