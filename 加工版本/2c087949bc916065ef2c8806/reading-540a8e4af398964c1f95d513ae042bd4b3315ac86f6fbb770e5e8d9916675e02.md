# 什么没有改变

---

## 正文

我的书 [Ultralearning](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/ultralearning/) 于 2019 年出版。它记录了 intensive self-education 的 process；这种方式启发了我的一些 self-guided projects，涉及 learning languages、computer science、art 等。

这本书后来出人意料地成为畅销书，销量超过 200,000 册，并有数十种译本。直到今天，我收到的新读者邮件，大部分仍来自通过 _Ultralearning_ 认识我的人。

我经常被问到一个问题：如果这本书在今天出版，会有哪些变化？2019 年，围绕 AI 的讨论还只是低语。如今，已经震耳欲聋。

今天，我想带大家重新看看 _Ultralearning_，看看什么变了、什么没有变，以及我认为 learning 和 education 的未来会怎样。

## 什么没有改变

我相信，Ultralearning 的基本主张至今仍然相当站得住脚：

Technology 正在 widening 拥有与缺乏 human capital 的人之间的 gulf。在学校里的 Learning 是 insufficient。要有所成就，我们需要不断增添自己的 skills 和 knowledge；考虑到我们身处 information-saturated environment，efficiently 地这样做至关重要。

AI 只是 accelerated 了这些 trends。

虽然一些[早期报道](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf)认为 AI 可能成为 equalizer，帮助平庸的 programmers 和 writers 以更高的 level 进行 produce，但我觉得，那些早期看法如今显得天真了。真要说有什么变化，那就是通往 professional programmer 的 skill tree 上那些富有成果的枝条只变得更高了——以前交给 junior devs 的 tasks，如今已完全在 automated agents 的能力范围之内。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning1.jpg)

一些预测者认为，这一 process 的最终结果将是所有 human skills 的 devaluing。如果 AI 很快就能比你做得更好，又何必费心 learning _任何东西_？

  我怀疑这是否会成为最终结果。我倾向于认为，在遥远的未来，仍会有人从事属于人的工作，哪怕仅仅是因为某些工作本身就 inherently humanistic。但 medium-term outcome 似乎明确支持了这样一种迫切需求：人需要 learn 更 deep、更 robust 的 skills，才能 compete。

AI 并没有 fundamentally changed learning 所涉及的 effort。_Ultralearning_ 是从一个特定视角写成的：一个渴望 learn，并愿意付出所需辛劳的人。这些人一直是少数，而 AI 无法改变所需的 intrinsic effort。

所以，就占总人口的 proportion 而言，我并不期待出色的 autodidacts 会爆发式增多，正如 Internet 到来时我们也没有看到这种情况。全世界的 knowledge 早已触手可及，但大多数人仍然更愿意去看搞笑视频。AI 当然没有改变这一点。

不过，在 tactical level 上，AI 创造了我写 _Ultralearning_ 时尚不存在的新 possibilities（以及 pitfalls）。那么，让我们沿着书中的九条 principles，看看其中的一些。

## Principle #1：Meta-learning（元学习）

这大概是最需要重写的一章。Self-education 一直受困于 knowledge 的 bootstrapping-problem：当你缺乏用来 organize 一个 effective learning project 的 knowledge 时，你该如何 organize 它？

我在书中提出的 solution 是鼓励人们做 research：在开始之前，弄清一项 skill 如何 works，与 experts 交谈，并 map out 自己需要 learn 的内容。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning2.jpg)

AI 已经 dramatically reduced 做这类 research 的 cost，而且不限于 academic subjects。如今，即使是冷门的 practical skills，也可以被 broken down 为 discrete subtopics、practice activities、facts 清单、concepts 等。

  如今，面对一个新的 topic area，我惯用的 approach 是打开 ChatGPT，让它从我的几个主要问题出发，先对这个 topic 做一次 Deep Research。得到的文档通常还比不上真正的 experts，但我很快就能明确自己需要朝哪些方向继续，以补全 research。

同样，如果你正在 learning 一组不那么 academic 的 skill set，使用 AI 可以找出当前的 best practices，并为你提供 learning project 的 basic building blocks。

做 meta-learning 时，我极少完全停留在 AI responses 之内。找到真正的 expert 或 teacher 的 curriculum，了解其中的 ground truth，总是有益的。如今，有了 AI，要找到这些 teachers、experts，以及引向他们的 organizing paradigms，容易多了。

## Principle #2：Focus（专注）

AI 没有改变这条 principle。Learning 任何东西都需要时间。即使你 efficiently 地做 projects，它们仍然意味着大量工作。如果你无法投入时间，就无法得到 results。

Learning 也需要 attention。如果你无法为一个 project 投入大块不受干扰的时间，就无法 build deep skills 和 understanding。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning3.jpg)

  自 _Ultralearning_ 出版以来，attentional ecosystem 只变得更糟了。我二十岁出头做 projects 时，主要的 distractions 是 Reddit threads，以及偶尔出现的 Facebook post。如今，手机上无穷无尽的 short-form video content，意味着我们可以整天不停地玩这台 attentional slot machine。

 目前，我觉得 AI-generated content 不如 human-generated content 有吸引力，因此我不认为它会让 addictive social media 的问题恶化太多。也许几年后，AI-generated feeds 会比 human-created content 更诱人，到时我就需要修正这一看法。

## Principle #3：Directness（直接性）

对你想要擅长的 skill 进行 Practice。做真实的事情，避免 substitutes。

AI 可能让这件事更难了。因为 AI 如此吸引人，我们很容易转而做 AI-mediated practice，而不是投入 directness 所倡导的那种艰难、令人害怕、有时还让人不自在的 real-world skill。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning4.jpg)

以 language learning 为例。在 _Ultralearning_ 中，我对 Duolingo 这类 apps 提供的 gamified drills 极为怀疑。在我看来，它们遗漏了太多用另一种 language 进行 conversing 的 actual skill，以至于你可能玩了好几年这些游戏，去餐厅点餐时仍然感到不自在。

从那以后，我听到有人声称，他们正在使用 AI 来 learn languages、writing——甚至 social skills(!!)。

当然，我们不难想象，有人本来就在进行 real conversations、publishing essays、参加 social events，只是用 AI 来补强一些 weak points。但更多时候，我担心人们正在利用 AI 所 creates 的 verisimilitude，试图彻底避开真正去做这些事情。

## Principle #4: Drill（原则 #4：专项练习）

directness 的 counterpart 是 drill：将一个 complex skill 进行 breaking down，分成更小的 parts，再对这些更小的 parts 进行 focusing，可以是 in isolation，也可以投入 greater focus，以实现 selective improvement。这些 drills 可以包括 Spanish 的 conjugation exercises、basketball 的 practicing layups、painting 的 making value studies，等等。

在这方面，AI 通过 AI-generated practice problems、flashcards、worksheets 或 feedback，带来了一系列新的机会。

例如，在我的 language learning projects 中，一个主要困难一直是：通过 flashcards 进行 vocabulary study 应占多大比重。一方面，一个 efficient spaced-repetition system，辅以精心设计的 mnemonics，可以让 acquire 几千个 words 的 basic vocabulary 快得多。另一方面，flashcards 可能导致 brittle knowledge，难以将其 generalize 到 real conversations。

我对 flashcards 态度矛盾的一个主要原因是，这个 paradigm 的 assumes 是每个 word 都是一个 atomic fact。但在我们 learn 新 words 时，[我们实际上在 learning 的内容](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/myl/MillerWords1987.pdf)并不仅仅是 definition 或 translation。相反，我们也在 learning contextual associations，了解那个 word 通常如何出现在 spoken 或 written language 中。正是通过这种方式，我们才知道 words _small_ 与 _petite_，或 _big_ 与 _grand_ 之间的 difference。这些 associations 必须 learned implicitly，而不能仅仅作为 definition 的一部分被 memorized。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning5.jpg)

如今，有了 AI，我们可以 generate flashcards，让 to-be-learned word 始终出现在一个 novel sentence 中，从而在提供所需 repetition 的同时，也提供 learning contextual cues 所需的 variation。在我看来，这相较于 flashcard paradigm 是一次重大升级。

Conjugations 是另一个在没有预先准备的 practice questions 时很难 learn 的领域。问题在于，需要 learned 的既不是 fixed association（例如，_agua_ `->` water），也不是 verbalized rule（例如，“将 -ar 进行 change，变为 -o，用于 first-person present tense”），而是一种 [procedural mapping](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2022/02/15/act-r/)，它需要接收 variable input，并给出 variable output。

要 effectively 地 learn 这样的 procedures，我们需要能够 vary 其 input/output relationship 的 flashcards，以展示这个 pattern 的所有 permutations。问题在于，在 AI 出现之前，这一直很难做到。当然，如今我们可以使用 AI 来 generate 同一组 basic practice problems 的 infinite variations，从而解决许多 skills 存在的 material gap。

## Principle #5: Retrieval（原则 #5：提取）

相比 review，recall 能让 Memory 得到更多 strengthened。如果你想将某些内容 learn by heart，就需要 practice remembering 它，而不只是看着它。

我见过许多说法，认为 AI 可以帮助 learning 的这一方面。例如，AI tools 可以根据你正在阅读的书籍 generate quizzes，让你 deepen 对内容的 knowledge。

我对这里的 utility 往往有些怀疑。不是因为 quizzes 或 practice questions 不好（它们当然不是），而是因为 retrieval 的许多价值来自 selecting 你应该 retrieve 哪些 knowledge。

例如，一种 naive 的 retrieval practice 做法，就是针对一篇 text 或一本书中的每个 factual claim 来 quiz 自己。但 learning 的主要目标，很少是对书中每个 factual claim 都形成 complete verbatim memory。相反，我们通常希望能够 restate main ideas，并 understand key points 和 concepts。

有时，我们可能有更个人化的目标，比如 remembering 关键 studies 的 authors，以便日后 research，或 knowing dates，以将 historical events 放入 chronological context。但对一篇 text 中的每一个 fact 进行 memorizing，几乎从来都不是对有限 studying time 的良好利用。

这种担忧并非多余。knowledge 的世界是 infinite。对一篇 text 中每个 fact 进行 memorize 所需的精力，就无法再用于其他 texts。我宁愿 remember 十本书的 gist——它们宏大而重要的 ideas——也不愿 know 其中仅仅一本书所包含的每一点 trivia。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning6.jpg)

由 teacher 设计的 Practice problems 和 quizzes 能避免这个问题，因为 teacher 心中有明确的 educational goals。当他们在 test 中 ask 一个 question 时，是因为他们认为 know 那个 fact 或 idea 很重要。但如果我们给 AI 一篇随意挑选的 text，而不提供这种 pedagogical context，它能聚焦于重要内容的可能性就会低得多——不是因为 AI 的能力不足，而是因为它没有一个有用的目标。如果你让一个人在没有任何 pedagogical goals 的情况下，根据一篇随意挑选的 text 来 generate 一个 quiz，他们也会做出一个糟糕的 quiz。

当然，Retrieval 不需要 quizzes 也能发挥作用。Free recall，也就是你只是尝试尽可能多地 remember 某个 source 中内容的 paradigm，效果非常好，而且绝对不需要 AI。围绕你正在 learning 的 topics 来 writing essays 也一样有效，尽管这可能很快就会成为一门失传的技艺。这些都是 low-tech tools，却能极为有效地 retrieving knowledge。

## Principle #6: Feedback（原则 #6：反馈）

Feedback 对 learning 至关重要。但在 learning 的过程中，我们得到的 feedback 往往是 sparse 或 incomplete 的，这会 slows down progress。

在 symbolic domains 中，skill 主要通过 tokens 和 text 来 mediated，我认为现有的 AI 可以在 enhance feedback 方面做很多事。如果我想作为 writer 获得 improve，就可以让 AI 来 critique 我对 research 的使用、word choice 和 storytelling。如果我想作为 programmer 获得 improve，就可以看到用于 solving 同一 task 的更 efficient 的 design patterns 或 algorithms。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning7.jpg)

 前段时间，我为自己书籍的一个 translation 版本录制了一些 Mandarin 宣传视频。script 是我自己写的，但随后我请 AI 提出建议，它修正了几处我说得不够 idiomatically 的地方。在 AI 出现之前，我得付钱请别人提供这些建议。

在 non-symbolic domains 中，AI 仍然 underperforms 人类，因此 AI feedback 的价值要有限得多。目前，我还无法轻松地使用 AI，就 art、skiing 或 interviewing ability 为我提供 feedback，因此 human feedback 仍然至关重要。

AI 也无法取代来自 environment 的 direct feedback 的必要性。Entrepreneurs 需要有关 product-market fit 的 data。Comedians 需要知道自己的 jokes 是否 funny。像我这样的 Writers 需要知道 audience 已经在想些什么、相信什么。这类 feedback 对 skill 至关重要，而 AI 无法提供替代。

更危险的情况出现在这样一些领域：AI 在理论上可以提供良好的 feedback，但它经过 trained 后却不这样做，因为人们往往不喜欢获得 true feedback。Sycophancy 十分猖獗。对我们中的许多人来说，听到关于自己 ideas 和 skills 的好话，比听到真相更令人向往。

## Principle #7: Retention（原则 #7：保持）

我对 mnemonics 一直感到矛盾。它们可以非常 powerful。恰当的 visual associations 或 spatial memories 的 chaining，可以在 hard-to-associate facts 之间形成 indelible links。但它们也需要一些时间来 learn，而且 apply 起来可能很 time-consuming。

AI 有潜力让 mnemonics 更 valuable。例如，我的朋友、也是我 language-learning 的启发者 [Benny Lewis](https://www.fluentin3months.com/about/) 告诉我，他最近一直在用 AI 帮助自己为 keyword mnemonic 进行 “sounds like” associations 的 generate。

  对于不熟悉这个 method 的人来说，基本 idea 是取一个 foreign language word，通过将它 mapping 到 English（或另一种你熟悉的 language）中一个 similar sounding word or phrase 来 create 一个 phonetic clue，然后将它 visually mapping 到一幅 highly memorable picture。

例如，如果你正尝试 remember French word _chavirer_ -> to capsize，你可以制作一个 “shave an ear” 的 phonetic clue，然后在脑海中想象一只硕大的耳朵坐在独木舟里，一边刮胡子，独木舟一边翻覆。在脑海中 Visualize 这个画面一两次，association 就能牢牢记住；相比之下，direct association 可能需要几十次 repetitions 才能扎根。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning8.jpg)

keyword method 是有效的，但它在 lab experiments 中[并非总是 performed well](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)。原因是，要正确运用它，往往需要太多时间和 training。Modern LLMs 很适合完成 generate 这类 images 所需的 wordplay tasks。

Spacing 是我认为 AI 能有所帮助的另一个领域，尤其是较新的 agentic AI paradigm。在 learning 中 applying spacing 的一个主要障碍是，要 keep track of 所有已经 learned 的内容，并确保一定程度的 regular re-exposure，在安排上简直是一场噩梦。Spaced repetition software 为 flashcards 做到了这一点，但正如前面讨论过的，它们的 applications 相当 narrow。

不过，我很容易想象这样一个未来：AI agent 通过重新呈现你最近 studied 的 material 中的 questions 和 ideas，帮助你 manage workload。在一些指导下，你甚至可以让它针对 major ideas 向你 quiz，从而 solve 前面提到的一些 retrieval problems。

## Principle #8: Intuition（原则 #8：直觉）

Understanding 对 learning 至关重要。但 gaining understanding 的 process 仍然有些神秘，人们对它的了解也仍然很有限。

虽然我总体上赞同 conceptual learning 的 [knowledge-in-pieces model](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-43620-9_26)，即 understandings 通过多次 exposures 逐步 built，但同样显而易见的是，一个 well-chosen analogy、metaphor 或 explanation 可以突然让整个 idea “click”。

在 _Ultralearning_ 中，我分享了 [Feynman Technique](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/the-feynman-technique-explained/)，这是我的一种来历有些存疑的 self-explanations method，我在 [MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/) 期间大量使用过它。基本 method 很简单：

1.  写下你想 explain 的 concept 或 idea。
2.  写出一个 explanation，就像你正在把它 teaching 给别人一样。
3.  每当你卡住时，就回到 study material 和 notes，重新阅读，直到你 understand。

这个 technique 是有效的，但它经常受阻于 #3。如果你即使更深入地阅读 notes 后仍然不 understand，就可能浪费大量时间，试图找到一个更好的 explanation。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning9.jpg)

  同样，如果 conceptual confusion 被一带而过，而不是深入探究，这个 method 就可能适得其反——你可能会绕开自己的无知，而不是直面它。这就是为什么这个 method 会受益于 specificity：如果你在 solving a problem 时遇到困难，就把 teaching 的 topic 设为那个具体的 problem，而不是笼统地讲它所 tests 的 concept。

AI 在 resolve 这两个 problems 方面有巨大的能力。首先，虽然我认为 AI explanations 仍然比好 teachers 稍逊一筹，但差距正在缩小，而且 well-posed questions 通常可以得到 accurate answers。将 AI 用作 Socratic tutor，是它帮助 build understanding 的方式之一。

其次，AIs 可以提出 pointed follow-up questions，揭示你甚至没有意识到自己存在的 gaps in knowledge。我现在经常 upload 自己所写 essays 中 explain 某些 science 或 history 内容的部分，询问 AI 我哪里弄错了。它经常吹毛求疵，但确实也有些时候，我存在 basic misconception。

当然，pitfall 在于，一个能够 explain 任何东西的 on-demand system，也会让人轻易跳过 Feynman Technique 的 steps #1 和 #2。让 AI generate explanation，粗略浏览一遍，然后说服自己原本也能独立 generated 它，是非常容易的。

使用 AI 来 learn 的 risk 在于，完全不 learning 始终是 lowest effort strategy，而大多数 models 的 designed 方式恰好允许你这样做。没有 guardrails 时，默认做法就是跳过 build intuition 所需的 mental work，即便 technology 在理论上可以帮助 constructing 更 deeper understanding。

## Principle #9: Experimentation（原则 #9：实验）

Experimentation，也就是无论在你试图 master 的 skill 内，还是在 learning 本身的 process 中，都尝试不同做法并弄清什么有效的 process，是 _Ultralearning_ 中反复出现的 theme。

新的 AI tools 让这些 possibilities 得以 acceleration。这不仅是因为现在出现了许多新的 learning methods，例如 on-demand Socratic tutoring、procedurally-generated practice problems、knowledge management、mnemonics generation 等，还因为许多看似有用的 applications 实际上是伪装起来的 pitfalls。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning10.jpg)

如果我必须回头重做 _Ultralearning_ 中写过的任何一项挑战，learning 它们的 possibilities 都会发生巨大变化。[MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/) 本可以利用 AI 来 fill in material gaps，给我额外的 practice problems，并在我的 self-explanations 只带来 confusion 时帮助我摆脱困境。[Year Without English](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/the-year-without-english-2/) 本可以拥有 auto-generated flashcards、grammar explanations，以及针对 conversation recordings 的 corrective feedback。我本可以 vibecoded 一款 software，自动针对我的 [portrait drawings](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/portrait-challenge/) 的 accuracy 给出 detailed corrective feedback。

不会改变的，是 learning skills 所需的 mental effort，以及真正 learning 它们时的喜悦与挣扎。尽管我们正经历重大的 technological changes，我仍然坚信，learning 新事物的 value 和 strain 都将是持久不变的。

## 原文

My book [Ultralearning](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/ultralearning/) was published in 2019. It documents the process of intensive self-education that inspired some of my self-guided projects learning languages, computer science, art and more.

The book went on to become a surprise bestseller, with over 200,000 copies sold and dozens of translated editions. To this day, the bulk of new reader emails I get are from people who discovered me through _Ultralearning_.

A question I get asked a lot is how the book would change if it were published today. In 2019, the conversation about AI was still a whisper. Now, it’s deafening.

Today, I’d like to walk through _Ultralearning_ and look at what’s changed, what hasn’t, and what I think the future holds for learning and education.

## What Hasn’t Changed

The basic message of Ultralearning, I believe, still holds up pretty well:

Technology is widening the gulf between the haves and have-nots of human capital. Learning in school is insufficient. To achieve, we need to continually add to our skills and knowledge, and doing so efficiently is imperative given our information-saturated environment.

AI has only accelerated those trends.

While some [early reports](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf) suggested AI might be an equalizer, helping mediocre programmers and writers produce at a higher level, I think those early takes now seem naive. If anything, the fruitful branches of the skill tree for becoming a professional programmer have only gotten higher—with tasks that were previously for junior devs now wholly within the grasp of automated agents.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning1.jpg)

Some prognosticators suggest that the culmination of this process will be the devaluing of all human skills. Why bother learning _anything_ at all if AI will soon do it better than you?

  I’m skeptical of this as a final outcome. I tend to think there will continue to be humans doing human jobs far into the future, if only because certain kinds of work are inherently humanistic. But the medium-term outcome seems to clearly back the urgent need for humans to learn deeper and more robust skills to compete.

AI has not fundamentally changed the effort involved in learning. _Ultralearning_ was written from a particular vantage point: a person eager to learn and willing to do the hard work required. These people have always been a minority, and AI cannot change the intrinsic effort required.

So, as a proportion of the population, I don’t expect an explosion in impressive autodidacts any more than we saw with the arrival of the Internet. The world’s knowledge is already at our fingertips, but most people will still prefer to watch funny videos instead. AI certainly isn’t changing that.

But, at a tactical level, AI has created new possibilities (and pitfalls) that didn’t exist when I wrote _Ultralearning_. So let’s look at some of those, following the nine principles of the book.

## Principle #1: Meta-learning

This is probably the chapter most in need of a rewrite. Self-education has always stumbled on the bootstrapping-problem of knowledge: how do you organize an effective learning project when you lack the knowledge to organize it?

My solution in the book was to encourage people to do research: figure out how a skill works, talk to experts and map out what you need to learn before you start.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning2.jpg)

AI has dramatically reduced the cost of doing this kind of research, and not only for academic subjects. Even obscure practical skills can now be broken down into discrete subtopics, practice activities, lists of facts, concepts and more.

  My go-to approach to tackling a new topic area these days is to fire up ChatGPT and get it to start with a Deep Research on the topic, beginning with some of my major questions. The resulting document isn’t usually on par with genuine experts, but I very quickly narrow in on what sorts of directions I need to take to fill in my research.

Similarly, if you’re learning a less academic skill set, using AI can surface the current best practices and give you the basic building blocks for a learning project.

I very rarely stay totally within AI responses for meta-learning. It’s always good to get to the ground truth of some genuine expert or teacher’s curriculum. Finding those teachers and experts and the organizing paradigms that lead to them is much easier now with AI.

## Principle #2: Focus

AI hasn’t changed this principle. Learning anything requires time. Even when you do projects efficiently, they’re still an enormous amount of work. If you can’t put the time in, you can’t get the results.

Learning also requires attention. If you can’t devote large chunks of undistracted time to a project, you’ll fail to build deep skills and understanding.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning3.jpg)

  The attentional ecosystem has only gotten worse since _Ultralearning_ was published. When I was doing projects in my early twenties, the major distractions were Reddit threads and the occasional Facebook post. Now, an endless treadmill of short-form video content on our phones means we can play the attentional slot machine all day without pause.

 Currently, I see AI-generated content as less appealing than human-generated content, so I don’t see it making the problem of addictive social media much worse. Perhaps in a few years AI-generated feeds will be more enticing than human-created content, and I’ll need to revise this point.

## Principle #3: Directness

Practice the skill you want to get good at. Do the real thing and avoid substitutes.

AI probably makes this harder. Because AI is so compelling, there’s a temptation to do AI-mediated practice rather than engaging in the hard, scary, and sometimes uncomfortable, real-world skill that directness suggests.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning4.jpg)

Take language learning, for instance. In _Ultralearning_, I was highly skeptical of the gamified drills offered by apps like Duolingo. To me, they simply omit so much of the actual skill of conversing in another language that you could play these games for years and still feel uncomfortable ordering food at a restaurant.

Since then, I’ve heard people claim that they’re using AI to learn languages, writing—and even social skills(!!).

Of course, one could easily imagine someone who is having real conversations, publishing essays and attending social events simply using AI to shore up some weak points. But, more often, I worry that people are using the verisimilitude that AI creates to try to avoid doing the real thing entirely.

## Principle #4: Drill

The counterpart to directness is drill: breaking down a complex skill into smaller parts, focusing on those smaller parts either in isolation or with greater focus to make selective improvement. These drills can include conjugation exercises for Spanish, practicing layups for basketball, making value studies for painting, and more.

Here AI presents a whole range of new opportunities through AI-generated practice problems, flashcards, worksheets or feedback.

For instance, one of the major difficulties in my language learning projects had been how much weight to put on vocabulary study through flashcards. On the one hand, an efficient spaced-repetition system, backed by some careful mnemonics, can make it much faster to acquire a few thousand words of basic vocabulary. On the other hand, flashcards can lead to brittle knowledge that is difficult to generalize to real conversations.

A major cause of my ambivalence with flashcards is that the paradigm assumes each word is an atomic fact. But what [we are actually learning](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/myl/MillerWords1987.pdf) when we learn new words is not merely a definition or translation. Instead, we’re also learning contextual associations for how that word typically appears in spoken or written language. It’s how we know the difference between the words _small_ and _petite_, or _big_ and _grand_. These associations have to be learned implicitly, and can’t simply be memorized as part of the definition.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning5.jpg)

Now, with AI, we can generate flashcards that always place the to-be-learned word in a novel sentence, giving us the needed repetition alongside the variation required for learning contextual cues. This, to me, is a major upgrade over the flashcard paradigm.

Conjugations are another area that is difficult to learn without premade practice questions. The issue is that what needs to be learned isn’t a fixed association (e.g., _agua_ `->` water) or a verbalized rule (e.g., “change -ar to -o for first-person present tense”) but rather a [procedural mapping](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2022/02/15/act-r/) that needs to take a variable input and give a variable output.

To learn procedures like this effectively, we need flashcards that vary the input/output relationship to show all permutations of the pattern. The problem is that this used to be hard to do before AI. Now, of course, we can use AI to generate infinite variations of the same basic practice problems, which solves the material gap that exists for a lot of skills.

## Principle #5: Retrieval

Memory is strengthened more by recall than by review. If you want to learn something by heart, you need to practice remembering it, not just looking at it.

I’ve seen a lot of claims that AI can be helpful with this aspect of learning. For instance, AI tools can generate quizzes based on the books you’re reading allowing you to deepen your knowledge of the content.

I tend to be a bit skeptical about the utility here. Not because quizzes or practice questions are bad (they certainly aren’t), but a lot of the value in retrieval comes from selecting what knowledge you ought to retrieve.

For instance, a naive way to do retrieval practice is simply to quiz yourself on every factual claim made in a text or book. But rarely is the main goal of learning a complete verbatim memory of every factual claim in a book. Instead, we typically want to be able to restate the main ideas and understand the key points and concepts.

Sometimes we may have more idiosyncratic goals, like remembering the authors of key studies for future research or knowing the dates to put historical events inside a chronological context. But memorizing every single fact in a text is almost never a good use of limited studying time.

This is not an idle concern. The world of knowledge is infinite. The effort needed to memorize every fact from one text is effort that cannot be spent on other texts. I’d much rather remember the gist of ten books—their big, important ideas—than know every bit of trivia contained in just one of them.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning6.jpg)

Practice problems and quizzes designed by a teacher avoid this problem because the teacher has in mind clear educational goals. When they ask a question on a test, it is because they think it is important to know that fact or idea. But if we give an AI a random text without this pedagogical context, the chance that it’s going to narrow in on what is important is much lower—not because of insufficiently capable AI, but because it doesn’t have a useful goal. If you asked a human to generate a quiz from a random text absent any pedagogical goals, they’d also make a bad quiz.

Retrieval, of course, doesn’t need quizzes to work. Free recall, the paradigm where you simply try to remember as much as you can from a source, works remarkably well and definitely doesn’t require AI. So does writing essays about topics you’re learning, which may soon become a lost art. These are low-tech tools that work amazingly well for retrieving knowledge.

## Principle #6: Feedback

Feedback is essential for learning. But we often get sparse or incomplete feedback in our learning efforts, which slows down progress.

In symbolic domains, where the skill is primarily mediated through tokens and text, I think currently-existing AI can do a ton to enhance feedback. If I’m trying to improve as a writer, I can get AI to critique my use of research, word choice and storytelling. If I’m trying to improve as a programmer, I can be shown more efficient design patterns or algorithms for solving the same task.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning7.jpg)

 A while back, I recorded some promotional videos in Mandarin for a translation of my book. I wrote the script myself, but then I asked AI to offer suggestions, and it fixed some places where I wasn’t speaking very idiomatically. Before AI, I would have had to pay someone for that advice.

In non-symbolic domains, where AI still underperforms human beings, the value of AI feedback is a lot more limited. I can’t easily use AI to give me feedback on art, skiing or interviewing ability at the moment, so human feedback remains essential.

AI also can’t replace the need for direct feedback from the environment. Entrepreneurs need data about product-market fit. Comedians need to know whether their jokes are funny. Writers like me need to know what their audience already thinks and believes. That kind of feedback is essential to the skill, and AI can’t offer a substitute.

The more dangerous cases are areas where AI could give good feedback, in theory, but it’s been trained not to because people often don’t like getting true feedback. Sycophancy is rampant. For a lot of us, hearing nice things about our ideas and skills is more desirable than hearing the truth.

## Principle #7: Retention

I’ve always had mixed feelings about mnemonics. They can be incredibly powerful. The right chaining of visual associations or spatial memories can make indelible links between hard-to-associate facts. But they also take a while to learn and can be time-consuming to apply.

AI has the potential to make mnemonics more valuable. My friend and language-learning inspiration, [Benny Lewis](https://www.fluentin3months.com/about/), for instance, told me that he’s been using AI these days to help him generate “sounds like” associations for the keyword mnemonic.

  For those unfamiliar with the method, the basic idea is to take a foreign language word and create a phonetic clue by mapping it to a similar sounding word or phrase in English (or another language you know well) and then visually mapping that to a highly memorable picture.

For instance, if you’re trying to remember the French word _chavirer_ -> to capsize, you can make a phonetic clue of “shave an ear,” then you have a mental picture of an oversized ear sitting in a canoe, shaving its beard while the canoe flips over. Visualize that mentally once or twice and the association sticks, whereas it may take dozens of repetitions for the direct association to take root.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning8.jpg)

The keyword method works, but it [hasn’t always performed well](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/) in lab experiments. The reason is that it often takes too much time and training to get right. Modern LLMs are well-suited to the kind of wordplay tasks required to generate these sorts of images.

Spacing is another area where I expect AI to be some help, particularly the newer agentic AI paradigm. A major hiccup in applying spacing in learning is that it is a logistical nightmare to keep track of all the things you’ve learned and ensure some measure of regular re-exposure. Spaced repetition software does this for flashcards, but, as already discussed, those have fairly narrow applications.

However, I can easily imagine a future where an AI agent helps you manage your workload by resurfacing questions and ideas from material you’ve recently studied. With some guidance, you may even solve some of the retrieval problems mentioned earlier by getting it to quiz you on the major ideas.

## Principle #8: Intuition

Understanding is central to learning. But the process of gaining understanding is still somewhat mysterious and poorly understood.

While I’m generally in favor of a [knowledge-in-pieces model](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-43620-9_26) of conceptual learning, where understandings are built bit by bit through many exposures, it’s also clear that a well-chosen analogy, metaphor or explanation can suddenly make the entire idea “click.”

In _Ultralearning_, I shared the [Feynman Technique](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/the-feynman-technique-explained/) my somewhat-apocryphal method of self-explanations that I made heavy use of during the [MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/). The basic method is simple:

1.  Write down the concept or idea you want to explain.
2.  Write out an explanation as if you were teaching it to someone else.
3.  Whenever you get stuck, go back to your study material and notes and re-read until you understand.

The technique works, but it is often frustrated by #3. If you don’t understand, even after reading the notes more deeply, you may waste a lot of time trying to find a better explanation.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning9.jpg)

  Similarly, the method can backfire when conceptual confusion is glossed over rather than dug into—you may maneuver around your own ignorance rather than confronting it. This is why the method benefits from specificity: if you’re having difficulty solving a problem, make the topic of your teaching that exact problem, not the concept it tests in general terms.

AI has massive power to resolve both of these problems. For starters, while I find AI explanations are still somewhat inferior to good teachers, the gap is closing, and well-posed questions can generally get accurate answers. Using AI as a Socratic tutor is one of the ways it can help build understanding.

Second, AIs can ask pointed follow-up questions to reveal gaps in knowledge you don’t even know you are missing. I now frequently upload portions of essays I write where I explain some bit of science or history and ask the AI what I’m getting wrong. Often it nitpicks, but there are definitely occasions where I have a basic misconception.

The pitfall, of course, is that an on-demand system that can explain anything can also make it easy to skip steps #1 and #2 of the Feynman Technique. It’s very easy to ask AI to generate the explanation, skim through it and convince yourself you could have generated it on your own.  

The risk of using AI to learn is that not learning at all is always the lowest effort strategy, and most models are designed to allow you to do exactly that. Without guardrails, the default is to skip over the mental work needed to build intuition, even if the technology can, in theory, assist in constructing a deeper understanding.

## Principle #9: Experimentation

Experimentation, the process of trying out different things and figuring out what works, both within the skill you’re trying to master and in the process of learning itself, is a recurring theme in _Ultralearning_.

The new AI tools offer an acceleration of these possibilities. Not only because many new possible methods for learning now exist, such as on-demand Socratic tutoring, procedurally-generated practice problems, knowledge management, mnemonics generation and more, but also because many of the seemingly-useful applications are really pitfalls in disguise.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ultralearning10.jpg)

If I had to go back and redo any of the challenges I wrote about in _Ultralearning_, the possibilities for learning them would have changed dramatically. The [MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/) could have used AI to fill in material gaps, given me extra practice problems and gotten me unstuck when my self-explanations only led to confusion. The [Year Without English](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/the-year-without-english-2/) could have had auto-generated flashcards, grammar explanations and corrective feedback on conversation recordings. I could have vibecoded software that could automatically give me detailed corrective feedback on the accuracy of my [portrait drawings](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/portrait-challenge/).

What wouldn’t have changed is the mental effort involved in learning skills, nor the joy and struggle in actually learning them. Despite the momentous technological changes we’re experiencing, I am still convinced that both the value and strain in learning new things will be an enduring constant.
