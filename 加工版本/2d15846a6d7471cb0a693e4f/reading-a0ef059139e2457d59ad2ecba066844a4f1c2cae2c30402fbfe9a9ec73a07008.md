# 我们只招聘最潮的人｜We only hire the trendiest

---

## 正文

我认识的一个人，姑且叫他 Mike，在 Microsoft 的 contract role 被 laid off 后正在找工作，我认识的许多人都有过这样的遭遇。和我一样，Mike 在 industry 已有 11 年。和我不同的是，他在那些时髦的 companies 没多少熟人，所以我把他的 resume 转给了我认识的一些 engineers，他们所在的 companies 都急着 hiring。我的 engineering 朋友们觉得 Mike 的 resume 没问题，但大多数 recruiters 在 resume screening phase 就 rejected 了他。

当我问他为什么会被 rejected 时，得到的典型回答是：

1.  Tech experience 属于 irrelevant tech
2.  “Experience 太 random，涉及 payments、mobile、data analytics 和 UX。”
3.  Contractors 通常在 technically 方面不是最强的

这个回答来自一位 recruiter，由一位 engineer 转述给我；那位 engineer 对 recruiter 的回答感到难以置信。为了方便称呼，姑且把这家 company 叫作 TrendCo。它是成千上万家声称自己拥有 world class engineers、只 hire 最优秀的人等等的 companies 之一。这里说的是某一家 company，但它代表了一大类 companies，也代表了 Mike 得到的那些回答。

总之，(1) 的潜台词是：“Mike 是个 .NET dev，而我们不喜欢有 Windows experience 的人。”

我熟悉 TrendCo 的 tech stack，多位 employees 都告诉过我，那是“一场灾难”。他们的 core systems 在不到 1k QPS 时就 top out，这导致它们在 load 下 go down。Mike 曾参与的 systems 能 handle 高出 multiple orders of magnitude 的 load，但他的 experience 显然是 irrelevant。

(2) 很难理解。我去 TrendCo 参加过 interviewed，它的卖点之一就是：这是一家 startup，你能在那里做许多不同的事情。TrendCo 几乎只 hires generalists，但 Mike 对他们来说显然又太 general 了。

把 (3) 和 (1) 放在一起，就能看出 TrendCo 对 Mike 真正不满的是什么。他不是他们喜欢的类型。TrendCo 的 median employee 是刚从大概五所“顶尖”学校之一毕业、拥有 0-2 年 experience 的人。他们有少量 experienced hires，但不多，而且大多数 experienced hires 的 resume 上都有些时髦的东西，而不是 Microsoft 这样无聊的老 company。

无论你是否认为有自己喜欢的类型、并 rejecting 不属于这一类型的人有什么不妥，正如 [Thomas Ptacek 所指出的](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11290662)，如果你喜欢的类型也是其他所有人都在争抢的类型，那么“你就是在与 market 上最富有（或资金过剩最严重）的 tech companies competing for talent”。

如果[你看看 new grad hiring data](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/u/1/d/1UnLz40Our1Ids-O0sz26uPNCF6cQjwosrZQY4VLdflU/htmlview?pli=1&sle=true#)，似乎 FB 正向拥有 zero experience 的人提供 > $100k/ salary, $100k signing bonus，以及 $150k in RSUs, for an amortized total comp > $160k/yr，其中包括 $240k in the first year. Google's package has > $100k salary、在 $10k range, and $187k 范围内的 variable signing bonus，以 RSUs 形式提供。这个数额比 FB 略低，但远高于大多数声称只 hire 最优秀的人的 companies 愿意为 new grad 支付的数额。请记住，[对于 contested candidates，compensation 可能高得多](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:C)，而且[如果你不是见过当今 competitive offers 是什么样的 hiring manager，那么 experienced candidates 的 compensation 很可能比你预想的更高](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11314449)。

由于追逐拥有最抢手 qualifications 的人，TrendCo 把自己的选择缩减到了两种：要么为 employees 支付高得离谱的费用，要么提供 non-competitive compensation packages。TrendCo 选择了后者，这在一定程度上解释了为什么他们的 senior devs 比例那么低——随着你的 senior 程度提高，compensation delta 也会 increases；当你提供的报酬比竞争对手少 $150k/yr 时，就必须拿出极有说服力的说辞，才能让对方选择 TrendCo。而且，随着人们的 experience 增多，他们也越不容易相信说辞中解释 stock options 有多值钱的那一部分。

先说清楚，我并不反感拥有时髦 backgrounds 的人。我认识不少这样的人，他们有无可挑剔的 interviewing skills，上一次找工作时拿到了 5-10 份很有吸引力的 offers。我曾和这样一个人共事：他刚毕业，total comp package 就超过 $200k/yr，而且每一分钱都花得值。但请稍微想一想。他从六家不同的 companies 拿到了很有吸引力的 offers，而他最多只会 accept 其中一份。包括午餐和 phone screens 在内，这些 companies 每家平均花了八个小时 interviewing 他。而且，由于他们实在太想 hire 他，那些真正认真的 companies 又各自平均花了五个小时的 engineer time，试图说服他接受自己的 offer。由于这些 companies 平均只有 ⅙ 的 chance 能 hiring 这个人，它们至少得花费 expected (8+5) \* 6 = 78 小时的 engineer time[1](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:T)。拥有出色 backgrounds 的人，平均而言确实相当出色，但他们真的很难 hire。hire 被 underrated 的人要容易得多，尤其是在你不支付 market rates 的时候。

对于这种一门心思 hiring 拥有时髦 backgrounds 的人的做法，我从桌子两边都见识过，而从任何一边看都很荒谬。

从 hiring 中 referring 的一方来说，我曾试图让自己所在的一家 startup 去 hire 我见过的最有趣、最有创造力的 programmer。他因为大学时的 low GPA，多年来可惜地一直处于 underemployed 状态。我们拒绝 hire 他，别人告诉我，他的 low GPA 意味着他不可能很聪明。几年后，Google 愿意给他一个机会，从那以后他就一直表现出色。实际上，是他说服我加入了 Google，而且[在 Google，我曾试图 hire 我认识的最 productive 的 programmers 之一，而他很快就被一位 recruiter 以不够 technical 为由 rejected 了](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)。

从 hiring 中 candidate 的一方来说，我既经历过炙手可热，也经历过几乎 unhireable。因为我的 undergrad 是在 Wisconsin 读的，而它是那 25 所声称自己跻身 top 10 cs/engineering school 的学校之一，所以毕业时 recruiters 几乎踏破了我的门槛。但这很荒唐——我上过 Wisconsin 并不能说明我本人怎么样；我只是碰巧在 Wisconsin 州长大。如果我在 Utah 长大，大概最后就会去 Utah 上学。当我和上过 Utah、Boise State 这类学校的人交流时，发现他们的 education 和我的基本一样。Wisconsin 作为 engineering school 的 rank，来自那些做出出色 research 的 professors，而这与[实际 teaching undergrads 的 effectiveness](https://danluu.com/teach-debugging/) 充其量也只是 weakly correlated。尽管我获得的 engineering education 在其他数百所学校也能获得，我却很容易就拿到了 interviews，并找到了一份好工作。

我在 [Centaur](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B01FSZU6FK/ref=as_li_qf_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=B01FSZU6FK&linkId=d15e514c6ecefa224be8f05d4d5837e3) 的那份好工作上干了 7.5 年。在 Austin 那些成立已有一段时间的 hardware companies 中，Centaur 的声誉相当好，我很容易就在当地的 hardware companies 中挑选工作。但我不认识任何听说过 Centaur 的 software 从业者，因此我无法获得大多数 software companies 的 interview。甚至有几次，我已经有了非常有力的 internal referrals，recruiters 却仍然不想和我谈，我觉得这很好笑，我的朋友们则觉得很沮丧。

即使能获得 interviews，过程也往往不顺利。典型的 rejection 理由大致是：“我们这里每天 process 数百万笔 transactions，确实需要一个拥有更多 relevant experience、无需 ramping up 就能 handle 这些事情的人。”后来 Google 愿意给我一个机会，我成为某个 project 上第二个开始认真对待 deep learning performance 的人；直到我加入前不久，它还只是一个 20%-time project。[我们 built 了世界上最快的 deep learning system](https://www.google.com/patents/US20160342889)。据我所知，他们现在已经做到那个 project 的 Nth generation 了，但即使我们 built 的 first generation，在此后数年里，其 per-rack performance 和 performance per dollar 也都优于当时任何其他 production system（当然，不包括那个 project 的 follow-ons）。

我在 Google 时，recruiters 总是不停地联系我，介绍工作机会。如今我在无聊的老 Microsoft，主动联系我的 recruiters 远没有那么多了。我一直在考虑找工作[2](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:P)，也想知道，如果真去找，我会有多时髦。属于 irrelevant tech 的 Experience？符合！Random experience？符合！Contractor？嗯，不是。不过三项占两项也不错。

我在这里想说的不是我个人怎么样。而是有这样一个人[3](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:M)，在不同时期对 employers 的吸引力天差地别，主要原因却是一些与实际 productivity 没多大关系的表面因素。在最终进入 Google 的人当中，这样的经历非常普遍。如果你在他们去 Google 工作之前就 hired 了他们，或许就捡到大便宜了！但没有人（除了 Google）愿意冒这个险。多花钱换取一个已知水平的人，自然有其道理；但像 TrendCo 这样不愿意这么做的 company，却只追逐拥有时髦 resumes 的人，从而 cripples 自己的 hiring pipeline。如果一个人在 Google 工作之前你不愿 hire，工作之后却愿意，那么你主要知道的，只是这个人在 whiteboard algorithms quizzes 上 above average（或者某一天运气不错）。

我并不是要专门挑 TrendCo 这类 startups 的毛病。无聊的老 companies 也有它们自己认定的时髦 background。我的一个朋友急着 hire，但我转给他的某些 resumes，他根本没法处理，因为他的 group 不允许 hire 任何没有 degree 的人。我认识的另一个人也处境类似，因为他的 group 有一条 bright-line rule，导致他们 reject 那些目前没有 employed 的人。

这些 decisions 不仅对 companies 而言是 non-optimal，还会在 employment outcomes 中造成 path dependence，使单个好（或坏）事件的影响跟随人们数十年。在各种 fields 关于 career earnings 的 literature 中，你都能看到类似的 effects[4](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:C)。

[Thomas Ptacek 有这么一句精彩的话](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=7260087)：“我们 interview 的一些人，此前唯一的 work experience 是 "Line of Business .NET Developer"，结果他们却向我们展示如何 write exploits，针对那些涉及 Fourier transforms 和需要 6 小时才能 run 的 BKZ lattice reduction steps 的 elliptic curve partial nonce bias attacks。”如果你所在的 company 不会因为别人不够时髦就直接 reject 他们，你就会听到许多这样的故事。我共事过的一些最优秀的人，上的是你从未听说过的学校，在进入 Google 之前，待的也是你从未听说过的 companies。有些人至今仍在你从未听说过的 companies。

如果你读过 [Zach Holman](https://zachholman.com/talk/firing-people) 的文章，可能记得，当他说自己被 fired 时，有人回应说：“如果一个 employer 决定 fire 你，那你不仅是在工作上失败了，作为一个人也失败了。”许多人把 employment status 和 credentials 当作衡量个人内在价值的 measures。但这些 success 标志，乃至 success 本身，都有很大一部分取决于运气。

### Solutions（解决办法）？

我能理解为什么会这样。在 individual level 上，我们容易犯 [fundamental attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_attribution_error)。在 organizational level 上，fast growing organizations 把很大一部分时间耗在 interviews 上，而减少 interviewing 所花时间的明显办法，就是只 interview 具有“good” qualifications 的人。遗憾的是，当你和其他所有人都在争抢同一个极小的 pool 时，这样做是 counterproductive 的。

下面是一些初步想法。我愿意听取更好的建议！

#### Moneyball（点球成金）

Billy Beane 和 Paul Depodesta 接手了 Oakland A's——一个 budget 远不及 top teams 的 baseball franchise——通过寻找并“hiring”那些相对于其 price 而言被 statistically underrated 的 players，打造出了一支可以说是 baseball 中最好的 team。我觉得真正令人惊叹的是，他们公开谈论过自己的做法，随后 Michael Lewis 还写了一本名为 [Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=65d86d3a72b4c1ba73e8d3d52796eae1) 的书，讲述他们如何这样做。尽管如此广为人知，仍然过了好几年，才有足够多的 competitors 充分领会，以至于 A's 的 strategy 不再为他们带来巨大的 edge。

你在 software hiring 中也能看到完全一样的事情。Thomas Ptacek 至少在过去半个十年里，也许更久，一直在谈论他们如何在 Matasano hired 那些 unusually effective 的人。Google 的高层经常谈论他们掌握的 hiring data，以及哪些做法没有奏效。我记得他们多年前就说过，专注于 top schools 并不 effective，也没有找到 performance 更好的 employees，但这并没有阻止 TrendCo 把 hiring efforts 集中在 top schools 上。

#### Training / mentorship（培训 / 指导）

你会看到很多关于 moneyball 的讨论，但不知为什么，人们对于……trainingball？Practiceball？却没有那么兴奋。不管你想给这种做法起什么名字，它就是把那些并非“the best”的人带进来，教他们如何成为“the best”。

这也是一个很容易透过 sports 看出 impact 的例子，因为有大量优质的 performance data。既然现在是 basketball season，我们不妨以 college basketball 为例：我们能 identify 出少数几个 programs，它们经常接收 unremarkable inputs，却 produce good outputs。而且，它们面对的 competitors 中，每个 team 都被期望去 coach 和 train 自己的 players。

说到 tech companies，大多数 competition 甚至根本没有尝试。在 median large company，你会接受几天的“orientation”，其中大多是 legal 的套话和 paperwork，偶尔还会有“training”，通常就是一套视频和一套 multiple-choice questions，提供这些是出于 compliance 的考虑，而不是为了教会任何人任何东西。你还会被分配一位 mentor，而这位 mentor 很可能不会提供任何实际的 mentorship。Startups 往往更糟！要比这做得更好并不难。

想想 companies 在 [hiring 和 retaining](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11314449) “the best”上花了多少钱，你会以为它们至少也会拿出其中一个（non-zero）fraction 用于 training。同样相当奇怪的是，companies 在试图 recruit 时，竟然不更加重视 training 和 mentorship。我在特定 roles 中学到的具体东西，对我而言价值极大，但几乎总是出于幸运的巧合，或者是我特意主动去做的事情。大多数 companies 并不重视这些。当然，recruiters 会告诉你，“你在这里会比在 Google 学到多得多的东西，这会让你更有价值”，暗示这值得你接受 $150k/yr 的 pay cut；但如果你问他们，具体做了什么来营造比 Google 更好的 learning environment，他们从来都拿不出一个好答案。

#### Process / tools / culture（流程 / 工具 / 文化）

我曾在两家都有 effectively infinite resources 可用于 tooling 的 companies 工作。其中一家，我们姑且叫它 ToolCo，确实非常重视 tooling，并在 tools 上投入巨大。人们用“神奇”“我见过最好的”和“我简直不敢相信这竟然能做到”之类的话描述那里的 tooling。我也理解为什么。比如，如果你想 build 一个有 millions of lines of code 的 project，他们的 build system 能让这件事在 5s 到 20s 之间完成（假设你没有 enable [LTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interprocedural_optimization)，或任何其他无法被 parallelized 的东西）[5](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:B)。在平常一天的工作中，你会用到多个看起来很神奇的 tools，因为它们远远领先于外界能用到的东西。

另一家，我们姑且叫它 ProdCo，[嘴上重视 tooling，却并不真正看重它](http://yosefk.com/blog/people-can-read-their-managers-mind.html)。人们描述 ProdCo 的 tools 时，会用“world class 的糟糕 software”“我的 productive 程度比以前在其他任何地方都低 2x”，以及“我简直不敢相信这竟然能做到”之类的话。ProdCo 有一篇关于新 build system 的 paper；他们声称的 parallelization/caching 所带来的 speedup、onboarding time 和 reliability 等 numbers，至少比 ToolCo 对应的 numbers 差 two orders of magnitude。而且，以我的经验，实际 numbers 比 paper 中声称的还要差。在 ProdCo 工作一天，你会用到多个 tools，它们在多个 dimensions 上都比 ToolCo 对应的 tools 差 multiple orders of magnitude。这些事情累积起来，很容易比“只 hiring the best”产生更大的影响。

Processes 和 culture 也很重要。我曾在一个既不 use version control，也没有 bug tracker 的 team 工作。[Joel test](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/fog0000000043.html) 上的每一个不假思索就该做对的 item，都有一些 teams 会做出错误选择。

虽然我只在一个完全 failed the Joel test 的 team 工作过（他们在 12 项中得了 1 分），但我工作过的每一个 team，都有明显的 deficiencies，在 technically 层面上 fix 它们很 trivial（不过在 culturally 层面上有时很 difficult）。我在 Google 时，我们 team 分处不同 locations 的两半之间，存在非常严重的 communication problems。我的 fix 简单得不能再简单：我开始为我们所有的 local meetings 和 discussions 打出 meeting notes，并接受 remote team 针对 notes 中令他们意外的事情提出的问题。这是任何人都能做的事，却给整个 team 带来了巨大的 productivity improvement。我真的从未遇到过一种 environment，让你无法通过如此 trivial 的事情来 massively improve productivity。有时人们并不同意（例如，让那个 non-version-control-using-team 开始 use version control 花了好几个月），不过那是另一篇文章的话题了。

在大多数 companies，Programmers 都被严重 underutilized 了。[hiring “the best”，然后又 crippling 他们](https://danluu.com/wat/)，究竟有什么意义？你完全可以通过 hiring 并不起眼的人，并为他们创造成功的条件，获得更好的 results，而且[这样便宜得多](https://twitter.com/patio11/status/706884144538648576)。

### 结论（Conclusion）

当我开始 programming 时，我常听人说 programmers 很接地气，不像那些精英主义者，连穿着都像制服一样，非得西装领带不可。你甚至可以穿 T 恤上班！但如果你觉得 programmers 并不精英主义，不妨哪天穿着西装、打着领带去参加 interview。你得格外努力，才能证明自己并不是一个糟糕的 cultural fit。我们喜欢认为，自己不同于那些根据外表评判人的行业，但我们做的其实是同一件事，只不过，我们不是说别人因为不打领带而是 bad fit，而是说他们因为打领带而是 bad fit；我们也不是说别人因为没有合适的 pedigree 而不够聪明……等等，这完全就是一回事。

4.  我不打算做 literature review，因为将 career earnings 与 external shocks 联系起来的 studies 实在太多了，但我要 cite 一个我觉得有趣的 result，即 [Lisa Kahn 的 2010 年 Labour Economics paper](http://citec.repec.org/d/eee/labeco/v_17_y_2010_i_2_p_303-316.html)。

    已有许多 studies 表明，对于某种特定的 negative shock（比如 recession），在这种 negative shock 期间毕业会 reduces lifetime earnings。但其中大多数 studies 表明，随着时间推移，这种 effect 会变小。当 Kahn 把 national unemployment 作为 economy 状况的 proxy 来考察时，她发现了同样的情况。但当 Kahn 考察 _state level unemployment_ 时，她发现这种 effect 实际上会随着时间推移而 compounded。

    关于 long run 会发生什么，overall evidence 是 equivocal。如果你深入查找，会发现一些 studies 中，earnings 在“仅仅”15 年后才 normalizes，造成了很大、但实际上是 one-off 的 earnings loss；另一些 studies 中，effect 则随着时间推移而恶化。这些 results 大多在 technically 意义上并不 contradictory，因为它们考察的是人们获得第一份工作时 economic distress 的不同 causes，而 results 的 differences 也可能是因为不同 circumstances 无法 generalize。但那个“好”的 result 是，一次糟糕的挫折之后，earnings 要花 15 年才能 normalize。即使对 literature 作非常乐观的解读，也能看出 external events 能够、也确实会对人们的 careers 产生非常大的 effects。而如果你想要一个关于“坏”case 的 bound 的 estimate，不妨看看，例如，[Guiso、Sapienza 和 Zingales 的 paper，其中声称将一座城市如今的 productivity 与这座城市在 1000 年是否有 bishop 联系起来](http://www.nber.org/papers/w14278)。

## 原文

An acquaintance of mine, let’s call him Mike, is looking for work after getting laid off from a contract role at Microsoft, which has happened to a lot of people I know. Like me, Mike has 11 years in industry. Unlike me, he doesn't know a lot of folks at trendy companies, so I passed his resume around to some engineers I know at companies that are desperately hiring. My engineering friends thought Mike's resume was fine, but most recruiters rejected him in the resume screening phase.

When I asked why he was getting rejected, the typical response I got was:

1.  Tech experience is in irrelevant tech
2.  "Experience is too random, with payments, mobile, data analytics, and UX."
3.  Contractors are generally not the strongest technically

This response is something from a recruiter that was relayed to me through an engineer; the engineer was incredulous at the response from the recruiter. Just so we have a name, let's call this company TrendCo. It's one of the thousands of companies that claims to have world class engineers, hire only the best, etc. This is one company in particular, but it's representative of a large class of companies and the responses Mike has gotten.

Anyway, (1) is code for “Mike's a .NET dev, and we don't like people with Windows experience”.

I'm familiar with TrendCo's tech stack, which multiple employees have told me is “a tire fire”. Their core systems top out under 1k QPS, which has caused them to go down under load. Mike has worked on systems that can handle multiple orders of magnitude more load, but his experience is, apparently, irrelevant.

(2) is hard to make sense of. I've interviewed at TrendCo and one of the selling points is that it's a startup where you get to do a lot of different things. TrendCo almost exclusively hires generalists but Mike is, apparently, too general for them.

(3), combined with (1), gets at what TrendCo's real complaint with Mike is. He's not their type. TrendCo's median employee is a recent graduate from one of maybe five “top” schools with 0-2 years of experience. They have a few experienced hires, but not many, and most of their experienced hires have something trendy on their resume, not a boring old company like Microsoft.

Whether or not you think there's anything wrong with having a type and rejecting people who aren't your type, as [Thomas Ptacek has observed](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11290662), if your type is the same type everyone else is competing for, “you are competing for talent with the wealthiest (or most overfunded) tech companies in the market”.

If [you look at new grad hiring data](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/u/1/d/1UnLz40Our1Ids-O0sz26uPNCF6cQjwosrZQY4VLdflU/htmlview?pli=1&sle=true#), it looks like FB is offering people with zero experience > $100k/ salary, $100k signing bonus, and $150k in RSUs, for an amortized total comp > $160k/yr, including $240k in the first year. Google's package has > $100k salary, a variable signing bonus in the $10k range, and $187k in RSUs. That comes in a bit lower than FB, but it's much higher than most companies that claim to only hire the best are willing to pay for a new grad. Keep in mind that [compensation can go much higher for contested candidates](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:C), and that [compensation for experienced candidates is probably higher than you expect if you're not a hiring manager who's seen what competitive offers look like today](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11314449).

By going after people with the most sought after qualifications, TrendCo has narrowed their options down to either paying out the nose for employees, or offering non-competitive compensation packages. TrendCo has chosen the latter option, which partially explains why they have, proportionally, so few senior devs -- the compensation delta increases as you get more senior, and you have to make a really compelling pitch to someone to get them to choose TrendCo when you're offering $150k/yr less than the competition. And as people get more experience, they're less likely to believe the part of the pitch that explains how much the stock options are worth.

Just to be clear, I don't have anything against people with trendy backgrounds. I know a lot of these people who have impeccable interviewing skills and got 5-10 strong offers last time they looked for work. I've worked with someone like that: he was just out of school, his total comp package was north of $200k/yr, and he was worth every penny. But think about that for a minute. He had strong offers from six different companies, of which he was going to accept at most one. Including lunch and phone screens, the companies put in an average of eight hours apiece interviewing him. And because they wanted to hire him so much, the companies that were really serious spent an average of another five hours apiece of engineer time trying to convince him to take their offer. Because these companies had, on average, a ⅙ chance of hiring this person, they have to spend at least an expected (8+5) \* 6 = 78 hours of engineer time[1](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:T). People with great backgrounds are, on average, pretty great, but they're really hard to hire. It's much easier to hire people who are underrated, especially if you're not paying market rates.

I've seen this hyperfocus on hiring people with trendy backgrounds from both sides of the table, and it's ridiculous from both sides.

On the referring side of hiring, I tried to get a startup I was at to hire the most interesting and creative programmer I've ever met, who was tragically underemployed for years because of his low GPA in college. We declined to hire him and I was told that his low GPA meant that he couldn't be very smart. Years later, Google took a chance on him and he's been killing it since then. He actually convinced me to join Google, and [at Google, I tried to hire one of the most productive programmers I know, who was promptly rejected by a recruiter for not being technical enough](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/).

On the candidate side of hiring, I've experienced both being in demand and being almost unhireable. Because I did my undergrad at Wisconsin, which is one of the 25 schools that claims to be a top 10 cs/engineering school, I had recruiters beating down my door when I graduated. But that's silly -- that I attended Wisconsin wasn't anything about me; I just happened to grow up in the state of Wisconsin. If I grew up in Utah, I probably would have ended up going to school at Utah. When I've compared notes with folks who attended schools like Utah and Boise State, their education is basically the same as mine. Wisconsin's rank as an engineering school comes from having professors who do great research which is, at best, weakly correlated to [effectiveness at actually teaching undergrads](https://danluu.com/teach-debugging/). Despite getting the same engineering education you could get at hundreds of other schools, I had a very easy time getting interviews and finding a great job.

I spent 7.5 years in that great job, at [Centaur](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B01FSZU6FK/ref=as_li_qf_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=B01FSZU6FK&linkId=d15e514c6ecefa224be8f05d4d5837e3). Centaur has a pretty strong reputation among hardware companies in Austin who've been around for a while, and I had an easy time shopping for local jobs at hardware companies. But I don't know of any software folks who've heard of Centaur, and as a result I couldn't get an interview at most software companies. There were even a couple of cases where I had really strong internal referrals and the recruiters still didn't want to talk to me, which I found funny and my friends found frustrating.

When I could get interviews, they often went poorly. A typical rejection reason was something like “we process millions of transactions per day here and we really need someone with more relevant experience who can handle these things without ramping up”. And then Google took a chance on me and I was the second person on a project to get serious about deep learning performance, which was a 20%-time project until just before I joined. [We built the fastest deep learning system in the world](https://www.google.com/patents/US20160342889). From what I hear, they're now on the Nth generation of that project, but even the first generation thing we built had better per-rack performance and performance per dollar than any other production system out there for years (excluding follow-ons to that project, of course).

While I was at Google I had recruiters pinging me about job opportunities all the time. And now that I'm at boring old Microsoft, I don't get nearly as many recruiters reaching out to me. I've been considering looking for work[2](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:P) and I wonder how trendy I'll be if I do. Experience in irrelevant tech? Check! Random experience? Check! Contractor? Well, no. But two out of three ain't bad.

My point here isn't anything about me. It's that here's this person[3](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:M) who has wildly different levels of attractiveness to employers at various times, mostly due to superficial factors that don't have much to do with actual productivity. This is a really common story among people who end up at Google. If you hired them before they worked at Google, you might have gotten a great deal! But no one (except Google) was willing to take that chance. There's something to be said for paying more to get a known quantity, but a company like TrendCo that isn't willing to do that cripples its hiring pipeline by only going after people with trendy resumes, and if you wouldn't hire someone before they worked at Google and would after, the main thing you know is that the person is above average at whiteboard algorithms quizzes (or got lucky one day).

I don't mean to pick on startups like TrendCo in particular. Boring old companies have their version of what a trendy background is, too. A friend of mine who's desperate to hire can't do anything with some of the resumes I pass his way because his group isn't allowed to hire anyone without a degree. Another person I know is in a similar situation because his group has a bright-line rule that causes them to reject people who aren't already employed.

Not only are these decisions non-optimal for companies, they create a path dependence in employment outcomes that causes individual good (or bad) events to follow people around for decades. You can see similar effects in the literature on career earnings in a variety of fields[4](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:C).

[Thomas Ptacek has this great line about how](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=7260087) “we interview people whose only prior work experience is "Line of Business .NET Developer", and they end up showing us how to write exploits for elliptic curve partial nonce bias attacks that involve Fourier transforms and BKZ lattice reduction steps that take 6 hours to run.” If you work at a company that doesn't reject people out of hand for not being trendy, you'll hear lots of stories like this. Some of the best people I've worked with went to schools you've never heard of and worked at companies you've never heard of until they ended up at Google. Some are still at companies you've never heard of.

If you read [Zach Holman](https://zachholman.com/talk/firing-people), you may recall that when he said that he was fired, someone responded with “If an employer has decided to fire you, then you've not only failed at your job, you've failed as a human being.” A lot of people treat employment status and credentials as measures of the inherent worth of individuals. But a large component of these markers of success, not to mention success itself, is luck.

### Solutions?

I can understand why this happens. At an individual level, we're prone to the [fundamental attribution error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_attribution_error). At an organizational level, fast growing organizations burn a large fraction of their time on interviews, and the obvious way to cut down on time spent interviewing is to only interview people with "good" qualifications. Unfortunately, that's counterproductive when you're chasing after the same tiny pool of people as everyone else.

Here are the beginnings of some ideas. I'm open to better suggestions!

#### Moneyball

Billy Beane and Paul Depodesta took the Oakland A's, a baseball franchise with nowhere near the budget of top teams, and created what was arguably the best team in baseball by finding and “hiring” players who were statistically underrated for their price. The thing I find really amazing about this is that they publicly talked about doing this, and then Michael Lewis wrote a book, titled [Moneyball](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393324818/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=abroaview-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393324818&linkId=65d86d3a72b4c1ba73e8d3d52796eae1), about them doing this. Despite the publicity, it took years for enough competitors to catch on enough that the A's strategy stopped giving them a very large edge.

You can see the exact same thing in software hiring. Thomas Ptacek has been talking about how they hired unusually effective people at Matasano for at least half a decade, maybe more. Google bigwigs regularly talk about the hiring data they have and what hasn't worked. I believe they talked about how focusing on top schools wasn't effective and didn't turn up employees that have better performance years ago, but that doesn't stop TrendCo from focusing hiring efforts on top schools.

#### Training / mentorship

You see a lot of talk about moneyball, but for some reason people are less excited about… trainingball? Practiceball? Whatever you want to call taking people who aren't “the best” and teaching them how to be “the best”.

This is another one where it's easy to see the impact through the lens of sports, because there is so much good performance data. Since it's basketball season, if we look at college basketball, for example, we can identify a handful of programs that regularly take unremarkable inputs and produce good outputs. And that's against a field of competitors where every team is expected to coach and train their players.

When it comes to tech companies, most of the competition isn't even trying. At the median large company, you get a couple days of “orientation”, which is mostly legal mumbo jumbo and paperwork, and the occasional “training”, which is usually a set of videos and a set of multiple-choice questions that are offered up for compliance reasons, not to teach anyone anything. And you'll be assigned a mentor who, more likely than not, won't provide any actual mentorship. Startups tend to be even worse! It's not hard to do better than that.

Considering how much money companies spend on [hiring and retaining](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=11314449) "the best", you'd expect them to spend at least a (non-zero) fraction on training. It's also quite strange that companies don't focus more or training and mentorship when trying to recruit. Specific things I've learned in specific roles have been tremendously valuable to me, but it's almost always either been a happy accident, or something I went out of my way to do. Most companies don't focus on this stuff. Sure, recruiters will tell you that "you'll learn so much more here than at Google, which will make you more valuable", implying that it's worth the $150k/yr pay cut, but if you ask them what, specifically, they do to make a better learning environment than Google, they never have a good answer.

#### Process / tools / culture

I've worked at two companies that both have effectively infinite resources to spend on tooling. One of them, let's call them ToolCo, is really serious about tooling and invests heavily in tools. People describe tooling there with phrases like “magical”, “the best I've ever seen”, and “I can't believe this is even possible”. And I can see why. For example, if you want to build a project that's millions of lines of code, their build system will make that take somewhere between 5s and 20s (assuming you don't enable [LTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interprocedural_optimization) or anything else that can't be parallelized)[5](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/#fn:B). In the course of a regular day at work you'll use multiple tools that seem magical because they're so far ahead of what's available in the outside world.

The other company, let's call them ProdCo [pays lip service to tooling, but doesn't really value it](http://yosefk.com/blog/people-can-read-their-managers-mind.html). People describing ProdCo tools use phrases like “world class bad software” and “I am 2x less productive than I've ever been anywhere else”, and “I can't believe this is even possible”. ProdCo has a paper on a new build system; their claimed numbers for speedup from parallelization/caching, onboarding time, and reliability, are at least two orders of magnitude worse than the equivalent at ToolCo. And, in my experience, the actual numbers are worse than the claims in the paper. In the course of a day of work at ProdCo, you'll use multiple tools that are multiple orders of magnitude worse than the equivalent at ToolCo in multiple dimensions. These kinds of things add up and can easily make a larger difference than “hiring only the best”.

Processes and culture also matter. I once worked on a team that didn't use version control or have a bug tracker. For every no-brainer item on the [Joel test](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/fog0000000043.html), there are teams out there that make the wrong choice.

Although I've only worked on one team that completely failed the Joel test (they scored a 1 out of 12), every team I've worked on has had glaring deficiencies that are technically trivial (but sometimes culturally difficult) to fix. When I was at Google, we had really bad communication problems between the two halves of our team that were in different locations. My fix was brain-dead simple: I started typing up meeting notes for all of our local meetings and discussions and taking questions from the remote team about things that surprised them in our notes. That's something anyone could have done, and it was a huge productivity improvement for the entire team. I've literally never found an environment where you can't massively improve productivity with something that trivial. Sometimes people don't agree (e.g., it took months to get the non-version-control-using-team to use version control), but that's a topic for another post.

Programmers are woefully underutilized at most companies. What's the point of [hiring "the best" and then crippling them](https://danluu.com/wat/)? You can get better results by hiring undistinguished folks and setting them up for success, and [it's a lot cheaper](https://twitter.com/patio11/status/706884144538648576).

### Conclusion

When I started programming, I heard a lot about how programmers are down to earth, not like those elitist folks who have uniforms involving suits and ties. You can even wear t-shirts to work! But if you think programmers aren't elitist, try wearing a suit and tie to an interview sometime. You'll have to go above and beyond to prove that you're not a bad cultural fit. We like to think that we're different from all those industries that judge people based on appearance, but we do the same thing, only instead of saying that people are a bad fit because they don't wear ties, we say they're a bad fit because they do, and instead of saying people aren't smart enough because they don't have the right pedigree… wait, that's exactly the same.

4.  I'm not going to do a literature review because there are just so many studies that link career earnings to external shocks, but I'll cite a result that I found to be interesting, [Lisa Kahn's 2010 Labour Economics paper](http://citec.repec.org/d/eee/labeco/v_17_y_2010_i_2_p_303-316.html).

    There have been a lot of studies that show, for some particular negative shock (like a recession), graduating into the negative shock reduces lifetime earnings. But most of those studies show that, over time, the effect gets smaller. When Kahn looked at national unemployment as a proxy for the state of the economy, she found the same thing. But when Kahn looked at _state level unemployment_, she found that the effect actually compounded over time.

    The overall evidence on what happens in the long run is equivocal. If you dig around, you'll find studies where earnings normalizes after “only” 15 years, causing a large but effectively one-off loss in earnings, and studies where the effect gets worse over time. The results are mostly technically not contradictory because they look at different causes of economic distress when people get their first job, and it's possible that the differences in results are because the different circumstances don't generalize. But the “good” result is that it takes 15 years for earnings to normalize after a single bad setback. Even a very optimistic reading of the literature reveals that external events can and do have very large effects on people's careers. And if you want an estimate of the bound on the "bad" case, check out, for example, the [Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales paper that claims to link the productivity of a city today to whether or not that city had a bishop in the year 1000](http://www.nber.org/papers/w14278).
