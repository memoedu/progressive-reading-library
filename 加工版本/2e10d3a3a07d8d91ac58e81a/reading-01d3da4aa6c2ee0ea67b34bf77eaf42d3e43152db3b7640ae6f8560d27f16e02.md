## 正文

你已经决定 donate 很多 money 了。也许你对某个 cause 或 organization 满怀热情；也许 Peter Singer [说服了你](https://www.ted.com/talks/peter_singer_the_why_and_how_of_effective_altruism)，让你觉得自己有义务这样做；也许你只是发现了[帮助别人很棒](http://blog.givewell.org/2013/08/20/excited-altruism/)。说不定，你只是刚中了彩票，想把它全部 give away，以免自己最后 bankrupt。

无论出于什么原因，在“帮助别人很棒”之后，下一步就是“帮助_更多_人_更_棒”——而要 maximizing 你所提供的帮助，就需要把 donations 的 financial details 处理好。下面就来讲怎么做。

## 决定向哪里、在什么时候 give

这一部分大多不在本指南的范围内，但在两个方面有一点关联。

最相关的问题可能是，你认为现在 donate 更 effective，还是先 save、以后再 donate 更 effective。这场 debate 的双方都有很有力的 arguments；Julia Wise 在 [effective altruism 博客](http://www.effective-altruism.com/category/giving-now-vs-later/)上写过一篇很好的 summary。你对这个问题的回答，会在很大程度上影响你现在应该如何 allocate 你的 money，下文会讨论这一点。

另一方面更偏向细节：你预计主要向 registered 501c3 non-profits 进行 donating，还是也会支持其他不那么 formal 的 causes（例如，funding start-ups 或 independent researchers）。这是因为有一种特殊的 investment vehicle，叫作 _donor-advised fund_（下文会讨论），它能提供一些 tax advantages，但只能 distributed 给 registered nonprofits。这没有前一点那么重要，但对你而言也可能有关。

## 想想你打算 give 多少

除非你对此已经有了坚定的看法，否则一开始通常所说的“一大部分”似乎是 10%。这是 [Giving What We Can](http://givingwhatwecan.com/) pledge 中的 amount，几个世纪以来也是标准的 church tithe，而且对于许多 career paths 来说都 manageable。但也有很多人 give 得更少，或设定不同的 schedules——我认识 Boston 的一对夫妇，他们决定[每结婚一年就 give 1%](http://madprime.org/articles/2013/12/31/eight-years-and-eight-percent-always-giving-more/)。

即使 salary 有限，也可以更大胆一些。例如，Julia Wise 写过[把一半 give 出去是什么体验](http://www.givinggladly.com/2013/06/whats-it-like-to-give-half.html)，也写过如果她单身、挣着 median personal income，她可能会怎么做（她仍然能设法 donate 22%）！不过，这需要很大的决心和并非人人都有的节俭习惯，所以最好还是从少量开始。

支持一开始少一些 donating 的另一个小理由是，如果你先 save 这笔 money，之后再决定 donate，你可能会从 tax deduction 中 benefit 更多。这是因为，如果你的 income increases，你的 marginal tax rate 也会 increase，因此 tax deduction 对你的 value 也会 increase。不过，这可能只是一个相当 small effect：即使你日后的 income doubles，你的 marginal tax rate 也不会 go up 超过 10%。所以，如果你目前还在上大学，或计划换一份 lucrative 得多的 job，这一点最 applicable。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:1)

## 不要 spend 很多 money

这是最不具有 charity-specific 性质的一步，因此也是其他地方讲得最多的一步。我能找到的最好建议，来自那些想为另一个目标 save up 的人；这个目标也更普遍，那就是很早就 retiring。最有名的可能是 [Mr. Money Mustache](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/)（“通过彪悍作风实现 Financial Freedom”），这个博客的作者在三十多岁时就已 save up 了足够的 money，得以 retire。作者写得相当有趣，虽然他的目标不是大量 donate，但他的大多数做法都很适用于这里。除了博客，他还有一份很长的[推荐阅读清单](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/the-mmm-reading-list/)。如果你用 Google 搜一搜，大概能找到一大堆类似的网站，毕竟 retiring early 对许多人来说都是非常诱人的幻想。

## 了解 taxes 如何 work

### Income 与 deductions（收入与扣除）

你支付的 tax 的 amount 取决于你的 _taxable income_，它是从你的 _adjusted gross income_ (AGI) 中，针对政府认定应当 tax-exempt 的某些项目 applying 一项 _tax deduction_ 而 calculated 得到的。这些 tax-exempt 项目之一就是 charitable contributions，因此这与你非常相关。

有两种 determining 你的 tax deduction 的方式。大多数 US citizens 采用 _standard deduction_，这是一项 \$6,000 的 unconditional deduction，不需要你进行任何 calculations。由于大多数人的 deductible expenses 从未 go above 这个数额，而且这样更省事，因此这是个不错的选择。[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:2)

不过，进行 large charitable donations 的人尤其可能有 exceeding standard deduction 的 deductible expenses。除了 donations，其他可能使你 exceed standard deduction 的项目还有 state 或 local income taxes 或 sales taxes（你可以 deduct 其中一种，但不能两种都 deduct），以及 property taxes。如果你在这些 categories 中支出的 sum exceeds $6,000，你就应该 file 一份 itemized return；这稍微复杂一些，但也复杂不了多少。如果你在 itemizing，就需要保留所有将要 deduct 的 expenditures 的 documentation，以备 IRS audits 你。

### Deduction caps（扣除上限）

在 US，针对 cash 的 charitable tax deductions 被 limited to 你的 adjusted gross income 的 50%，针对 capital gains 则是 30%。这需要留意，但我要强调，这大概不会成为你的问题：即使是专注于此的 earner-to-give，也极少会 donate 那么多。而且，即便你 save up 了很多钱，然后一次全部 donate，你仍然可以 [carry over 这笔 donation](http://www.hhcpa.com/blogs/income-tax-accountants-cpa/can-you-carry-over-your-charitable-contributions)，并在最长五年内继续 deducting，因此这仍然可能没有影响。不过，这是一个有时会出现的奇怪 corner case；如果你最终有了高得离谱的 income 之类的情况，就需要留意。

（注意：对于 capital gains deduction limit，你或许能够使用 tax-loss harvesting 来 ameliorate 它；参见下文关于“investments”的章节。）

### Marginal tax rates（边际税率）

US income tax 是 _progressive_ 的：你 earn 得越多，支付的 taxes 所占的 percentage 就越大。这意味着，当你的 wages 更高时，deduction 对你就更 valuable。如果你今天 make \$20,000, then your marginal tax rate is 15%, so a deduction of \$1,000 会使你的 taxes reduces \$150; but if next year you make $500,000，那么你的 marginal tax rate 就是 39.6%，而同样的 deduction，金额为 \$1,000 reduces your taxes by $396。

所以，如果你预计未来可能处于更高的 tax bracket（而且届时你还不会 hit deduction cap），这就是支持你 saving 一些额外的钱、到时再 donate 的一个理由。这个理由大概并不很有分量——即使是最低与最高 marginal tax rate 之间的 difference，也只有约 30%，而现在和以后 donating 之间的其他 differences 很容易就会盖过这一点。但这仍然值得记住。

### Bunching donations（集中捐赠）

由于实质上存在一个 floor，为 \$6,000 on your tax deduction for a single year, it can be advantageous to shuffle your deductions around. For instance, if you plan on donating \$10,000 每年，那么你可以交替在 12 月下旬和 1 月上旬 donating 这笔钱，这样你会在一个 calendar year 中 donate 两次，而在下一个完全不 donate。在你不 donate 的年份，你会采用 \$6,000 standard deduction, and during the years you do, you can take a \$20,000 的 itemized deduction，使你在两年期间的 net taxable income decreasing $6,000。

在适当的情况下，这个方案每年能为你省下几百到一千美元。不过，其中有一些 caveats 和 corner cases。在另一篇文章中，我通过实际的 math 做了[更 detailed 的 analysis](https://www.benkuhn.net/bunching)，以弄清它能为你省下多少钱。

## Donation matching（捐赠配捐）

有些 employers 会 match donations，直至某个 limit；例如 General Electric 的这个 limit 可高达 $50,000。对 donating 很多的人来说，这基本上就是白送的钱！在 comparing 潜在 employers 的 salaries 时，要查看他们的 donation matching policy。

对于 large corporations，GreatNonprofits 有一张列出各种 matching programs 的[表格](http://greatnonprofits.org/nonprofitnews/corporate-matching-gift-programs-money-unused/)——你可以按“maximum amount matched”进行 sort，找到对 employee 最好的那些。（当然，不要让这些 rankings 过度左右你的决定——还有很多其他重要的事情需要考虑，比如 field、base salary、growth potential、intangible benefits、EA movement 内部的 diversification 等。）

## Donor-advised funds（捐赠人建议基金）

如果你计划 saving 一些钱，以后再 giving it away，你可以把它放进一个 [donor-advised fund](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donor-advised_fund) (DAF)；这是一种 tax-deductible investment account，只能向 registered nonprofits 进行 paid out。这使你能够在把钱放进 DAF 的那一年采用 tax deduction，而不是在你实际把钱 distribute 给 charity 的那一年。

我对 DAFs 的印象是，它们可能有帮助，但前提是你计划在人生较晚的时候，将 saved income 中的一大笔 lump sum 用于 donating（大约是你的 AGI 的 200%）。到那时，如果你自己 invest 这些钱，然后照常 donate，那么即便有上文提到的 five-year carryover，compound interest 的魔力也可能让你积累起足够大的一笔钱，以至于你无法 deduct 全部金额。（例如，如果你通常 donate 你的 AGI 的 10%，而在某一年额外 donate 210%，那么你接下来五年的 total donation 就是你的 AGI 的 260%，而五年的 donation cap 是 250%。）如果你预计在人生较晚的时候，通过正常的 annual donations 就会接近 50% cap，同时也想现在 save，那么 DAF 也可能有用，但我要再次强调，这会相当少见。

另一方面，DAFs 有几个值得注意的缺点：

-   如前所述，_ceteris paribus_，最好在人生较晚的时候才 count 你的 donations，因为你的 marginal tax rate 会更高（不过这种 effect 通常很小）。

-   此外，DAFs 通常被 restricted to 向 registered nonprofits giving。如果你以后决定想把钱投入一家 start-up，或 fund 某人开展 independent research，你就会受到束缚。

-   我的（有限）印象是，DAFs 收取的 management fee 比 pure savings vehicles 更高。

-   最后，run DAF 的人并没有 legally obligated 去尊重你的意愿。他们不这样做的情况很少见，但 technically 是可能的，而且一旦发生，你可能没有多少 recourse。

由于这些缺点，DAFs 大多只有在你预计以后会碰到 tax deduction caps 时才有用（因此你希望把 giving 分摊到更多年份）。

想进一步阅读的话，Brian Tomasik 对 [DAFs 的优缺点](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/advanced-tips-on-personal-finance.html#daf)有一番很好的讨论。如果你想 create 一个，Ben West 为 80,000 Hours 写过[一篇操作指南](http://80000hours.org/blog/192-how-to-create-a-donor-advised-fund)。

## Saving（储蓄）

你也应该为自己 save 一些 money，以应对 unemployment、紧急情况、未来的机会、investing in 你自己的 human capital 等。你可以把 money save 在指定的 retirement account 中（它有 tax benefits，但如果你在 retire 之前 withdraw，也会有 penalties）；也可以放在 stocks 或 bonds 这样的 investment vehicles 中（它们更 flexible/liquid，但受到的 taxed 更多）；或者放在 bank account 中（它最 liquid，但提供的 interest 非常低）。

### 401k plans（401k 计划）

这些是你为 retire 后 save money 的方式。你的 employer 可能提供 401k matching。如果确实提供，这几乎就是白送的 money。你大概应该接受，除非以下条件全部成立：

-   你打算永远不 retire；
-   你打算 donate 这些 money；
-   你认为，一笔 $X right now is more effective than a donation of $2X 的 donation（或者你的 employer 在 matching 中提供的任何数额），在你的 401k 变得 accessible 时。[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:3)

向 401k plans 作出的 Contributions 的 capped 数额是 $17,500；你的 employer 所作的 match 可能少于这个数额。此外，关于 tax structure 还有各种细节，Brian Tomasik 已经[讨论过](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/advanced-tips-on-personal-finance.html#401k)。

### Investments（投资）

investing 中常见的建议是，你不应该尝试 beat the markets，也不应该相信那些声称能 beat the markets 的人；相反，只需 invest 于一个 [exchange-traded fund](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-traded_fund)（ETF），它会 tracks 某个 basket of stocks 的 performance，例如 S&P 500。

如果你选择这条路，使用 [Wealthfront](https://www.wealthfront.com/) 可能是 advantageous，因为它使用一种称为“tax-loss harvesting”的 strategy 来 improve 其 tax characteristics。基本上，它们会为你 buy 一个 ETF 会 buy 的那些 stocks，但随后会 sell 那些 perform poorly 的 stocks，以 realize 一笔 capital loss，这笔 capital loss 可以从你的 taxable income（最高 $3,000）或其他 taxable capital gains 中 deducted。如果你最后 donating 你的 investments，你可以直接 donate 那些 appreciated stock；即使它超过你 income 的 30% 这一 cap，你也可以使用 deferred capital losses 来 offset 其余部分，从而形成一笔完全 tax-deductible 的 donation。

### Bank accounts（银行账户）

Bank accounts 比 investments 更 liquid，也更不 volatile：把它们变成 cash 更容易，而且它们的 value 不会有那么大的上下波动。不过，你为这种 liquidity 和 stability 付出的代价，是低得多的 interest rate，往往基本为 zero。因此，让大量 spare cash 闲置在 bank account 中可能不是个好主意——如果可以，你大概会想把它 invest 出去。

## 与 accountant 谈谈

Accountants 很聪明，而且非常了解 taxes 如何运作！比我懂得多得多。如果你有任何不寻常的需求——嗨，或许只要有任何需求——就找一位咨询，请其提供帮助。这大概会很值得。

## Decide where and when to give

This part is mostly outside the scope of this guide, but has a bit of relevance, in two ways.

The most relevant question is probably whether you think it’s more effective to donate now, or save and donate later. There are strong arguments on both sides of this debate; there’s a good summary by Julia Wise on the [effective altruism blog](http://www.effective-altruism.com/category/giving-now-vs-later/). How you answer this question has a lot of implications for how you should allocate your money right now, which will be discussed below.

The other part, which is more of a detail, is whether you expect to be donating mostly to registered 501c3 non-profits, or also to other less formal causes (for example, funding start-ups or independent researchers). This is because there’s a special investment vehicle called a _donor-advised fund_ (discussed below) which provides some tax advantages, but can only be distributed to registered nonprofits. This isn’t as important as the previous point, but may be relevant to you.

## Think about how much you want to give

Unless you have strong views on this already, the standard “large chunk” to start with seems to be 10%. This is the amount in the [Giving What We Can](http://givingwhatwecan.com/) pledge, has been the standard church tithe for centuries, and is manageable for many career paths. But many people also give less or set different schedules—I know one couple in Boston who decided to give [1% for each year they’d been married](http://madprime.org/articles/2013/12/31/eight-years-and-eight-percent-always-giving-more/).

It’s also possible to be more adventurous, even on a limited salary. For instance, Julia Wise has written about [what it’s like to give half](http://www.givinggladly.com/2013/06/whats-it-like-to-give-half.html) and what she might do if she were single and making the median personal income (she could still manage to donate 22%)! But that takes a lot of dedication and frugal habits that not everyone has, so it’s definitely best to start small.

Another small point in favor of donating less at the beginning is that if you save the money and later decide to donate it, you may benefit more from the tax deduction. That’s because, if your income increases, your marginal tax rate will also increase, so the value to you of a tax deduction will increase. This will probably be a fairly small effect, though: even if your income doubles later in life, your marginal tax rate won’t go up by more than 10%. So this is most applicable if you’re currently in college or planning to switch to a much more lucrative job.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:1)

## Don’t spend lots of money

This is the least charity-specific step, hence the one most-covered elsewhere. The best advice I can find comes from people who want to save up for a different (and more widespread) goal: retiring very early. The best known is probably [Mr. Money Mustache](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/) (“Financial Freedom Through Badassity”), a blog written by a guy who saved up enough money to retire at thirty-something. The author is a pretty entertaining writer, and although his goal isn’t to donate a lot, most of the stuff he does transfers quite well. In addition to the blog, he also has a long [recommended reading list](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/the-mmm-reading-list/). If you Google you can probably turn up truckloads of similar sites, since retiring early is a very attractive fantasy for a lot of people.

## Be aware of how taxes work

### Income and deductions

The amount of tax you pay is based on your _taxable income_, which is calculated from your _adjusted gross income_ (AGI) by applying a _tax deduction_ for certain things that the government decided should be tax-exempt. One of these tax-exempt things is charitable contributions, so this is very relevant.

There are two ways of determining your tax deduction. Most US citizens take the _standard deduction_, which is an unconditional deduction of $6,000 that doesn’t require you to do any calculations. Since most people never go above that in deductible expenses, and it’s less effort, this is a good choice.[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:2)

However, people making large charitable donations are especially likely to have deductible expenses exceeding the standard deduction. In addition to donations, other things that may cause you to exceed the standard deduction are state or local income taxes or sales taxes (you may deduct one or the other but not both) and property taxes. If the sum of what you spend in these categories exceeds $6,000, you should file an itemized return, which is a little bit more complicated, but not much. If you’re itemizing, you need to keep documentation of any expenditures that you’ll deduct in case the IRS audits you.

### Deduction caps

In the US, charitable tax deductions are limited to 50% of your adjusted gross income for cash, or 30% for capital gains. This is something to be aware of, but let me stress that this will probably not be an issue for you: it’s absolutely not usual for even a dedicated earner-to-give to donate that much. And even if you save up a lot and donate it all at once, you can [carry over the donation](http://www.hhcpa.com/blogs/income-tax-accountants-cpa/can-you-carry-over-your-charitable-contributions) and keep deducting it for up to five years, so it still may not matter. But it is a weird corner case that comes up sometimes, and it’s something to be aware of if you end up having a ridiculously high income or something.

(N.B.: For the capital gains deduction limit, you may be able to use tax-loss harvesting to ameliorate it; see the section on “investments” below.)

### Marginal tax rates

The US income tax is _progressive_: the more you earn, the greater the percentage you pay in taxes. That means that a deduction is more valuable to you when your wages are higher. If today you make $20,000, then your marginal tax rate is 15%, so a deduction of $1,000 reduces your taxes by $150; but if next year you make $500,000, then your marginal tax rate is 39.6%, and the same deduction of $1,000 reduces your taxes by $396.

So if you’re anticipating that in the future you might be in a higher tax bracket (and you won’t have hit the deduction cap at that time), this is a point in favor of saving some extra money to donate then. It’s probably not a very large point—the difference between even the lowest marginal tax rate and the highest is only about 30%, and this could easily be swamped by other differences between donating now and later. But it’s something to keep in mind.

### Bunching donations

Because there’s essentially a floor of $6,000 on your tax deduction for a single year, it can be advantageous to shuffle your deductions around. For instance, if you plan on donating $10,000 every year, then you could alternate between donating it in late December and early January, so that you donate twice in one calendar year and not at all in the next. During the years you don’t donate, you’ll take the $6,000 standard deduction, and during the years you do, you can take a $20,000 itemized deduction, decreasing your net taxable income over a two-year span by $6,000.

In the right circumstances, this plan can save you hundreds to a thousand dollars a year. However, there are some caveats and corner cases. In a separate post, I did a [more detailed analysis](https://www.benkuhn.net/bunching) with actual math to figure out how much it will save you.

## Donation matching

Some employers will match donations up to a certain limit, which can be as much as $50,000 for e.g. General Electric. For someone donating a lot, this is basically free money! When comparing salaries for potential employers, check their donation matching policy.

For large corporations, GreatNonprofits has a [table](http://greatnonprofits.org/nonprofitnews/corporate-matching-gift-programs-money-unused/) of various matching programs—you can sort by “maximum amount matched” to find the best ones for an employee. (Of course, don’t let these rankings guide your decisions too strongly—there are plenty of other important things to think about, like field, base salary, growth potential, intangible benefits, diversification within the EA movement, etc.)

## Donor-advised funds

If you plan on saving some money and giving it away later, you can put it in a [donor-advised fund](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donor-advised_fund) (DAF), which is a tax-deductible investment account that can only be paid out to registered nonprofits. This allows you to take a tax deduction during the year that you put money into the DAF, instead of the year that you actually distribute the money to a charity.

My impression of DAFs is that they may be helpful, but only if you plan on donating a large lump sum of saved income later in your life (around 200% of your AGI). At that point, if you invest the money yourself and then donate it normally, then even with the five-year carryover mentioned above, the magic of compound interest may leave you with a large enough lump that you wouldn’t be able to deduct all of it. (For instance, if you typically donate 10% of your AGI, and in one year you donate an additional 210%, then your total donation for the next five years is 260% of your AGI, and the donation cap over five years is 250%.) A DAF could also be useful if you expect to get close to the 50% cap later in life through normal annual donations and also want to save now, but I stress again that this would be pretty unusual.

On the other hand, DAFs have a couple notable downsides:

-   As mentioned before, _ceteris paribus_ it’s better to count your donations later in life because your marginal tax rate will be higher (though this effect is usually small).

-   Additionally, DAFs are usually restricted to giving to registered nonprofits. If you later decide you want to put money into a start-up, or fund someone to do independent research, your hands will be tied.

-   My (limited) impression is that DAFs charge a higher management fee than pure savings vehicles.

-   Finally, the people who run the DAF are not legally obligated to respect your wishes. It’s unusual for them not to, but technically possible, and you may not have much recourse if it happens.

Because of these downsides, DAFs are mostly only useful if you expect to run into the tax deduction caps later (and hence want to spread out your giving over more years).

For further reading, Brian Tomasik has a good discussion of [the pros and cons of DAFs](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/advanced-tips-on-personal-finance.html#daf). If you want to create one, Ben West wrote [a how-to](http://80000hours.org/blog/192-how-to-create-a-donor-advised-fund) for 80,000 Hours.

## Saving

You should also save some money for yourself, for unemployment, emergencies, future opportunities, investing in your own human capital, etc. You can save money either in a designated retirement account (which has tax benefits, but also penalties if you withdraw from it before you retire); investment vehicles like stocks or bonds (which are more flexible/liquid but more taxed); or a bank account (which is the most liquid, but offers very low interest).

### 401k plans

These are how you save money for when you retire. Your employer may offer 401k matching. If they do, this is practically free money. You should probably take it unless ALL of the following are true:

-   you plan never to retire;
-   you plan to donate the money;
-   you think that a donation of $X right now is more effective than a donation of $2X (or however much your employer offers in matching) when your 401k becomes accessible.[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/giving-101/#fn:3)

Contributions to 401k plans are capped at $17,500; your employer may match less than that. There are also various details about tax structure that Brian Tomasik has [discussed](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/advanced-tips-on-personal-finance.html#401k).

### Investments

Common advice in investing is that you shouldn’t try to beat the markets, or believe people who claim to beat the markets; instead, just invest in an [exchange-traded fund](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-traded_fund) (ETF), which tracks the performance of a certain basket of stocks like the S&P 500.

If you go this route, it may be advantageous to use [Wealthfront](https://www.wealthfront.com/), which uses a strategy called “tax-loss harvesting” to improve its tax characteristics. Basically, they will buy the stocks for you that an ETF would, but they then sell the ones that perform poorly in order to realize a capital loss that can be deducted from your taxable income (up to $3,000) or other taxable capital gains. If you end up donating your investments, you can donate the appreciated stock directly, and even if it goes over the cap of 30% of your income you can use deferred capital losses to offset the rest, leading to a completely tax-deductible donation.

### Bank accounts

Bank accounts are more liquid and less volatile than investments: it’s easier to turn them into cash, and their value doesn’t go up and down as much. However, you pay for that liquidity and stability with a vastly lower interest rate, often basically zero. For this reason it may not be a good idea to have a lot of spare cash sitting around in a bank account—if you can, you probably want to invest it.

## Talk to an accountant

Accountants are smart and know a lot about how taxes work! Way more than I do. If you have any unusual needs—or heck, probably any needs at all—consult one for assistance. It will probably be worth it.
