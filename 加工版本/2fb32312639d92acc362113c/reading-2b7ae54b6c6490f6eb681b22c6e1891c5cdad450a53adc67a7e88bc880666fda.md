# Linear models: Comments on Ridley

---

## 正文

Matt Ridley 最近在 WSJ [发表](http://www.wsj.com/articles/the-myth-of-basic-science-1445613954)了一篇文章，主张 basic scientific research 并不会 lead to 更多 innovation。这篇文章有值得赞同的地方，也有令人不赞同的地方。[Jack Stilgoe](https://www.theguardian.com/science/political-science/2015/oct/25/countering-libertarian-arguments-against-science-funding) 和 [Anton Howes](https://antonhowes.tumblr.com/post/131947650019/innovation-vs-science) 都对此发表了看法，你可以在[这里](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/10/26/linear-models-comments-on-ridley/#comment-3172)阅读他们文章的 summaries。

这里首先要说的是，这里所谈的 linear model of innovation，已经被研究 science 和 technology 实际如何 work 的 scientists（！）所形成的 consensus rejected，无论他们提出什么 policy recommendations。（关于它为何一直存在的一种可能 explanation，参见[这里](http://www.nesta.org.uk/blog/smartest-guys-room-theory-innovation-policy)）Ridley、Stilgoe、Howes、Mazzucato 和我都 reject 它。只不过，随后各自 drawn 出了不同的 implications。Ridley 断言，是 technology 在 drives science；Stilgoe 说，reality 比 linear model 更 complex；Mazzucato 则说，因为 reality 是 complex 的，所以这意味着 government investment 有更多 areas 可以进入。

我基本赞同这样一种 vision：technological advance 是由许多 agents carried out 的 process，而且到处都有 feedback loops。因此，是 Science<->Technology，而不只是单向地从一方到另一方。

thermodynamics 这个 example 实在太好了，我忍不住要贴出 Handbook of the Economics of Innovation 第 1 章中的一些 quotes。

> Rudolf Diesel 是一位 trained engineer，是“new inventor”的一个好 specimen，接受过 science 方面的 trained，是一位“rational”的 engineer，把追求 efficiency 放在首位。Diesel 并没有 tinkering 和 tweaking，而是从 first thermodynamic principles 出发。他开始寻找一种 incorporated theoretical Carnot cycle 的 engine，在这个 cycle 中，通过 isothermal expansion obtained maximum efficiency，从而没有 energy 被 wasted，而且还可以 used 一种 cheap、crude fuel（最初 Diesel 在他的 engines 中 used 的是 coal dust）。$$...$$ Parsons 比 Diesel 更能体现 second Industrial Revolution。第一台 turbine 之所以被 built，仅仅是因为“thermodynamics 告诉他，这是可以做到的” （J. Mokyr）

从 Anderson 的书 Introduction to Flight 中，我们可以看到 aircraft industry 中一个类似的 development：

> 直到现在，Mathematics 在 flying 方面的 use 一直相当 useless（14th Annual Report of the Aeronautical Society of Great Britain, 1879）。来自 pure mathematicians 那片乐土的 Mathematical theories，被发现适合以极佳的 accuracy 来 describe aircraft 所 produced 的 airflow，以至于它们可以被 applied directly 于 airplane design。（Theodore von Karman, 1954）

所以，Ridley 认为 science 和 innovation 只是从 technology flows 而来，这确实显然是错的。但他为什么错了？这些事情为什么会发生？

这是因为，有些 insights 仅靠对 technological artefacts 进行 tinkering，是非常、非常难以获得的。你大概可以仅靠反复 tinkering 来 design 一台 steam engine 或一艘 ship。你只需要想好 main components，把它们 build 出来，放在一起，然后 iterate。就是这样。tinkering 就是以这种方式 produces advances 的。Tinkering 比把一切 systematising 并从 first principles 出发 deducing 更容易，这也就是为什么最初 science 会 lagged behind technology。

但接下来，我敢说，如果不 understanding 现有 engines 如何以及为何 work，你就不能仅靠尝试 improve 它们来 design 一台 efficient steam engine。那时，你就必须考虑 specific materials、thicknesses、thermodynamic cycles、regulators、fuel 和 design。Efficient steam engine 指的是尽可能最好的 steam engine，而不只是一台能简单 works 的 steam engine。在 steam engine concept space 中有许多 possible designs，但只有少数几个能被称为 most efficient。仅靠 guessing 很难到达那里。你通常需要坐下来，laying out 一个 formal model，然后 building 和 iterating，才能到达那里。而且，如果你甚至不知道 relevant factors（如果你不知道 meta-material 是该走的路，因为你根本不知道 meta-material 是什么，那会怎样？\[因为没有人对此做过 research\]），你就无法到达那里。在许多情况下，Basic science 就是在 exploring nature，看看我们遗漏了什么，补上书中那些 fine details。

如今，science 已经相当 advanced，technology 的 advances 很少再令它感到意外。正如 Dosi 和 Nelson 在 Handbook 中指出的，

> 自 Industrial Revolution 以来，sciences 对 technology 的 relative contribution 一直在 increasing，而这样的 science base 又在很大程度上是 publicly funded research 的 product；与此同时，这些 research 所 produced 的 knowledge 在很大程度上是 open 的，可以供 potential innovation 使用（更多内容见 David, 2001a,b, 2004; Nelson, 2004; Pavitt, 2001）。然而，这并不足以 corroborate 任何从 pure 到 applied science、再到 technological applications 的 simple “linear model”。首先，Rosenberg (1982)、Kline and Rosenberg (1986)、Pavitt (1999) 和 Nelson (1981) 在其他地方提出的 point 仍然 apply：scientific principles 很有帮助，但很少足够。Rosenberg (2009) 讨论了一个对此很有启发的 case，而且它确实来自一个“science-based”的 area——medical innovation。Semiconductors technology 是另一个很好的 example。几十年来，旨在 advance products 和 process technology 的努力——其中关键涉及 progressively make circuits smaller and smaller 的 ability——一直在利用 material science 及其 underlying solid-state physics 中的 understandings。然而，technological knowhow 中那些更加 pragmatic 和 tacit 的 elements 始终是 crucial 的。
> 
> 其次，scientific advances 因 technological advances 而成为可能，是相当常见的，尤其是在 instruments 的 fields 中：想想 electronic microscope 对 life sciences 中 scientific advances 的 example（更多内容见 Rosenberg, 1982, 1994）。

所以，我同意 Howes：是的，science 不是 progress 的唯一 source，但恕我不能赞同 Ridley，它的 importance 并不是 myth。

接下来，我必须同意 Ridley：即使那些伟大的 inventors 和 scientist 没有出生，technology 也确实仍然会 progressed。我在[这里](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/25/you-didnt-invent-that-or-deflating-entrepreneurship-and-on-historical-explanations/)和[这里](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/23/innovation-causality-and-government/)提出过这个 point。这并不是说，没有 Steve Jobs，我们也会拥有 iPhone。我们不会。但我们会得到某种类似的东西。想想 technological paradigm（smartphone）与某个 particular implementation（iPhone）之间的 difference。

但这并不足以论证 technology 的 unstoppability，或证明它有某种自己的生命。technology 如何 develops，往往受到 societal norms 和 interests，以及 historical events 的 influenced。以 rocketry 为例。World War II 之前，人们就对 rocketry 有兴趣，但后来 Nazis 从中看到了 potential new weapons，于是 boosted 了 rocket development。接下来，就是 US、Soviet Union 和 Space Race。也许没有 World War II，我们会拥有另一种 rockets，它们更 geared towards launching civil satellites 和 saving money，而不是向 space launching super heavy payloads。或者以 human cloning 为例：我们拥有 produce human clones 的 technology，但 'technium' 还没有迫使我们通过 cloning 来 produce adult humans。

一个更好的说法似乎只是：我们 humans 喜欢某些东西，而且通常希望它们更好、更便宜。所以 market 就 delivers 这些东西。反过来，technology use 会 changes 我们看待世界的方式，这是真的。也许 selfie-stick 在 10 年前显得荒唐，而如今有了 front facing phone cameras，它对一些人来说就有道理了。但有人会进一步声称，selfie stick 是 'technium' 的 appendage，正以某种方式利用我们吗？我没有读过这个 theory 最初出自的那本书，但就目前给出的 arguments 而言，我觉得它并不 plausible。

接下来是 government 的 role。我必须先照例自我宣传一下，告诉你，我已经写了十几篇关于 Mazzucato 及其 theories 的文章。你可以在[这里](https://artir.wordpress.com/series-de-articulos/)的相关部分查阅。一个概括性的 TL;DR 是：government 并没有那么 important，而它的 importance 表现得更明显的地方是在 basic science，这与她的 thesis 相矛盾，因为她反对只 funds basic science 的 State。

那么，government spending 在 R&D 上是否 useful？这是一个需要 case by case 考察的 empirical question。它可能很难被精确 measure，也可能出现这样的情况：它 crowds in，却未能 increase productivity，所以仅仅 measuring private sector 是否 spends 更多，并不能很好地 measure productivity increases。也可能出现 government activity 将 research 和 talent 从某些 areas redirects 到另一些 areas 的情况。我们当然登上了 Moon，但 NASA 中的所有那些 talent 当时都在做这件事，而不是其他事情。幸运的是，对于 Science，一个 area 中获得的 knowledge 可以在另一个 area 中 reuse，但事实仍然是，我们可以 build 一些 counterfactuals，在其中，如果没有那里的 government spending，我们反而会 better off。另一方面，它也可能 helpful。即使 companies 确实有办法 partially fixing public goods problem，而且也有专门从事 research 的 non-for-profits organisations，我们仍有理由认为，必定存在一些可能 useful 的 policies。设想一个 institution，倾听 companies 的 scientific needs，并就这些 needs performs research，无论是 basic 还是 applied。（类似于 Germany 的 Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft。）所以我会说，有些情况下它会 increases welfare，有些情况下则不会。比如，Military research spending 在 public R&D spending 中占据了 sizeable part，但 consensus 是，它对 non-military applications [并不 useful](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/28/is-war-necessary-for-economic-growth-a-review-and-some-aviation-history-love/)。Handbook 指出，大约 50% 的 public R&D spending 无法用 public goods argumentation 来 justifiable。在这里，你既可以像 Mazzucato 那样说，还需要更多东西，也可以说，也许 market failure fixing 就足够了。我希望将来有机会为这个 field 做一份 summary，它实在太庞大了。我能肯定的是，答案不会是“是的，始终如此”（也不会是“不，从来不会”）。

最后，我想谈谈 vision 和 stories 的 role。我认为，Ridley 批评那种流行的、认为 basic science 是 all-important 的 idea，是对的；但他说它 useless，就不那么对了。Stilgoe 指出，scientists 需要找到另一个 story 来讲述，以取代 linear model，这也是对的。但 Mazzucato 正是在这里登场了，而到目前为止，除了零星几位不起眼的 bloggers，她还没有受到公开质疑。她采用了一些当时流行的 views，并赋予它们一种看似 coherent theory 的东西（就像 Keynes 所做的那样）。这个 story 正在填补 linear model 的 disintegration 留下的空白。但我认为，它同时是 linear model（linear governmental model）和 Great Man Theory of Innovation 的另一个 version（如果没有那些实际做出其所作所为的 great men，innovation 就不会 march forward）。

我认为，一个更 realistic 的 model of innovation 应当从 microfoundations 开始（或者换一个词说，应当是 [reductionistic，而不是 holistic](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/10/04/the-follies-of-holism-a-reductionist-critique/)）。我们应当 study 那些从事 science 和 innovation 的人。他们 posses 哪些 traits？每个人都 equally capable to innovate 吗？然后，study 他们如何在 moving upwards 时 fit into organisations。他们喜欢 science per se 吗？innovators、scientists 或 entrepreneurs 的 supply with respect to earnings 有多 elastic？然后，研究 organisations 如何 relate to each other。这样会让我们更清楚地了解正在发生什么，并为所有 involved 的 factors assign 一个 relative importance，而不是 dismissing 其中一些，或 absurdly magnifying 另一些。

## 原文

Matt Ridley [recently published](http://www.wsj.com/articles/the-myth-of-basic-science-1445613954) a piece in the WSJ, arguing that basic scientific research does not lead to more innovation. There are things to like and dislike in the piece. [Jack Stilgoe](https://www.theguardian.com/science/political-science/2015/oct/25/countering-libertarian-arguments-against-science-funding) and [Anton Howes](https://antonhowes.tumblr.com/post/131947650019/innovation-vs-science)have weighed on this, and you can read summaries of their articles [here](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/10/26/linear-models-comments-on-ridley/#comment-3172).

The first thing to say here is that the linear model of innovation being talked about here is something that is rejected by the consensus of scientists (!) studying how science and technology actually work, whatever their policy recommendations. (See [here](http://www.nesta.org.uk/blog/smartest-guys-room-theory-innovation-policy) for a possible explanation of its persistence) Ridley, Stilgoe, Howes, Mazzucato, and I reject it. Only that then different implications are drawn. Ridley asserts that it is technology that drives science, Stilgoe says that reality is more complex than the linear model, Mazzucato says that because reality is complex, but that means there are more areas for government investment.

I basically agree with the vision of the technological advance as a process carried out by many agents, and with feedback loops everywhere. So Science<->Technology, and not just one way or the other.

The example of thermodynamics is so good that I can't help pasting some quotes from chapter 1 of the Handbook of the Economics of Innovation

> Rudolf Diesel was a trained engineer, a good specimen of the “new inventor,” trained in science, a “rational” engineer, in search of efficiency above all else. Rather than tinkering and tweaking, Diesel started from first thermodynamic principles He began searching for an engine that incorporated the theoretical Carnot cycle, in which maximum efficiency is obtained by isothermal expansion so that no energy is wasted, and a cheap, crude fuel can be used to boot (originally Diesel used coal dust in his engines). \[...\] Parsons, even more than Diesel, personified the second Industrial Revolution. The first turbine was built simply because “thermodynamics told him that it could be done”  (J. Mokyr)

From Anderson's book Introduction to Flight, we get a similar development for the aircraft industry:

> Mathematics up to the present have been quite useless to use in regards to flying (14th Annual Report of the Aeronautical Society of Great Britain, 1879) Mathematical theories from the happy hunting grounds of pure mathematicians are found suitable to describe the airflow produced by aircraft with such excellent accuracy that they can be applied directly to airplane design. (Theodore von Karman, 1954)

So Ridley is indeed clearly wrong that science and innovation just flows from technology. But why is he wrong? Why do these things happen?

It happens because there are some insights that are very, very hard to get just by tinkering with technological artefacts. You can probably design a steam engine or a ship just by tinkering around. You can just think of the main components, build them, place them together, and iterate. And that's it. That way is how tinkering produces advances. Tinkering is easier than systematising and deducing everything from first principles, and that's why originally science lagged behind technology.

But then, I bet you can't design an efficient steam engine justo by trying to improve existing engines without understanding how and why they work. Then you have to think of specific materials, thicknesses, thermodynamic cycles, regulators, fuel, and design. Efficient steam engine means the best possible steam engine, not just one that simply works. In the steam engine concept space are lots of possible designs, but just a couple you could call the most efficient. It's hard to get there just by guessing. You usually get there by sitting down, laying out a formal model, and then building and iterating. And if you aren't even aware of relevant factors (What if you don't know that a meta-material is the way to go because you don't know what a meta-material is? \[Because no one has done research on that\]), you won't get there. Basic science in many cases is exploring nature to see what we are missing, filling in the fine details in our books.

Right now, science is quite advanced, and it is rarely the case that it is surprised by advances in technology. As Dosi and Nelson point out in the Handbook,

> Since the Industrial Revolution, the relative contribution of sciences to technology has been increasing, and in turn such a science base has been largely the product of publicly funded research, while the knowledge produced by that research has been largely open and available for potential innovation to use (more in David, 2001a,b, 2004; Nelson, 2004; Pavitt, 2001). This, however, is not sufficient to corroborate any simple “linear model” from pure to applied science, to technological applications. First, the point made elsewhere by Rosenberg (1982), Kline and Rosenberg (1986), Pavitt (1999), and Nelson (1981) continues to apply: scientific principles help a lot but are rarely enough. An enlightening case to the point, indeed in a “science-based” area—medical innovation—is discussed in Rosenberg (2009). Semiconductors technology is another good example. For many decades, efforts to advance products and process technology—crucially involving the ability to progressively make circuits smaller and smaller— have taken advantage of the understandings in material science and the underlying solid-state physics. However, much more pragmatic and tacit elements of technological knowhow have persistently been crucial.
> 
> Second, it is quite common that scientific advances have been made possible by technological ones, especially in the fields of instruments: think of the example of the electronic microscope with respect to the scientific advances in life sciences (more in Rosenberg, 1982, 1994).

So, with Howes, yes science is not the only source of progress, but its importance, _pace_ Ridley, is not a myth.

Then, I have to agree with Ridley that it is true that had the great inventors and scientist not been born, technology would have progressed anyway. I made this point [here](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/25/you-didnt-invent-that-or-deflating-entrepreneurship-and-on-historical-explanations/) and [here](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/23/innovation-causality-and-government/). This is not to say that we would have had the iPhone without Steve Jobs. We wouldn't. But we would have gotten something similar. Think of the difference between a technological paradigm (the smartphone) and a particular implementation (the iPhone).

But this does not suffice to argue for the unstoppability of technology, or that it has some life of its own. The way technology develops is very often influenced by societal norms and interests, and also historical events. Take rocketry. There was an interest in rocketry before World War II, but  then the Nazis saw potential new weapons then, and so boosted rocket development. From there, to the US, the Soviet Union, and the Space Race. Maybe without World War II we would have a different kind of rockets, more geared towards launching civil satellites and saving money, than launching super heavy payloads to space. Or take human cloning: we have the technology to produce human clones, yet 'technium' has not yet forced us to produce adult humans via cloning.

A better story seems just that we humans like some things, and prefer them generally better and cheaper. So that's what the market delivers. In turn, technology use changes the way we see the world, that's true. Maybe the selfie-stick was absurd 10 years ago, and it makes sense for some people now once we have front facing phone cameras. But would one go as far as saying that the selfie stick is an appendage of 'technium' that uses us in some way? I have not read the book where that theory originates, but with the arguments presented, it doesn't seem plausible to me.

We then have the role of government. I must first make my mandatory self-publicity and tell you that I have over a dozen articles written on Mazzucato and her theories. You can consult them [here](https://artir.wordpress.com/series-de-articulos/) in the relevant section. A broad TL;DR would be that government wasn't that important, and that where it was more evident that was important was in basic science, contradicting her thesis, as she argues against a State that just funds basic science.

And is government spending in R&D useful? It is a case by case, empirical question. It can be difficult to measure with precision, and it can be the case that it crowds in, but then fails to increase productivity, so just measuring whether the private sector spends more would not be a good measure of productivity increases. It can also be the case that government activity redirects research and talent from one areas to others. Sure we got to the Moon, but all that talent sitting in NASA was doing that, and not other things. Fortunately, with Science, it is possible to reuse gained knowledge in one area in another area, but still the fact remains that one could build counterfactuals where we would be better off absent government spending there. On the other hand, it could also be helpful. Even when it is true that companies have ways of partially fixing the public goods problem, and that there are non-for-profits organisations dedicated to research, it's plausible to think that there must be policies that could be useful. Suppose one institution that listens to the scientific needs of companies, and performs research in that, be it basic or applied. (Something like Germany's Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft). So I'd say that there will be cases where it increases welfare, and cases where it won't. Military research spending, for one, accounts for a sizeable part of public R&D spending, yet the consensus is that it is [not useful](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/08/28/is-war-necessary-for-economic-growth-a-review-and-some-aviation-history-love/) for non-military applications. In the Handbook it is pointed out that around 50% of public R&D spending is not justifiable by public goods argumentation. Here, you can either say (with Mazzucato) that more than that is needed, or that perhaps the market failure fixing is enough. I would like to do a summary of this field at some point in the future, it is huge. What I will say for sure is that the answer won't be 'yes, always' (or no, never).

To finish up, I'd like to comment on the role of vision and stories. I think Ridley is right in attacking the prevalent idea that basic science is all-important, but not that right in saying it's useless. Stilgoe is also right in pointing out the need for another story for scientists to tell instead of the linear model. But here is where Mazzucato comes in, and so far she has not been publicly challenged except by some humble bloggers here and there. She has taken some views that were prevalent, and gave it something that seemed a coherent theory (like Keynes did). That's the story that is filling the gap left by the disintegration of the linear model. But I see it as another version of both the linear model (the linear governmental model), and the Great Man Theory of Innovation (without the great men that actually did what they did, innovation would not march forward).

I think a more realistic model of innovation should begin from microfoundations (or to use another word, be [reductionistic, not holistic](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/10/04/the-follies-of-holism-a-reductionist-critique/)). We should study the people who do science and innovation. What traits do they posses? Is everyone equally capable to innovate? Then, study how, moving upwards, they fit into organisations. Do they like science per se? How elastic is the supply of innovators, scientists, or entrepreneurs with respect to earnings? Then, how organisations relate to each other. That would give is a better picture of what is going on, and assign a relative importance to all the factors involved, instead of dismissing ones, or absurdly magnifying others.
