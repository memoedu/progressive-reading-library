# Flanderization

---

## 正文

我的一个朋友已经落下了“那个天真的人”这样的 stereotype。我们玩 [Cards Against Humanity](http://cardsagainsthumanity.com/) 时，他总是那个因为不知道牌上的话是什么意思而弃牌的人。有人说了句傻乎乎的影射时，他总是那个问这是什么意思的人。他不喝酒，不谈性，甚至几乎不说脏话。因此，当他“breaks character”，做了比平时稍微出格一点的事——比如去看一年一度的 Harvard-Yale 橄榄球赛——他往往会招来温和的打趣和无伤大雅的取笑。

你的 image 被你的 stereotype 接管的这种 effect，在互联网上被称为 [Flanderization](http://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flanderization)。人们没有足够的时间深入了解大多数其他人，因此，他们对另一个人的 mental image 最终成了对那个人几个最令人印象深刻的 features 的 caricature——我成了“那个谈论 [effective altruism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism) 的家伙”，我的室友成了“那个信 Christian、学 physics 的家伙”，诸如此类。而当我们 break 自己的 stereotypes 时，往往会引来惊讶的目光和一些无伤大雅的调侃。

但实际上，这并不像看起来那么无害。到目前为止，我的朋友似乎并没有让自己的 Flanderization circumscribe 他的 behavior。但我记得，高中时，同样的事情曾经经常发生在我身上——每当我做了什么 out of character 的事，就会遭到嘲笑，虽然“无伤大雅”，却也足以让我有点刺痛——这抑制了我 change 和 improve 自己的 self-presentation 的 ability。我花了很长时间才意识到，我完全可以承受那些嘲弄，照样 change 自己的 character。

说起来，我也和其他所有人一样，没少参与这种善意的调侃。但遗憾的是，身为 problem 的一部分，并没有让我对它的 cause 多出什么 insight。下面是几个 guesses：

1.  这是最省力的 response。开玩笑所需的精力，远少于说些真正有意思的话，或与我的朋友认真聊聊他做的那些 out of character 的事。
2.  幽默与严肃的交谈所获得的 rewards 是 Skewed 的。如果我说了什么让人发笑的话，就会获得 immediate social rewards。但如果我说些严肃的话，就远没有那么令人兴奋，即使从长远来看更有价值。
3.  面对人们不 conforming 于我对他们的 expectations，我正在 unconsciously lashing out。我不太确定这里会有什么 mechanism，但似乎有可能，我只是在 subtly expressing negative affect，因为我对朋友的 predictions 结果是 wrong 的。

我不确定其中哪一个是 dominant，也不知道该怎么办。理想情况下，应该有某种 solution，既能让交谈保持有趣，又不会用嘲弄迫使人们 conforming 于那些他们已经不再 endorse 的、关于他们的旧 stereotypes。但这似乎是一个相当棘手的 problem——我想不出好的 solution。大家有什么想法？

## 原文

One of my friends has earned a stereotype of “the naive one”. When we play [Cards Against Humanity](http://cardsagainsthumanity.com/), he’s the one discarding cards because he doesn’t know what they mean. When someone makes a silly innuendo, he’s the one to ask what it means. He doesn’t drink, doesn’t talk about sex, and hardly even swears. As a result, when he “breaks character” and does something a little edgier than usual—like go to the annual Harvard-Yale football game—he often gets reactions of gentle ribbing and harmless making-fun.

This effect of your image being taken over by your stereotype is known by the Internet as [Flanderization](http://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flanderization). People don’t have enough time to get to know most others in any depth, so their mental image of another person ends up being a caricature of a few of their most memorable features—I become “that guy who talks about [effective altruism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism)”, my roommate becomes “the Christian physics guy”, and so on. And when we break our stereotypes we often get raised eyebrows and some harmless teasing.

But actually, it’s not harmless as it seems. So far my friend seems not to be letting his Flanderization circumscribe his behavior. But I remember that the same thing used to happen to me a lot in high school—whenever I did something out of character I’d get laughed at, “harmlessly” but just enough to sting a bit—and it chilled my ability to change and improve my self-presentation. It took me a long time to realize that I could just deal with the mockery and change my character anyway.

Now, I’m as guilty of participating in well-intentioned teasing as anyone else. But unfortunately being part of the problem doesn’t give me any insight into its cause. A couple guesses:

1.  It’s the easiest response. Making jokes requires a lot less effort than saying something genuinely interesting or engaging with my friend about things he does that are out of character.
2.  Skewed rewards for humorous vs. serious conversations. If I say something that people laugh at, I get immediate social rewards. But if I say something serious, it’s not nearly as exciting, even if it’s more valuable in the long term.
3.  I’m unconsciously lashing out in response to people not conforming to my expectations of them. I’m not quite sure what the mechanism here would be, but it seems plausible that I’m just subtly expressing negative affect that my predictions about my friend turned out to be wrong.

I’m not sure which one of these is dominant, or what to do about it. Ideally there’d be some solution that keeps conversations fun while not mocking people into conforming to old stereotypes about them that they no longer endorse. But that seems to be a pretty hard problem—I don’t see a good solution. Thoughts?
