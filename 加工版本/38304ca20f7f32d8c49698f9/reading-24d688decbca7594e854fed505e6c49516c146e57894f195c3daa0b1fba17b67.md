# 对 deep atheism 的 naturalistic exploration

---

## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

2025年5月19日

_在这些粗略的工作笔记中，我探讨 Joe Carlsmith 的“deep atheism”这一 concept，意指对 universe 抱有 distrust 的 fundamental stance。conventional atheism 只是 rejects God 的 existence，而 deep atheism 则 maintains，reality 本身对 human flourishing 是 fundamentally hostile 或 indifferent 的。（我会讨论这一 terminology 的一些 ambiguities。）我讨论 formative experiences——从个人悲剧到遭遇 nature 的 brutality——如何能 shift 一个人对 universe 的 stance，而且往往发生在 emotional disposition 和 instinct 的层面，而不是 intellectual 或 scientific belief 的层面。我也探讨“optimistic cosmism”，这种 stance recognizes 并 values 那些使 local order 和 meaning 成为可能的、许多 a priori surprising properties。即使 universe 并不关心我们，它仍允许像 humans 这样的 tiny physical systems 去 understand、transform 并 improve 自己的 environment。这个 fact 对我们而言十分熟悉，也很容易被视为理所当然，但它 rests on 许多关于 universe 的 profound 而 surprising 的 facts。_

我写下这些 speculative notes，是为了发展自己对 deep atheism[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn1) 的思考，并探讨这样一些问题：我们是否生活在一个 fundamentally friendly universe 中？一个会照顾我们的 universe？还是一个 hostile universe？如果是后者，它究竟有多 hostile？deep atheism 为什么重要，它还与什么 connect？我的 instinct 是，这是一个重要的 idea——的确，让我惊讶的是，如今这个 idea 频繁地出现在我的思考中，而且常常是在看似无关的领域。

遗憾的是，当我提到 deep atheism 时，人们常常反对说，虽然它隐含的问题很有意思，但这个 term 是个 misnomer（“那不是关于 atheism 的，而是关于 universe 的 character 的！”）。一个人可能 fervently believe in God（因此并不是 atheist），但同时又相信这个 God 是 mean、evil 或者干脆就是 chaotic 的。在我看来，这样的人会是“deep atheist”，却不是 atheist。又或者，他们相信一个 loving God，却也相信一个 hostile universe，是某种 God-as-only-salvation model。这类 beliefs 让 deep atheism 这个 term 有些棘手，也是我认为它不会流行起来的一个原因，尽管这个 concept 很重要。不过，在这篇文章中，我仍会把“deep atheism”用作 working term[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn2)。

Carlsmith 并没有 precisely define deep atheism。相反，他通过许多 examples 来探讨这个 concept，逐步发展这个 idea，而不是进行 premature definition。因此，我的理解可能与他的原意有所不同。事实上，文章中许多看起来有意思（也许还很 profound）的内容，对我而言仍有些晦涩。而且，尽管 deep atheism 这个 concept 与关于 problem of evil、theodicy、providence 及相关 topics 的庞大 philosophical 和 religious literatures 相联系，但在我发展自己理解的过程中，这些工作笔记只与那些 literature 建立了有限的联系。

## 对 deep atheism 的 naturalistic exploration

什么可能让一个人相信 universe 是 fundamentally unfriendly 的？Carlsmith 通过多个 examples 探讨这个问题。一个人该如何面对孩子因 leukemia 而离世？或者一场住宅火灾？又或者，ichneumon wasp 把 eggs 产在 caterpillar 体内，随后 larvae 从内向外慢慢 devour 那只活着的 caterpillar，这又该如何面对？他谈到了 Timothy Treadwell，也就是“灰熊人”的事例。这个人非常信任他者（有些人可能会说是天真），曾与 grizzly bears 共同生活了几年，经常说这样的话：“我全心全意地爱它们；我会保护它们；我愿为它们而死。但我不会死在它们的爪掌之下。”这是一种非常强烈的 providence 观，认为 universe 对他是 friendly 的。遗憾的是，他和女友最终被其中一只熊杀死并吃掉了。正如 Carlsmith 引用 Werner Herzog 的话所说，Treadwell 没有认真对待“Nature 的 overwhelming indifference”。

当然，这些 examples 并不是要充当支持（或反对）deep atheism 的 scientific evidence。如果你的孩子死于 cancer，其影响并不是改变你对 infant mortality rates 的 statistical picture。相反，它会在直觉深处改变你，甚至可能改变你对 universe 的 fundamental stance。例如，你可能开始相信，universe 远比自己先前所认为的更加 harsh、更加 hostile。Carlsmith 的文章主要聚焦于这个层面，聚焦于人们对 universe 所持的 fundamental stances 或 orientations，以及哪些 experiences 有助于 form 或 shift 这些 stances。它不是在问“我们如何才能 scientifically decide deep atheism 是否 correct？”，而是在对“人们的 stances 实际上是通过哪些方式 formed 或 changed 的？”进行 naturalistic exploration[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn3)。

他给出的一个 example，是 C. S. Lewis 在《A Grief Observed》中对 God 的极大愤怒，这是 Lewis 在妻子因 cancer 离他而去后写下的日记。这本书对我有很深的个人意义，而他使用这个 example，让我深受触动[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn4)。他引用了 Lewis 的话；Lewis 在32岁 converting to Christianity 后，就把 God 放在自己生活的中心，此时却开始追问，God 会不会是 evil 的：

> 来吧，回避能让我们得到什么？我们身处耙齿之下，无法逃脱。直视 Reality，它便令人难以承受。而这样的 reality，又如何或为何会在这里那里开花（或溃烂），成为那种叫作 consciousness 的可怕 phenomenon？……如果 H. \[Lewis 已故的妻子\]“不再存在”，那么她就从未存在过。我把一团 atoms 误认成了一个人。根本没有，也从未有过任何人……不，我真正恐惧的并不是 materialism。如果它是 true 的，我们——或者说，我们误以为是‘我们’的东西 —- 就能脱身，从耙齿之下逃出来。过量服用安眠药就能做到。我更害怕的是，我们其实是陷阱里的老鼠。或者更糟，是 laboratory 里的老鼠……迟早，我必须用直白的语言面对这个问题。除了我们自己绝望的愿望，我们有什么 reason 相信，按我们所能 conceive 的任何 standard，God 是‘good’的？难道所有 prima facie evidence 所 suggest 的，不恰恰是 opposite 吗？我们又有什么能与之相抗衡？

再次强调，这不是 intellectual argument 或 scientific evidence 的问题。这是一个身处巨大痛苦中的人，正在经历一件很可能改变他对 God 和 universe 的 fundamental stance 的事。在某种意义上，对于我们默认 assume universe 有多 hostile 或 friendly，我们之中没有谁是 undecided 的——我们都有一种 stance，都有 judgement、response 和 action 的 patterns，它们建立在 genetic predisposition、culture 和 environment、conditioned response、emotional response、habitual patterns，以及（往往最不重要的）intellectually-grounded beliefs[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn5) 的某种组合之上。Carlsmith 用这样一个问题来说明这一点：如果 theist 的 God 已死，还能剩下什么样的 spirituality？他回答道：

> 看到一个人在医院里受苦，是一回事；在这种 suffering 中看到我们整个 society 和 history 的 sickness，是另一回事；而在其中看到 being 本身的 poison、consciousness 的 rot、任何 contingent thing 那令人恐惧的 helplessness，则又是另一回事……我们可以把当代许多形式的“spirituality”看作在 expressing 某种“existential positive”。它们不必 believe in Big-Man-God，却仍然以某种 reverence 和 affirmation 转向 Ultimate Reality……Mystical traditions……通常旨在 disclose reality 本身某种 core 而 universal 的 dimension，而这个 dimension 被体验为在某种深层意义上是 positive 的——例如，引发 ecstatic joy、relief、peace 等等……或许，Ultimate 并没有像 three-O theism 中那样，被明确说成是“good”的，更不用说“perfect”；但它仍是人们想要前往的方向；仍是人们要去接受，而不是抗拒或忽视的东西；它仍是“sacred”的。. 相比之下，secularist 认为 Ultimate Reality 就其本身而言，是某种 blank。reality 的 Specific arrangements（flowers、happy puppies、stars 等）–- 都挺好。但 Real、Absolute、Ground of Being –- 那是 neutral 的。在这个意义上，secularist 把 Nature 的“overwhelming indifference”回报给 Nature，或 Nature 的 source。

Carlsmith 认为，Eliezer Yudkowsky 是最早认真对待 artificial intelligence 所带来的 existential risk 的人之一，也是 deep atheist 的一个 prototypical example，并引用他的话说：“你并不 safe。从来都不……只有在父母辜负了自己、自己的 gods 已死、手中的 tools 已经粉碎之后，一个人才会开始真正寻找 Way。”Carlsmith 评论道：“我认为，这不仅仅是 standard atheism。许多 atheists 会找到其他 \`gods,' in the extended sense I have in mind. That's why I said \`上文的 shallow atheism，’。Deep atheism 试图更用力地 propagate 它的 godlessness。成为一个更加彻底的孤儿。处处从 theist 的错误中学习。”他还说：“因此：我们最 basic 的 condition，一种几乎被 epistemology 这个 concept 本身所 presupposed 的 condition，就是 vulnerability。面对那个最初也最可怕的 Other 的 Vulnerability：God、Creator、Uncontrolled、Real。而在没有进一步 evidence 的情况下，Real 可能是任何东西。它当然可能吃掉你，还有你的孩子。噢，的确，它可能做出远远更糟的事。”

## 一位 optimistic cosmist

我是一个 deep atheist 吗？不完全是。我 _确实是_ 一个 atheist——我不认为存在 Abrahamic 以及许多其他 religions[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn6) 所指的 God。但我是一个或许可以称为 _optimistic cosmist_ 的人。如果你考虑目前所理解的 fundamental laws of physics，它们并不明显暗示 friendliness。粗略看来，整体上的 universe 似乎是 indifferent，甚至是 hostile。然而，不知怎么，我们却生活在一个相对 friendly 且 safe 的 local enclave 之中。因为熟悉，我们对此习以为常，但这有赖于 universe 具有许多 _a priori_ 令人惊讶的 properties，使 tiny physical systems（比如 human beings！）能够开辟出 safe 且 friendly 的 local enclaves，尽管 cosmic indifference 表面上似乎存在。

这些令人惊讶的 properties 包括：mathematics exists，而且能够由同样这些 tiny physical systems 来 expressed 和 explored physically，并带来惊人的 results[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn7)；这些 tiny physical system 有可能 develop simple 但 powerful 的 scientific theories，并 understand、control 和 improve 它们的 environment，使其变得 friendly 且 meaningful；stable、self-maintaining、fault-tolerant、self-reproducing systems 能够 emerge、persist，并 evolve 成能够做到上述事情的 entities[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn8)。这些 properties 没有一个是显而易见的。我们很容易设想出这样的 universes：其中任何 system 都无法使其所在的 universe 不再 incomprehensible 或 uncontrollable。然而，我上面提到的 properties（以及许多其他 properties）意味着，我们生活在一个能够 build 出对 utopias[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn9) 的好得令人惊讶的 approximations 的 universe 中，至少在 limited periods 内，以及 cities 或 planets 这样的 limited regions 中如此。在这样的 universe 中，支持 ambient friendliness 的 platforms——比如 immune systems、legal and judicial systems 以及 military defense——不仅是 possible 的，而且还可能是 common 的。cosmic indifference 与 universe 对 local friendliness 的惊人 amenability 相结合，defines 了 optimistic cosmist position。它 acknowledges universe 表面上的 hostility，同时 recognizing 我们在其中 create meaningful enclaves 的能力所具有的 unexpectedness 和 value。这令人惊叹，而思考那些情况并非如此的 universes 也很有意思[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn10)[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn11)。

## 将 deep atheism 和 optimistic cosmism 与 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 及 Artificial Superintelligence 联系起来

我对 deep atheism 反应强烈的一个原因，是我对 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 的兴趣。这是 Nick Bostrom[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn12) 提出的一个 term，所指的 idea 是（大致而言）：许多 civilizations 的 technology tree 都会包含一种会 destroys 该 civilization 的 technology。虽然我们 humans 迄今已经 survived technological advances，但 universe 并不保证 technology 会继续是 manageable 的。事实上，可以说我们已经坐在一颗以 thermonuclear weapons 形式存在的定时炸弹上。

如果 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 是 true 的，而且所有或大多数 emerging civilizations 最终都会 destroy 自身，那么这就是 universe 的 friendliness 所受到的一种 limit。是的，universe 会允许我们短暂地 create friendly local enclaves。但使这些 enclaves 成为可能的同样那些 properties，也 ensure 我们最终会 destroy 自身。相信 Vulnerable World Hypothesis，是 deep atheism 的一种特定 form；也是对 optimistic cosmism 的一种特定 denial。正如 Carl Sagan 在 1978 年指出的，它也是 Fermi Paradox 的一种可能 explanation——为什么我们没有看到 aliens 无处不在，以及（或许）为什么 friendliness 没有从 local 走向 near-universal：

> 当然，并非所有 scientists 都接受其他 advanced civilizations exist 这一 notion。最近思考过这个 subject 的少数人正在问：如果 extraterrestrial intelligence 是 abundant 的，为什么我们还没有看到它的 manifestations？……为什么这些 beings 没有为了自身方便而 restructured 整个 Galaxy？……为什么它们不在这里？我们很容易据此 deduce，advanced extraterrestrial civilizations 至多只有少数几个——要么因为我们是最早 emerged 的 technical civilizations 之一，要么因为所有这类 civilizations 的 fate 都是在发展到更远之前 destroy 自身。

这或许就是过去一年里，我如此频繁地想到 deep atheism 的原因。我习以为常且根深蒂固的 optimistic cosmism，正受到 [science 和 technology 带来的 existential risk](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/xrisk.html) 这一前景的考验，尤其是 artificial superintelligence 带来的风险。

## 将 deep atheism 视为 science？

Carlsmith 在文章中花了很大篇幅，将 deep atheism 作为一种 natural phenomenon 来探索，我也这样做了。也就是说，把它作为 psychological and cultural experience 的问题来探讨。但当然，我们也可以主要把它当作一个 scientific question：它是 true 的吗？我们能够提出什么 evidence 和 argument 来支持或反对它？它是 true 的，究竟又意味着什么？

要使它成为 well formulated 的问题，需要做大量工作。仅举一个很小的例子，像“friendliness”和“hostility”这样的 notions，就棘手得令人意外。对我们 human beings 而言，它们似乎非常自然，但这种自然感是一条漫长 evolutionary chain 的 outcome。friendliness 的 Orientations 存在于许多 mammals 和一些其他 species 之中，却在 biosphere 的许多部分 largely absent[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn13)。shark 所感受到的，很少是我们会视为 empathy 或 friendliness 的东西；amoeba 就更少了。从这个角度看，friendliness 和 hostility 这样的 notions 似乎在很大程度上只是 parochial concerns，是 earth 上某条特定 evolutionary path 的一种 quirk。wasp-caterpillar parasitism 在我们看来很可怕，但从任何其他意义上说，它也那么糟糕吗？或许这只是 provincial thinking。当然，我确实相信，可以使这些 notions 远不那么 parochial。但这会很棘手，而这也说明，要把“deep atheism 在什么意义上是 correct 的？”变成一个 well formulated scientific question，就必须面对大量纠缠在一起的 issues。

## 结语

在本文开头，我提到了「deep atheism」这个术语的一些难题。我非常喜欢这个术语的一点，是它强调了这个 concept 与 religion 的 relationship。我曾列出一长串我们 develop 这些面向 universe 的 stances 的方式——genetic predisposition、culture 和 environment，等等。这份清单还应加入一个重要项目：广义的 religion 和 myth[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn14)，也就是强有力的、shared stories，它们帮助 create cultures 和 shared identity，乃至 shared consciousness。historian Tom Holland 在他的书 _Dominion_ 中论证，来自 Christian gospels 的 ideas 实际上帮助 reshape 了整个世界对于 good behaviour、对于怎样才算 live a successful life 的 ideas，尤其是 kindness 的 role 等等。思考哪些 myths 承载着 deep atheism（或它的 opposite），很有意思。而更一般地说，思考哪些 stances 能够（或应该）由 myth 承载，以及如何承载，也很有意思。我逐渐开始相信，这些 shared myths define 了 civilization 所获得的 outcomes 中多得令人惊讶的一部分。

我怀着忐忑写下并发布了这些笔记。这一切似乎都有点想得太玄了！不过，在我和许多其他人看来，humanity 很可能再过几年或几十年就会 creating artificial superintelligence。而 universe 的 friendliness 将以一种新的方式受到检验。适当地想得玄一些也未尝不可——我们需要一些不同寻常的 ideas，才能应对这个非同寻常的时刻！

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

May 19, 2025

_In these rough working notes, I explore Joe Carlsmith's concept of "deep atheism", meaning a fundamental stance of distrust toward the universe. While conventional atheism merely rejects the existence of God, deep atheism maintains that reality itself is fundamentally hostile or indifferent to human flourishing. (I discuss some ambiguities with the terminology.) I discuss how formative experiences -- from personal tragedies to encounters with nature's brutality -- can shift one's stance toward the universe, often at the level of emotional disposition and instinct rather than intellectual or scientific belief. I also explore "optimistic cosmism", a stance that recognizes and values the many a priori surprising properties that enable local order and meaning. Even if the universe does not care for us, it permits tiny physical systems like humans to understand and transform and improve their environment. This fact is very familiar to us, and it's tempting to take for granted, but rests on many profound and surprising facts about the universe._

I wrote these speculative notes to develop my thinking about deep atheism[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn1), and to explore questions like: do we live in a fundamentally friendly universe? One that looks out for us? Or a hostile universe? If so, just how hostile is it? Why does deep atheism matter, and what else does it connect to? My instinct is that it's an important idea – certainly, I'm surprised how often the idea now arises in my thinking, often in seemingly unrelated areas.

Unfortunately, when I mention deep atheism, people often object that while the implied question is interesting, the term is a misnomer ("that's not about atheism, it's about the character of the universe!"). Someone could believe fervently in God (and thus not be an atheist), and yet also believe in a mean or evil or just plain chaotic God. They'd be a "deep atheist" but not an atheist, as far as I can tell. Or perhaps they believe in a loving God, but a hostile universe, a sort of God-as-only-salvation model. These kinds of beliefs make the term deep atheism somewhat challenging, and are a reason I don't expect the term to catch on, despite the importance of the concept. Still, for this essay I'll use "deep atheism" as a working term[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn2).

Carlsmith doesn't precisely define deep atheism. Instead, he explores the concept through many examples, gradually developing the idea rather than engaging in premature definition. My understanding may thus differ somewhat from what he intended. Indeed, much that seems interesting (and perhaps profound) in the essay remains somewhat opaque to me. And while the concept of deep atheism connects to the vast philosophical and religious literatures on the problem of evil, theodicy, providence, and related topics, these working notes only make limited connections to that literature, as I develop my own understanding.

## A naturalistic exploration of deep atheism

What might lead someone to believe the universe is fundamentally unfriendly? Through multiple examples, Carlsmith explores this question. How should one respond to losing a child to leukemia? Or to a house fire? Or to the way the ichneumon wasp lays its eggs inside a caterpillar, only for the larvae to slowly devour the living caterpillar from the inside out? He talks about the case of Timothy Treadwell, the "Grizzly Man", a very trusting (some might say naive) person who lived for several years with grizzly bears, often saying things like: "I love them with all my heart; I will protect them; I will die for them. But I will not die at their claws and paws." It was a very strong view of providence, of the universe being friendly toward him. He and his girlfriend were, unfortunately, eventually killed and eaten by one of the bears. As Carlsmith says, quoting Werner Herzog, Treadwell failed to take seriously the "overwhelming indifference of Nature".

These examples are of course not intended as scientific evidence in favour of (or against) deep atheism. If your child dies of cancer, the impact isn't to change your statistical picture of infant mortality rates. Rather, it changes you at a gut level, may even change your fundamental stance toward the universe. You may, for instance, come to believe the universe is a much harsher and more hostile place than you formerly believed. Carlsmith's essay focuses mostly at this level, at the fundamental stances or orientations people have toward the universe, and what kinds of experiences help form or shift those stances. It's not asking "how can we scientifically decide whether deep atheism is correct?" but rather a naturalistic exploration[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn3) of "what are some of the ways in which people's stances are actually formed or changed?"

One example he gives is C. S. Lewis's tremendous anger at God in "A Grief Observed", the diary Lewis wrote after his wife was taken from him by cancer. This book has a lot of personal meaning for me, and I found myself very moved by the use of the example[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn4). He quotes Lewis, a man who had placed God at the center of his life after converting to Christianity at age 32, coming to ask if maybe God is evil:

> Come, what do we gain by evasions? We are under the harrow and can't escape. Reality, looked at steadily, is unbearable. And how or why did such a reality blossom (or fester) here and there into the terrible phenomenon called consciousness?… If H. \[Lewis's dead wife\] 'is not,' then she never was. I mistook a cloud of atoms for a person. There aren't, and never were, any people… No, my real fear is not materialism. If it were true, we – or what we mistake for 'we' —- could get out, get from under the harrow. An overdose of sleeping pills would do it. I am more afraid that we are really rats in a trap. Or worse still, rats in a laboratory… Sooner or later I must face the question in plain language. What reason have we, except our own desperate wishes, to believe that God is, by any standard we can conceive, ‘good’? Doesn’t all the prima facie evidence suggest exactly the opposite? What have we to set against it?

Again, this isn't a question of intellectual argument or scientific evidence. This is a man in tremendous pain going through an experience that may well change his fundamental stance toward God and the universe. In some sense, none of us is undecided about how hostile or friendly we default assume the universe is – we have a stance, patterns of judgement and response and action, based on some combination of genetic predisposition, culture and environment, conditioned response, emotional response, habitual patterns, and (often least important) – intellectually-grounded beliefs[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn5). Carlsmith illustrates this with the question of what sort of spirituality can be left over, if the theist's God is dead, replying:

> To see a man suffering in the hospital is one thing; to see, in this suffering, the sickness of our society and our history as a whole, another; and to see in it the poison of being itself, the rot of consciousness, the horrific helplessness of any contingent thing, another yet…. we could see many forms of contemporary “spirituality” as expressing a form of “existential positive.” They need not believe in Big-Man-God, but they still turn toward Ultimate Reality… with a kind of reverence and affirmation… Mystical traditions… generally aim to disclose some core and universal dimension of reality itself, where this dimension is experienced as in some deep sense positive – e.g. prompting of ecstatic joy, relief, peace, and so forth… Perhaps the Ultimate is not, as in three-O theism, explicitly said to be “good,” and still less, “perfect”; but it is still the direction one wants to travel; it is still something to receive, rather than to resist or ignore; it is still “sacred.”. The secularist, by contrast, sees Ultimate Reality, just in itself, as a kind of blank. Specific arrangements of reality (flowers, happy puppies, stars, etc) –- fine and good. But the Real, the Absolute, the Ground of Being –- that’s neutral. In this sense, the secularist repays to Nature, or to the source of Nature, her “overwhelming indifference.”

Carlsmith considers Eliezer Yudkowsky, one of the first people to take seriously the existential risk from artificial intelligence, as a prototypical example of a deep atheist, quoting him as saying: "You are not safe. Ever… No one begins to truly search for the Way until their parents have failed them, their gods are dead, and their tools have shattered in their hand." Carlsmith remarks: "This, I suggest, isn't just standard atheism. Lots of atheists find other \`gods,' in the extended sense I have in mind. That's why I said \`shallow atheism,' above. Deep atheism tries to propagate its godlessness harder. To be even more an orphan. To learn, everywhere, from the theist's mistake." And: "Thus: our most basic condition, presupposed almost by the concept of epistemology itself, is one of vulnerability. Vulnerability to that first and most fearsome Other: God, the Creator, the Uncontrolled, the Real. And the Real, absent further evidence, could be anything. It could definitely eat you, and your babies. Oh, indeed, it could do far, far worse."

## An optimistic cosmist

Am I a deep atheist? Not exactly. I _am_ an atheist – I don't think there is a God, in the sense of the Abrahamic and many other religions[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn6). But I am what might be called an _optimistic cosmist_. If you consider the fundamental laws of physics, as currently understood, they don't obviously suggest friendliness. The universe in the large seems indifferent or even hostile to casual inspection. And yet we somehow live inside a relatively friendly and safe local enclave. Familiarity makes us take this for granted, but it depends upon the universe having many _a priori_ surprising properties that enable tiny physical systems (like human beings!) to carve out safe and friendly local enclaves, despite that appearance of cosmic indifference.

Those surprising properties include things like: mathematics exists and can be expressed and explored physically by those same tiny physical systems, with astonishing results[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn7); it's possible for those tiny physical system to develop simple but powerful scientific theories, and to understand and control and improve their environment, to make it friendly and meaningful; stable, self-maintaining, fault-tolerant, self-reproducing systems can emerge and persist and evolve into entities capable of the above[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn8). None of these properties are obvious. We can easily conceive of universes which are incomprehensible and uncontrollable by any system within that universe. Yet the properties I mentioned above (and many others) mean we live in a universe where we can build surprisingly good approximations to utopias[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn9), at least for limited periods and in limited regions like cities or planets. Where platforms for ambient friendliness – things like immune systems, legal and judicial systems, and military defense – are not only possible but potentially common. This combination of cosmic indifference and the universe's surprising amenability to local friendliness defines the optimistic cosmist position. It acknowledges the universe's apparent hostility while recognizing the unexpectedness and value of our ability to create meaningful enclaves within it. This is astounding, and it's interesting to ponder universes in which it's not true[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn10)[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn11).

## Connecting deep atheism and optimistic cosmism to the Vulnerable World Hypothesis and Artificial Superintelligence

One reason I respond strongly to deep atheism is my interest in the Vulnerable World Hypothesis. This is a term introduced by Nick Bostrom[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn12), for the idea that (roughly) the technology tree of many civilizations will contain a technology that destroys that civilization. While we humans have survived technological advances so far, the universe offers no guarantee that technology will continue to be manageable. Indeed, arguably we're already sitting on a ticking time bomb, in the form of thermonuclear weapons.

If the Vulnerable World Hypothesis is true, and all or most emerging civilizations eventually destroy themselves, then it's a limit on the friendliness of the universe. Yes, the universe would allow us to briefly create friendly local enclaves. But the same properties that enable such enclaves also ensure that eventually we destroy ourselves. Believing the Vulnerable World Hypothesis is a particular form of deep atheism; and a particular denial of optimistic cosmism. It's also, as Carl Sagan observed in 1978, one possible explanation for the Fermi Paradox – why we don't see aliens everywhere, and (perhaps) why friendliness hasn't gone from local to near-universal:

> Of course, not all scientists accept the notion that other advanced civilizations exist. A few who have speculated on this subject lately are asking: if extraterrestrial intelligence is abundant, why have we not already seen its manifestations?… Why have these beings not restructured the entire Galaxy for their convenience?… Why are they not here? The temptation is to deduce that there are at most only a few advanced extraterrestrial civilizations – either because we are one of the first technical civilizations to have emerged, or because it is the fate of all such civilizations to destroy themselves before they are much further along.

This is perhaps why deep atheism has come to mind so often for me over the last year. My habitual and ingrained optimistic cosmism is being tested by the prospect of [existential risk from science and technology](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/xrisk.html), especially from artificial superintelligence.

## Deep atheism as science?

Carlsmith spends much of his essay exploring deep atheism as a natural phenomenon, and I've done that too. That is, asking about it as a question of psychological and cultural experience. But, of course, one can also treat it as primarily a scientific question: is it true? What evidence and argument could we give for or against it? What would it even mean to be true?

A great deal of work would be necessary to make it well formulated. Just as one tiny example, notions like "friendliness" and "hostility" are surprisingly tricky. They seem very natural to us, as human beings, but that naturalness is the outcome of a long evolutionary chain. Orientations of friendliness are present in many mammals and some other species, but are largely absent in many parts of the biosphere[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn13). A shark doesn't feel much we'd regard as empathy or friendliness; much less so an amoeba. In this view, notions like friendliness and hostility seem like largely parochial concerns, a quirk of a particular evolutionary path on earth. The wasp-caterpillar parasitism seems terrible for us, but is it so bad in any other sense? Perhaps that's merely provincial thinking. Now, I certainly believe those notions could be made much less parochial. But it'd be tricky, and this is illustrative of the large tangle of issues that would need to be confronted to make "In what sense is deep atheism correct?" into a well formulated scientific question.

## Concluding thoughts

Early in the essay I mentioned some challenges with the term "deep atheism". One thing I very much like about the term is that it emphasizes the relationship of the concept to religion. I gave a long list of ways we develop these kinds of stances toward the universe – genetic predisposition, culture and environment, and so on. An important addition to make to that list is religion and myth[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/deepatheism/index.html#fn14), broadly construed: powerful, shared stories that help create cultures and shared identity, even a shared consciousness. In his book _Dominion_ the historian Tom Holland has argued that ideas from the Christian gospels actually helped reshape the entire world's ideas of good behaviour, of what it means to live a successful life, especially the role of kindness and so on. It's interesting to ponder which myths carry deep atheism (or its opposite). And, in general, to ponder what stances can (or should) be carried by myth, and how. I've gradually come to believe these shared myths define a surprising amount about the outcomes civilization gets.

I wrote and released these notes with trepidation. It all seems a little galaxy-brained! On the other hand, it seems likely to me and many others that humanity is years or decades away from creating artificial superintelligence. And the friendliness of the universe is going to be tested in a new way. Some galaxy-braining is in order – we need some unusual ideas to rise to this extraordinary occasion!
