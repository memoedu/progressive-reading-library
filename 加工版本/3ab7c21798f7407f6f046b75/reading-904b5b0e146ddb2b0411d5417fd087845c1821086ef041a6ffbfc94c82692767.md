# 背景

---

## 正文

有些人一直在问我的 job search 进行得怎么样——我是如何了解到现在工作的这家 company，又如何决定选择它的。我想就此写一点东西，给有兴趣做类似事情的人作为 case study，也因为我在这个过程中学到了一些有趣的东西。

## 背景

大学最初几年，我优先考虑的是在多个可能从事的 fields 中 getting experience——我尝试过在 Fog Creek、Jane Street 和 GiveWell 工作，也尝试过 cofounding 一家 startup。到那段时间结束时，对于自己想做什么，我得出了几个 conclusions。

从 altruistic career choice 的 considerations 来看，我决定自己大概应该专注于做那些最有可能做得特别出色的事情，而不是试图 naively maximize earnings 或 maximize direct good done——基本上是因为，我很不确定拥有很多钱是否会有帮助，也很不确定什么能带来最多的 direct good，但把事情做得出色是一种 robustly good outcome，日后可以转化为许多不同的优势。

从过去的经历来看，与 technology 和 software 相关的事情似乎最有潜力让我做得出色，而且日后利用这些 abilities 改善世界的 opportunities 也最广泛，因此它们似乎是最值得进一步探索的 options。不过，我已经做过一次 software internship，虽然那段经历很有趣，但我不想再做非常相似的事情——我猜自己在 standard software-engineering internships 上已经遇到了 diminishing returns。

与此同时，我也逐渐意识到，在 Harvard 读第四年似乎并不是特别 high-value，而且凭借我的 Advanced Placement credits，只要愿意，我就可以在三年内 graduate。因此，我意识到自己需要在夏季的 job search 上投入很多精力，确保找到的工作既不是上一次 internship 的重复，又能在我决定不回 Harvard 时，让我乐意将它转为 full-time job。

## 寻找

我通过许多不同的方式寻找 opportunities：

-   我先列出了一批记得听说过、而且可能特别有趣的 companies。

-   我尝试通过关注 [Y Combinator](http://blog.ycombinator.com/)，以及浏览一些我认为眼光不错的 venture capitalists 的 portfolios，来了解其他有趣的 firms，例如 [Founders Fund](http://www.foundersfund.com/)。

-   我的室友 Carl 为数量多得离谱的 internships 参加过 interviewed，到现在大概已经认识 Silicon Valley 一半的 recruiters 了。他给了我许多很棒的 recommendations，我的其他一些室友和同学也是如此。

-   我尝试寻找那些以 selective 或 interview process 困难而闻名的 companies，以便 calibrate 我对自己作为 candidate 有多优秀的 assessment。（过去几年，我在这方面遇到过困难，因为我只完成过少数几次 interview processes。）

-   此前，我通过 rationality 和 effective altruism communities 结识了一些年长的朋友，他们与 technology companies 有广泛联系。我写信询问他们是否知道什么有趣的 job opportunities 的线索。我认真地沿着他们提供的 chain of references 追踪了大约 three degrees，并使用 [Boomerang](http://www.boomeranggmail.com/hp1/index.html) 确保自己没有漏掉任何 follow-ups。

做完这些之后，我手上有了一份大约包含 20-30 家 companies 的名单，接下来需要加以筛减。

## 筛选

下一步是弄清楚，我究竟想从一份 job 中得到什么。我最终确定的 criteria 是：

-   **Learning**——尤其是考虑到我可能不读 Harvard 的第四年，我想确保无论最后在哪里工作，都能继续学到东西。

-   **Responsibility**——我希望有很大机会 responsible for 大事，因为这样我就更有能力为 employer 的 success 作出 meaningful contributions。这似乎对 accumulating career capital、connections 等很重要。

-   **Importance**——我想在一家看起来 likely to succeed 的 company 工作。对于“likely to succeed”，我并没有非常复杂的 heuristics，但我通常会尽量避开 consumer products，因为 consumers 太疯狂，而我又不理解他们。此外，consumer products 更 visible，也更受到 hyped 和 glamorized，因此会 over-subscribed。

    与 Paul Graham 的看法相反，我也会避开那些看起来很蠢的 ideas，专注于听起来是在 solving real problems 或 fixing real inefficiencies 的事情。

我觉得这些都是相当直截了当的 criteria。唯一值得注意的遗漏大概是 salary：我不确定自己长期是否想用 tech skills 来 earn to give。而且，即使我想，我的 lifetime earnings 对我现在 develop 的 career capital 和 skills 的依赖，也不亚于对 salary 的依赖。因此，我不觉得第一份 job 是 high-paying 有那么重要。

## 申请

最后，在考虑了几十家 firms 之后，我向五家提交了申请（D. E. Shaw、Bridgewater、Planet Labs、Clever 和 ALR）；当我决定 accept an offer、结束寻找时，待办清单上还有另外几家。

这些 application processes 有的极其 rigorous（Shaw 让我坐飞机去 New York，花整整一天参加每场一小时、围绕 math problems 的轮番盘问），也有的相当随意（ALR 只安排了几次 Skype interviews——考虑到我最初申请的是 internship，这就更容易理解了）。

五家都给了我 offers，我最终选择了 ALR，这是一家使用 machine learning 来理解 loans 如何 work 的 start-up，原因有几个：

-   它是我申请的机构中规模最小的，因此我会有最多的 responsibility。

-   他们的 product 是一种 investment vehicle，这意味着，就 product importance 而言，他们的影响超出了自身规模：他们只有少数 customers，但我的 code 将 handling 价值数百万美元的 investments。

-   我的大部分工作都会围绕 machine learning 展开，它似乎是一套 powerful toolset，而我想进一步了解它。

-   他们有充分理由说明，自己尤其 well-positioned，能够 fix 一处很大的 market inefficiency，这预示着他们日后有不错的 success chances。

开始工作大约一个月后，我认定在 ALR 工作确实比再读一年大学更酷，于是给 cofounders 发了一封友好的 email，回顾我为他们取得的所有进展，并建议他们 hire 我 full-time，他们很快就这么做了。

（按惯例补一句 P.S.：我们正在 hiring！如果你觉得这听起来有趣，[给我发封 email](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)。）

## 经验教训

-   ALR 是我通过询问朋友的朋友找到的 firms 之一——实际上，ALR 的 cofounder 与我之间有 three or degrees of separation，而我之所以知道他们的存在，也只是因为当朋友的朋友告诉我“过几个月再来问问”时，我很认真地进行了 following up。我怀疑，许多最令人兴奋的 opportunities 都是这样找到的，所以在 job searches 期间，充分动用你的 network 是值得的，即使你_可以_只依靠 Internet searches 和偶然的 word-of-mouth（就像我以前一直做的那样）。

-   在我尝试过的其他寻找工作单位的 strategies 中，最不 effective 的大概就是列出以前听说过的地方。不过，这可能是我的 search criteria 所造成的——我没有考虑许多最知名的 internship programs（Google、Facebook、Microsoft、Amazon），因为我觉得在那里学不到多少东西，也不会对什么重要的事情承担 responsibility。

-   我怀疑，在 ALR 已经工作一个月之后才提出 convert to full-time，在我们讨论 compensation 时多少 weakened 了我的 negotiating power，因为当时我没有 competing offers。（另一方面，我可能也获得了额外的 leverage，因为我已经为他们完成了一些相当重要的工作。我不完全确定哪种 effect dominated。）

-   Interviews 占用的时间和精力比我预想的更多。我很庆幸自己只在五个地方参加了 interviewed。我希望自己当时能更果断一些：当我认为某些 interview processes 不会影响我的决定时，就将其 cutting off，转而在其他地方开始新的 processes。例如，在我基本决定想去 Bay Area 工作之后，我仍继续在 D. E. Shaw 进行 interviewing，而我觉得这并不值得花那些时间。

-   我觉得，自己本可以更 efficient 地利用 interview process，了解我正在考虑的那些地方。部分原因可能在于，phone screens 比实际到办公室走访要 lower-bandwidth 得多。另一部分原因则是，我没有准备好一组值得观察的事项，或要问 interviewers 的问题。

我肯定遗漏了这个过程中许多最有意思的部分。关于具体经过，你还有其他问题吗？comment threads 就是用来做这个的！

## 原文

Some people have been asking me how my job search went—how I found out about and decided on the current company that I’m working for. I thought I’d write a bit about it, as a case study for other folks interested in doing similar things and because I learned some interesting stuff along the way.

## Background

For my first few years of college I prioritized getting experience in a bunch of different potential fields—I tried working at Fog Creek, Jane Street, and GiveWell, and cofounding a startup. By the end of that I came to a couple conclusions about what I wanted to do.

In terms of altruistic career choice considerations, I decided I should probably focus on doing the things I could be most awesome at, rather than trying to naively maximize earnings or maximize direct good done—basically, because I’m fairly uncertain about whether having lots of money will be helpful, and I’m fairly uncertain about what does the most direct good, but being awesome at things is a robustly good outcome that can be parlayed into many different advantages later.

Historically, technology- and software-related things seemed to have some of the greatest potential for me to be awesome at them, and also the widest breadth of opportunities to improve the world with those abilities later, so they seemed like the most promising options to pursue further. But I had already done one software internship, and while it was a fun experience, I didn’t want to do anything very similar—I guessed that I’d hit diminishing returns for standard software-engineering internships.

I was concurrently in the process of realizing that studying at Harvard for a fourth year didn’t seem especially high-value, and that I could graduate in three years if I wanted to thanks to my Advanced Placement credits. So I realized that I needed to put a lot of effort into my summer job search to make sure I found something that wasn’t a repeat of my previous internship, and that I would be happy turning into a full-time job if I decided I didn’t want to go back to Harvard.

## Searching

I looked for opportunities in a bunch of different ways:

-   I started by listing a bunch of companies that I remembered hearing about that might be especially interesting.

-   I tried to learn about other interesting firms by following [Y Combinator](http://blog.ycombinator.com/) and looking through the portfolios of some venture capitalists that I thought had good taste, e.g. [Founders Fund](http://www.foundersfund.com/).

-   My roommate Carl interviewed for an absurd number of internships and probably knows half of the recruiters in Silicon Valley by this point. He gave me a bunch more excellent recommendations, as did some of my other roommates and classmates.

-   I tried to find companies that were known for being selective or having a difficult interview process, so that I could calibrate my assessment of how good a candidate I was. (In past years I had difficulty with this since I had only completed a few interview processes.)

-   I had previously made a number of older friends who were well-connected to technology companies through the rationality and effective altruism communities. I wrote to them asking if they had any leads on interesting job opportunities. I assiduously followed the chain of references they gave me out to about three degrees, using [Boomerang](http://www.boomeranggmail.com/hp1/index.html) to make sure I didn’t lose any follow-ups.

At the end of all of these I had a list of about 20-30 companies, which I then had to prune.

## Filtering

The next step was to figure out what I actually wanted from a job. The criteria I eventually decided on were:

-   **Learning**—especially since I thought I might skip my fourth year of Harvard, I wanted to be sure I would continue to learn things wherever I ended up working.

-   **Responsibility**—I wanted to have a good chance of being responsible for big things, because I’d be better able to make meaningful contributions to my employer’s success. That seems important for accumulating career capital, connections, etc.

-   **Importance**—I wanted to work at a company that seemed likely to succeed. I didn’t have very complicated heuristics for “likely to succeed”, but I generally tried to avoid consumer products because consumers are crazy and I don’t understand them. Plus consumer products are more visible, hyped and glamorized, hence over-subscribed.

    In defiance of Paul Graham, I also avoided ideas that seemed dumb, and focused on things that sounded like they were solving real problems or fixing real inefficiencies.

I think these were all pretty straightforward criteria. The only notable omission is probably salary: I’m not sure that in the long term I want to use my tech skills to earn to give. And even if I do, my lifetime earnings depend as much on the career capital and skills I develop right now as they do on my salary. So I didn’t think it was that important to have my first job be high-paying.

## Applying

In the end, after considering dozens of firms, I applied to five (D. E. Shaw, Bridgewater, Planet Labs, Clever and ALR), and had a few more on my to-do list when I decided to accept an offer and end my search.

The application processes ranged from extremely rigorous (Shaw flew me to New York for a full day of one-hour grillings on math problems) to fairly casual (ALR gave me just a couple Skype interviews—more understandable since I was applying for an internship originally).

I got offers from all five and ended up picking ALR, a start-up that uses machine learning to understand how loans work, for a few reasons:

-   It was the smallest place I applied to, so I’d have the most responsibility.

-   Their product was an investment vehicle, which means that they punch above their weight in terms of product importance: they had only a few customers, but my code would be handling millions of dollars’ worth of investments.

-   Most of my work would be on machine learning, which seemed like a powerful toolset that I wanted to learn more about.

-   They had a good case that they were especially well-positioned to fix a large market inefficiency, which bodes well for their chances of success down the road.

About a month after I started, I decided that working for ALR was in fact cooler than doing another year of college, so I sent the cofounders a nice email recounting all the progress I had made for them and suggesting they hire me full-time, which they promptly did.

## Lessons

-   ALR was one of the firms I found by asking friends of friends—actually, the cofounder of ALR was three or degrees of separation from me, and I only even knew they existed because I was meticulous about following up when a friend of a friend told me to “check back in a couple months”. I suspect that many of the most exciting opportunities are found this way, so it pays to really pump your network during job searches even if you _could_ just rely on Internet searches and random word-of-mouth (as I’d previously been doing).

-   Of the other place-finding strategies I tried, probably the least effective was listing places I’d heard about before. This might be an artifact of my search criteria, though—I ignored a lot of the best-known internship programs (Google, Facebook, Microsoft, Amazon) because I thought I wouldn’t get to learn very much or be responsible for anything important.

-   I suspect that asking to convert to full-time after I’d already been working at ALR for a month weakened my negotiating power somewhat when we were discussing compensation, because I had no competing offers at the time. (On the other hand, I probably got additional leverage because I had done some fairly important work for them already. I’m not totally sure which effect dominated.)

-   Interviews took up more time and attention than I expected. I’m glad I only interviewed at five places. I wish I had been more aggressive about cutting off interview processes when I didn’t think they would affect my decision, and starting up new processes at other places instead. For instance, I kept interviewing at D. E. Shaw after I had basically decided that I wanted to work in the Bay Area, and I don’t think it was worth the time.

-   I think I could have been more efficient about using the interview process to learn about the places I was looking at. Part of this might be that phone screens are just a lot lower-bandwidth than actually visiting the office. Part of it was also that I didn’t have a good set of things to look for or ask my interviewers.

I’m sure I’m leaving out a lot of the most interesting parts of this process. Do you have other questions about how it worked? That’s what comment threads are for!
