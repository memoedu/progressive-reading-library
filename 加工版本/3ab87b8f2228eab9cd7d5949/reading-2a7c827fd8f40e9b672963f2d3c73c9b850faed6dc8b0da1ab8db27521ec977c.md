[Michael Nielsen](https://michaelnotebook.com/)

[Astera Institute（Astera 研究所）](https://astera.org/)

2023年11月26日

_改编自简短发言，介绍我过去和现在的一些创作兴趣。为2023年11月在旧金山举行的一场 workshop 准备。_

如何 accelerate science 和 technology 的 progress？这个问题一直处于我几乎所有创作工作的核心[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn1)。除了研究具体的 scientific problems，我还做了大量工作，旨在 improving 人类用于 discovery 的更广泛的 tools 和 systems。要简短地介绍我的职业生涯并不容易，不过，下面这份我的书籍和类书籍项目清单，或许能提供一个引人联想的侧面（描述中有些自夸之处，敬请见谅）：

1.  ["A Vision of Metascience"（与 Kanjun Qiu 合著，2022）](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html)：在这本短书中，我们 sketch 了一种 approach，用来 rapidly and radically improving 我们的 scientific institutions。
2.  ["Quantum Country"（与 Andy Matuschak 合著，2019）](https://quantum.country/)：人们觉得 quantum mechanics 很难，其中一个原因是有太多新的 notation 和 terminology。实在令人难以招架！为了 reduce 这个 barrier，我们在这本书中 built 了一个 _memory system_——一种新的 interface，让人们几乎不费力就能 remember 读过的内容。我们希望，这能帮助消除 rote memory 的负担所构成的 understanding 的 barrier。我们称它为 _mnemonic medium_，并打算把它作为一个供更广泛使用的 prototype。因此，虽然表面上 "Quantum Country" 是对 quantum mechanics 和 quantum computing 的 online introduction，但它也是一种新的书——一次 design 新 medium 的尝试，这种 medium 从根本上超越纸质书，transforming 阅读体验。
3.  ["Neural Networks and Deep Learning"（2015）](https://neuralnetworksanddeeplearning.com/)：对该 field 的 core principles 的一次 distillation；一次关于 interactive learning 的 experiment；以及一次关于 open online publishing 的 impact 的 experiment（根据 Google Analytics，有 7 million users、26 million pageviews，覆盖 235 个国家）。
4.  ["Reinventing Discovery"（2011）](https://www.amazon.com/Reinventing-Discovery-New-Networked-Science/dp/0691160198/)：一套关于 collective intelligence 的 theory；一篇探讨 improved political economy of science 的 essay；一部倡导 open science 的作品；并帮助将 "open science" 打造成了一个 worldwide brand。
5.  ["An Introduction to Majorization and Its Applications to Quantum Mechanics"（草稿，2002）](https://michaelnielsen.org/papers/maj-book-notes.pdf)：一部 monograph，它 finds 并 develops 了一个隐藏在 quantum mechanics 中的 mathematical structure，这个 structure governing dynamics、measurement 和 entanglement。
6.  ["Quantum Computation and Quantum Information"（与 Ike Chuang 合著，2000）](https://www.amazon.com/Quantum-Computation-Information-10th-Anniversary/dp/1107002176/)：帮助为该 field distill 出一套 canon；自出版以来的 23 年间一直是 standard text；是过去 40 年里 physics 中 most-cited 的作品。

除了写书，我还做过许多项目！但我确实热爱写书，尤其是那些有助于 catalyze 和 distill nascent research fields 与 creative communities 的书。我觉得这些 proto-fields 虽然混乱，却令人无法抗拒。通常只有你和寥寥几位同道，在迷雾与困惑中，与 fundamental questions 苦苦搏斗。往往并没有明确的办法来 address 那些 fundamentals。比如，在今天的 metascience 中就能看到这一点：对于如何 empirically answer 这样一些 basic questions，我们目前只有 weak 且 incomplete 的 ideas：在什么情况下，一种针对 science 的 organizational approach 会比另一种更好？

正如你们所见，我的工作几乎全都围绕着 accelerating science 和 technology。然而，过去一年里，我发现自己十分矛盾。我开始怀疑，accelerating science 和 technology 究竟是不是一个好主意。一方面，我相信我们仍处在 science 和 technology 的极早期，还有许多美好的事物有待 discovered：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/assets/wonderful.png)

另一方面，我也想知道，还有哪些 dangerous 的事物有待 discovered。假设你能向一位 oracle advisor 请教，它理解关于 science 和 technology 的_一切_。不只是当今的 science 和 technology，而是未来所有 _possible_ 的 science 和 technology。这样的 oracle 当然能帮助你 make 许多美好的事物。但我也想知道，它能帮助你 make 哪些 dangerous 的事物？scientific understanding 是否最终会 inevitably enable 既 catastrophically dangerous 又 inherently ungovernable 的 technologies？如果是这样，我们如何防止这种情况发生[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn2)？

换句话说：随着我们不断 improve 自己的 understanding，越来越多 helpful technologies 变得容易为 individuals 所获取。不幸的是，同样的 progress 也使越来越多 destructive technologies 变得容易为 individuals 所获取：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/assets/graph.png)

我们最深刻的 understanding 以非常 general 的方式，使 universe 变得更加 malleable；因此，它往往是 intrinsically dual use 的，既 enabling 美好的事物，也 enabling 可怕的事物。例如：relativity 和 quantum mechanics 的 insights 帮助促成了 transistor 和许多 medical breakthroughs；它们也促成了 hydrogen bomb。我们是否能只得到美好的事物，而不得到可怕的事物，这一点并不清楚。人们自然会想，这最终会通向何处。是否有可能 make 出既比 hydrogen bomb 更加 destructive、又远比它更容易 construct 的 technologies？这或许听起来荒谬，但 science 和 technology 的历史充满了这样的 discoveries：从先前的视角看，它们似乎是 impossible 的——事实上，有时甚至是 inconceivable 的——后来却变得稀松平常。树浆上用 Graphite 画出的弯弯曲曲的线条，有时能帮助我们 recombine material elements，创造奇迹！想想看，在早期埃及人眼中，steam engine、radio、Wright flyer 或 iPhone 之类的东西，会显得多么 impossible。

把上一段的担忧说得更 abstractly 一些：scientific knowledge 最终是否会 advance 到这样的程度，使我们 discover 出以下 technologies：(a) trivial to make，甚至可能由仅仅拥有正确 knowledge 的 individuals 轻易 made；而且 (b) 它们会 threaten civilization？

这些就是我所谓的 _recipes for ruin_，意思是 simple、easily implementable，却又 catastrophic 的 technologies[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn3)。

近来，关于 recipes for ruin 的具体 possibilities 已有许多讨论——engineered pandemics、artificial superintelligence (ASI)，等等。Nick Bostrom 在讨论 "Vulnerable World Hypothesis" 时，对这一更广泛的 general possibility 做了富有洞察力的 analysis[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn4)。一个多世纪以来，science fiction 作家也一直在 explored Recipes for ruin；其中一些 examples 颇具幻想色彩，包括 easily-made black holes、easily-made anti-matter bombs，以及 destabilizing states of matter，例如 Kurt Vonnegut 的书 "Cat's Cradle" 中的 Ice-9，还有许多其他例子。撇开 Science fiction，Earth 的过去也有许多发人深思的 recipes for ruin 的 partial examples，包括：2.4 billion 年前 photosynthesis 的偶然 creation，使 earth 的 atmosphere 从 oxygen-free 变为 oxygenated，suffocating 了大多数 organisms；self-replicating life 的兴起，彻底 transformed 了 Earth；甚至还包括 fire 这样的 exothermic reactions。如果 Earth 的 atmosphere 再 oxygen-rich 一点，那么 fire 就会严重得多，intense firestorms 也会常见得多；我们很可能根本不会在这里思考这种可能性。

事实上，并不能明显断定，不存在仍然 unknown、hard-to-ignite 却又 catastrophic 的 reactive pathways。一个著名的例子是，Edward Teller 和他的合作者 Emil Konopinski、Cloyd Marvin 在 1946 年考虑过这种可能性，当时他们写了一份非凡的 report[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn5)，investigating hydrogen bombs 是否可能通过 self-sustaining reaction，将全世界的整个 atmosphere set alight。他们正确地 concluded，不会如此。但是，正如 Toby Ord 所指出的[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn6)，一项类似的 calculation 在 1954 年 Castle Bravo nuclear test 中 missed 了一条 crucial reactive pathway。在后来那次 analysis 中，一个 missing neutron 导致了 15 megatonne yield，而不是 5 megatonnes，并害死了许多无辜的人。幸好 error 出在 Castle Bravo analysis 中，而不是 atmospheric ignition analysis 中。

人们很容易把关于这类 recipes for ruin 的讨论斥为出于恐惧的 catastrophizing。Progress 来自 optimistic plans！但要制定这样的 optimistic plans，什么才是明智的基础？即使暂且放下 pandemics 或 ASI 等迫在眉睫的担忧，这里仍有一组关于 science 和 technology 的 long-run development 与 governance 的 fundamental questions。最基本地说，也就是重申我先前的问题：对 nature 的 sufficiently deep understanding，是否会使 individual make catastrophic、ungovernable technologies 变得几乎 trivial？

这是一个关于 science 和 technology 极为长远的未来的问题。在人生的大部分时间里，我都忽略了这个问题，把注意力放在更迫近的担忧上。但我逐渐相信，它是我们 civilization 的一个 central question。如果你相信答案是“不，这样的 technologies 永远不会被 discovered”，那么你可能会认为，我们现有的 systems of governance 已经 sufficient。但如果你像我一样，相信答案是“是的，这样的 technologies 可能被 discovered”，那么问题就变成了：我们可以运用什么 institutional ideas 或 governance mechanisms，避免进入这个 dangerous realm？换句话说：明智的 accelerationism 是否 possible？如果是，要如何实现？如果不是，又该怎么办？或者再换一种说法，借用有史以来最伟大的 scientist-technologists 之一 John von Neumann 的提问：“我们能在 technology 之下 survive 吗”[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn7)？

这一切都在 challenges 我始终珍视的一条 principle：science 总体上是 good 的，benefiting humanity，因此应当 aggressively pursued，只对明显 negative applications 局部施加 constraints。比如，我们可能 ban leaded gasoline 之类的东西，但我们当然不会 ban 构成这类 inventions 背景的那种 basic research。然而，如今我对这条更广泛的 principle 产生了怀疑，并思考该用什么来取代它。关键在于，任何替代方案都必须仍然让我们获得 science 和 technology 的大部分 benefits——事实上，甚至可能 accelerate 它的许多 aspects——同时又不让我们 irreversibly 暴露于 catastrophic danger[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn8)。我生性极其乐观，但这一组问题确实让我苦苦挣扎。

通常的回应是，我们通过 governance feedback loops 来应对这一问题。举个例子：在成长过程中，我一直认为人类很可能会深受以下问题之害：acid rain；ozone hole；nuclear war；peak oil；climate change；等等。但在每一种情况下，人类都以非凡的智慧作出了回应——Vienna and Montreal protocols 等帮助我们应对了 ozone hole；许多想法（Swanson's Law、carbon taxes 等）正在帮助我们应对 climate change；诸如此类。threat 越严重，response 就越强；其结果是一种 co-evolution[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn9)：在我们 develop destructive technologies 的同时，我们也在寻找 governing 它们和 blunting 它们的 negative effects 的方法。但如果一种 sufficiently powerful technology 对 individuals 而言也足够容易 make，我就看不出通常的 governance feedback loop 如何能足以应对。它必须被更强有力的东西取代。什么样的 strengthening 才会奏效？这正是我正在思考的问题：什么 governance principles 能够确保 science and technology 与 society 之间形成 healthy co-evolution？我喜欢这一思考方向，因为尽管我觉得这个问题极具挑战性，它也为我提供了一个根本上乐观而积极的立足点。感谢大家的聆听！

November 26, 2023

_Adapted from brief remarks to describe some of my past and current creative interests. Prepared for a workshop held November 2023 in San Francisco._

How to accelerate the progress of science and technology? This question has been at the heart[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn1) of nearly all my creative work. Beyond working on specific scientific problems, I've also done a lot of work aimed at improving the broader tools and systems humans use for discovery. It's challenging to convey my career briefly, but a perhaps evocative slice is this list of my books and book-like projects (with apologies for immodesty in the descriptions):

1.  ["A Vision of Metascience" (joint with Kanjun Qiu, 2022)](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html): In this short book we sketch an approach to rapidly and radically improving our scientific institutions.
2.  ["Quantum Country" (joint with Andy Matuschak, 2019)](https://quantum.country/): One reason people find quantum mechanics difficult is because there's so much new notation and terminology. It's overwhelming! To reduce that barrier, we built a _memory system_ into this book – a new kind of interface that makes it almost effortless for people to remember what they've read. The hope is that this will help lift the burden of rote memory as a barrier to understanding. We call it the _mnemonic medium_, and it's intended as a prototype for much wider use. And so while ostensibly "Quantum Country" is an online introduction to quantum mechanics and quantum computing, it's also a new kind of book — an attempt to design a new medium that goes essentially beyond paper books, transforming the experience of reading.
3.  ["Neural Networks and Deep Learning" (2015)](https://neuralnetworksanddeeplearning.com/): A distillation of the core principles of the field; an experiment in interactive learning; and an experiment in the impact of open online publishing (7 million users, 26 million pageviews, 235 countries, according to Google Analytics).
4.  ["Reinventing Discovery" (2011)](https://www.amazon.com/Reinventing-Discovery-New-Networked-Science/dp/0691160198/): A theory of collective intelligence; an essay on an improved political economy of science; a work of open science advocacy; and helped make "open science" into a worldwide brand.
5.  ["An Introduction to Majorization and Its Applications to Quantum Mechanics" (draft, 2002)](https://michaelnielsen.org/papers/maj-book-notes.pdf): A monograph which finds and develops a mathematical structure hidden in quantum mechanics, governing dynamics, measurement, and entanglement.
6.  ["Quantum Computation and Quantum Information" (joint with Ike Chuang, 2000)](https://www.amazon.com/Quantum-Computation-Information-10th-Anniversary/dp/1107002176/): Helped distill a canon for the field; the standard text for the 23 years since publication; the most-cited work in physics of the last 40 years.

I've done many projects apart from writing books! But I do love writing books, especially books that help catalyze and distill nascent research fields and creative communities. I find such proto-fields messy but irresistible. It's usually just you and a tiny handful of co-conspirators, wrestling with fundamental questions, amidst fog and confusion. Often there's no clear way to address those fundamentals. You see this in metascience today, for instance: right now we have only weak and incomplete ideas about how to empirically answer such basic questions as: when is one organizational approach to science better than another?

As you can see, my work has nearly all been about accelerating science and technology. And over the last year I find myself very conflicted. I've begun to question whether accelerating science and technology is a good idea at all. On the one hand, I believe we're still in the very early days of science and technology, and many wonderful things remain to be discovered:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/assets/wonderful.png)

On the other hand, I also wonder what dangerous things remain to be discovered. Suppose you could consult an oracle advisor who understood _everything_ about science and technology. Not just about current day science and technology, but about all _possible_ future science and technology. Such an oracle could certainly help you make many wonderful things. But I also wonder what dangerous things it could help you make? Might it be that scientific understanding will eventually and inevitably enable technologies which are both catastrophically dangerous and inherently ungovernable? If so, how can we prevent that from happening[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn2)?

Put another way: as we improve our understanding over time, it makes more and more helpful technologies easily available to individuals. Unfortunately, the same progress also sees more and more destructive technologies also become easily available to individuals:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/assets/graph.png)

Our deepest understanding makes the universe more malleable in very general ways; as a result it tends to be intrinsically dual use, enabling both wonderful and terrible things. For instance: the insights of relativity and quantum mechanics helped lead to the transistor and many medical breakthroughs; they also led to the hydrogen bomb. It's not clear you can get the wonderful things without the terrible things. It's natural to wonder where this eventually leads. Will it become possible to make technologies both much more destructive than the hydrogen bomb, and also far easier to construct? This perhaps seems ridiculous, but the history of science and technology is filled with discoveries that from a prior point of view appeared impossible – indeed, sometimes even inconceivable – but which become routine. Graphite squiggles on tree pulp sometime help us recombine material elements to do the marvelous! Think of how impossible things like the steam engine, radio, the Wright flyer, or the iPhone would have appeared to the early Egyptians.

Stating the concern of the last paragraph more abstractly: will scientific knowledge eventually advance so much that we discover technologies which are: (a) trivial to make, perhaps even easily made by individuals who merely have the right knowledge; and (b) which threaten civilization?

These are what I call _recipes for ruin_, meaning simple, easily implementable but catastrophic technologies[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn3).

There's been much recent discussion of specific possibilities for recipes for ruin – engineered pandemics, artificial superintelligence (ASI), and so on. Nick Bostrom has done an insightful analysis of the broader general possibility in his discussion of the "Vulnerable World Hypothesis"[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn4). Recipes for ruin have also been explored for more than a century by science fiction writers, with sometimes fanciful examples including easily-made black holes, easily-made anti-matter bombs, destabilizing states of matter such as Ice-9 in Kurt Vonnegut's book "Cat's Cradle", and many more. Science fiction aside, there are many suggestive partial examples of recipes for ruin from the Earth's past, including: the chance creation of photosynthesis 2.4 billion years ago, which saw the earth's atmosphere go from oxygen-free to oxygenated, suffocating most organisms; the rise of self-replicating life, which utterly transformed the Earth; and even exothermic reactions such as fire. If the Earth's atmosphere were a little more oxygen-rich then fire would be significantly worse, and intense firestorms much more common; it is perhaps likely we wouldn't be here to contemplate the possibility.

Indeed, it's not obvious there aren't still unknown hard-to-ignite but catastrophic reactive pathways. Famously, Edward Teller and his collaborators Emil Konopinski and Cloyd Marvin considered this possibility in 1946, when they wrote a remarkable report[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn5) investigating whether hydrogen bombs might set the world's entire atmosphere alight in a self-sustaining reaction. They concluded, correctly, that they wouldn't. But, as has been pointed out by Toby Ord[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn6), a similar calculation missed a crucial reactive pathway in the 1954 Castle Bravo nuclear test. One missing neutron in that later analysis resulted in a 15 megatonne yield rather than 5 megatonnes, and killed many innocent people. It's a good thing the error was in the Castle Bravo analysis, and not the atmospheric ignition analysis.

It's tempting to dismiss discussion of such recipes for ruin as fearful catastrophizing. Progress comes out of optimistic plans! But what is a wise foundation from which to make such optimistic plans? Even if you put aside immediate concerns like pandemics or ASI, there's a fundamental set of questions here about the long-run development and governance of science and technology. Most basically, and to reiterate my earlier question: will a sufficiently deep understanding of nature make it almost trivial for an individual to make catastrophic, ungovernable technologies?

This is a question about the very long run of science and technology. For most of my life I've ignored this question, focusing on more immediate concerns. But I've come to believe it is a central question for our civilization. If you believe the answer is "no, such technologies will never be discovered", then you may think the existing systems of governance we have are sufficient. But if you believe, as I do, that the answer is "yes, such technologies may be discovered", then the question becomes: what institutional ideas or governance mechanisms can we use to stay out of this dangerous realm? Put another way: is a wise accelerationism possible? If so, how? And if not, what to do about it? Or to put it still another way, as done by one of the great scientist-technologists to ever live, John von Neumann: "can we survive technology"[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn7)?

All of this challenges a principle I've always held dear: that science is on net good, benefiting humanity, and should be pursued aggressively, with constraints only applied locally to obviously negative applications. We may, for example, ban things like leaded gasoline, but we certainly don't ban the kind of basic research that forms the background to such inventions. But I now doubt the broader principle, and wonder what to replace it with. Crucially, any replacement must still give us most of the benefits of science and technology – indeed, perhaps even accelerate many aspects of it – but without irreversibly exposing us to catastrophic danger[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn8). I'm by temperament an extremely optimistic person, but I'm really struggling with this set of questions.

The conventional response is that we deal with this using governance feedback loops. As an example: I grew up thinking humanity would likely suffer tremendously from: acid rain; the ozone hole; nuclear war; peak oil; climate change; and so on. But in each case, humanity responded with tremendous ingenuity – things like the Vienna and Montreal protocols helped us deal with the ozone hole; many ideas (Swanson's Law, carbon taxes, and so on) are helping us deal with climate change; and so on. The worse the threat, the stronger the response; the result is a co-evolution[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html#fn9): while we develop destructive technologies, we also find ways of governing them and blunting their negative effects. But if a sufficiently powerful technology is sufficiently easy for individuals to make, I don't see how the usual governance feedback loop is sufficient. It must be replaced by something stronger. What sorts of strengthening would work? And that's what I'm thinking about: what governance principles would ensure a healthy co-evolution of science and technology with society? What I like about this is that while I'm finding it an extremely challenging question, it's also a fundamentally optimistic and positive place to stand. Thanks for your attention!
