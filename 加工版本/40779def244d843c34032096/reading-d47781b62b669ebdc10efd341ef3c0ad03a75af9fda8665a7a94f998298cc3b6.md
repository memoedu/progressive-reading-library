# Creative Skills 的两个 Components

---

## 正文

Creative skills 常常显得笼罩着一层神秘色彩。虽然学习 math 和 languages 可能很难，但至少它们还算 straightforward。要变得擅长，需要大量 practice，但也有前人走熟的道路可循。

 相比之下，creative skills 仿佛带着一种无法学会的光环。你要么拥有 creative talent，要么没有。   但事实并非如此。Creative skills 与你想 master 的其他任何 skill 一样，都是 learnable 的。在这篇文章中，我想说明如何做到这一点。

## Creative Skills 的两个 Components

你想学习的任何 creative skill，无论是 painting、writing、music、design，还是别的什么，本质上都有两个 components：

1.  **Technical skills（技术技能）。**
2.  **Ideational skills（构思技能）。**

Technical skills 是 creativity 中实际上并不需要——嗯——creativity 的那些方面。以我的 [portrait drawing challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/portrait-challenge/) 为例。虽然我为自己一个月的努力所取得的成果感到自豪，但其中完全没有涉及 creativity。这次挑战的 difficulty 纯粹是 technical 的。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Before-after-1024x576.png)

Technical skill 是 creative expertise 中最令 non-artists 印象深刻的方面。能够 flawlessly 地 play 一段非常 difficult 的 guitar solo，或用 paint 或 pencil 来 render 一个 image 的 compelling likeness，都很难做到。但严格来说，这并不需要多少 creativity。

相比之下，Ideational skills 更常是我们所说的 creativity 本身。我们认为的“creativity”，是做出既 valuable 又 original 的东西的 ability。在 practitioners 中，这些 ideational skills 通常更受欣赏，因为当你花了足够多的时间 practicing technical skills 后，它们往往就会失去那层神秘光环。

杰出的 artists 通常既擅长 creative skills 的 technical components，也擅长其 ideational components，尽管有些 artists 的 talents 显然更偏向其中一边。John Singer Sargent 的 paintings 是 technically virtuosic 的——尤其是当你了解到他的大部分 work 都是以 _alla prima_ 完成时；这意味着他的每幅 painting 大体上都是一气呵成，而不是像 Renaissance 时期更常见的那样，meticulously building up layers：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/sargeant-portrait.jpg)

相比之下，Vincent van Gogh 是拥有 mediocre technical skills 的 ideational genius 的最佳 paradigm。他无法跟上自己所在的 Parisian atelier 中的其他 artists，最终离开了。即使到了他 painting 的 mature phase，他也经常多次 repeated 同一个 subject，因为他很难把 details 处理正确：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/vangogh-portrait-917x1024.jpeg)

不过，尽管我个人非常喜欢 Sargent，但显然，Van Gogh 作为 painter 的 impact 要大得多，这在很大程度上归功于他 original 且 compelling 的 style。

记住这个 distinction，接下来让我们看看如何擅长 creative skills 的各个方面。

## 如何 Achieving Technical Proficiency

Technical proficiency 是两者中更容易 master 的一个。它绝非轻而易举，但 technical skills 可以通过刻苦努力和 practice 来提升，而 ideational genius 往往无法以同样的方式获得。

话虽如此，许多人很难 acquire creative skills 中的 technical proficiency，是因为他们采用了错误的方式。所有 technical skills 都可以 break down 为三个 essential parts：

1.  **Methods（方法）**。
2.  **Concepts（概念）**。
3.  **Practice（练习）**。

第一部分是，要学习一种 creative skill，你需要从一种 method 开始。这可能很难，因为许多 artists 已经如此充分地 overlearned 了自己的 skills，以至于他们看起来不再遵循任何特定的 method。

这是 portrait drawing 中最初令我困惑的一个方面。看到 street artists 在几分钟内就能 draw 出一个 good likeness，或者 technically excellent artists 只需寥寥几次 flicks of their brush 就能 render 一个 likeness，会让人觉得也许根本不存在 method，art 中的成功不过是 innate talent 或 intuition。

但这是对实际情况的误解。在许多 domains 中，researchers 发现，几乎所有 expertise 都遵循相似的 pattern。Skills 最初是 learned explicitly 的，但随着 practice 越来越多，部分 explicit learning 会 become automated——它们变得更像 background intuition，而不是你能够轻易表述出来的 method。[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18550 "关于详细内容，我在自己的书 <em>Get Better at Anything</em> 中，对 expertise 的 development 有更深入的讨论。")

例如，开始 portrait drawing challenge 时，我没有找到很好的 methods。因此，我以为只要投入大量带有 feedback 的 bulk practice，就是 improvement 的关键。我认为 high volumes 的 practice 是 essential 的，这一点是对的；但我以为 methods 是 irrelevant 的，这一点错了。

 事实上，大多数 portrait artists 都会使用多种 methods 来 gain accuracy。其中一些是 perceptual 的，通过各种手段 overcome optical illusions 或我们自然的 sensory limitations，从而 correctly assess 一个 image 中的 relative sizes、angles 和 tonal values。另一些则是 conceptual 的，从 lighting、geometry 和 anatomy 的 knowledge 出发，infer 那些并非立即 visible 的 shapes。

学习任何 technical skill 的起点，都是先了解所使用的 methods 是什么，然后 practice 它们，直到它们成为第二天性。

technical proficiency 的下一部分，与其说在于 rote practice，不如说在于 creating 一个 mental model，理解 medium 所提供的 possibilities 及其附带的 constraints。我很喜欢这段短片，它讨论了 Steven Spielberg 如何在电影 _Hook_ 中 achieved 一个 scene：其中一个 Lost Boys 看起来迅速挥下自己的剑，[距离 Robin Williams 的头仅几毫米](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/VU7YwZk8Fj8)。

在还没有 CGI 的时代，Spielberg 通过 backwards 地 filming 整个 scene 来 achieved 这个 effect，甚至包括 actors 的 dialog；因此，看似 near-fatal 的 sword strike，实际上是剑迅速 lifting up 后再 in reverse 呈现的结果。

technical virtuosity 的最后一个关键，是极大量的 practice。在这里，precision 和 fluency 都很重要。

Precision 是必要的，这里的意思是遵守你所采用的 method 能够提供的 accuracy，因为这正是你学习 underlying skill 的方式。因此，无论你是在学习 drawing a portrait 的 method，还是 playing a guitar solo，如果你允许自己犯大量 mistakes，你的 practice 就会是 ineffective 的。

Fluency 很重要，因为 motor skills，例如 guitar 的 strumming 或 paintbrush 的 flicking，如果以不同的 rhythms 或 movements 来 executed，就会完全不同。  我过去常常为 precision 与 fluency 之间的 tension 而苦恼。在 visual arts 中，“tighter”、更 controlled 的 styles，与“looser”、更 expressive 的 styles 之间，往往存在 contrast。有时，我觉得自己需要慢下来，变得更 precise 才能 improve。另一些时候，我又觉得只要放松一些，或许就能做得更好。   但我认为，这个 distinction 有些像一种 illusion。Looser 的“expressive”movements，与 tightly controlled 的 movements，只不过是不同种类的 motor skill。这两类 movements 都需要 practice 才能 master，但这并不 negate 对更广泛的 technical proficiency 的需要。就像生活中的许多 skills 一样，解决办法是既 practice 这些 movements，也 practice 支撑它们的 methods。

## 如何拥有更好的 Ideas

creative skills 的另一个 component 是拥有 good、original ideas。即使这一点也并非完全脱离 technical proficiency。如果你想了解在一种 medium 中 expressing good、original ideas 的 opportunities，就必须先了解这种 medium 能 affords 什么，以及它会 constrains 什么。因此，Creativity 至少在一定程度上依赖于 proficiency。

但把 technical virtuosity 简单归结为 creativity 是错误的，因为 creativity 所依赖的不仅仅是非常擅长这门 craft 的 technical aspects。

拥有真正 good、original ideas，是两个不同 components 的 mixture：

1.  **Knowledge.**（知识）
2.  **Judgement.**（判断力）

Knowledge 是最重要的部分。有充分的 evidence 表明，creativity 本身在很大程度上是一种 random process。将更 creative 的 artists 与不那么 creative 的 artists 区分开的，与其说是 generate 大量 random ideas 的 ability（我们都有这种 ability），不如说是更 creative 的 artists 拥有更好的 quality filters，因此能更 reliably 地 judge 哪些 ideas 确实是 good。

Vincent van Gogh 就是一个例子。尽管他在 technically 方面颇为吃力，但他对所处时代的 artistic landscape 极为 attuned。他的哥哥是一位 art dealer，而 Van Gogh 与 art world 中的 emerging trends 有着大量接触。因此，尽管与那些 Salon-trained contemporaries 相比，他常被描述为一个“outsider”，但他拥有 insider knowledge。

同样，在我那些成功的 serial authors 朋友身上，相比他们的 writing skill，更令我佩服的是他们能迅速 ascertain 哪些 ideas 可能会是 popular 且 engaging 的。

一般而言，成功的 creatives 是其 fields 的 outsiders，这种说法是一个 myth。[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18550 "关于 creative success 的一些 data，我推荐 Dean Simonton 的 <em>Greatness</em>。") 尽管最 creative 的 works 可能来自一个 field 的边缘，来自那些较少投入 dominant styles 或 paradigms 的人，但真正 creative 的 outsider art 实际上几乎不存在。

这表明，immersion 于一项 creative endeavor 周围的 social world，对于真正成功的 creativity 至关重要。也就是说，如果你脱离了那个对什么才算 good、original work 作出 fundamental judgements 的世界，就必然无法 produce 符合这些标准的东西。

creative idea generation 的第二个 aspect，与其说在于拥有 creative ideas，不如说在于拥有真正 create 它们的 disposition。Creativity，尤其是 original work，需要 effort 和 risk-taking。你必须尝试可能行不通的东西，而且尤其对于许多 high-art pursuits 而言，它们在经济上也可能没有回报。

大量 valuable creativity 被扼杀，并不是因为 artist 缺少 original ideas，而是因为要求其 conform 于某种特定 style 或 genre 的 pressure 本身就很强。正如 social psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi 所说：

> 通常的看法是，如果 creativity 是 rare 的，那是因为 supply-side limitations；换言之，是因为 geniuses 很少。实际情况似乎是，creativity 的 limits 位于 demand-side。如果 creativity 太少，那是因为无论 individually 还是 collectively，我们都无法足够迅速地 change 自己的 cognitive structures，从而 recognize 并 adopt new ideas。[3](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-3-18550 "Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly. <em>The Systems Model of Creativity: The Collected Works of Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi</em>. Germany: Springer Netherlands, 2015.")

当 Creative ideas 处于 originality 的 Goldilocks zone 时，它们就能 succeed——足够 new，能够 attract interest，但对 artistic taste 的世界而言仍然是 comprehensible 的。

## 将它们结合起来

当然，擅长 creative skills 说起来容易，做起来难。要成为一名 excellent painter、musician、writer 或 woodworker，需要大量的 practice、learning、immersion 和 experimentation。

不过，尽管路途可能漫长，但如果有一张地图，坚持走下去就会容易得多。

## 原文

Creative skills often seem shrouded in mystery. While learning math and languages may be difficult, at least they are somewhat straightforward. Getting good requires a lot of practice, but there are well-worn paths to take.

 In contrast, creative skills have an aura of unlearnability to them. Either you have creative talent, or you don’t.   But this isn’t true. Creative skills are as learnable as any other skill you’d like to master. In this essay, I’d like to show how.

## The Two Components of Creative Skills

Any creative skill you’d like to learn, whether it’s painting, writing, music, design or something else, has essentially two components:

1.  **Technical skills.**
2.  **Ideational skills.**

Technical skills are those aspects of creativity which don’t actually require, well, creativity. Take my [portrait drawing challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/portrait-challenge/) as an example. Although I’m proud of what I was able to accomplish with one month’s work, there was zero creativity involved. The difficulty in this challenge was purely technical.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Before-after-1024x576.png)

Technical skill is the aspect of creative expertise most impressive to non-artists. Being able to play a very difficult guitar solo flawlessly or render a compelling likeness of an image with paint or pencil is hard to do. But, strictly speaking, there isn’t much creativity required.

Ideational skills, in contrast, are more often what we refer to as creativity itself. What we think of as “creativity” is the ability to do something that is both valuable and original. Among practitioners, there’s usually a greater appreciation for these ideational skills, as technical skills often lose their aura of mysticism when you’ve spent enough time practicing them.

Great artists are usually good at both the technical and ideational components of creative skills, although some artists’s talents clearly lie more heavily on one side or the other. John Singer Sargent’s paintings are technically virtuosic—especially when you appreciate that most of his work was done _alla prima_, which means he did each painting largely in one go, rather than meticulously building up layers as was more common during the Renaissance:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/sargeant-portrait.jpg)

In contrast, Vincent van Gogh is the best paradigm of an ideational genius with mediocre technical skills. He couldn’t keep up with the other artists in his Parisian atelier and eventually left. Even when he was in the mature phase of his painting, he frequently repeated the same subject multiple times because he struggled to get the details right:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/vangogh-portrait-917x1024.jpeg)

Still, as much as I’m personally a fan of Sargent’s, it’s clear that Van Gogh has had far more impact as a painter, in no small part due to his original and compelling style.

With this distinction in mind, let’s look at how how to get good at each aspect of creative skills.

## Achieving Technical Proficiency

Technical proficiency is the easier of the two to master. It’s hardly trivial, but technical skills are amenable to hard work and practice in a way that ideational genius often is not.

That being said, many people struggle to acquire technical proficiency in creative skills because they go about it the wrong way. All technical skills break down into three essential parts:

1.  **Methods**.
2.  **Concepts**.
3.  **Practice**.

The first part is that to learn a creative skill you need to start with a method. This can be difficult because many artists have so overlearned their skills that they no longer appear to follow any particular method.

This was an aspect of portrait drawing that initially confounded me. Seeing street artists draw a good likeness in minutes or technically excellent artists render a likeness with a few flicks of their brush suggests that perhaps there isn’t a method at all, that success in art is simply an innate talent or intuition.

But this misunderstands the situation. Across many domains, researchers have found that nearly all expertise follows a similar pattern. Skills start out learned explicitly but, after more and more practice, parts of that explicit learning become automated—they become more like background intuition than a method you can easily articulate.[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18550 "For details, I have an extended discussion on the development of expertise in my book, <em>Get Better at Anything</em>.")

When I started my portrait drawing challenge, for instance, I didn’t turn up very good methods. So I assumed that simply putting in a lot of bulk practice with feedback would be the key to improvement. I was right that high volumes of practice are essential, but I was wrong in assuming that methods were irrelevant.

 The fact is, most portrait artists use a variety of methods to gain accuracy. Some of these are perceptual, using various means to overcome optical illusions or our natural sensory limitations to correctly assess the relative sizes, angles and tonal values in an image. Others are conceptual, building up from knowledge of lighting, geometry and anatomy to infer shapes that aren’t immediately visible.

The starting point for learning any technical skill is first to learn what the methods used are, and then practice them until they become second nature.

The next part of technical proficiency is less about rote practice and more about creating a mental model of the possibilities afforded by the medium and the constraints attached. I love this little clip discussing how Steven Spielberg achieved a scene in the movie _Hook_ where one of the Lost Boys appears to bring down his sword quickly [just a few millimeters from Robin Williams’s head](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/VU7YwZk8Fj8).

In a pre-CGI world, Spielberg achieved this effect by filming the entire scene backwards, including the dialog of the actors, so that what appears to be a near-fatal sword strike is actually the sword lifting up quickly, in reverse.

The final key to technical virtuosity is an enormous amount of practice. Both precision and fluency matter here.

Precision, in the sense of adhering to accuracy afforded by the method you’ve adopted, is necessary because it is how you learn the underlying skill. Thus, whether you’re learning a method for drawing a portrait or playing a guitar solo, your practice will be ineffective if you permit yourself to make a ton of mistakes.

Fluency matters because motor skills, like the strumming of a guitar or the flicking of a paintbrush, will be completely different if they’re executed with different rhythms or movements.   I used to struggle with the tension between precision and fluency. In visual arts, there’s often a contrast between “tighter” more controlled styles, and “looser” more expressive ones. Sometimes it feels like I need to slow down and get more precise to improve. Other times it feels like I’d do better to just loosen up.   But I think this distinction is a bit of an illusion. Looser “expressive” movements are simply a different kind of motor skill than tightly controlled ones. Both of these movements need practice to master, but that doesn’t negate the need for broader technical proficiency. Like many skills in life, the solution is to practice both the movements and the methods that support them.

## How to Have Better Ideas

The other component of creative skills is having good, original ideas. Even this isn’t entirely removed from technical proficiency. If you want to understand the opportunities for expressing good, original ideas within a medium, you first have to understand what the medium affords and what it constrains. Creativity, then, is at least somewhat reliant on proficiency.

But it’s a mistake to simply collapse technical virtuosity down to creativity, because creativity relies on more than just being really good at the technical aspects of the craft.

Having really good, original ideas is a mixture of two different components:

1.  **Knowledge.**
2.  **Judgement.**

Knowledge is the most important part. There’s good evidence that creativity itself is a largely random process. What separates more creative artists from less creative ones is less the ability to generate lots of random ideas (all of us have that ability), but that the more creative artists have better quality filters and thus can judge more reliably which ideas are actually good.

Vincent van Gogh is a case in point. Although he struggled technically, he was incredibly attuned to the artistic landscape of his time. His brother was an art dealer, and Van Gogh had a lot of contact with the emerging trends in the art world. So, even though he is often billed as an “outsider” compared to his Salon-trained contemporaries, he had insider knowledge.

Similarly, of my friends who are successful serial authors, I’m more impressed by their ability to quickly ascertain which ideas are likely to be popular and engaging than their writing skill.

Generally speaking, it’s a myth that successful creatives are outsiders to their fields.[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18550 "For a look at some of the data on creative success, I recommend Dean Simonton’s <em>Greatness</em>.") While the most creative works may come from the edges of a field, from those who are less invested in the dominant styles or paradigms, genuinely creative outsider art is practically non-existent.

This suggests that immersion in the social world that surrounds a creative endeavor is essential for truly successful creativity. That if you’re detached from the world that forms the fundamental judgements on what counts as good, original work, you’ll inevitably fail to produce something that qualifies.

The second aspect of creative idea generation is less about having creative ideas, and more about having the disposition to actually create them. Creativity, particularly original work, requires effort and risk-taking. You have to attempt something that may not work and, particularly in the case of many high-art pursuits, may not pay off financially either.

A lot of valuable creativity is suffocated not because the artist lacks original ideas, but because the pressure to conform to a particular style or genre is itself strong. As social psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi put it:

> The common belief is that if creativity is rare, it is because of supply-side limitations; in other words, because there are few geniuses. The truth seems to be that the limits to creativity lie on the demand-side. If there is too little creativity, it is because both individually and collectively we cannot change our cognitive structures rapidly enough to recognize and adopt new ideas.[3](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/07/16/how-to-learn-creative-skills/#easy-footnote-bottom-3-18550 "Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly. <em>The Systems Model of Creativity: The Collected Works of Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi</em>. Germany: Springer Netherlands, 2015.")

Creative ideas succeed when they’re in the Goldilocks zone of originality—new enough that they’ll attract interest, but still comprehensible to the world of artistic taste.

## Putting Them Together

Getting good at creative skills, of course, is easier said than done. To become an excellent painter, musician, writer or woodworker requires a ton of practice, learning, immersion and experimentation.

But even though the road may be long, it is much easier to persist in the endeavor if you have a map.
