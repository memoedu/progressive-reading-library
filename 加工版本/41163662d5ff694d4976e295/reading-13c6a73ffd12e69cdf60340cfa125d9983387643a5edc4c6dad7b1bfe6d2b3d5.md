## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

发布：2022 年 2 月 4 日（持续整理中，想法尚不完整，最近一次更新于 2024 年 1 月 8 日）

下面是我偶尔用来举办 workshops 的一个 template。它远非完美，而且我经常大幅偏离这个 pattern。不过，我希望其中有些想法能让别人觉得有用。欢迎提出经过深思熟虑的改进或 variation 建议。

**人：**如果出色的人来 attend，而你又营造了一种让他们能自在 sharing 的 atmosphere，事情就会成功。理想的 workshop 就是一群人在屋顶下兴奋地交谈。而且屋顶也不是必需的。

**只 invite 那些你非常欣赏、以至于想听他们 give a talk 的人：**我很喜欢给 **所有** attendee list 上的人发邮件，说：“如果你想 speak，我会非常乐意听你讲。不过也请记住：完全没有必须 speak 的要求。”当你这样给所有人发邮件时，就会 creates common knowledge：你认为他们每个人都很了不起，都有极有分量的东西可以 contribute（但没有任何义务）。这既让大家知道你有多欣赏他们每一个人，也让他们知道你有多欣赏其他所有 attendees。他们每个人都理应在那里。这对于 junior people 和那些可能觉得自己是 outsiders 的人尤其重要。按照下面描述的 time structure，这种 invitation-to-speak 会将人数 limits 在大约 20-25 位 attendees。我通常发现，大约 3/4 的人想 speak（不过 variation 相当大）。

**不要、不要、不要仅仅因为某人是 senior，或因为你觉得有某种义务，就 invite 他们：**再问一次：你期待听他们 speak 吗？如果不期待，就不要 invite 他们。我不在乎他们是不是得过 Nobel Prize。这里的 anti-pattern 是：“我们应该 invite 某某，因为他们也是这个圈子里的人。”这种想法可以理解。但这样做，你是在牺牲其他所有人的体验。话虽如此：20-25 人并不多，有时你会伤害到自己喜欢和欣赏的人的感情（一个常见的 pattern 是：你正从四个 communities 中各 invite ~6 人，但碰巧在其中一个 community 里认识 ~30 人）。这很难处理。我知道自己有时也会在这里妥协，但我会尽量坚决地_不_这么做。我知道，有些人觉得自己有资格 attend 某些 events，而我没有 invite 他们，这让他们非常生气。这很遗憾，我当然不会因此感到任何个人的快乐（恰恰相反），但是：我作出那些选择，是为了让 event 办得更好。

**Intertwining（交织）：**有一种我很喜欢的 pattern——尽管实行起来可能令人害怕——就是把那些本该彼此认识、却尚未相识的人和 communities 聚到一起。我把这看作是在 releasing latent potential。理想情况下，这种 latent potential 是巨大的。当你把一个 pre-existing community 聚到一起时，outcome 往往相当 predictable。当你让 communities 或 networks 彼此认识时，_unpredictable 的事情就会发生_。无论是自己思考，还是与其他 organizers 讨论，我都会非常明确地提出并回答这些问题：我们正在把哪些 communities 聚到一起，为什么？这真的令人害怕：workshop 开始前一个小时，我常常心怀恐惧，想着：“可是，如果他们彼此无话可说怎么办？如果他们讨厌彼此怎么办？如果他们觉得无聊怎么办？”但实际发生的事情（谢天谢地）始终是活力与兴奋的迸发。请 Invite 好奇、generative、慷慨的人，那些会彼此 excite、enable、amaze 的人。不要混蛋[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/workshops.html#fn1)；对喜欢苛责别人的人要非常谨慎；要非常留心观察是否有人感到不自在；如果有人正在让别人感到不自在，就迅速采取行动。

**Time structure（时间安排）：**星期五晚上和星期六一整天。我一般会在星期五 6pm 左右开始。留一个小时供大家 mingle，让熟人重新熟络起来，也让大家开始结识新朋友。Opening remarks + 2 场 talks 会让我们进行到 8:30-9:00pm。食物一般是在之前、也可能在之后提供些小吃，而不是一顿正餐（这样大家会站着 mingle，而不是坐下来）。你把合适的一群人聚到一起的标志是：他们不想回家。有几次令人难忘的经历，大部分 attendees 到了 2am 还在聊天，我只好把他们赶回去。第二天不晚于 9am 重新开始。午餐至少留 90 分钟，2 小时更好，另外至少安排两次半小时的 breaks。星期六晚上，我一般让大家自由活动；视当时的气氛，可能会有一个较大的 subgroup 一起去吃晚饭。

**周末：**不过，选工作日还是周末，是个难以决定的问题。两者都有复杂的问题。要知道，无论你选哪一种，都会让某些人难以 attend，并对此保持体谅。总的来说，尽量留意并体谅人们的 needs。你不可能做到完美，但付出这份努力能让情况改善很多。需要考虑的事情包括：不同的 income levels；出行带来的负担，尤其是从遥远地方赶来；家庭责任；childcare；language barriers；酒精；food needs；总体上的 accessibility；宗教方面的责任；年龄；physical needs；还有许多其他因素。

**尽量以两场真正令人大开眼界、出人意料的 talks 开场：**理想情况下，大家应该一下子精神起来，心想：“天哪，我以前从来没见过_那样的东西_。”找到这样的 talks 很难。但这值得你为之下很大功夫。成功时，它会带来极大的兴奋，让人觉得这里与众不同，并创造一个 novel context，让之后发生的一切都从中受益。注意，这里的 anti-pattern 是以那些所有人基本都预料得到的 talks 开场。这非常常见，也确实是一种 anti-pattern，会让所有人进入 business-as-usual mode。你希望人们感到自己置身于 new creative territory，而不是 business-as-usual。

**Talks（演讲）：**很多人会说：“哦，应该少花点时间在 talks 上，多留点时间 mingle。”这 misdiagnoses 了问题。我的偏好是请大家准备 15-20 分钟的 talk，但预期自己会 hold the floor 达 30-40 分钟，全程穿插 questions 和 discussion。当一个人多年来一直竭尽所能地做某件事时，_我希望他们能 have the floor，进行 talk in-depth_。这样你们会形成 deep、rich 的 shared context，随后它就会成为精彩的 in-person conversations 的 foundation。你可以在开头自己先提几个问题，为穿插其中的 questions 作铺垫；尽量为 good questioning 作示范，但不要 dominate。尤其值得提出的是 humble questions，坦露自己的无知，或对 fundamental issues 的 uncertainty。

**不要 swag：**我多少就是厌恶这种浪费和过度。还有：如果 swag 对某位 attendee 很重要，那么他们往往就不是你真正希望来的人。

**优先让 workshops 变得容易举办，随时想办就能办：**Organizers 往往会在无关紧要的小细节上投入大量精力。再说一次，重要的是：_让出色的人来 attend，_ _并营造一种让他们能自在、坦诚地 sharing 自己的 understanding 的 atmosphere。_如果你能做到这一点，其他事情基本都不重要。从某种意义上说，一个 workshop 不过是一间屋子、一封精心撰写的 email invitation，以及一份准备 invite 的出色人选名单。

**认真打磨 invitation email：**你想传达的是，这将是一场特别而难忘的 event；你也希望说清楚，你正在邀请的这个人会 contribute 什么特别的东西，又会从中获得什么特别的东西。我有时会写上十几稿，努力找到恰到好处的表达。有时最后只有一两句话，但它们都是经过精心选择的，力求独特，而且（我希望）令人印象深刻。如果你做不到，那么就值得反思一下，自己为什么想举办这场 workshop。

**Opening and closing words（开场与结束致辞）：**Organizers 常常说起来没完没了。_务必_感谢并特别提及那些促成 event 的人。如果你确实有经过深思熟虑、发自内心而又不同寻常的话，就说出来：人们会非常积极、认真地回应。但不要只是为了让自己觉得重要，或因为觉得自己应该说点什么，就开口讲话。

**Location（地点）：**找一个 immersive 的地方，让大家能享受其中、感到受到欢迎，而且不大需要离开。舒适的家就很好。任何有独特之处的空间都很好：比如有一批精心挑选的藏书，或一些艺术品。平淡无奇的办公室不那么好，不过，如果你能用的只有这样的地方，那就用吧——空间不应成为障碍。你在铁皮棚里也能举办 workshop。

**Variations（变体）：**显然，这只是一个 template。其中一些想法也适用于不同的 structures。只说三个 observations：(1) 如果时长远超 24 hours，就 increase free and unstructured time 的 density；(2) 人数的 changes 会 qualitatively change event 的 nature（transitions 大约出现在 10、25、60、100 和 250 人时）；(3) increasing 时长会 qualitatively changes 一场 event 的 nature（week-long、month-long 和 6 month-long 的 events 有着非常不同的 capabilities）。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

Released: Feb 4 2022 (ongoing, incomplete thoughts, most recent update January 8, 2024)

The following is a template I've used occasionally to run workshops. It's far from perfect, and I often deviate a lot from this pattern. Still, I hope there are some ideas others find useful. Thoughtful suggestions for improvement or variation are welcome.

**People:** If wonderful people attend, and you create an atmosphere where they're comfortable sharing, it'll work. An ideal workshop is a group of people talking excitedly with a roof. And the roof is optional.

**Only invite people you admire so much you want to hear them give a talk:** I love emailing the **entire** attendee list and saying "If you'd like to speak, then I'd love for you to do so. But also: there's absolutely no requirement to speak". When you email everyone like this it creates common knowledge: you think they're all amazing with something huge to contribute (but with no obligation). This tells people both how much you admire them individually, and also how much you admire all the other attendees. They all belong there. This is especially important for junior people and people who may feel like outsiders. With the time structure described below, this invitation-to-speak limits you to about 20-25 attendees. Usually I find about 3/4 of people want to speak (with quite a bit of variation).

**Do not, do not, do not invite people just because they're senior or you feel some obligation:** Again: are you excited to hear them speak? If not, don't invite them. I don't care if they won the Nobel Prize. The anti-pattern here is: "We should invite so-and-so, because they're part of the crowd". It's understandable. But you are compromising everyone else's experience this way. That said: 20-25 people is not a lot, and sometimes you'll hurt the feelings of people you like and admire (a common pattern: you're inviting ~6 people from four communities, but you happen to know ~30 people in one of those communities). This is challenging to deal with. I know I sometimes compromise here, but I try to be pretty hard core about _not_ doing so. I know I've made some people very angry by not inviting them to certain events they felt entitled to attend. That's a pity, and I certainly don't take any personal joy from it (quite the reverse), but: I made those choices to serve the event better.

**Intertwining:** A pattern I love – though it can be scary to carry out – is to bring together people and communities who should know each other but don't yet. I think of it as releasing latent potential. Ideally that latent potential is enormous. When you bring a pre-existing community together, the outcome is often fairly predictable. When you introduce communities or networks to one another, _the unpredictable happens_. I am very explicit about posing and answering these question, both with myself and with other organizers: which communities are we bringing together, and why? This is genuinely scary: an hour before the workshop starts I'm often in a state of fear, thinking "but what if they have nothing to say to one another? what if they hate each other? what if they're bored?" But instead, what (thankfully) has always happened is an outpouring of energy and excitement. Invite curious, generative, generous people, people who will excite and enable and amaze one another. No assholes[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/ongoing/workshops.html#fn1); be very careful with the censorious; be very careful to observe if anyone is uncomfortable; and act swiftly if anyone is making others feel uncomfortable.

**Time structure:** Friday night and all day Saturday. I tend to start around 6pm Friday. An hour to mingle, for people who know each other to get reacquainted, and for people to begin meeting new people. Opening remarks + 2 talks takes us through to 8:30-9:00pm. Food tends to be nibblies before and maybe after, not a full meal (this keeps people standing and mingling, rather than seated). The sign that you've gotten the right group together: they won't want to go home. On some memorable occasions most attendees have still been chatting at 2am, and I've had to throw them out. Re-start no later than 9am. At least 90 minutes for lunch, 2 hours is better, plus at least two half hour breaks. I tend to leave people to themselves on Saturday night; depending on the mood, a large subgroup may go together for dinner.

**Weekends:** That said, weekday or weekend is a hard call. There are complicated issues with both. Know and be sensitive to the fact that whichever you choose will make it difficult for some people to attend. In general, try to be mindful of and sensitive to people's needs. You won't be perfect, but the effort can greatly improve things. Things to think about: differing income levels; the impositions of travel, especially from distant lands; family commitments; childcare; language barriers; alcohol; food needs; accessibility in general; religious commitments; age; physical needs; and many more.

**Try to begin with two really eye-opening talks, out of left field:** Ideally, people should sit up and think "my goodness, I'd never seen anything like _that_ before". It's tough to source these. But it's worth working very hard to do so. When it succeeds it creates immense excitement, a sense that this is different, and a novel context which benefits everything else that happens. Note that the anti-pattern here is to start with the talks everyone is pretty much expecting. This is very common, and a genuine anti-pattern, putting everyone into a business-as-usual mode. You want people to feel that they are in new creative territory, not business-as-usual.

**Talks:** Many people will say "Oh, there should be less time on talks, more time to mingle". This misdiagnoses the problem. My preference is to ask people to prepare 15-20 minutes of talk, but expect to hold the floor for 30-40 minutes, with questions and discussion intermingled the whole way. When someone has spent years doing something to the best of their ability, _I want them to have the floor to talk in-depth_. You develop a deep, rich shared context that is then the foundation for wonderful in-person conversations. You can seed the intermingled questions by asking a few yourself early on; try to model good questioning, but not dominate. It's especially good to ask humble questions, revealing ignorance, or uncertainty about fundamental issues.

**No swag:** Part of me is just repulsed by the waste and excess. Also: if swag is important to an attendee, they're often not a person you really want there.

**Prioritize making it easy to run workshops at the drop of a hat:** Organizers often put a huge amount of effort into irrelevant minor details. Again, what matters is: _getting wonderful people to attend,_ _and creating an atmosphere where they feel comfortable openly sharing their understanding._ If you can do this, nothing much else will matter. In some sense, a workshop is just a room, a well-crafted email invitation, and a list of great people to invite.

**Work hard on the invitation email:** What you want is a sense that this will be a special and memorable event; you also want to make clear what special thing the person you're inviting will contribute, and what special thing they will get out of it. I will sometimes go through a dozen drafts, trying to find just the right thing. Sometimes the result will just be one or two sentences, but they're carefully chosen to be unique and (I hope) striking. If you can't do this, then it's worth reflecting on why you want to run the workshop.

**Opening and closing words:** Organizers often drone on. _Do_ thank and single out the people who made the event happen. And if you have something genuinely thoughtful and heart-felt and unusual to say, say it: people will be very responsive and thoughtful in response. But don't just speak to feel important, or because you feel you should.

**Location:** Find somewhere immersive, where people will enjoy themselves, feel welcomed, and little need to leave. A comfy home is good. Any space with special qualities is good: a thoughtful collection of books, or artwork. Unremarkable offices are not so good, though if that's all you have available, go for it – space shouldn't be a barrier. You can run a workshop in a tin shed.

**Variations:** Obviously, this is just one template. Some of the ideas carry over to different structures. Just three observations: (1) if you go much beyond 24 hours, increase the density of free and unstructured time; (2) changes in the number of people qualitatively change the nature of the event (with transitions around 10, 25, 60, 100, and 250); (3) increasing the length qualitatively changes the nature of an event (week-long, month-long, and 6 month-long events have very different capabilities).
