# Beyond lifespan as a metric in aging research: why reprogramming is promising

---

## 正文

> 这个 study 很酷，但没有 lifespan effects 啊，兄弟
> 
> — 所有人，包括直到最近还这样想的我

aging 领域历来关注两个目标：更长的 lives 和更健康的 lives。总体趋势是，extend lifespan 的 interventions 往往也会 improve health，而反过来则不成立。我怀疑，有些人甚至会把某种 model organism 中的 lifespan extension 视为将某种东西称为有前景的 aging drug 的 necessary conditions 之一。这个 heuristic 有其局限。

我写 [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity) 时，reviewed 了若干 interventions。在这个过程中，我考察的是对 health 的各种 effects，以及 lifespan extension。如果发现某种东西并不 extend lifespan，我的兴趣就会减弱，仿佛它是次一等的 intervention。毕竟，_longevity_ 意味着 long life，对吧？能将 lifespan extends 20% 的东西，听起来比能使 health 出现各种 improvements 却不能使 lifespan increase 的东西更令人兴奋。而且，improve lifespan 的 interventions 反正也往往会 improve healthspan，那么，我们难道不应该把 lifespan 当作一个 heuristic，用来判断什么 works、什么不 works，作为让我们对某种 intervention 产生兴趣的“更高门槛”吗？某种程度上是这样，但我在这里要论证的是，短期内过分关注这一点，可能会严重限制长期能够取得的成果。我们想要的是出于 _正确的原因_ 而 extend lifespan（以及 healthspan）的 interventions，也就是通过 reversing 或 slowing aging 来做到这一点，而且其方式能够与其他 interventions 一起 scale 和 compound。

我在别处写过[我认为 aging 是什么](https://nintil.com/what-is-aging/)，而 aging 的本质强烈暗示了这样一个事实：没有任何一种 drug 能够 comprehensively target aging；不同的 drugs 只会 impact aging mechanisms 的某个 subset。要获得 lifespan increases，也可以只 target 其中一个很小的 subset，小到这种 intervention 不配被称为 aging drug。

来看看 mice。Mice 不是小号的 humans（此处需要 citation），但它们呈现出 humans 也具有的所有 hallmarks of aging 和各种 damage accumulation，而 C. elegans 或 Drosophila 则并非如此，因此它们是 study aging 大多数方面的不错的 model，尽管距离 replicating _human_ aging 还很远。在一个规模足够大的 cohort 中，大多数（50-90%）mice 会死于各种 cancer，通常是 lymphomas（Seluanov et al. [2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41568-018-0004-9.pdf?origin=ppub#:%7E:text=For%20example%2C%2050%25%20to%2090,to%20be%20extremely%20cancer%20resistant.)）。

这个 observation implies，mice 中的 lifespan extension _requires_ 一种能直接或间接 addresses cancer 的 intervention。

接受某种 well targeted 且 effective 的 cancer treatment 的 Mice 会活得更久，但除此之外应该仍然 age normally；事实上，正如预期的那样，用 chemotherapy 或 immunotherapy treating cancer 会 prolongs mice 的 lifespan（Bekesi et al., [1976](https://cancerres.aacrjournals.org/content/canres/36/2_Part_2/631.full.pdf); Straus et al. [1973](https://cancerres.aacrjournals.org/content/canres/33/7/1724.full.pdf), Hotz et al. [2021](https://www.science.org/stoken/author-tokens/ST-18/full)）。我尚未找到在 lifespan 之外还 tests 各种 functional measures 的研究，但我的预期是，尤其对于后面那篇 BioNTech paper，这些 therapies 几乎完全是通过 delayed oncogenesis 来 extending lifespan，应该不会表现出任何 aging 的 reversal 或 delay。

Cancer drugs 是 lifespan-extending drugs，却不是 aging drugs，不过有一点例外：slow down cancer 的一种方式，是 slow down cell growth 或 downregulate protein synthesis。不出所料，rapamycin，以及更广泛意义上的 mTOR downregulation（关键的 ITP hits 似乎都在这样做；而且一些 senolytics，例如 dasatinib，本身就是 chemotherapies），会 slow down cancer progression（Hidalgo, [2000](https://www.nature.com/articles/1204091); Law [2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1040842805000855?casa_token=L35HtM2ZoNEAAAAA:R0u6aAV6Ay1RammEQH7T2t_QDj6tCeTJVRClvrlf7L7JuzilF8tHIroDTxLQdDLq4oE1lTm8zoo)），因此，至少部分属于 mTOR-dependent 的这一大类 interventions，其 effects 可能仅仅是 anti-cancer drugs 的 effects。需要说清楚：mTOR downregulation 确实具有 cancer-independent effects，而且这些 effects 在不同 model organisms 中也非常 robust；rapamycin 在 C. elegans 中同样 works，而其 causes of death 中并没有 cancer。相对明确的是，如果这些 interventions 确实在 extend lifespan，那么它们几乎肯定是通过 slowed tumor progression 或 occurrence 来做到的。也许还存在一些 immune 或 inflammation effects，而且有研究认为，cancer incidence 的 age-related increase 完全或主要是由 immune decline 引起的，但[我认为情况很可能并非如此](https://nintil.com/immunosenescence)：DNA mutations 很重要，而且没有任何已知的 anti-aging intervention 能 reverses 它们（不过，upregulating DNA repair 的 Lifelong treatments 应该会有帮助）。事实上，有些 interventions 可能会让它们变得更糟！DNA mutations 会随 age 而 accumulate，而对这些 cells 进行 partially reprogramming，或许会使它们越过临界点，变成 cancer，正如 Tamir Chandra 在[这里](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41587-022-00002-4)指出的那样。

mTOR downregulation-dependent interventions 通常会同时 increase lifespan 和 health span，这一点我们已经知道。对于其他 modalities，情况则不一定明确。这些使用 senolytics 的 studies（Xu et al., [2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC6082705/) 或 Qixia et al. [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-021-00491-8)）显示，treadmill endurance 或 grip strength 等各种 functional parameters 出现了 improvements，但其中存在 dasatinib（一种 chemotherapeutic agent）或 PCC1（一种 paper 指出可能具有 anticancer effects 的 compound），意味着这些 lifespan effects 可能来自 directly targetting cancer。如果我们考察 senescent cells 的 ablation 的 transgenic models，例如 Baker et al. [2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC3468323/) 或 Wang [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-021-00107-6)，这两篇 papers 并没有展示 lifespan effects（它们没有考察这一点），只有各种 health improvements。这完全没问题！事实上，使用这些完全不涉及任何类似 anti-cancer drugs 的东西的 transgenic models 来 removing senescent cells，确实能够 extend lifespan（Baker [2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16932)）。如果能知道确切的 mechanism，那会很有意思！但结果也完全可能并非如此。那样一来，我们就应该不再把 senolytics 看作一种有意思的 anti-aging therapy，连同其 health benefits 一并抛弃吗？显然不应该：如果它们如预期那样 working，它们就在 removing senescent cells，而这正是 hallmarks of aging 之一。

我写这篇文章，是因为我对 partial reprogramming 持乐观态度。截至今天，尚未有研究表明 partial reprogramming（或者按 Altos 那帮潮人的叫法[1](https://nintil.com/lifespan-aging/#sidenote-1)，[rejuvenation programming](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1484228104465764353?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E1484228104465764353%7Ctwgr%5E%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fpublish.twitter.com%2F)）能在 wildtype mice 中 extend lifespan；目前最接近的是 [Ocampo 2016](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674\(16\)31664-6?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS0092867416316646%3Fshowall%3Dtrue)，它在 progeroid mice 中实现了 extending lifespan。对这篇出色的 paper，一个常见的 critique 是：“那并不是 wildtype mice，你所做的不过是 curing progeria”；而且，progeria mice 确实是死于 progeria，而不是 cancer，因此那篇 paper 中 reprogramming 对 lifespan 的 effects 来自 ameliorating progeria。好吧！但其 healthspan effects 必然来自 reversing progeria 所 causes 的部分 damage，而且 progeria 确实会 recapitulate natural aging 的某些方面（Ashapkin et al., [2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fgene.2019.00455)）。现在，通过一些较近的研究，我们知道 reprogramming 在普通 mice 中也能对 health 起作用（Wang [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23353-z); Rodriguez-Matellan [2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213671120303854); Chondronasiou [2022](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.20.477063v1.full.pdf)）。但它也许对 cancer 毫无作用。也许它会让 cancer 更糟！但这些 health effects 依然是真实的！

另一个让人对 Rejuvenation Programming™ 没那么兴奋的原因是，在类似的 progeroid mice 中，rapamycin 能带来与 Ocampo paper 中 observed 的那些 effects 类似的 effects（Liao et al., [2016](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247\(16\)31451-6?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS2211124716314516%3Fshowall%3Dtrue); Ramos et al. [2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC3613228/)）。此外，虽然这方面的研究较少，rapamycin 也能够 _reverse_ aging 的某些方面，而不仅仅是 slow down 它们（Flynn et al., [2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.12109); Dai et al. [2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.12203)）。在这种情况下，Lifespan extension 和 systemic improvement 并非 reprogramming 所独有。

还有一个反对的理由是，有 reports 表明，使用完全无关的 therapies，能够获得比迄今 reprogramming 所见的任何 effects 都更大的 effects：正如 Ocampo lab 的 postdoc Kevin Perez [points out](https://twitter.com/kgiperez/status/1485264717174251524) 的那样，在 wildtype mice 中，overexpressing VEGF 能使 lifespan extends 40%，并且按多项 metrics 衡量能 improves health。那为什么还对 reprogramming 如此感兴趣呢！

当我们 assessing 一种 intervention 在 humans 中会有多大前景时，我们是在猜测。不存在一种 robust model，能够 takes in animal data，然后给出它在 humans 中将如何 work 的 prediction。围绕 IGF-1/GHR axis 的各种 manipulations 已经能够在 mice 中 extend lifespan，Laura Deming 的 FAQ 在[这里](https://www.ldeming.com/longevityfaq#insulin-igf)对此有记载。然而，在 humans 中，Lanier syndrome [并不会 extend lifespan](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC6416709/)（尽管它似乎有一些 health benefits）。也就是说，“如果它在 mice/worms/flies/yeast 中 extends lifespan”，那么它也会在 humans 中这样做，这只是一个主要基于 theoretical considerations 的 heuristic，因为迄今我们还没有公认的 hits，只有有依据的猜测。反过来也一样：PCSK9 inhibition 能在 humans 中小幅 extends lifespan（诚然，其方式非常狭窄），但在以合理 diet 喂养的 wildtype mice 中，我们可以预期的 effects 可能会非常微弱，因为 mice 根本不会死于 atherosclerosis。

在 assessing 一篇 aging paper 是否有趣或 promising 时，通常会使用一组 heuristics，其依据是一些以往经验和大量 theory：

1.  Mice 比 worms 或 flies 更 similar to humans，因此来自 mice 的 data 应当更 reliable
2.  来自 outbred mice 的 Data 应当更 robust，因为它避免了可能 specific to 某一个特定 genotype 的 effects
3.  在 model X 中的 Lifespan extension 应当 lead to 在 model Y 中的 lifespan extension
4.  一个 showing 更多 systemic effects 的 intervention，更有可能在人类中产生一些 effects，或 translate 到另一个 model
5.  如果 mechanism 是 known（如果它是出于正确的原因而 works，而不是不管出于什么原因而 working），那么这个 effect 就更有可能是 real
6.  与 slowing down aging 相比，Reversal of aging 更容易 measure，更令人印象深刻，也更难 attain。
7.  如果这个 effect 是在 wild type mice 中 observed 的（而不是在 disease model 中），那么这个 effect 就更有可能是 robust

这些 heuristics 往往用于通常的 longevity interventions：small molecules，或在该 species 中 overexpressing 已有的 factors。但随着这个领域向前发展，我们将开始做更有趣的事情：

8.  如果一个 species 在某种程度上 ages slower，而且有一些关于其 mechanism 的 data，那么在例如 mice 中 expressing 相关的 genes，就可能 lead to 该 mechanism 也在 mice 中 working。

最后，还有一个值得考虑的 heuristic，也是与 reprogramming 最相关的一个：

9.  如果一个 intervention 使一个 old cell 变得 identical to 一个 young cell，那么这个 intervention 就非常 promising。

organism 的 Aging 不只是构成它的 cells 的 aggregate 的 aging，但如果能在 single cell 中解决这个问题，那么这就在一定程度上有助于从整体上解决这个问题。

Reprogramming 是迄今唯一能够做到这一点的 intervention。任何 dose 的 rapamycin、VEGF 或其他东西都做不到！Reprogramming 可以取 young cells、old cells（Lapasset et al. [2011](http://genesdev.cshlp.org/content/25/21/2248.short)）或 senescent cells（参见[这里](https://nintil.com/reversible-senescence/)），将它们变成 iPSCs，然后再变回 cell of origin，而据我们迄今的观察，这样得到的 cell 看起来是 young。除了这些 data，也有研究探讨这可能是如何 working 的：如果接受[这一想法，即一个 significant contributor](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3951490) 于 mammalian aging 的因素是 epigenome 中 entropy 的 increase，而且这需要被 undone，那么我们就不能指望一次一个 methylation mark 地手动 undo 这种 damage；我们需要一个 system，将 methylation reset 回 cell 还是 young 时的状态，而这个 system 很可能会 derived from 当前关于 OSKM 的研究。这不同于 CRISPR-like approaches，后者使用 guide RNAs 来 precisely edit 少量 sites。随 age 而 changes 的 methylation sites 数量很大，逐个 site 地 edit 整个 epigenome 并不现实：相反，通过研究 OSKM 如何 works，我们很可能能够安全地获得 full reprogramming 的全部 benefits。在短短几年里，我们已经从将 OSKM 作为 induce tumors 的有效方式（Abad et al., [2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12586)），走到了上文链接的 papers 中 inducing tumor-free 的 health improvements。再过几年，我们将发现 novel factors 和更 safer 的 reprogram 方式。

简而言之，OSKM 比其他 modalities 更 promising，因为对于 de-differentiation 和 re-differentiation protocol 中的 near-complete reversal of aging，我们已有一个 working demonstration，也有一些关于它为何可能 work 的 reasoning，而任何其他 modality 都没有这个已经 _proven_ 的 upper bound。同样重要的是，要明确 reprogramming _不_会做什么：DNA mutations 会 remain，ECM crosslinks 或 reprogramming 可能无法 get rid of 的各种 intracellular aggregates 也会如此。Evolution 必须 evolve 出一种方式，来 get rid of 随 age 而 accumulates 的 damage，从而让婴儿出生时是 young；但如果正在 growing 一个 embryo，那么能够被 diluted away 的 damage 就不需要 fix。不幸的是，对于我们成年人来说，我们无法同样轻易地通过 dilution 来 fix damage。

[Altos Labs、NewLimit、Turn 或 Retro](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1483839960662740994) 正在下注，我猜它们依据的 reasoning 与我这里的类似。这种 reasoning 是 heuristic，而所有这些投入 reprogramming 的资金，最终可能只换来一个非常昂贵的 proof：reprogramming 终究无法与 oncogenesis decoupled，因此我们需要放弃它……但也许它们会成功。即使它们失败，如果在这个过程中，我们没有学到关于 aging 的新东西，并利用这些认识继续取得进展，我也会非常惊讶。令人兴奋的时代就在前方！

## 原文

> Cool study, but no lifespan effects bro
> 
> — Everyone, including myself until recently

The aging field has historically focused on the twin aims of longer and healthier lives. As a trend, interventions that extend lifespan also tend to improve health whereas the opposite is not true. I suspect that some would even consider lifespan extension in some model organism as one of the necessary conditions to call something a promising aging drug. This heuristic has limits.

When I wrote the [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity) I reviewed a number of interventions. When doing so, I was looking at various effects on health, and lifespan extension. If I found something that did not extend lifespan, I would be less interested, it would be like a second class intervention. Ultimately _longevity_ implies long life right? Something that extends lifespan by 20% sounds more exciting that something that causes various improvements in health but no increase in lifespan. And also interventions that improve lifespan also tend to improve healthspan anyway, so shouldn't we use lifespan as a heuristic to know what works and what doesn't, a "higher bar" for us to be interested in an intervention? Kind of, but I'll argue here that thinking too much about that in the short term can severely limit what can be accomplished in the longer term. What we want are interventions that extend lifespan (and healthspan) for _the right reasons_, that is by reversing or slowing aging, and doing so in ways that scale and compound with other interventions.

I have written elsewhere about [what I consider aging to be](https://nintil.com/what-is-aging/), and what aging is heavily hints at the fact that no one drug will comprehensively target aging; instead different drugs will impact a subset of aging mechanisms. To get lifespan increases, one can also target a narrow subset of those, so narrow that said intervention wouldn't deserve to be called an aging drug.

Consider mice. Mice are not little humans (citation needed), but they present all the hallmarks of aging and kinds of damage accumulation that are also present in humans, which is not true for C. elegans or Drosophila, so they make for a decent model to study most aspects of aging, even if they are far from replicating _human_ aging. Most (50-90%) mice in a reasonably large cohort will die of various kinds of cancer, typically lymphomas (Seluanov et al. [2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41568-018-0004-9.pdf?origin=ppub#:%7E:text=For%20example%2C%2050%25%20to%2090,to%20be%20extremely%20cancer%20resistant.)).

This observation implies that lifespan extension in mice _requires_ an intervention that addresses cancer directly or indirectly.

Mice on some form of well targeted and effective treatment for cancer will live longer, but otherwise should age normally, indeed treating cancer with chemotherapy or immunotherapy as expected prolongs lifespan in mice (Bekesi et al., [1976](https://cancerres.aacrjournals.org/content/canres/36/2_Part_2/631.full.pdf); Straus et al. [1973](https://cancerres.aacrjournals.org/content/canres/33/7/1724.full.pdf), Hotz et al. [2021](https://www.science.org/stoken/author-tokens/ST-18/full)). I have not been able to find work that tests various functional measures besides lifespan, but my expectation is that, especially for the latter BioNTech paper, these therapies are extending lifespan almost exclusively via delayed oncogenesis, and should not show any reversal or delay of aging.

Cancer drugs are lifespan-extending drugs, but not aging drugs, except for the fact that one way to slow down cancer is to slow down cell growth or downregulate protein synthesis: unsurprisingly, rapamycin and broadly mTOR downregulation (As the key ITP hits all seem to do; and some senolytics like dasatinib are chemotherapies) slow down cancer progression (Hidalgo, [2000](https://www.nature.com/articles/1204091); Law [2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1040842805000855?casa_token=L35HtM2ZoNEAAAAA:R0u6aAV6Ay1RammEQH7T2t_QDj6tCeTJVRClvrlf7L7JuzilF8tHIroDTxLQdDLq4oE1lTm8zoo)), so the effects of the broad class of interventions that are at least in part mTOR-dependent may just be anti-cancer drugs. To be sure: mTOR downregulation does have cancer-independent effects that are also very robust across model organisms, rapamycin also works in C. elegans which doesn't have cancer among its causes of death. What seems relatively clear is if these interventions are working to extend lifespan, they almost surely must be doing so via slowed tumor progression or occurrence. Perhaps there are some immune or inflammation effects as well, and there is some work suggesting that the age-related increase in cancer incidence is exclusively or mostly due to immune decline, but [I think this is probably not the case](https://nintil.com/immunosenescence): DNA mutations matter and no known anti-aging intervention reverses these (Lifelong treatments upregulating DNA repair should help though). In fact, some interventions could make them worse! DNA mutations accumulate with age and perhaps partially reprogramming these cells tips them over into cancer, as noted by Tamir Chandra [here](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41587-022-00002-4).

mTOR downregulation-dependent interventions will generally increase both lifespan and health span, that we already know. For other modalities this is not necessarily clear. These studies using senolytics (Xu et al., [2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC6082705/) or Qixia et al. [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-021-00491-8)) showed improvements in various functional parameters like treadmill endurance or grip strength, but the presence of dasatinib (a chemotherapeutic agent) or PCC1 (a compound the paper notes may have anticancer effects) means that the lifespan effects could have come from directly targetting cancer. If we look at transgenic models of ablation of senescent cells like Baker et al. [2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC3468323/) or Wang [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-021-00107-6) these two papers don't show lifespan effects (They didn't look at that), only various health improvements. And that's fine! As it happens, removing senescent cells using these transgenic models that don't involve anything remotely looking like anti-cancer drugs can extend lifespan (Baker [2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16932)). It would be interesting to know the exact mechanism! But it could have turned out not to be the case. Should we then discount senolytics as an interesting anti-aging therapy, throwing away the health benefits? Obviously not: If working as intended they are removing senescent cells, one of the hallmarks of aging.

The reason I am writing this essay is that I'm optimistic about partial reprogramming. As of today, partial reprogramming (Or as the cool kids at Altos call it[1](https://nintil.com/lifespan-aging/#sidenote-1) , [rejuvenation programming](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1484228104465764353?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E1484228104465764353%7Ctwgr%5E%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fpublish.twitter.com%2F)) has not been shown to extend lifespan in wildtype mice, the closest we have is [Ocampo 2016](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674\(16\)31664-6?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS0092867416316646%3Fshowall%3Dtrue), extending lifespan in progeroid mice. A common critique of this great paper is that "it was not wildtype mice, all you're doing is curing progeria"; and indeed the progeria mice die of progeria not cancer, so the reprogramming effects on lifespan in that paper are coming from ameliorating progeria. Ok! But the healthspan effects must be coming from reversing some of the damage that progeria causes, and progeria does recapitulate some aspects of natural aging (Ashapkin et al., [2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fgene.2019.00455)). Now we know that reprogramming also works for health in regular mice, from some more recent work (Wang [2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23353-z); Rodriguez-Matellan [2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213671120303854); Chondronasiou [2022](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.20.477063v1.full.pdf)). But maybe it does nothing for cancer. Maybe it makes cancer worse! But the health effects are still real!

Another reason to be less excited about Rejuvenation Programming™ is that rapamycin gets you similar effects to those observed in the Ocampo paper in similar progeroid mice (Liao et al., [2016](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247\(16\)31451-6?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS2211124716314516%3Fshowall%3Dtrue); Ramos et al. [2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC3613228/)). Moreover, although more rarely studied, rapamycin is also able to _reverse_, not merely slow down some aspects of aging (Flynn et al., [2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.12109); Dai et al. [2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.12203)). Lifespan extension and systemic improvement is not something unique to reprogramming in this case.

And yet another reason against is that there are reports of using completely unrelated therapies that obtain effects larger than anything seen so far with reprogramming: As Kevin Perez, a postdoc at the Ocampo lab [points out](https://twitter.com/kgiperez/status/1485264717174251524), overexpressing VEGF extends lifespan by 40% and improves health as measured by a number of metrics, in wildtype mice. Why so much interest in reprogramming then!

When assessing how promising an intervention will be in humans we are making guesses. There is no robust model that takes in animal data and spits out a prediction for how it'll work in humans. Various manipulations around the IGF-1/GHR axis have been able to extend lifespan in mice as documented by Laura Deming's FAQ [here](https://www.ldeming.com/longevityfaq#insulin-igf). However in humans, Lanier syndrome [does not extend lifespan](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/pmc/articles/PMC6416709/) (Though it seems to have some health benefits). That is, "if it extends lifespan in mice/worms/flies/yeast" it will do so in humans is a heuristic based on largely theoretical considerations, because so far we have no agreed-on hits, just informed guesses. The reverse is also true: PCSK9 inhibition extends lifespan in humans on the margin (in a very narrow way admittedly) but the effects we can expect in wildtype mice fed a reasonable diet will probably be very weak sauce, because mice just don't die of atherosclerosis.

When assessing whether an aging paper is interesting or promising there is a set of heuristics that is commonly used, based on some prior experience and heaps of theory:

1.  Mice are more similar to humans than worms or flies, so data from mice should be more reliable
2.  Data from outbred mice should be more robust because it avoids potential effects specific to one specific genotype
3.  Lifespan extension in model X should lead to lifespan extension in model Y
4.  An intervention showing more systemic effects is more likely to have some effects in humans, or to translate to another model
5.  If the mechanism is known (if it works for the right reasons, rather than working for whatever reason) then the effect is more likely to be real
6.  Reversal of aging is easier to measure, more impressive than, and harder to attain than, slowing down aging.
7.  If the effect was observed in wild type mice (as opposed to a disease model) then the effect is more likely to be robust

These heuristics tend to be used in the context of the usual longevity interventions: small molecules, or overexpressing existing factors in that species. But as the field moves forward we'll start to do more interesting things:

8.  If a species ages slower in some way, and there is some data on the mechanism, expressing the relevant genes in e.g. mice could lead to the mechanism also working in mice.

Lastly, here is one more heuristic to consider, and the most relevant for reprogramming:

9.  If an intervention makes an old cell identical to a young cell, said intervention is very promising.

Aging of the organism is not just aging of the aggregate of cells that compose it, but if one solves the problem in a single cell, then that goes some way towards solving the problem in general.

Reprogramming is the only intervention that has so far being able to do this. No dose of rapamycin or VEGF or anything else! Reprogramming can take young cells, old cells (Lapasset et al. [2011](http://genesdev.cshlp.org/content/25/21/2248.short)), or senescent cells (See [here](https://nintil.com/reversible-senescence/)), and make them into iPSCs, then back into the cell of origin, and this resulting cell as far as we have looked looks young. In addition to this data, there is also work on how this might be working: if one buys [the idea that a significant contributor](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3951490) to mammalian aging is an increase in entropy in the epigenome, and that this is to be undone, we can't hope to manually undo this damage one methylation mark at a time, we need a system that will reset methylation back to what it was when the cell was young, and that system will very likely be derived from current work in OSKM. It's a departure from CRISPR-like approaches where guide RNAs are used to precisely edit a small number of sites. The number of methylation sites that changes with age is large, and it is impractical to edit the entire epigenome site by site: Instead by studying how OSKM works we will likely be able to reap the full benefits of full reprogramming but safely. In the span of a few years we moved from OSKM being a great way to induce tumors (Abad et al., [2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12586)) to inducing tumor-free improvements in health in the papers linked above. In a few more years we will discover novel factors and safer ways to reprogram.

In short, OSKM is more promising than other modalities because we have a working demonstration, and some reasoning as to why it may work, of near-complete reversal of aging in the de-differentiation and re-differentiation protocol, and this _proven_ upper bound is just not there for any other modality. It's important too to pinpoint what reprogramming does _not_ do: DNA mutations would remain, as would ECM crosslinks or various intracellular aggregates that reprogramming might not get rid of. Evolution had to evolve a way to get rid of damage that accumulates with age so that babies are born young, but damage that can be diluted away doesn't need a fix if you're growing an embryo. Unfortunately for us adults, we can't as easily fix damage by dilution.

[Altos Labs, NewLimit, Turn, or Retro](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1483839960662740994) are making a bet on I assume reasoning similar to what I have here. This reasoning is heuristic and the end result of all this money being poured into reprogramming may end up being just a very expensive proof that after all reprogramming can't be decoupled from oncogenesis and thus we need to abandon it.... but maybe they succeed. And even if they fail, I would be very surprised if in the process of getting there we don't learn new things about aging that we can then use to continue to make progress. Exciting times ahead!
