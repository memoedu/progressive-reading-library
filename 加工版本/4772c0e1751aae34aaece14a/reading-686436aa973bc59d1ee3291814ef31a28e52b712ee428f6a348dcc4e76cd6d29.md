## 正文

我最近偶然看到了 Nick Bostrom 的一个想法：让自己最坚定的 beliefs 经受一次 [hypothetical apostasy](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2009/02/write-your-hypothetical-apostasy.html)，在其中尽力集结自己能够提出的、反对这些 beliefs 的最强 arguments。你或许已经看出来了，我坚定地信奉 [effective altruism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism)——也就是运用 evidence 和 reason，寻找 improve the world 的最佳方式。因此，我觉得针对 effective altruism movement 写一篇 hypothetical apostasy 会有所收获。

## 如何阅读本文

希望这一点已经很清楚了，不过还是先声明一下：本文的语气相当尖锐。这是为了尝试“进入角色”。_这并不代表我目前对于 effective altruism movement 的 mental state。_ 我在不同程度上赞同自己在这里提出的某些 critiques，但我并不打算放弃 effective altruism，也不打算停止与 EA movement 合作。这次 apostasy 纯粹是 hypothetical。

另外，考虑到 hypothetical apostasy 的性质，我猜，对于 effective altruist 读者而言，本文的尖锐语气可能尤其容易 trigger defensive rationalization。请在通读时记住这一点。（例如，要 counteract 这种 effect，一个好办法或许是想象自己不是 effective altruist，但你的朋友是，而且正在通读本文的是他们：他们应该如何 update 自己的 beliefs？）

如果你想留言，我已经[将本文同步发布到 Less Wrong](http://lesswrong.com/r/discussion/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/)，那里的讨论质量很高，而且 comment system 比我自己拼凑出来的那个更好。如果你有 Less Wrong account，请到那边留言！

最后，如果你此前从未听说过 effective altruism，我不建议让本文成为你对它的第一印象！你会得到非常偏颇的认识，因为我没有费心提及 EA movement 所有出色的地方。

## Abstract（摘要）

据我所知，Effective altruism 是第一次有一套实质上有用的 ethics 以及用于 analyze 个人对世界的 effect 的 frameworks，获得了足够广泛的认同，以至于形似一场 social movement。（我会说，这些 principles 大致是 _altruism_、_maximization_、_egalitarianism_ 和 _consequentialism_；它们共同 imply，相比尝试在世界上 do good 的 social default，有许多 improvements——选择 earning to give，而不是 doing direct charity work；在 developing world 工作，而不是在本地；运用 evidence 和 feedback 来 analyze effectiveness，等等。）遗憾的是，作为一场 movement，effective altruism 未能运用这些 principles，来 acquire 关于如何 improve the world 的 correct nontrivial beliefs。

为作澄清，请考虑我上面所用的“trying”一词的两种含义之间的 distinction。我们姑且称它们为“actually trying”和“pretending to try”。Pretending to try 去 improve the world，大致就是在回应要求自己 improve the world 的 social pressure 时，向自己的大脑 querying 一件能够 improves the world 的事情，取出第一个 search result，然后就照着做。例如，有一段时间，我想通过 developing 用于 checking informally-written proofs 的 computerized methods，来尝试 improve the world，从而让 higher math 的 teaching 更加 scalable，实现 democratizing education，等等。_巧的是_，computer programming 和 higher math 恰好就是我最擅长的两件事。这就是 pretending to try。Actually trying 则是考察那些能够 improve the world 的事情，弄清其中哪一件 maximizes utility，然后去做那件事。例如，我现在在 Harvard 经营一个 effective altruist student organization，因为我意识到，即使我是一名相对糟糕的 leader，而且不太喜欢这件事，只要我足够努力地做，它仍然是非常 high-impact 的。这并不是说我已经在 actually trying，但我已经更接近了。

借助 pretending 与 actually trying 之间的这一 distinction，我会把很多 effective altruism 概括为“pretending to actually try”。作为一个 social group，effective altruists 已经成功注意到了 pretending/actually-trying distinction。但他们似乎就此止步，认定知道 fake trying 与 actually trying 之间的 difference，就意味着具备 actually try 的 ability。从 Empirically 来看，事实绝非如此。许多 effective altruists 最终仍然在 satisficing——寻找那些表面上在 core EA standards 下 acceptable 的 actions，然后从中挑选那些由于其他本质上 random 的 factors 而显得有吸引力的 actions。这比社会默认的做法更有可能 converge on good actions，因为这些 principles 比社会默认的 principles 更好。尽管如此，它未能在 core EA principles 所直接显明的内容之外取得多少进展。因此，虽然“doing effective altruism”给人的感觉像是 truth-seeking，但它最终往往只是一种更可信的 pretend to try 的方式。

下面，我会介绍 effective altruists 未能超越 social-satisficing algorithm 的各种表现；这一 algorithm 就是先确立一些可信地 acceptable 的 alternatives，再根据本质上 random 的 preferences 从中挑选。我还会展示其他一些领域，在这些领域中，effective altruism 的 norms 未能 guard against motivated cognition。这两种 phenomena 都给 effective-altruist consensus 增添了我所谓的“epistemic inertia”：effective altruists 的 beliefs 越来越容易受到 truth-seeking process 之外的 pressures 影响，这意味着 EA consensus 更难根据新的 evidence 或 arguments 进行 update，并阻碍了 movement 向前发展。我主张，这源于 effective altruists 不愿深入思考“成为一场成功的 social movement”这类 issues，而只是思考“correctly applying utilitarianism individually”。通过引入一条额外的 effective altruism principle——例如“group self-awareness”——或许可以解决这个问题，但现在要往 effective altruism 的 DNA 中加入新东西，可能已经太晚了。

## Philosophical difficulties（哲学上的困难）

[目前](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2008/01/protecting-acro.html)存在[广泛的](http://www.amirrorclear.net/academic/ideas/negative-utilitarianism/) [disagreement](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/hedonistic-vs-preference.html)，[其参与者是](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/suffering-nature.html) [effective](http://lesswrong.com/lw/91/average_utilitarianism_must_be_correct/) [altruists](http://rationalaltruist.com/2013/06/03/my-outlook/comment-page-1/#comment-445)，涉及 population ethics 的 correct framework。这对于 determining improve the world 的 best way 至关重要：不同的 population ethics 可能 lead to drastically different choices（或者至少我们会如此 expect _a priori_），而如果 EA movement 甚至无法就其 instrumental goals 达成 converge，它就会迅速 fragment 并失去其 power。然而，在 discovering correct population ethics（或者，从 moral anti-realist standpoint 出发，constructing arguments，使其能够 lead to convergence on 某一种 population ethics）方面，甚至在 determining 哪些 ethics lead to 哪些 interventions being better 方面，都几乎没有进展。

## Poor cause choices（欠佳的事业选择）

许多 effective altruists 向 [GiveWell’s](http://www.givewell.org/) top charities 进行 donate。这三个 charities 全都在 global health 领域开展 work。这是因为 GiveWell 知道 global health 是 highest-leverage cause 吗？不是。这是因为它是唯一拥有足够 data、能够就其说出非常有用的内容的 cause。几乎没有理由认为，这与特别 high-leverage 存在 correlates with 关系——相反，支持 [existential risk prevention](http://intelligence.org/2013/06/05/friendly-ai-research-as-effective-altruism/)、[vegetarian advocacy](http://www.everydayutilitarian.com/essays/how-much-does-it-cost-to-buy-a-vegetarian/) 和 [open borders](http://www.facebook.com/l.php?u=http%3A%2F%2Fopenborders.info%2F&h=aAQGxloz1) 等 causes 的 heuristic 但 less rigorous arguments 表明，这些可能更加 efficient。

此外，我们目前的“best known intervention”很可能在未来发生变化（朝着更 cost-effective 的方向）。这里有两个 competing effects：我们可能 discover 比目前认为 best 的 interventions 更好的 donate 对象，但也可能用尽目前 best known intervention 的 opportunities，不得不转向第二种。到目前为止，我们似乎处于第一种 effect dominates 的 regime 中，而且没有 evidence 表明我们很快就会达到 tipping point，尤其考虑到 effective charity research 这个 field 还如此新。

考虑到这些 considerations，effective altruists 现在就在向 global health causes 进行 donating，相当令人惊讶。即便对于那些希望用 donations 来树立榜样的人，donor-advised fund 也会拥有其中许多 benefits，而没有任何 downsides。况且，当你认为 donating 并不是（除了 example-setting 之外）best possible course of action 时，却为了强调应当 figuring out best possible course of action 然后付诸行动而进行 donating，这似乎本末倒置。

## Non-obviousness（非显而易见性）

Effective altruists 常常对 effective altruism 的 idea 直到最近才出现表示惊讶。例如，我的学生团体最近邀请 Elie Hassenfeld 来做讲座，他在其中发表了类似的看法，我也听过在 EA organizations 工作的其他人表达同样的感受。但似乎没有人真的为此担心——只是因为自己想到了别人都没想到的东西而沾沾自喜。

ideas 的“market”至少在一定程度上是 efficient 的：大多数 simple、obvious 且 correct 的东西，在能够被想到之后，很快就会有人想到。如果像 effective altruism 这样 simple 的 meme 至今尚未扎根，我们至少应当先试着理解原因，再全力支持它。没有这样的尝试——换言之，non-obviousness 并没有让 effective altruists 担心自己遗漏了什么——这一事实是反对“effective altruists 在 actually trying”这一 hypothesis 的 strong indicator。

## Efficient markets for giving（捐赠的有效市场）

人们常说，“nonprofits 不是 doing good 的 market；它们是 warm fuzzies 的 market”。这被用来作为 justification，说明为什么通过 donating 可以 do immense amounts of good。然而，尽管大多数 donors 的确没有明确地试图 purchase utililty，但仍然有大量 money 是以此为目的的。

[Gates Foundation](http://www.gatesfoundation.org/) 就是这样的 organization 的一个 example。他们是 effectiveness-minded 的，而且有 \$60 billion 作为后盾。80,000 Hours 已经 [noted](http://80000hours.org/blog/239-show-me-the-harm)，仅凭 vaccine programs，他们就可能已经 saved over 6 million lives——考虑到他们只 spent 了 endowment 中相对较小的一部分，他们获得的 exchange rate 一定比我们目前 best guesses 中的好得多。

那么，为什么不直接向 Gates Foundation 进行 donate 呢？Effective altruists 需要更好地说明，他们在 exploiting 而 Gates 没有 exploiting 的“market inefficiencies”究竟是什么。既然 [Against Malaria Foundation](https://www.againstmalaria.com/) 这个 GiveWell 的 top charity 属于他们的主要 research areas 之一，Gates Foundation 为什么没有 fund 它？答案只是能力不足或类似原因，这似乎并不可信。

markets 的一条 general rule 是，如果你不知道自己的 edge 是什么，你就是那个冤大头。许多 effective altruists 被问到自己的 edge 是什么时，会给出类似“确实在 being strategic/thinking about utility/caring about results”的回答，然后就不再思考了。这并不是一个 compelling case：正如前面所说，为什么其他人都没有做这些事情，并不清楚。

## Inconsistent attitude towards rigor（对严谨性的不一致态度）

Effective altruists 坚持在 charity recommendations 中采用 extraordinary rigor——例如，参见 GiveWell 的工作。然而，对于许多 ancillary problems——仅举几例，包括现在还是以后进行 donating、choosing a career，以及 deciding 要在多大程度上走向“meta”（在 direct work、earning to give、doing advocacy 和 donating to advocacy 之间）——他们似乎乐于依据 intuition 和 gut feelings，在 not-obviously-wrong alternatives 之间进行 choose。

## 对 psychological 的 understanding 不足

John Sturm 提出，而我也同意，这些 issues 中有许多在性质上是 psychological 的：

> 我认为，这些 problems 中有许多根源于 commitment level 的 issue：
> 
> 比如，我很乐意改变自己对 charity 的 mentality，却不愿改变自己对生孩子的 mentality。我的初步判断是——从 EA 和整体 ethical perspective 来看——生孩子对我而言会是一个重大错误（即使 taking into account 那些通常用来为自己做事辩解的 EA excuses）。不过，至少现在，我就是不在乎自己正在 ignoring 自己的 ethics 和 EA；我想生孩子，就是这样。
> 
> 在这种情况下，与其说我是在“偷懒”，不如说我根本没有 trying。但当有人问起时，我更容易给出某种连我自己都不认为是 true 的 EA excuse（比如生孩子会让我更快乐、更 productive）——这样一来，我看起来就像一个偷懒或粗心的 altruist，而不是根本就不是 altruist。
> 
> 我正在 building 的 model 是这样的：生活中有许多不同的 areas，我都可以在其中 apply EA。有些 areas，我全心全意地愿意；有些 areas，我完全不愿意。于是，在那些看起来像是我在做一个偷懒的 EA 的 areas 中，又有两类：一类是我愿意，而且想成为更好的 EA……另一类是我不愿意，却只是在 pretending（对自己、对别人，或对两者都是如此）。
> 
> 这里的要点是：当我们要求某人成为一个不那么偷懒的 EA 时，我们是在 (1) 帮助他们把自己想做的事情做得更好，以及 (2) 试图让他们要么做得比自己愿意做的更多，要么承认自己是“bad”的。

一般而言，大多数 effective altruists 会以下列方式之一 respond to effective altruism 与其他 goals 之间的 deep conflicts：

1.  Unconsciously 用 motivated reasoning 来 resolve cognitive dissonance：“通过诗歌 spread effective altruism 显然是我的 comparative advantage！”
2.  Deliberately and knowingly 地 use motivated reasoning：“亲爱的 Facebook group，支持成为 EA 诗人的最佳 utilitarian arguments 是什么？”
3.  选择最容易的“honest”出路：“如果 effective altruism 强迫我进入 finance，而不是写诗，我在 psychologically 上就无法 do effective altruism，所以我还是成为一个 effective altruist 诗人吧。”

第三种方式是否 defensible 尚有争议——不过，对于一个自称重视 rationality 和 self-improvement 的 community 而言，effective altruists 对于通过 self-modification 来拥有更多 altruistic intentions，表现出的兴趣少得令人惊讶。这显然值得进一步研究。

此外，EA norms 甚至不 proscribe 前两种方式，从而形成了一种 group norm，使人们在 engaging in 一定程度的 motivated cognition 时也不会注意到。这对 movement 的 converge on the truth 的能力相当有害。（和前面一样，effective altruists 在这方面仍然比 general population 做得更好；核心 EA principles 足够有力，能让人们注意到那些最 obvious、显然违背这些 principles 的 motivated cognition。但这还远远不够。）

## 历史上的 Historical analogues

除了 GiveWell 的 [history of philanthropy project](http://www.givewell.org/history-of-philanthropy) 部分构成例外之外，还没有人研究过好的 historical outside views。尽管 effective altruism 没有 direct precursors（这本身就令人担忧；见上文），但有一个明显相似的 movement：communism，“from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs”这一理念就源于其中。Communism 也以其各种惨重的 failures 而著称。Effective altruists 需要更加担忧自己将如何 avoid 类似 class 的 failures——而且，总的来说，他们需要更加了解，成为一个日益庞大的 social movement 会有哪些 pitfalls，以及哪些 benefits。

Aaron Tucker 的阐述比我能做到的更好：

> 具体而言，Communism/Socialism 是一个由 philosophers 发起、随后由 technocrats 延续的 movement；他们认为 reason 和 planning 可以让世界变得好得多，而且，如果他们 coordinated 起来 take action，解决一切问题，就能够 eliminate poverty、disease 等等。
> 
> 据我所知，Marx 完全理解了“actually trying vs. pretending to try”这一 distinction（“Philosophers 只是 explained 了世界，但真正的 problem 是 change 它”是他的一句话），而且，他非常强烈地抨击那些 unreflectively 地试图解决问题的人：他们采用的方式，只是在自己出发时所处的 culture 中显得合理——problem 并不是 bourgeoisie 没有 trying to help 人们，而是 bourgeoisie 所拥有的唯一 help conception，大体上只是 actually improving proletariat 的生活这一过程的 epiphenomenal 之物——给他们 education 和 voting rights 之类不错的 boureoisie 东西，却不做任何事情来 improve 他们生活的 material condition，也不去解决他们为什么一开始就没有那些东西、为什么不自己创造那些东西的问题。
> 
> 所以，如果 Marx 理解了 pretend/actually try 这一 distinction，而他的 followers 接管了 countries，而且他们还有大量出色的 technocrats，那这看起来简直就是完美的 EA 实践，可它完全没有奏效。

## 单一文化 Monoculture

Effective altruists 并不十分 diverse。绝大多数是 white、“upper-middle-class”、在 intellectually 和 philosophically 方面有所倾向、来自 developed country，等等（而且我认为，其中 male 的比例也显著偏高，尽管我对此不那么确定）。尽管支持 diversity 的 multiple-perspectives argument 到如今已经老生常谈，它似乎仍然相当切题，尤其是在考虑 global health interventions 等问题时；这些 interventions 的 beneficiaries 在 culturally 方面与我们非常不同。

Effective altruists 也并不十分 humanistically aware。EA 源于 analytic philosophy，并从那里 spread 到 math 和 computer science。因此，他们过于草率地把许多 arguments 斥为 moral-relativist postmodernist 的空话，例如：effective altruists 把 Westernized 的“the good” conception 强加给他们试图帮助的人，从而 promoting cultural imperialism。即使 EAs 相当确信 utilitarian/reductionist/rationalist worldview 是 correct 的，outside view 仍然是：真正 engaging with 更具 diversity 的 opinions 非常有帮助。

例如，[Facebook group](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/) 中围绕 effective altruism 的 discourse，以前质量相当高。但随着 movement grows，传统的 discussion 场所正被尚未吸收 norms of discussion 或 standards of proof 的新人淹没。如果不尽快 rectified 这种情况，EA community 将完全不再有用：将没有任何场所可供 group truth-seeking process operate。然而，似乎没有人意识到这个 problem 的严重程度。有人曾做过一些不太用心的修正尝试，但基本没有取得什么结果。

## Movement building issues（运动建设问题）

拥有一个 effective altruism “movement” 的全部意义，在于它将 bigger than the sum of its parts。以 movement 的形式 organized，应该让 effective altruism 成为一种 large、semi-monolithic 的 actor，能够真正办成大事，而不只是 make marginal contributions。

但实际上，large movements 与 truth-seeking 几乎从不相伴而行。随着 movements 壮大，它们会产生更多的“epistemic inertia”：对它们而言，update on evidence 会变得困难得多。这是因为，它们不得不依靠 social methods 来 propagate 自己的 memes，而不是依靠 truth-seeking behavior。但那些因 social pressure 而非 truth-seeking 被吸引到 EA 中的人，需要长得多的时间才能改变自己的 beliefs，因此，一旦 movement 中这类人 reaches a critical mass，它就会难以 update on new evidence。如上所述，随着 Facebook group 变得越来越没有用，这种情况已经在 effective altruism 中发生。

## Conclusion（结论）

我已经介绍了几个方面，其中 effective altruism movement 由于以下 effects 的共同作用，未能 converge on truth：

1.  Effective altruists 过早地“stop thinking”，并且以“不明显 conflict with EA principles”为目标进行 satisfice，而不是以“increases utility”为目标进行 optimizing。（例如，这种 effect 导致他们在 choose donations 时表现很差。）
2.  Effective altruism 对其实践者提出了很高的要求，而 EA group norms 并未适当地 guard against 为逃避这些要求而产生的 motivated cognition。（例如，这经常导致人们 choose bad careers。）
3.  Effective altruists 没有注意到值得深入探究的重要领域，具体而言，是与“成为一个 successful movement”有关的问题，而不是与“correctly implementing utilitarianism”有关的问题。（例如，他们忽视了 group epistemology、这个 movement 的 historical precedents、movement diversity 等方面的问题。）

这些问题本身就令人担忧，但没有意识到它们才是真正的问题。monoculture 令人担忧，但对它漫不经心的态度更糟。缺乏 rigor 固然令人遗憾，但人们没有注意到这一点才是真正的问题。

要么 effective altruists 尚未意识到，他们同样受制于任何 large movement 都会面临的 failure modes；要么他们没有动力去做那些枯燥的基础工作，例如，认真接触那些在你的 inside view 看来令人厌烦、但在 outside view 看来却 useful on expectation 的 viewpoints。无论是哪一种情况，都表明这个 movement 的 staying power 令人担忧。

更重要的是，这也表明 effective altruists 存在 epistemic failure。EA 内部至今没有其他人做出 substantial critique，这一点是一个极其严重的警讯。如果 effective altruists 不知道针对 EA movement 的 strong critiques，他们为什么不去寻找呢？这表明，尽管 movement 内部强调 rationality，许多 effective altruists 的 beliefs 却是基于 social 而非 truth-seeking 的 behavior。

如果不解决这些问题，effective-altruism-the-movement 就无法帮助我 achieve 比我独自行动所能实现的更多的 good。它所做的只会是增加 epistemic inertia，因为 shift EA consensus 比 update 我个人的 beliefs 需要付出更多努力。

## 这些问题是 solvable 的吗？

在我看来，上面的第三个问题（作为 social movement 缺乏 self-awareness）subsumes 另外两个：如果 effective altruism 作为 movement 足够 introspective，它很可能就能注意到并解决另外两个问题，以及未来无疑会出现的其他问题。

因此，我提议为 effective altruism 增加一项 principle。除了是 _altruistic_、_maximizing_、_egalitarian_ 和 _consequentialist_ 的之外，我们还应该是 _self-aware_ 的：我们应该仔细思考与成为一个 successful movement 相关的问题，以确保我们能够超越 EA principles 的 obvious applications，想出 non-trivially better 的办法来 improve the world。

## How to read this post

Hopefully this is clear, but as a disclaimer: this piece is written in a fairly critical tone. This was part of an attempt to get “in character”. _It does not indicate my current mental state with regard to the effective altruism movement._ I agree, to varying extents, with some of the critiques I present here, but I’m not about to give up on effective altruism or stop cooperating with the EA movement. The apostasy is purely hypothetical.

Also, because of the nature of a hypothetical apostasy, I’d guess that for effective altruist readers, the critical tone of this piece may be especially likely to trigger defensive rationalization. Please read through with this in mind. (A good way to counteract this effect might be, for instance, to imagine that you’re not an effective altruist, but your friend is, and it’s them reading through it: how should they update their beliefs?)

If you want to comment, I’ve [cross-posted to Less Wrong](http://lesswrong.com/r/discussion/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/), which has high-quality discussion and a better comment system than the one I hacked together. If you have a Less Wrong account, please comment on that one!

Finally, if you’ve never heard of effective altruism before, I don’t recommend making this piece your first impression of it! You’re going to get a very skewed view because I don’t bother to mention all the things that are awesome about the EA movement.

## Abstract

Effective altruism is, to my knowledge, the first time that a substantially useful set of ethics and frameworks to analyze one’s effect on the world has gained a broad enough appeal to resemble a social movement. (I’d say these principles are something like _altruism_, _maximization_, _egalitarianism_, and _consequentialism_; together they imply many improvements over the social default for trying to do good in the world—earning to give as opposed to doing direct charity work, working in the developing world rather than locally, using evidence and feedback to analyze effectiveness, etc.) Unfortunately, as a movement effective altruism is failing to use these principles to acquire correct nontrivial beliefs about how to improve the world.

By way of clarification, consider a distinction between two senses of the word “trying” I used above. Let’s call them “actually trying” and “pretending to try”. Pretending to try to improve the world is something like responding to social pressure to improve the world by querying your brain for a thing which improves the world, taking the first search result and rolling with it. For example, for a while I thought that I would try to improve the world by developing computerized methods of checking informally-written proofs, thus allowing more scalable teaching of higher math, democratizing education, etc. _Coincidentally_, computer programming and higher math happened to be the two things that I was best at. This is pretending to try. Actually trying is looking at the things that improve the world, figuring out which one maximizes utility, and then doing that thing. For instance, I now run an effective altruist student organization at Harvard because I realized that even though I’m a comparatively bad leader and don’t enjoy it very much, it’s still very high-impact if I work hard enough at it. This isn’t to say that I’m actually trying yet, but I’ve gotten closer.

Using this distinction between pretending and actually trying, I would summarize a lot of effective altruism as “pretending to actually try”. As a social group, effective altruists have successfully noticed the pretending/actually-trying distinction. But they seem to have stopped there, assuming that knowing the difference between fake trying and actually trying translates into ability to actually try. Empirically, it most certainly doesn’t. A lot of effective altruists still end up satisficing—finding actions that are on their face acceptable under core EA standards and then picking those which seem appealing because of other essentially random factors. This is more likely to converge on good actions than what society does by default, because the principles are better than society’s default principles. Nevertheless, it fails to make much progress over what is directly obvious from the core EA principles. As a result, although “doing effective altruism” feels like truth-seeking, it often ends up being just a more credible way to pretend to try.

Below I introduce various ways in which effective altruists have failed to go beyond the social-satisficing algorithm of establishing some credibly acceptable alternatives and then picking among them based on essentially random preferences. I exhibit other areas where the norms of effective altruism fail to guard against motivated cognition. Both of these phenomena add what I call “epistemic inertia” to the effective-altruist consensus: effective altruists become more subject to pressures on their beliefs other than those from a truth-seeking process, meaning that the EA consensus becomes less able to update on new evidence or arguments and preventing the movement from moving forward. I argue that this stems from effective altruists’ reluctance to think through issues of the form “being a successful social movement” rather than “correctly applying utilitarianism individually”. This could potentially be solved by introducing an additional principle of effective altruism—e.g. “group self-awareness”—but it may be too late to add new things to effective altruism’s DNA.

## Philosophical difficulties

There is [currently](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2008/01/protecting-acro.html) [wide](http://www.amirrorclear.net/academic/ideas/negative-utilitarianism/) [disagreement](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/hedonistic-vs-preference.html) [among](http://www.utilitarian-essays.com/suffering-nature.html) [effective](http://lesswrong.com/lw/91/average_utilitarianism_must_be_correct/) [altruists](http://rationalaltruist.com/2013/06/03/my-outlook/comment-page-1/#comment-445) on the correct framework for population ethics. This is crucially important for determining the best way to improve the world: different population ethics can lead to drastically different choices (or at least so we would expect _a priori_), and if the EA movement can’t converge on at least their instrumental goals, it will quickly fragment and lose its power. Yet there has been little progress towards discovering the correct population ethics (or, from a moral anti-realist standpoint, constructing arguments that will lead to convergence on a particular population ethics), or even determining which ethics lead to which interventions being better.

## Poor cause choices

Many effective altruists donate to [GiveWell’s](http://www.givewell.org/) top charities. All three of these charities work in global health. Is that because GiveWell knows that global health is the highest-leverage cause? No. It’s because it was the only one with enough data to say anything very useful about. There’s little reason to suppose that this correlates with being particularly high-leverage—on the contrary, heuristic but less rigorous arguments for causes like [existential risk prevention](http://intelligence.org/2013/06/05/friendly-ai-research-as-effective-altruism/), [vegetarian advocacy](http://www.everydayutilitarian.com/essays/how-much-does-it-cost-to-buy-a-vegetarian/) and [open borders](http://www.facebook.com/l.php?u=http%3A%2F%2Fopenborders.info%2F&h=aAQGxloz1) suggest that these could be even more efficient.

Furthermore, the our current “best known intervention” is likely to change (in a more cost-effective direction) in the future. There are two competing effects here: we might discover better interventions to donate to than the ones we currently think are best, but we also might run out of opportunities for the current best known intervention, and have to switch to the second. So far we seem to be in a regime where the first effect dominates, and there’s no evidence that we’ll reach a tipping point very soon, especially given how new the field of effective charity research is.

Given these considerations, it’s quite surprising that effective altruists are donating to global health causes now. Even for those looking to use their donations to set an example, a donor-advised fund would have many of the benefits and none of the downsides. And anyway, donating when you believe it’s not (except for example-setting) the best possible course of action, in order to make a point about figuring out the best possible course of action and then doing that thing, seems perverse.

## Non-obviousness

Effective altruists often express surprise that the idea of effective altruism only came about so recently. For instance, my student group recently hosted Elie Hassenfeld for a talk in which he made remarks to that effect, and I’ve heard other people working for EA organizations express the same sentiment. But no one seems to be actually worried about this—just smug that they’ve figured out something that no one else had.

The “market” for ideas is at least somewhat efficient: most simple, obvious and correct things get thought of fairly quickly after it’s possible to think them. If a meme as simple as effective altruism hasn’t taken root yet, we should at least try to understand why before throwing our weight behind it. The absence of such attempts—in other words, the fact that non-obviousness doesn’t make effective altruists worried that they’re missing something—is a strong indicator against the “effective altruists are actually trying” hypothesis.

## Efficient markets for giving

It’s often claimed that “nonprofits are not a market for doing good; they’re a market for warm fuzzies”. This is used as justification for why it’s possible to do immense amounts of good by donating. However, while it’s certainly true that most donors aren’t explicitly trying to purchase utililty, there’s still a lot of money that is.

The [Gates Foundation](http://www.gatesfoundation.org/) is an example of such an organization. They’re effectiveness-minded and with $60 billion behind them. 80,000 Hours has already [noted](http://80000hours.org/blog/239-show-me-the-harm) that they’ve probably saved over 6 million lives with their vaccine programs alone—given that they’ve spent a relatively small part of their endowment, they must be getting a much better exchange rate than our current best guesses.

So why not just donate to the Gates Foundation? Effective altruists need a better account of the “market inefficiencies” that they’re exploiting that Gates isn’t. Why didn’t the Gates Foundation fund the [Against Malaria Foundation](https://www.againstmalaria.com/), GiveWell’s top charity, when it’s in one of their main research areas? It seems implausible that the answer is simple incompetence or the like.

A general rule of markets is that if you don’t know what your edge is, you’re the sucker. Many effective altruists, when asked what their edge is, give some answer along the lines of “actually being strategic/thinking about utility/caring about results”, and stop thinking there. This isn’t a compelling case: as mentioned before, it’s not clear why no one else is doing these things.

## Inconsistent attitude towards rigor

Effective altruists insist on extraordinary rigor in their charity recommendations—cf. for instance GiveWell’s work. Yet for many ancillary problems—donating now vs. later, choosing a career, and deciding how “meta” to go (between direct work, earning to give, doing advocacy, and donating to advocacy), to name a few—they seem happy to choose between the not-obviously-wrong alternatives based on intuition and gut feelings.

## Poor psychological understanding

John Sturm suggests, and I agree, that many of these issues are psychological in nature:

> I think a lot of these problems take root a commitment level issue:
> 
> I, for instance, am thrilled about changing my mentality towards charity, not my mentality towards having kids. My first guess is that - from an EA and overall ethical perspective - it would be a big mistake for me to have kids (even after taking into account the normal EA excuses about doing things for myself). At least right now, though, I just don’t care that I’m ignoring my ethics and EA; I want to have kids and that’s that.
> 
> This is a case in which I’m not “being lazy” so much as just not trying at all. But when someone asks me about it, it’s easier for me to give some EA excuse (like that having kids will make me happier and more productive) that I don’t think is true - and then I look like I’m being a lazy or careless altruist rather than not being one at all.
> 
> The model I’m building is this: there are many different areas in life where I could apply EA. In some of them, I’m wholeheartedly willing. In some of them, I’m not willing at all. Then there are two kinds of areas where it looks like I’m being a lazy EA: those where I’m willing and want to be a better EA… and those where I’m not willing but I’m just pretending (to myself or others or both).
> 
> The point of this: when we ask someone to be a less lazy EA, we are (1) helping them do a better job at something they want to do, and (2) trying to make them either do more than they want to or admit they are “bad”.

In general, most effective altruists respond to deep conflicts between effective altruism and other goals in one of the following ways:

1.  Unconsciously resolve the cognitive dissonance with motivated reasoning: “it’s clearly my comparative advantage to spread effective altruism through poetry!”
2.  Deliberately and knowingly use motivated reasoning: “dear Facebook group, what are the best utilitarian arguments in favor of becoming an EA poet?”
3.  Take the easiest “honest” way out: “I wouldn’t be psychologically able to do effective altruism if it forced me to go into finance instead of writing poetry, so I’ll become an effective altruist poet instead”.

The third is debatably defensible—though, for a community that purports to put stock in rationality and self-improvement, effective altruists have shown surprisingly little interest in self-modification to have more altruistic intentions. This seems obviously worthy of further work.

Furthermore, EA norms do not proscribe even the first two, leading to a group norm that doesn’t cause people to notice when they’re engaging in a certain amount of motivated cognition. This is quite toxic to the movement’s ability to converge on the truth. (As before, effective altruists are still better than the general population at this; the core EA principles are strong enough to make people notice the most obvious motivated cognition that obviously runs afoul of them. But that’s not nearly good enough.)

## Historical analogues

With the partial exception of GiveWell’s [history of philanthropy project](http://www.givewell.org/history-of-philanthropy), there’s been no research into good historical outside views. Although there are no direct precursors of effective altruism (worrying in its own right; see above), there is one notably similar movement: communism, where the idea of “from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs” originated. Communism is also notable for its various abject failures. Effective altruists need to be more worried about how they will avoid failures of a similar class—and in general they need to be more aware of the pitfalls, as well as the benefits, of being an increasingly large social movement.

Aaron Tucker elaborates better than I could:

> In particular, Communism/Socialism was a movement that was started by philosophers, then continued by technocrats, where they thought reason and planning could make the world much better, and that if they coordinated to take action to fix everything, they could eliminate poverty, disease, etc.
> 
> Marx totally got the “actually trying vs. pretending to try” distinction AFAICT (“Philosophers have only explained the world, but the real problem is to change it” is a quote of his), and he really strongly rails against people who unreflectively try to fix things in ways that make sense to the culture they’re starting from—the problem isn’t that the bourgeoisie aren’t trying to help people, it’s that the only conception of help that the bourgeoisie have is one that’s mostly epiphenomenal to actually improving the lives of the proletariat—giving them nice boureoisie things like education and voting rights, but not doing anything to improve the material condition of their life, or fix the problems of why they don’t have those in the first place, and don’t just make them themselves.
> 
> So if Marx got the pretend/actually try distinction, and his followers took over countries, and they had a ton of awesome technocrats, it seems like it’s the perfect EA thing, and it totally didn’t work.

## Monoculture

Effective altruists are not very diverse. The vast majority are white, “upper-middle-class”, intellectually and philosophically inclined, from a developed country, etc. (and I think it skews significantly male as well, though I’m less sure of this). And as much as the multiple-perspectives argument for diversity is hackneyed by this point, it seems quite germane, especially when considering e.g. global health interventions, whose beneficiaries are culturally very foreign to us.

Effective altruists are not very humanistically aware either. EA came out of analytic philosophy and spread from there to math and computer science. As such, they are too hasty to dismiss many arguments as moral-relativist postmodernist fluff, e.g. that effective altruists are promoting cultural imperialism by forcing a Westernized conception of “the good” onto people they’re trying to help. Even if EAs are quite confident that the utilitarian/reductionist/rationalist worldview is correct, the outside view is that really engaging with a greater diversity of opinions is very helpful.

The discourse around effective altruism in e.g. the [Facebook group](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/) used to be of fairly high quality. But as the movement grows, the traditional venues of discussion are getting inundated with new people who haven’t absorbed the norms of discussion or standards of proof yet. If this is not rectified quickly, the EA community will cease to be useful at all: there will be no venue in which a group truth-seeking process can operate. Yet nobody seems to be aware of the magnitude of this problem. There have been some half-hearted attempts to fix it, but nothing much has come of them.

## Movement building issues

The whole point of having an effective altruism “movement” is that it’ll be bigger than the sum of its parts. Being organized as a movement should turn effective altruism into the kind of large, semi-monolithic actor that can actually get big stuff done, not just make marginal contributions.

But in practice, large movements and truth-seeking hardly ever go together. As movements grow, they get more “epistemic inertia”: it becomes much harder for them to update on evidence. This is because they have to rely on social methods to propagate their memes rather than truth-seeking behavior. But people who have been drawn to EA by social pressure rather than truth-seeking take much longer to change their beliefs, so once the movement reaches a critical mass of them, it will become difficult for it to update on new evidence. As described above, this is already happening to effective altruism with the ever-less-useful Facebook group.

## Conclusion

I’ve presented several areas in which the effective altruism movement fails to converge on truth through a combination of the following effects:

1.  Effective altruists “stop thinking” too early and satisfice for “doesn’t obviously conflict with EA principles” rather than optimizing for “increases utility”. (For instance, they choose donations poorly due to this effect.)
2.  Effective altruism puts strong demands on its practitioners, and EA group norms do not appropriately guard against motivated cognition to avoid them. (For example, this often causes people to choose bad careers.)
3.  Effective altruists don’t notice important areas to look into, specifically issues related to “being a successful movement” rather than “correctly implementing utilitarianism”. (For instance, they ignore issues around group epistemology, historical precedents for the movement, movement diversity, etc.)

These problems are worrying on their own, but the lack of awareness of them is the real problem. The monoculture is worrying, but the lackadaisical attitude towards it is worse. The lack of rigor is unfortunate, but the fact that people haven’t noticed it is the real problem.

Either effective altruists don’t yet realize that they’re subject to the failure modes of any large movement, or they don’t feel motivation to do the boring legwork of e.g. engaging with viewpoints that your inside view says are annoying but that the outside view says are useful on expectation. Either way, this bespeaks worrying things about the movement’s staying power.

More importantly, it also indicates an epistemic failure on the part of effective altruists. The fact that no one else within EA has done a substantial critique yet is a huge red flag. If effective altruists aren’t aware of strong critiques of the EA movement, why aren’t they looking for them? This suggests that, contrary to the emphasis on rationality within the movement, many effective altruists’ beliefs are based on social, rather than truth-seeking, behavior.

If it doesn’t solve these problems, effective-altruism-the-movement won’t help me achieve any more good than I could individually. All it will do is add epistemic inertia, as it takes more effort to shift the EA consensus than to update my individual beliefs.

## Are these problems solvable?

It seems to me that the third issue above (lack of self-awareness as a social movement) subsumes the other two: if effective altruism as a movement were sufficiently introspective, it could probably notice and solve the other two problems, as well as future ones that will undoubtedly crop up.

Hence, I propose an additional principle of effective altruism. In addition to being _altruistic_, _maximizing_, _egalitarian_, and _consequentialist_ we should be _self-aware_: we should think carefully about the issues associated with being a successful movement, in order to make sure that we can move beyond the obvious applications of EA principles and come up with non-trivially better ways to improve the world.
