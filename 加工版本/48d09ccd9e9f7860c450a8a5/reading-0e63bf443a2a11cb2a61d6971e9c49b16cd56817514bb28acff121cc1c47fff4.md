## 第 4 部分 - Howard Nielsen Interview

## 70 年代初的 Teaching 与 Football

**Michael Nielsen:** 好，上次我们说到——让我想想，基本上说到你在 Clontarf teaching。你已经结婚，在 Walker's Road 有了房子。其实，我想大概再过不久，我就要出现在故事里了。我不确定，还有没有什么特别值得一提的事？

**Howard:** 没什么了。说到 teaching，我在 '74 年离开了 Clontarf——'72、'73、'74——去 Mount Nebo 待了一年。所以，1975 年我在 Mount Nebo 那所 school 当 principal。

**Michael:** 你这么快就当上了 principal。

**Howard:** 是啊，嗯，他们肯定是人手不够之类的。我也不太确定。那是所 One teacher school。这期间唯一的另一件事跟 football 有关——我们在 1973 年 won the Premiership。

**Michael:** 你是不是一到 Brisbane，就差不多立刻开始在 Mayne playing 了？

**Howard:** 差不多。就是那时候，我决定不想继续参加 circuit 上的 tennis tournaments 之类的了。我已经不再是 junior，也就是 under 19——我大概差了一个星期左右，就能再以 junior 身份待一年——不过我决定不继续了，而是去了 Teachers College。

我在 college 的头一两个星期里，一直寄住在 O'Sullivan 家，Terry O'Sullivan 得知我 played Aussie rules。他在工作的 Australia Post 有个朋友，是 Mayne supporter。他说：“是啊，他来自 South Australia，应该会 play footy。”这推断放在谁身上都够牵强的，不过我就是这么到了 Mayne。

结果，在我 signed 大概一个星期后，当时在 Grand Hotel 的我爸有个来自 Coorparoo AFL 的朋友，对我很感兴趣——实际上，他们还提出给我一辆二手车，算是拉拢一下。不过我已经跟 Mayne signed 了，所以不可能再过去。而且 Coorparoo 在城的另一边。于是我在 '69 年开始为 Mayne playing。那是 Mayne 多年来第一次 bottom of the table。你自己琢磨这算怎么回事吧。所以，那一年我赢得了 Best and Fairest。

**Michael:** 你是说，你是 bad team 里 best 的那个 :-)？

**Howard:** 差不多是这个意思。team 里有一些 good players，不过之前一年，还有再前一年他们 won the Premiership 的时候的 players，已经走了不少。有几个老将 retired——其中大约三四个曾为 Queensland played，所以他们是那个 level 的。于是他们正在 rebuilding。

**Michael:** 你们是哪一年 won the Premiership 的？是 '73 还是 '74？

**Howard:** '73。'74 年我们也进了 grand final，不过那次 lost 了。

## Tommy Blacker 与 Vietnam

**Michael:** 我记得从小就见到 Tommy Blacker 和他家里人。你们显然是很好的朋友。你们在 Mayne team 里都是 fullbacks 吗？

**Howard:** 他是 back pocket——就是 full back 旁边的 player，负责 looks after 对方的 rover，通常是个头较小的人。现在这些全都变了，不过当时就是这样。我也是通过 footy 才认识 Tommy 的。他之前为 Mayne played 过，但后来被 conscripted 去了 Vietnam，所以在那儿待了一年半。

**Michael:** 我记得 Tommy 说话总是很大声，而且明显有点 deaf，我猜那是 Vietnam 的 artillery 造成的。

**Howard:** 他在 artillery section。他们应该有 headphones 之类的，不过那些大概只能 minimised 那种 pain，而不能 excluded 它。

**Michael:** 你没有被 drafted，不过我想你认识不少被 drafted 的人。这件事有没有一直压在你心上？你是不是很清楚它随时可能发生？

**Howard:** conscription 是通过 lottery 决定的。抽中了 Number 15——15th of January。我是 14th of January，所以只差了一天。当时你当然会在意。你得等 lottery 进行，然后，如果想 avoid conscription，就必须 apply。不少去读 teacher education 的人获准不用去。所以，即使抽中，我也很有可能不用去。tennis fraternity 里也有人，虽然抽中了他们的 number，却还是没去。

随着年龄渐长，你会更能体会 war 的意义。那些年，Australia 的 anti-Vietnam sentiment 日益高涨，因此人们对这件事的讨论也多了很多。我很庆幸自己没去。他们之所以要被 conscripted，就是因为不能指望人们自愿去。

## 不同世代对 War 的 Attitudes

**Michael:** 跟仅仅上一代相比，这变化真有意思。你说过，你爸和 Uncle Darcy 曾为谁能去而争执。那是 World War II，还是 Korean War？

**Howard:** Second World War。人们的 attitudes 就是非常不一样。在 30 年代和 40 年代，Australia 跟 Great Britain 非常亲近。Great Britain 要求什么，Australia 基本就照办，尽管在 Second World War 开始时，Australia 其实并没有什么危险——后来 Japanese 加入，才真的有了危险。

Vietnam 则是 American-led。人们还不太接受这样一种想法：Americans 叫我们做什么，我们照办也没问题。虽然也有一些这样的倾向，但情况不一样。America 在 Vietnam 的行动引起了很大争议，你应该从 Forrest Gump 之类的电影里看到过——它把那种 animosity 表现得相当到位。

**Michael:** 我的理解是，Great Britain 实际上任由 Singapore fall，而这对 Australian public opinion 造成了相当大的影响。当时人们有一种默认的认识，认为他们会 prioritise 对前 Commonwealth states 的 defense，结果 Australians 突然意识到，他们并不会这样做。Australia 得自己 make 自己的 alliances，自己 fight 自己的 battles。

**Howard:** 甚至在 First World War 时，这种想法就已经有一点萌芽了。Churchill 在 Australians 当中可算不上受欢迎，因为我们被当成从海外赶来的 cannon fodder，帮助 Brits fight 一场 war，而他们已经说服 Australia，相信这场 war 非打不可——Second World War 也一样。Australia 还真没经历过哪场 war，是自己选择参与，而不是由另一个 country 带头的。

**Michael:** World War II 的话，他们应该还是会参加。Australia 要 maintain neutrality 会非常困难。

**Howard:** 我不是说他们应该 neutral——毕竟 Germans 和 Japanese 都在 perpetrating 那些事。Americans 进入 Europe 是为了帮助 Britain；Australia 则一直都在那里。后来他们面对 Japanese，处境又更加困难，部分原因就是 Britain 放弃了 Singapore。当时人们的心情应该很复杂——prime ministers 大概不会，他们还是照自己的那一套来，但公众未必如此。

有件事，对当时大约 11 岁的我来说，可能挺有意思——我们在 fruit block 干活，有一年，我想是 1960 年，爸爸雇了一个 German 男人，以及他的妻子和两个孩子来当 pickers。他叫 Erwin Koch，妻子叫 Bärbel。他其实在 Second World War 当过 soldier。我跟他熟了起来——我们基本上是一起干活的伙伴，还经常互相扔土块。他当时大概快 40 岁。

我记得问过他，当 soldier 是什么感觉。他很厌恶。他说：“我就是很厌恶。我没有有意识地 shoot 过任何人，但我得避免自己被 shot。”这样一来，你也就能明白 Germans 为什么 lost 了——许多 soldiers 宁可轻易 surrendered，也不愿被 shot，尽管他们也面临着被 German hierarchy 以 treason 为由 shot 的危险。

所以，我平平静静地度过了 Vietnam conscription 那个阶段。

## 早期的 Political Involvement

**Michael:** 你是什么时候开始 get involved in politics 的？一旦进了 student union，你就已经有点涉足 politics 了。那时候你加入 Labour Party 了吗？

**Howard:** 没有，那是后来的事。有意思的是，我在 teaching 才一年之后，就确实 apply 了 Teachers' Union 的 General Secretary 这个 job。我告诉几个人时，他们扬起眉毛，说我根本不可能得到那个 job——我确实没得到，因为 union movement 内部早就安排好了。所以我对 representing 和 advocating 感兴趣，但我也喜欢 teaching，就没有继续争取。

我第一次加入 Labour Party 是在结婚以后。我是在一次为了让 Joh 下台的 election campaign 中认识一些 Labour Party 的人的，我记得是这样。后来我加入了 party，大概是二十五六岁的时候。就开始去参加 Arana Hills 当地 Labour Party 的 meetings。

我始终不怎么喜欢 party 内有 factions 这种事。以为自己不成为某个 faction 的 member 就能担任任何 powerful position，是 unrealistic 的。那段经历挺有意思：有人想让我加入他们的 faction，而我决定不加入。我和另一个人创办了一个叫 Forum 的东西——基本上是一群不想属于任何 faction，却乐意参加 forum 的人，只要他们不受其中产生的任何 decision 约束就行。这是一个可以 without fear or favour 地 discuss issues 的机会。

Wayne Swan 对这个 concept 产生了兴趣。他当时是 Queensland 的 Labour Party 的 Secretary。他派了几个自己 factional 的人来参加我们的 meetings。其中一个人后来向我透露，他来是因为 Wayne Swan 想知道我们在做什么。

## 参加 Pre-selection

**Howard:** 我开始以 candidate 身份 running 的时候，可能让我去 run 的地方，只有那些 Labour 不大可能 won 的 seats。其中有一个，是 Petrie 还是 Dixon 的 seat，我在那里 ran for pre-selection，那个 seat 属于 borderline。

它的运作方式是，先由住在那个 electorate 的 Labour Party members 进行 plebiscite。然后你再去一个 central forum，那里完全是 factionally organised 的。factional organisation 获得的 value 是 local vote 的三倍。但我在当地开展了非常有力的 campaign，居然 won 了当地的 pre-selection plebiscite。

这引起了一点轰动。大家想知道这是怎么回事——这意味着 electorate 里有相当多据说属于某个 faction 的 Labour Party members，并没有 vote for 那个 faction。他们 voted for 我。因为那是 secret ballot，谁也无法证明什么。而在 centralised pre-selection 中，所有 state representatives 都是 factional operatives。每个人都知道谁在 voting for 谁。

情况糟糕到，到了 voting 的时候，他们都不亲自 vote。他们把自己的 voting paper 交给一位 factional leader——他们不能 vote secretly，不能按自己的意愿 vote。他们必须按照自己的 faction 来 vote。

最后我只输了 one vote 或 one and a half votes，因为我在当地 plebiscite 中的 value 相当高——我得到了 58%，而那里的主要 faction 通常大概能得到 52 或 53%。他们说，我是大约十年来第一个在 winnable seat 中，真正赢过 faction、win 了 local plebiscite 的人。

**Michael:** 这帮助我理解了 United States 一件让我觉得很奇怪的事。很多人看着 Trump 和他的 cronies，感到警惕，这是完全有道理的。他们想：“他怎么可能会 won 呢？”但如果看看 across the aisle 的另一边，Democrats 有另一套问题，而且也相当严重。就好像 Democratic Party 已经被 captured from the inside 了——大量 inside baseball，让他们对 electorate 来说并不怎么有吸引力。

我记得 80s 的 Queensland politics。当时有一个显然相当糟糕的 premier，Joh Bjelke-Petersen，但 opposition 似乎也没法振作起来——也许原因类似，被 factions 从内部 captured，而这些 factions 对自己的那些争斗比对 winning elections 更感兴趣。

**Howard:** 当时 party 的 leader，一个叫 Bill 什么的人，完全是 factional 的、union-oriented 的，非常严厉。他拥有 full control。他要的就是这样。

我在 pre-selection process 期间意识到了这一点。在 local vote 和 centralised vote 之间，我给很多人打了电话，意识到自己已经彻底没戏了。那时还有 refugee issues 和 vote boat people 那些事，Labour Party 都处理得不好，environmental 方面的事也乏善可陈。所以我想，不如轰轰烈烈地退场。

在 centralised vote 时，你有机会作一次五分钟的 speech。我决定告诉他们，我眼中的情况是什么样的。

我说：“我加入 party，是因为一本叫 The Light on the Hill 的书，我至今仍然相信其中的理念，Labour Party 也是以它为基础的。眼下我没看到多少光，但倒是看到了一座大山。”

“我家里有两个孩子。他们会想知道我今天怎么样。现在，我能告诉他们的是，Labour Party 里有一位 senior person，对像在座各位这样的人拥有 full power——在这次 pre-selection 中，你们甚至都不能为自己 vote。能吗？”

许多人垂下了目光。

“有一个我很熟悉的人，Rosemary Hume，她是我的 competitors 之一，我知道她有 factional support。她现在就在这个房间外面，等着轮到她，我知道她听得见我在说什么。所以我邀请她现在走进房间，说她将 withdraw from 这个 process。”

你真该看看大家望向门口的样子。他们知道她不会，我也知道她不会。

“我希望能告诉我的孩子，事情其实没有那么糟，还是有一些好人的。”

然后我看着 Bill，说：“你现在能不能说，这个房间里的任何人都可以按自己的意愿 vote？不会有人来收走 voting slips？我会等你回答。”

当然，我输了。但事后有几个人对我说：“那真是太棒了。你根本想不到，这会给这个房间里的人带来多大的影响。这让我们开始认真思考自己在 party 中的 role。”

这跟 Democrats 有点像——rationally 来说，除了他们自己的 mistakes，以及他们作 decisions 时那种愚蠢的方式，他们没道理不几乎一直 in power。至于 Policy-wise，因为有 biases，很难说得确切。但在 America，金钱所起的作用比在 Australia 大得多。

至少在 Australia，人们可以从 humble beginnings 起步，成为 member of parliament，甚至 prime minister。没有别人的支持，他们到不了那个位置——不是靠自己有钱才走到那里的。

**Michael:** Jimmy Carter 是个 peanut farmer。Barack Obama 的出身也相当普通——更多是做 community organiser 和 academic。

**Howard:** 确实如此。

## 成为 principal

**Michael:** 你为什么想成为 principal？很多 teachers 整个 career 都不想当 principal。

**Howard:** 我喜欢发生有意思的事。在 classroom 里也能这样——你可以一辈子当 teacher，拥有非常有意思的经历，因为你会和自己 teaching 的孩子们一起把日子过得有意思。

不过，从我在 college 担任 student union president 的经历，以及后来那个举办 workshops 和 conferences 的 national network 中，我意识到，自己大概有能力让人们参与有价值的交流，这些交流能让人充满干劲，也能有力地造福 community。One-Teacher Schools 这个想法很吸引我——认识不同的 parents 应该会很有意思。

我当时不知道，自己的第一次 appointment 会是 Mount Nebo。用我朋友的话来说，那大概是 Queensland 最好的 One-Teacher School 职位。

我在那里做得不错——接受 inspectors 的 assessed，也和 community 一起做事。那年我们举办了一场大型 pioneer fair，大概有几百人来到 Mount Nebo，参加以 natural environment 为主题的周末 fair。他们以前从没举办过这样的活动。那很有意思。

如果你是 principal，就有 autonomy 去作出改变。如果你是 teacher，是否拥有那样的 autonomy，可能就取决于你的 principal。不过，更重要的是，我意识到自己似乎天生有些能力，能 facilitate conversations，让人们愿意去做那些原本可能觉得自己做不到的事。这会让人充满干劲。

**Michael:** 能举一个当时的例子吗？

**Howard:** 就是那场 pioneer fair。有一天，我和一位 parent 聊天，问道：“你们以前在这里办过 fete 或 fair 吗？”“我不知道。”“我们应该办一次。如果要办，你会怎么做？”事情就这样一步步发展，最后我们真的办成了。

我有一个有利条件，就是前任 principal 已经在那里待了 20 年，而且大概早在 15 年前就已经不受欢迎了。有些 parents 之间积怨很深。我没有理会以前发生的事，而是把这里当成一个全新的、良好的环境，它也确实如此。那些 parents 都很好——只是其中几位之前受到的对待不太好。

**Michael:** Mount Nebo 似乎是一个相当特别的 community——有很多怀抱理想的人，对世界有着不同寻常的想法。不过，并不是每个人都那么务实。

**Howard:** 那里有不少有趣的人。在 parents 当中，有一位 solicitor，还有一位 35 岁就退休的 architect——他们都是为了摆脱激烈竞争的生活才来到 Mount Nebo 的。我们和其中几位成了很好的朋友。

## 在 Classroom 中采用不同的 Approach

**Howard:** 说到在 classroom 里能做些什么——我记得在 Clontarf Primary School，有一件事效果特别好，当时我有一个很大的 double teaching space。我们有 66 名 students，由两位 teachers 带。

我提议把 classroom 建成一座 city，我的 co-teacher 也同意了，条件是由我来组织。每个月，各个 groupings——我记得有六个 groups，每个大约十人——都可以 nominate 并 elect 自己的 councillor，来 represent 自己 group 的 views。

每个月都会 elected 一位新的 councillor，负责 represent 自己的 group，并 conduct discussions。然后，每个月再从他们当中 elected 一人担任 mayor，任期一个月。

效果非常好。有些孩子平时并不被看作 leaders，但当他们处于 leader 的 position 时，其他许多孩子都惊讶地发现，只要给他们一些 responsibility，他们就能表现得那么好，那么 responsible。

很多 one-teacher schools 并没有真的这样做，不过，在那样一个大的 classroom 里，我觉得值得一试。事实也确实如此。

## Evergreen 与失去 GR

**Michael:** 你在 Evergreen 有一位 teacher's assistant。我曾和你聊过，在我刚入学的那一年半里，你既是 principal 又是 teacher，采取了相当 laissez-faire 的 approach。你基本上就是指着那些书说：“玩得开心。”不过，我依稀记得你告诉过我，那位 assistant 对此并不怎么热衷。

**Howard:** 我不觉得她是在坚决反对，但那确实是她不熟悉的做法。她有疑问，所以我解释了为什么想这么做，以及这样做会如何奏效。最后，她渐渐明白这样是对的。有意思的是，有一阵子她变得有点过于 laissez-faire 了，所以我不得不和她谈谈她与 students 的沟通方式。态度友善，和让 students 显得有些不尊重人，是有区别的——他们有点像是在开玩笑，但又不完全是。所以，我们就这件事谈了谈。

这些事很容易判断失误——什么可以，什么不可以。每个人对什么可以接受，都有自己的看法。不聊一聊，你是不会知道的。

**Michael:** 我记得，你对 school 有非常牢固的 control。谁在主持大局，这一点毫无疑问。但在这个前提下，大家有相当多的 autonomy，可以自己作 decisions——只要不干蠢事就行。

**Howard:** 是的。

**Michael:** 我还记得 Evergreen 的另一件事，非常伤心。我们有一只特别好的狗，叫 GR。说起这件事，我可能会掉眼泪。她在后院被球打中了，尖叫了一声，突然跑开，冲到了路上。那条路几乎没什么车——大概每 15 分钟才有一辆——但偏偏来了一辆车，撞了她，她就死了。

**Howard:** 那真的很难过。我记得，整件事像是变成了慢动作。我看见 GR 跑到了前面的草地上。我想：“希望她别跑到路上去。”与此同时，我听到 400 或 500 metres 外有辆车开过来。我心里想着：“不，不会发生这种事的。”

我大声叫她回来，但我想，球打中了她的眼睛，她可能一时看不见了。我得过去把她抱起来。我们把她埋在了 school 的后院。

老 GR 真是一只好狗。你知道她为什么叫 GR 吗？

**Michael:** 不知道。

**Howard:** GR 是 Great Reluctance 的缩写，意思是万分不舍。所以，每次离开家，我都可以说，自己是万分不舍地离开家人的。有点开玩笑，但也有几分真心。

狗其实就是家人。我们住在 Everton Hills 时，她曾保护我们，不让隔壁邻居靠近。那人有点危险，常常醉醺醺的——坐过几次牢。她能察觉到这一点。对他来说，我们家就是禁入区。她并不是对谁都这样——像是凭一种直觉。

现在，我们和 Goldie 经历的并不一样——她不是遭遇意外而死，但她已经 18 岁了。

**Michael:** GR 当时才四五岁。

**Howard:** 真奇妙，即便过了 45 年，那些情绪还是会回来。这恰恰说明，家里的狗有多重要。

后来，我们就养了 Seiko。

**Michael:** 对！我们是在 Evergreen 养的 Seiko。她小时候真漂亮——你把她带回来时，她还是只小狗。

**Howard:** 我很确定是这样。她以前特别喜欢在地上挖洞。

**Michael:** 她以前简直能把我吓得魂飞魄散。

**Howard:** 是吗？

**Michael:** 她看到我放学回家，总是特别兴奋。随着她越长越大——Labradors 都是这样——她表达兴奋的方式，就是朝我猛冲过来。她未必意识到，自己长得比我快。她会一跃而起，把我撞倒。大概有一两个月，我对她有点提防。我很爱她，但她并不总是知道自己的力气有多大。

**Howard:** 她还没听话到该有的程度。最后，我们总算教会她别把我们的孩子弄死。

**Michael:** 我记得那次 Centenary Parade，还有那些 floats。

**Howard:** 那是 Shire 的百年庆典——也可能是 150 周年。妈妈为代表 Evergreen 的 float 进行 design，并把它制作出来，赢得了很多赞誉。我记得它还拿了最佳 float 奖。

**Michael:** 我不记得那个 float 是妈妈做的了。我记得它给我的印象很深——小时候，我觉得它简直不可思议。

**Howard:** 我们在什么地方还留着它的照片。我应该把它们放进书里。

在 Evergreen 的那两年过得很好。tennis 在 Evergreen、Kulpi 以及周边那些地方都很盛行。那里的 client group、demography 和 Monash 非常相似——都是 farming 的人，还有几个经营当地商店的人。在 Darling Downs 的那一带，他们刚经历过 government 决定关闭不少 one teacher schools 的时期。后来，大概十年后，他们也把 Evergreen 关闭了。

**Michael:** 但它当时已经开办了将近 100 年，对吧？

**Howard:** 是的。那个百年庆典其实是 Shire Council 的——150 周年。我们决定在 Evergreen 做点有意思的事。

## 搬到 Croydon

**Michael:** 我记得 Evergreen 是一个比较安宁、运转也比较正常的 community——有一点小风波，但也就是任何一群人聚在一起都会有的程度。而后来我们搬到 Croydon，那里显然一下子混乱了太多。就连去那里的路途都颇像一场冒险。

**Howard:** 别人建议我们不一定要一路开车过去，而是把车装上去 Cairns 的火车，再从 Cairns 开过去。结果在火车运输途中，车上所有轮胎都被扎破了。我们只好先在 Cairns 把轮胎修好，然后开车经过几个内陆小镇，前往 Croydon。

其中有 150 kilometres 是满地石头的土路。一块石头打穿了我们的挡风玻璃——本来不该发生这种事，因为我们装了挡风玻璃防护罩，可那块石头偏偏从防护罩顶上擦了过去。我们大概是在没有挡风玻璃的情况下开完最后 50 kilometres，进入 Croydon 的。这开头实在算不上好。

**Michael:** 你对 Croydon 的第一印象是什么？

**Howard:** 跟我预想的差不多。一个偏远小镇，一家酒馆，过去比当时繁盛得多，所以还留着成规模的建筑。有 courthouse，也有 police station。school 比较新——不是原来的那所 school。

有件鲜为人知的事：早在 1890s 或 1880s，Wendy 的曾祖父或祖父就是 Croydon School 的 principal。因为 gold rush，当时 Croydon 的三四所 schools 大概有一千个孩子。

**Michael:** 那里最多的时候好像有 10,000 人，等你去的时候就只剩下大约 250 人了？

**Howard:** 整个 Croydon Shire 跟 Victoria 一样大，voting population 却只有 150。所以把孩子算上，大概有 300 人。

酒馆老板是个好人。上一次 mayoral election 时，他们遇到了麻烦——有人想把他的酒馆烧掉。我们到那里的第一晚，flying doctor 就来了。有个人喝醉了，从酒馆出来时，脑袋撞在路缘石上。大家不得不叫 flying doctor，因为那里虽然有 hospital，却没有 doctor，也没有像样的 X-ray machinery。

这就是我们到那里的第一天。

**Howard:** 那天晚上我们住在旅馆，因为送家具来的卡车司机把所有东西一股脑堆进了 school residence，紧紧挤在墙边，弄得人都没法好好进去。我们只好第二天再回去收拾。不过住酒馆倒也还行。

## 教从没玩过的孩子们踢 Football

**Howard:** 我只在那里待了六个月。你大概只待了三四个月。我教孩子们怎么 play Aussie rules。AFL 寄来了很不错的 videos、成套的 footy jumpers、football boots 和 footballs。我们把孩子们教到了能去 Cairns 跟当地一支 team 进行 play against 的程度。女孩们 played softball，男孩们 played Aussie rules。他们以前从来没 played 过。

**Michael:** 对那些孩子来说，那一定是很了不起的经历。

**Howard:** 他们至少有一半从没见过那样的水——海浪、Pacific 的海水。有些可能在 Gulf Country 的 Normanton 见过，但有不少从没见过。

我们在一所有联系的 school 的 library 里过夜。他们允许我们在那里睡两晚。不过我们得先赶到那里——路很远，我记得要八个小时。

通常按照 school rules，要么由 parents 经许可带孩子们去，要么就找一辆 bus。那里没有 bus。一位 parent 说：“我们可以把他们放在卡车后面。”于是大约七八个孩子坐在一辆 five-ton 卡车的后车厢里，其他人坐小汽车或皮卡。

我在 P&C meeting 上对 parents 说：“这件事得请你们签字同意，因为这不符合 rules。如果你们想这么做，愿意承担这个风险——而且我相信这对你们来说不是什么新鲜事，因为你们去露营时也这么干。”

这确实有点冒险。放在现在绝不可能这样做。

主要原因不只是去 play football 或 softball——更是让他们有机会去见识一下 Cairns。

我们 play against 的，结果竟然是 Cairns 的 primary school rep team。我事先跟 Cairns 的 teachers 说过，不要安排我们对上他们最好的 team，因为这些孩子以前连一场真正的 game 都没参加过。他们把这件事忘了。

我没有告诉 Croydon 的孩子们发生了什么。他们就那么上场去 play 了。我们大概以 eight goals to one 输掉了，但他们表现得真的很好。

不过——有一个小男孩，是个 Aboriginal 孩子，叫 Jackie。他脾气有点急。刚开始没多久，他拿着 ball，一个 Cairns 的孩子把他 tackled 到地上，这是允许的。对方那个孩子得到了一个 free kick。Jackie 气疯了。他说：“这家伙凭什么 tackling 我？”然后一拳打在那个孩子头上。

我说：“他可以那样做，但你不能打他的头。”这也算一点边做边学。

我碰巧就在不远处的 sideline，于是上前制止。umpire 明白是怎么回事。这件事很难忘。

所有孩子都去游了泳。考虑到那边水里有那些危险的家伙，海滩附近当时肯定有一个 salt water pool。

## Accelerated Learning（加速学习）

**Michael:** 你还记得 Samantha 吗？她在 Croydon 跟我同 year。

**Howard:** Cara 是在 Evergreen 跟你同 year。Samantha 在 Croydon 是 year two，这其实挺幸运的，因为我知道，对你的 strategy 就是稍微放开手，让你自己去做，只在你想知道什么的时候帮一下忙。你有该做的事情，但那并不是你在 school 做的事情中最令人兴奋的部分。

Cara 是个聪明的小家伙。她能 work independently，Samantha 也一样。在我看来，连续两年里，我先后有两个 year ones 和两个 year twos，而通常你不会指望这个年纪的孩子能做到 responsible 和 autonomous。当然，teaching 还是做了很多，但能 work independently 是一件很重要的事。

**Michael:** 我记得，回到 Brisbane 的那些大 schools 是一种非常奇怪的经历。Samantha 和我在三四个月里 covered 的 reading，后来才知道，按他们的说法大约要五年才能完成。然后到了 school，却被告知：“这就是我们 progress 的 normal rate，因为大家的聪明程度只够做到这样。”我心里想，跟我一起的每一个孩子都能按这个 rate 来。你在某种程度上已经明白，这种说法是不对的。

我记得有时要很费劲才能跟上 Samantha。

**Howard:** 哦，是吗？

**Michael:** 她在 reading 上超过了我。我们之间有竞争。我记得自己落在她后面，心想：“等一下，我得加把劲了。”然后回到 Brisbane，却只能按大约 10% 的 pace 前进，还被告知，如果你能更快，那就是你有什么特别之处。我们三个孩子，三个都做到了——其实是四个孩子，四个都做到了，因为我上 year two 的时候，又来了一个小孩，他之前在别的地方一直被人为地拖慢 pace，后来看到我们两个，也开始每三周就完成另一个 grade 的 reading。

所以回到 Patrick's Road 后，我非常怀疑这种做法究竟有什么 normal 可言。对 teachers 来说似乎很方便，对孩子们却真是太不方便了。

**Howard:** system 就是那样。幸运的是，你有一年半没受它束缚，也知道原本可能是什么样子。

Pine Community School 的设立，就是想用更 systematic 的方式来 manage 这件事。但后来我们在 teacher 那里遇到了一点问题。

**Michael:** 真的是 teacher 的问题，还是 community 有点失控了？

**Howard:** 有些 parents 对这件事抱有 unrealistic expectations。他们以为自己明白，其实并不明白。我们聘请的 teacher——从 evidence 看，她知道自己在做什么——但她没能足够好地 manage 孩子们的 behaviour。有些 parents 自己就有 behaviour problems。这很遗憾——也是一次从经历中学习的机会。当时我们担心，这可能会在 emotionally 方面影响你和 Stuart。所以才让你们离开。

**Michael:** 我也说不准。最让人有压力的是意识到你和妈妈承受着多大的压力。我能理解原因。school 内部很古怪——parents 对 school 到底该办成什么样子有很多矛盾，这些也反映在孩子们的 behaviour 上。其实对 Stuart 和我的影响还不算太坏，但你们的压力很大。让我们离开绝对是件好事。我记得有个孩子挨了一拳，倒了下去。这就是因为 teacher 没有做好 control——这种事是不能发生的。

**Howard:** 几年前，我去参加了 Pine Community School 的 40th anniversary 活动，而且因为我们在 Samford Commons 举办的一些 workshops，还有 sustainability 方面的事情，我跟那里的 teachers 和 principals 有过不少往来。他们现在办得很好。已经搬到另一个地方了。能再次见到一些老面孔很高兴，还有一些孩子，我已经不太记得他们的样子，却还记得他们的名字。

**Michael:** 爸，我们已经聊了快 90 minutes 了。要不就到这里？

**Howard:** 好。我脑子有点累了。今天就到这里吧。

## Part 4 - Howard Nielsen Interview

## Teaching and Football in the Early 70s

**Michael Nielsen:** All right, so last time we got to—let's see, we were up to basically you were teaching at Clontarf. You'd gotten married, you had a house at Walker's Road. Actually, I think I probably entered the story rather soon after this. I don't know, was there anything else particularly notable?

**Howard:** Not really. In terms of teaching, I left Clontarf in '74—'72, '73, '74—to do a year up at Mount Nebo. So I was principal of the school at Mount Nebo in 1975.

**Michael:** You became a principal very quickly.

**Howard:** Yeah, well, they must have been shorthanded or something. I'm not quite sure. One teacher school. The only thing that happened in between was with football—we won the Premiership in 1973.

**Michael:** Did you start playing at Mayne sort of immediately when you came to Brisbane?

**Howard:** More or less. That's when I decided I didn't want to continue doing tennis tournaments on the circuit or anything like that. I'd finished being a junior, under 19—I missed by about a week or something to stay on as a junior for another year—but decided not to and went to Teachers College.

In my first week or two at college, having been boarding with the O'Sullivans, Terry O'Sullivan became aware that I played Aussie rules. He had a friend at his workplace, Australia Post, who was a Mayne supporter. He said, "Yeah, he comes from South Australia, he can probably play footy." That's a pretty big stretch for anybody, but that's how I came to be at Mayne.

Turns out about a week after I'd signed, my dad, who was at the Grand Hotel at the time, had a friend from Coorparoo AFL who became very interested—in fact they offered me a secondhand car as a bit of an enticement. But I'd already signed with Mayne, so that wasn't going to happen. And Coorparoo was on the other side of the city. So I started playing for Mayne in '69. That was the first year in a long time that Mayne had ever been bottom of the table. Pick your eyes out of that one. So I won the Best and Fairest that year.

**Michael:** You're the best on a bad team, is that what you're saying :-)?

**Howard:** Something like that. There were some good players in the team, but they'd lost quite a few from their previous year and the year before when they'd won the Premiership. A few old hands retired—about three or four of them had played for Queensland, so they were at that level. So they were rebuilding.

**Michael:** What year did you win the Premiership? Was it '73 or '74?

**Howard:** '73. We were in the grand final in '74 as well, but lost that.

## Tommy Blacker and Vietnam

**Michael:** I remember growing up seeing Tommy Blacker and his family. You were obviously pretty good mates. Were you both fullbacks in the Mayne team?

**Howard:** He was a back pocket—the player alongside the full back who looks after the opposition rover, normally a smaller person. That's all changed now, but that's how it was then. I only just met Tommy through footy. He'd played for Mayne previously but had been conscripted to go to Vietnam, so he'd spent a year and a half there.

**Michael:** I remember Tommy always spoke very loudly and was obviously a little bit deaf, which I assume was from artillery in Vietnam.

**Howard:** He was in the artillery section. They would have had headphones and all that, but that probably only minimised the pain, not excluded it.

**Michael:** You weren't drafted, but I presume you knew a lot of people who were. Did it hang over you? Were you sharply aware of it?

**Howard:** The conscription was a lottery. Number 15—15th of January—came up. I was 14th of January, so I missed by one day. At the time you're certainly aware of it. You wait until the lottery happens, and then if you want to avoid conscription, you have to apply. Quite a few people who went to teacher education were allowed to not go. So it was fairly likely I might not have gone anyway. There were also guys from the tennis fraternity who missed even though their number came up.

As you get a bit older you get more of a sense of the meaning of war. In those years there was a buildup of anti-Vietnam sentiment in Australia, so there were a lot more conversations about it. I was glad I didn't go. That's why they're conscripted—because they couldn't rely on people wanting to go.

## Generational Attitudes Toward War

**Michael:** It's such an interesting change from just one generation prior. You were talking about your dad and Uncle Darcy fighting over the ability to go. Was that for World War II or the Korean War?

**Howard:** Second World War. People's attitudes were just very different. In the 30s and 40s, Australia was very attached to Great Britain. Whatever Great Britain requested, Australia sort of did, even though at the beginning of the Second World War Australia wasn't really in any danger—turns out it was later when the Japanese joined.

With Vietnam it was American-led. People hadn't quite gotten on to the idea that it was okay to do what Americans told us to do. There was a certain amount of that, but it was different. There was a lot of fuss about America being in Vietnam, as you'd have seen from movies like Forrest Gump—it captured the animosity pretty well.

**Michael:** My understanding is that Great Britain essentially allowed Singapore to fall, and that had quite an impact on Australian public opinion. There was this felt understanding that they were going to prioritise defense of former Commonwealth states, and then suddenly Australians realised they weren't. It was up to Australia to make its own alliances and fight its own battles.

**Howard:** Even a little bit of that had been born in the First World War. Churchill wasn't exactly top of the pops with Australians because we were seen as cannon fodder coming in from overseas to help the Brits fight a war they'd persuaded Australia had to be fought—same with the Second World War. Australia's never really had a war it's chosen to go into without it being led by another country.

**Michael:** They would have in the case of World War II. It would have been very hard for Australia to maintain neutrality.

**Howard:** I'm not saying they should have been neutral—because of what the Germans were perpetrating and also the Japanese. The Americans came into Europe to help Britain out; Australia had been there all the time. Then they were in more trouble with the Japanese, partly as a result of Britain giving up Singapore. There would have been mixed emotions—probably not from the prime ministers, who stuck to their knitting, but the public might not have.

The thing that was probably of interest to me as about an 11-year-old—we worked on the fruit block and one year, I think 1960, Dad employed a German guy and his wife and two kids as pickers. Erwin Koch was his name, and his wife was Bärbel. He was actually a soldier in the Second World War. I got to know him quite well—we were basically workers together, used to chuck dirt clods at each other. He was probably late 30s.

I remember asking him what it was like to be a soldier. He hated it. He said, "I just hated it. I didn't consciously shoot anybody, but I had to avoid being shot myself." You can understand then why the Germans lost—a lot of the soldiers would have surrendered quite easily rather than getting shot, even though they were in danger of being shot for treason from the German hierarchy.

So I went through the Vietnam conscription phase without event.

## Early Political Involvement

**Michael:** When did you start to get involved in politics? Once you're in the student union, you're already sort of shading into politics. Had you joined the Labour Party at that point?

**Howard:** No, that came later. Interestingly, I did apply for the job as General Secretary of the Teachers' Union after only one year of teaching. When I told a couple of people, they raised their eyebrows and said I had no chance in hell of getting that job—which I didn't, because it was already set up within the union movement. So I was interested in representing and advocating, but I also enjoyed teaching, so I didn't pursue it.

I first joined the Labour Party when I was married. I met Labour Party guys during an election campaign to get rid of Joh, I think it was. I ended up joining the party, probably in my mid-twenties. Just started going to meetings of the Labour Party locally at Arana Hills.

I wasn't ever really enamoured with the idea of factions in the party. It was unrealistic to think you would hold any powerful position without being a member of a faction. I had an interesting time with people wanting me to join their faction and me deciding not to. Another guy and I started something called the Forum — basically a group of people who didn't want to belong to a faction but were happy to be part of a forum, as long as they weren't tied to any decision that came out of it. It was an opportunity to discuss issues without fear or favour.

Wayne Swan became interested in the concept. He was Secretary of the Labour Party in Queensland at the time. He sent a couple of his factional people to our meetings. One of them disclosed to me later that he was there because Wayne Swan wanted to find out what we were doing.

## Running for Pre-selection

**Howard:** When I started running as a candidate, the only places I was likely to run were seats that were unlikely to be won by Labour. In one, the seat of Petrie or Dixon, I ran for pre-selection in one that was borderline.

The way it works is you have a plebiscite with Labour Party members who live in that electorate. Then you go to a central forum, which is all factionally organised. The factional organisation gets three times the value of the local vote. But I ran a really strong campaign on the ground and actually won the local pre-selection plebiscite.

That caused a bit of a stir. People wanted to know how that happened—it meant quite a few Labour Party members in the electorate who were supposedly part of a faction didn't vote for the faction. They voted for me. Because it was a secret ballot, nobody could prove anything. Whereas at the centralised pre-selection, all the state representatives were factional operatives. Everybody knew who was voting for who.

It was so terrible that when it came to voting, they didn't personally vote. They handed their voting paper to a factional leader—they couldn't vote secretly, couldn't vote the way they wanted. They had to vote according to their faction.

I ended up losing by just one vote or one and a half votes because the value of my local plebiscite was pretty high—I got 58%, whereas the main faction there would normally have gotten about 52 or 53%. They said I was the first person in about ten years to actually win a local plebiscite against a faction in a winnable seat.

**Michael:** It helps me understand something I find quite strange in the United States. So many people look at Trump and his cronies and are quite rightly alarmed. They think, "How can he possibly have won?" But if you look across the aisle, the Democrats have a different set of problems, but they're pretty bad. It's as though the Democratic Party has been captured from the inside—a lot of inside baseball that makes them not particularly palatable to the electorate.

I remember Queensland politics in the 80s. You had this obviously pretty terrible premier, Joh Bjelke-Petersen, but then an opposition that didn't seem able to get its act together either—maybe for similar reasons, captured from the inside by factions more interested in their own games than winning elections.

**Howard:** The leader of the party at the time, a guy called Bill something or other, was totally factional, union-oriented, very strict. He had full control. That's how he wanted it.

I realised that during the pre-selection process. I rang a lot of people between the local vote and the centralised vote and realised I was done like a dinner. It was also the time of the refugee issues and vote boat people stuff that the Labour Party wasn't handling well, and the environmental stuff was nothing to write home about. So I thought I might as well go with a bit of a bang.

At the centralised vote you get a chance to give a five-minute speech. I decided to tell them the way it was for me.

I said, "I joined the party because of a book called The Light on the Hill, which I still believe in, which the Labour Party is based on. I don't see too much light at the moment, but I do see a big hill."

"I've got a couple of kids at home. They're going to want to know how I went today. At the moment, what I've got to tell them is that there's a senior person in the Labour Party who has full power over people like you sitting in this audience—you can't even vote for yourself in this pre-selection. Can you?"

A whole lot of eyes lowered.

"There's a person I know very well, Rosemary Hume, who was one of my competitors, and I know she had factional support. She's outside this room right now waiting for her turn, and I know she can hear what I'm saying. So I invite her to come into the room now and say she's going to withdraw from this process."

You should have seen people looking at the door. They knew she wouldn't, and I knew she wouldn't.

"I want to be able to tell my kids that actually it isn't that bad, that there are some good people around."

Then I looked at Bill and said, "Are you able to now say that anybody in this room can vote however they want? There's not going to be a collection of voting slips? I'll wait for an answer."

Of course I lost. But a couple of people said to me afterwards, "That was amazing. You've got no idea how that will affect people in this room. It's making us think seriously about our role in the party."

That's a little bit like the Democrats—rationally, there's no way they shouldn't be in power more or less all the time, apart from their mistakes and the stupid way they go about decisions. Policy-wise it's hard to say exactly because of biases. But the dollar makes the difference in America, much more than in Australia.

At least in Australia, people can rise from humble beginnings to become a member of parliament or even prime minister. They don't get there without support from others—not born out of their own richness.

**Michael:** Jimmy Carter was a peanut farmer. Barack Obama's beginnings were fairly humble too—more a community organiser and academic.

**Howard:** That's true.

## Becoming a Principal

**Michael:** Why did you want to become a principal? A lot of teachers go their entire career and don't want to be a principal.

**Howard:** I like interesting things to happen. That can happen in a classroom—you can be a teacher your entire life and have a very interesting experience because you'll make it that way with the kids you're teaching.

But I realised from my role at college as student union president, and then the national network running workshops and conferences, that I probably had a capacity to get people engaged in worthwhile conversations that would be energising and powerful in terms of benefits for the community. One-Teacher Schools appealed to me as a good idea—it'd be fun meeting different parents.

I didn't know my first appointment was going to be Mount Nebo, which was probably the best One-Teacher School job in Queensland, as my mate said.

I did well there—being assessed by inspectors, working with the community. We ran a big pioneer fair that year, like hundreds of people coming up to Mount Nebo for a weekend fair focused on the natural environment. They'd never had anything like that before. That was fun.

You've got autonomy to make changes if you're the principal. If you're a teacher, you might not have that autonomy depending on your principal. But more than that, I realised I seem to have some natural capacity to facilitate conversations and get people to agree to do things they otherwise might not think they can do. It energises people.

**Michael:** What's an example from that time?

**Howard:** The pioneer fair. I was talking with a parent one day and asked, "Have you ever had a fete or fair up here?" "I don't know." "We should do that. What would you do if you were going to do it?" One thing led to another and we did it.

I had the advantage that the principal before me had been there for 20 years and had outworn his welcome probably 15 years earlier. There was bad blood between some parents. I just ignored what had happened before and treated it as a new good environment, which it was. The parents were really good—but a few of them had been treated not so well.

**Michael:** Mount Nebo seems like quite a remarkable community—a lot of idealistic people, people with unusual ideas about the world. Not everybody necessarily practical.

**Howard:** There were quite a few interesting characters. Among the parent group you had a solicitor and a retired architect at age 35—people who'd come to Mount Nebo to get out of the rat race. We became quite good friends with a couple of them.

## A Different Approach in the Classroom

**Howard:** Just about what you can do in a classroom—I remember something that worked really well at Clontarf Primary School when I had the big double teaching space. We had 66 students with two teachers.

I suggested, and my co-teacher agreed as long as I organised it, that we construct the classroom as a city. On a monthly basis the various groupings—I think there were six groups of about ten—could nominate and elect their own councillor to represent their group's views.

Each month a new councillor was elected with responsibility to represent their group and conduct discussions. Then each month one of them would be elected mayor for a month.

It worked really well. Kids who hadn't normally been seen as leaders, when they got to their position of being a leader, they surprised a lot of other kids about how good and responsible they could be if given some responsibility.

A lot of one-teacher schools didn't really do that, but in a big classroom like that, I thought it would be worthwhile. Which it was.

**Michael:** My memory is that you had very firm control over the school. There was no doubt who was running things. But within that, there was quite a lot of autonomy for people to make decisions—as long as you didn't do something stupid.

## Moving to Croydon

**Michael:** My memory of Evergreen was that it was a relatively peaceful, relatively functional community—a little bit of drama, but at the level you get with any group of people. Whereas then we moved to Croydon, and it was very evidently a huge jump in dysfunction. Even the road up was quite an adventure.

**Howard:** We were advised not to drive up necessarily, but to have our car put on a train to Cairns and then drive from Cairns. What happened was the car had all its tires punctured during the train trip. We had to get them fixed in Cairns, then drove through a couple of inland towns to Croydon.

There was 150 kilometres of dirt road with rocks. A rock went through our windscreen—it shouldn't have because we had a windscreen protector, but it just skimmed over the top. We drove probably the last 50 kilometres without a windscreen into Croydon. Not exactly the best beginning.

**Michael:** What was your first impression of Croydon?

**Howard:** Pretty much what I thought it would be. An isolated town, a pub, a history bigger than it was then, so consolidated building structures. There was a courthouse, a police station. The school was relatively new—not the original school.

Little known fact: Wendy's great-grandfather or grandfather was the principal of Croydon School way back in the 1890s or 1880s. There were probably a thousand kids at the three or four schools in Croydon at the time because of the gold rush.

**Michael:** It peaked at like 10,000 people, and then it was like 250 by the time you were there?

**Howard:** The whole Croydon Shire, which was as big as Victoria, had a voting population of 150. So probably about 300 people including kids.

The publican was a nice guy. They'd been in trouble with the last mayoral election—somebody tried to burn his pub down. Then the flying doctor came in the first night we were there. Some guy was drunk, going out of the pub, cracked his head on a curbing. They had to call the flying doctor because there was a hospital but no doctor, and they didn't have proper X-ray machinery.

That was our first day.

**Howard:** We stayed at the hotel that night because the truckies who brought our furniture had just dumped it all inside the school residence and backed it up against the wall so you couldn't get in properly. We had to go back the next day to get it fixed up. But staying at the pub was okay.

## Teaching Football to Kids Who'd Never Played

**Howard:** I was only there for six months. You were only there for probably three or four months. I taught the kids how to play Aussie rules. The AFL had sent nice videos, sets of footy jumpers, football boots, and footballs. We taught the kids well enough to go into Cairns and play against a Cairns team. The girls played softball, the boys played Aussie rules. They'd never played before.

**Michael:** That must have been incredible for those kids.

**Howard:** At least half of them had never seen water—sea waves, water in the Pacific. Some might have seen it at Normanton in the Gulf Country, but quite a few hadn't.

We stayed overnight in the library of a school that we had contact with. They allowed us to sleep there for two nights. We had to get there—it's a long trip, eight hours I think.

Normally under school rules parents would take the kids with permission, or you'd get a bus. There was no bus. One parent said, "We can put them in the back of a truck." So about seven or eight kids went in the back of a five-ton truck, and the rest went in cars or utes.

I said to the parents at the P&C meeting, "I'm going to need you to sign off on this because this is not according to the rules. If you want to do it and take that risk—and I'm sure this is not new for you guys because you go camping and do that."

That was a bit of a risk. It would never happen now.

The main reason wasn't just to play football or softball—it was to have that experience of seeing Cairns.

We played against what turned out to be the primary school rep team from Cairns. I'd told the Cairns teachers to not put us against their best team because these kids had never had an actual game before. They forgot about that bit.

I didn't tell the Croydon kids what had happened. They just went out to play. We got done by about eight goals to one, but they acquitted themselves really well.

Except—there was one young kid, an Aboriginal kid, Jackie. He was a bit temperamental. Very early in the piece, he had the ball and a kid from Cairns tackled him to the ground as you're allowed to do. The other guy got a free kick. Jackie went berserk. He said, "What's this guy tackling me?" and belted the kid in the head.

I said, "He's allowed to do that, and you're not allowed to punch him in the head." A little bit of on-the-job learning.

I happened to be on the sideline not far away, so I intervened. The umpire understood what was going on. A memorable event.

All the kids got to have a swim. There must have been a salt water pool close to the beach, given the baddies in the water up there.
