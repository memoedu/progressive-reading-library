## 正文

_（注：我在 2 个月前就把这篇文章发布到了 LessWrong，但忘了点击这里的发布按钮——哎呀！）_

当我有一堆 tasks 要做时，常常会因为 indecision 而动弹不得——真不知道该从哪里开始！我通常的 solution 是把这些 tasks 按 priority order 进行 **sorting**——一般是隐含地使用 comparison-based sorting method，逐一查看 list，把每个新 item 放到正确的 position。不过，最近我通过 **rating** 获得了出乎意料的大幅 improvement——给每个 item 的 priority 打一个满分为 10 的 score，然后按 score 的 order 来做。我不知道这对其他人能在多大程度上 generalises，但还是想用这篇短文说说为什么这种 method 更好，没准有人也有共鸣！（注——这篇文章对简单想法进行了大量 over-analysis！继续阅读，后果自负）

**理由 1：** 我避免去想那些最难的 comparisons

与 standard computer-implemented sorting algorithms 中的情况不同，对任意一对 tasks 进行 comparing 所花的时间 _并不_ 相同。大多数 comparisons 都是 obvious 的，但对 close tasks 进行 comparing 就感觉困难得多，也更加 ambiguous。在实际进行 priority ordering 时，把一个 task 放在 similar tasks 之间恰好正确的位置，才是最耗费时间和注意力的事情，也是最容易让我泄气的地方。

相比之下，对任何 task 进行 Rating 都感觉很容易——我只需几秒钟，就能把 gut feel convert 成一个 number。如果感觉 ambiguous，比如我无法在 7 和 8 之间作决定，那就直接填 7.5！Ratings 可以是 continuous 的。然后，如果最后有几个 tasks 的 rating 相同，我就可以 arbitrarily tie break（例如，按它们被录入 list 的 order）——如果它们的 rating 相同，那么进一步 prioritising 也不可能有 _那么_ 大的用处！回头来看，最难的 comparisons 其实是最没用的——如果我先做 fourth most important task，再做 third most important，那没什么大不了的；但如果先做 tenth，再做 first，那就搞砸了！

**理由 2：** Rating 意味着只需查看 list 一遍，而 sorting 则要来回查看。

我发现，这让事情快了很多，cognitively 也更轻松——要把每个 task sort 到 list 中，我每处理一个 item 都需要回头重读，找到正确的 position，这会分散注意力，也会打断 flow。而进行 rating 时，我只需读一遍，in isolation 考虑每个 task

**技术旁注（Technical aside）：** 我在 comparing 的是 insertion sort 和 bucket sort：前者是 quadratic time，后者是 linear time，但 constant 更差。不过，我认为 time complexity 在这里反而会分散注意力。我很少对包含 >20 个 elements 的 lists 进行 priority order。更复杂的 O(nlog(n)) sorting algorithms，例如 merge 或 quick sort，或许会更好，但 overhead 似乎太高，不值得费心考虑

**示例（Examples）**：我发现自己需要 _非常频繁_ 地对 tasks 进行 priority order，常常一天要做好几次，所以更擅长这件事对我很有价值！例如，每天开始时对 tasks 进行 ordering、processing 我的 to-do list、决定在 coaching session 中 focus on 我的哪些 bugs、决定在会议中讨论什么、规划接下来几周要 research 什么，等等。“以 10 分为满分对 priority 进行 Rate”在每种情境下的含义有所不同，但我通常都很容易有相应的 gut feel。只要有 _任何_ order（哪怕是 first-come-first-served 这样的 arbitrary order！），就能突破不知从何开始的 indecisiveness，而这所带来的价值大得出人意料。不过，由于我的时间通常有限，不可能把所有事情都做完，因此有一个粗略的 priority order 就是显著的 upgrade。

## 原文

_(Note: I published this on LessWrong 2 months ago, but forgot to click post on here - oops!)_

I often get paralysed by indecision when I have a bunch of tasks to do - it’s hard to figure out where to start! My standard solution is **sorting** the tasks into priority order - usually implicitly with a comparison-based sorting method where I go through the list one-by-one and put each new item in the right position. But I’ve recently gotten a surprisingly large improvement by **rating** - giving each item a score out of 10 for priority, and going in order by score. I have no idea how much this generalises to other people, but I’m sharing this quick post making the case for why this method is better in case anyone else vibes! (note - this post contains major amounts of over-analysis of simple ideas! Read on at your own risk)

**Reason 1:** I avoid thinking about the hardest comparisons

Unlike in standard computer-implemented sorting algorithms, comparing any pair of tasks does _not_ take the same amount of time. Most comparisons are obvious, while comparing close tasks feels much harder and more ambiguous. In practice, putting a task in exactly the right place among similar tasks is what absorbs the most time and attention when priority ordering, and is where I most easily get discouraged.

Rating, in comparison, feels easy for any task - I can just convert my gut feel into a number in a few seconds. If it feels ambiguous, eg I can’t decide between a 7 or 8, I just put a 7.5! Ratings can be continuous. And then, if I end up with several tasks with the same rating, I can just arbitrarily tie break (eg, by the order that they were entered into the list) - if they have the same rating, then prioritising further can’t be _that_ useful! In hindsight, the hardest comparisons are in fact the least useful - if I do the fourth most important task before the third most important, that’s no big deal, but doing the tenth before the first is a screw-up!

**Reason 2:** Rating means going through the list once, sorting goes back-and-forth.

I find that this makes things much faster, and cognitively easier - to sort each task into the list I need to read back through for each item to find the right position, which is distracting and flow-breaking. While for rating, I just read through once and consider each task in isolation

**Technical aside:** I’m comparing insertion sort, which is quadratic time, with bucket sort, which is linear time with a worse constant, but I think time complexity is a distraction here. I rarely priority order lists with >20 elements. Fancier O(nlog(n)) sorting algorithms like merge or quick sort might be better, but seem too high overhead to be worth worrying about

**Examples**: I find that I need to priority order tasks _lot_, often multiple times a day, so getting better at this is valuable to me! Eg ordering tasks at the start of each day, processing my to-do list, deciding which of my bugs to focus on in a coaching session, deciding what to discuss in a meeting, planning what to research over the next few weeks, etc. “Rate out of 10 for priority” differs in meaning in each context, but I normally have some easy corresponding gut feel. There’s a surprising amount of value in having _any_ order at all (even an arbitrary one like first-come-first-served!) to break past indecisiveness about where to start. But since I generally have limited time and won’t get to everything, having a rough priority order is a significant upgrade.
