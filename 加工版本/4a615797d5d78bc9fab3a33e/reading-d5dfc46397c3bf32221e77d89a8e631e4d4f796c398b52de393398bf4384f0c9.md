# Intuition for the utility of finance

---

## 正文

在 effective altruists 之间，以及更广泛的人群中，偶尔会有人争论：在 financial sector 工作是否会造成 harm，如果会，又会造成多少。

人们很容易得出 finance 根本没有帮助的 conclusion，因为事情看起来是 zero-sum：如果我向你 sell 一份 Google 的 share，那么无论我以什么 price 把它 sold 给你，wealth 的 total amount 都 stays the same。按照这个 logic，如果我通过 trading Google shares 来 make money，我不过是在从那些 trading 自己的 Google shares 时不如我聪明的人那里 taking 它。

不过，这忽略了一个重要的 consideration，那就是 _不同的人可以对同一个 asset 赋予不同的 values_。举一个简单的 example，就在 Google IPO 之后，Larry Page 对自己最后一份 Google 的 share 所 valued 的数额，可能远低于它以 cash 计的 price，因为他 held 的 Google shares 如此之多，以至于他对它们 price falling 的 risk 是 highly exposed 的。如果 Page 是 risk-averse，他可能会强烈倾向于持有 cash，而不是 Google shares，即使它们的“market value”是相同的。

对于 clothes 这样的 physical goods，人们似乎愿意接受这个 argument；很少有人会因为 seller 和 buyer 的 assets 的 value 在 transaction 后 unchanged，就主张 garment-selling 是 zero-sum。但对于 financial transactions，这个 argument 却一直存在。

这或许是因为人们低估了 liquidity 的重要性——也就是在你想要 money 时能拿到它的能力。正如 [Arnold Kling 所 points out 的](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2008/12/lectures_on_mac_6.html)，banking financial 的目的，是把由其 borrowers 的 payments 所 backed 的 illiquid、risky assets（例如 mortgages），turn 成其 investors 想要的 liquid、non-risky assets（例如 bank deposits）。US 的 financial system 已经足够 well-developed，以至于我们几乎把拥有所需的足够 liquidity 视为理所当然。但 _Poor Economics_ 提供了一些极好的 examples，说明当 banking system 不存在时，人们多么迫切地需要它：

-   Chennai 的 Fruit vendors 显然以几乎 _per day_ 5% 的 rate 向他们的 wholesaler borrow。（其实，仔细想想，这实在令人难以置信，以至于我怀疑它实际上是另一种 cost，只是因为某种 pricing game 而被称为“interest”。）

-   Pakistan 的 Rural moneylenders 所 charge 的 average 是 78 percent，尽管 default rates 只有 2 percent（不过，还有更多款项是 repaid late 的）。spread 似乎大多用于 overhead 和 debt enforcement costs。显然，这使除了最 extremely productive 的 investments 之外的所有 investments 都变得 impossible。

-   bank savings accounts 的 overhead 如此 immense，以至于 banks 需要 charge withdrawal fees 来弥补，这使许多穷人无法承担这一 cost。他们转而求助于“rotating savings and credit associations”之类的办法：一群人定期聚在一起，每个人都向一个 common pot 进行 contributes，然后由一个人（轮流）拿走整个 pot。

显然，这并不是对 on the margin 的 banking activity 的 justification：US 距离上面讨论的任何一种情形都相当遥远。但这表明，仅仅对 risk 和 maturity 进行 shuffling around，就可能具有远超人们直觉所想的 value。

## 原文

There’s occasional debate among effective altruists, and among people more generally, about whether working in the financial sector does harm, and if so how much.

It’s easy to come to the conclusion that finance isn’t helpful at all, because things look zero-sum: if I sell you a share of Google, the total amount of wealth stays the same no matter what price I sold it to you at. By this logic, if I make money trading Google shares, I’m just taking it from other people who were less clever than me about trading their Google shares.

This neglects an important consideration, though, which is that _different people can place different values on the same asset_. As a trivial example, just after the Google IPO, Larry Page might have valued his last share of Google much less than its price in cash, because he held so many Google shares that he was highly exposed to the risk of their price falling. If Page was risk-averse, he might have strongly preferred to have cash instead of Google shares, even if their “market value” was the same.

People seem to be willing to buy this argument for physical goods like clothes; few people argue that garment-selling is zero-sum because the value of the seller’s and buyer’s assets are unchanged after the transaction. But the argument persists for financial transactions.

This may be because people underestimate the importance of liquidity—that is, ability to get your money when you want it. As [Arnold Kling points out](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2008/12/lectures_on_mac_6.html), the point of a banking financial is to turn illiquid, risky assets backed by payments from its borrowers (e.g., mortgages) into liquid, non-risky assets that its investors want (e.g., bank deposits). The financial system in the US is sufficiently well-developed that we practically take it for granted that we have as much liquidity as we need. But _Poor Economics_ provides some excellent examples of how badly people need a banking system when it’s not there:

-   Fruit vendors in Chennai apparently borrow at a rate of almost 5% _per day_ from their wholesaler. (Actually, thinking about it, this is so unbelievable that I suspect it’s secretly another cost that gets called “interest” because of some kind of pricing game.)

-   Rural moneylenders in Pakistan charge an average of 78 percent although default rates are only 2 percent (though many more are repaid late). The spread appears to go mostly to overhead and debt enforcement costs. Obviously this makes all but the most extremely productive investments impossible.

-   The overhead for bank savings accounts is so immense that banks need to charge withdrawal fees to make up for it, making the cost prohibitive for many of the poor. Instead, they resort to things like “rotating savings and credit associations”, where a group of people get together regularly, everyone contributes something to a common pot, and then one person (on a rotating basis) takes the whole pot.

Obviously, this is not a justification of banking activity on the margin: the US is quite far away from any of the situations discussed above. But it suggests that simply shuffling around risk and maturity can have much more value than one might intuitively think.
