## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

我最喜欢的媒体作品之一，是 Carl Sagan 关于“the Pale Blue Dot”（PBD）photograph 的著名独白。它的文字很精彩，但由 Sagan 亲口讲述、配上那张照片时，尤其动人：

我喜欢 PBD 的 image 和 text，虽然这里不打算赘述它的优点，但我已经[在别处详尽地写过这些](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html)。在这篇札记里，我想转而讨论 text 中两个明显的问题，以及这些问题能否、又该如何解决。

第一个问题是 nihilism。Sagan 几乎直截了当地说，我们 human beings 并不怎么 matter。我们是 tiny 且 inconsequential 的。那些 generals 和 emperors——为了一个 dot 的 fraction 而 fight，是多么愚蠢。他在 diminishing 通过 violence 所能赢得的东西，以此说明人们不该试图通过 violence 获胜。我们喜欢这个 conclusion，因此很容易接受这条 line of argument。但如果认真看待它，那就意味着我们做的事几乎都不 matter。毕竟，如果想成为一个 dot 的 fraction 的 momentary master 是愚蠢的，那么，担心自己有没有工作、伴侣是否幸福、孩子的学业、如何烹制烤牛肉，或者其他几乎任何事情，岂不是更加愚蠢。

当然，仅仅因为我不喜欢这个 nihilistic conclusion，并不意味着它就是 wrong。也许 nihilism 是 correct 的！也许我们做的任何事情确实都不怎么 matter。但至少，我认为这个 implied conclusion 让我们值得考虑，text 中是否还有其他问题。

第二个问题是 internal inconsistency：在强烈 implied 了 life 并不怎么 matter 之后，Sagan 却这样收尾：“也许没有什么比这幅展示我们 tiny world 的遥远 image，更能 demonstration 出 human conceits 的 folly。对我而言，它 underscores 了我们的 responsibility：更 kindly 地对待彼此，并 preserve 和 cherish 这个 pale blue dot，这是我们迄今所知唯一的家园。”

这很说不通。Sagan 实际上既在说“嘿，我们是 tiny 而 insignificant 的，所以以 power 和 glory 为目标并没有道理”，又用这一点来说，我们需要更 kinder 地对待彼此。他想两头兼得。如果 winning glory 因为发生在非常 small scale 上而不 matter，那么 loving each other 也不怎么 matter。到底是哪一种？

喜欢 Pale Blue Dot 的人往往不太喜欢这个 observation。他们想替 Sagan 开脱。当然，如果你创造性地 reinterpret 他的 meaning，或者诉诸 special pleading，你是可以这样做的。但无论 Pale Blue Dot 多么美——而且我确实觉得它壮丽非凡——它还是在一些基本问题上弄错了。

那么，该怎么办？我有点想举起双手，说一句“Sagan 搞错了”，然后气冲冲地走开。但我认为 Sagan 有很多地方说对了，而且它[有着令人惊叹的洞见](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html)。它指向某种 deep 而 true 的东西，而 Sagan 已经接近将其表达出来。与其替他开脱或气冲冲地走开，我们不如问：什么是我们能够保留的？它又在指向什么？

归根结底，我认为正确的 approach 相当明显：尽可能深刻地理解，在 cosmos 的 scale 上，我们确实是 tiny creatures；但仍然对我们 actions 中的 deep meaning 保持信心。这些 actions 是 matter 的，无论是我们的 crimes 还是 kindnesses。认为 power 和 war 不 matter，是一个 mistake。那些 generals 和 emperors 并不是因为在 small arena 中行动才 wrong。他们在 grossly violate 其他 human beings 时才是 wrong 的，而这正是因为 human beings 确实 matter。事实上，他们是在一个非常 important arena 中行动，即 human experience 的 arena。Judaism、Christianity 和 Islam 的 scriptures 在表达以下意思的种种变体时，都说对了：“谁 destroys 一个 life，谁就 destroys 一整个 world；谁 saves 一个 life，谁就 saves 一整个 world”。

让我们对 PBD text 做一次 detailed critique。我知道这有些冒昧，但我想 identify 其中我觉得 correct 的部分。开头极其出色。它确实将我们的 everyday experience——我们的 individual lives、今天活着的全部 8 billion 人、曾经活过的 110 billion 人——与那个 tiny point of pale light 联系了起来：

> 从这个遥远的 vantage point 看去，Earth 也许并不显得有什么特别值得关注的地方。
> 
> 但对我们来说，情况不同。再看看那个 dot。那就是这里。那就是家园。那就是我们。你爱的每个人、你认识的每个人、你听说过的每个人、曾经存在过的每一个 human being，都在那里度过自己的一生。我们的 joy 和 suffering 的 aggregate，数以千计满怀确信的 religions、ideologies 和 economic doctrines，每一个 hunter 和 forager，每一个 hero 和 coward，每一个 civilization 的 creator 和 destroyer，每一个 king 和 peasant，每一对相爱的年轻伴侣，每一位母亲和父亲、满怀希望的孩子、inventor 和 explorer，每一位 morals 的 teacher，每一个 corrupt politician，每一个“superstar”、每一个“supreme leader”，我们 species 的 history 中的每一个 saint 和 sinner，都生活在那里——生活在一粒 suspended in a sunbeam 的 mote of dust 上。

我对下一段稍有疑虑。但仔细想来，这些话大体是 correct 的。我想提出两点温和的 critiques。首先，这里已经有些 insinuation——后来表达得更加 explicit——认为 generals 和 emperors 的 triumphs 并不 matter。但事实上，它们确实 matter[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn1)。如我前面所说，如果它们不 matter，那我们做的任何事情都不 matter。其次，与其说这是强烈的 critique，不如说是句题外话：当讨论的是一个 very-nearly-spherical object 时，谈论“这个 pixel 的一个 corner”有些不协调。不过，这一段总体而言非常好。

> Earth 是 vast cosmic arena 中一个非常 small 的舞台。想想那些 generals 和 emperors 为了在 glory 和 triumph 中成为一个 dot 的 fraction 的 momentary masters，所造成的血流成河。想想这个 pixel 某个 corner 的 inhabitants 对另一个 corner 那些 scarcely distinguishable 的 inhabitants 施加的无尽 cruelties，想想他们的 misunderstandings 多么频繁，他们多么渴望 kill one another，他们的 hatreds 又多么炽烈。

下一段有很多问题：

> 我们的 posturings、我们想象中的 self-importance、我们认为自己在 Universe 中拥有某种 privileged position 的 delusion，都受到了这点 pale light 的 challenged。我们的 planet 是 great enveloping cosmic dark 中一个 lonely speck。在我们的 obscurity 中，在这一切 vastness 之中，没有任何迹象表明，会有来自别处的援助，将我们从自身手中拯救出来。

这与后面关于“human conceits 的 folly”的评论有关。其 implication 是，human beings 并不 important。但我们_确实_ important。你既可以理解，我们生活在“great enveloping cosmic dark 中的一个 lonely speck”上，_也_可以相信 human lives 极其 matter。当然，对于这个问题，人们所持的 positions 可能有的更 healthy，有的没那么 healthy——很多人几乎没有_足够_的观念，却对 importance 有着 elevated need，而这导致了大量 violence 和 pursuit of glory。但 dismiss 我们的 importance 是一个严重的 mistake。我们可以选择相信自身的 meaning。

这一段最后一句没有问题——它衔接到下一段，后者优美地提醒我们，humanity 承担着 stewardship，与 text 的其余部分略有不同：

> Earth 是迄今已知唯一 harbor life 的 world。至少在不久的将来，我们的 species 没有其他地方可以 migrate。Visit，可以。Settle，还不行。不管愿不愿意，眼下 Earth 就是我们坚守的地方。

最后一段又令人困扰：

> 有人说，astronomy 是一种 humbling 且 character-building 的 experience。也许没有什么比这幅展示我们 tiny world 的遥远 image，更能 demonstration 出 human conceits 的 folly。对我而言，它 underscores 了我们的 responsibility：更 kindly 地对待彼此，并 preserve 和 cherish 这个 pale blue dot，这是我们迄今所知唯一的家园。

第一句话可以说是 true 的，但以我的口味来看，它有点过于像 astronomy agitprop 了（抱歉，Carl！）。第二句话是 false 的。事实上，许多更为直接而具体的 errors，都能更好地 illustrated 出 human conceits 的 folly。而 text 中没有任何内容 underscores 我们更 kindly 地对待彼此的 responsibility。argument 是什么？因为我们是 tiny 的？ants 因为 small，就必须 kindly 地对待彼此吗？amoeba 又如何？这里没有 argument，只有一种巧妙营造的氛围。那个 _conclusion_ 很可能是 true 的——我当然相信它——但这个 argument 并没有 show 这一点。确实，这里根本没有 argument。

如果你围绕 correct 的 elements 重新组织 text，得到的就是：

> 从这个遥远的 vantage point 看去，Earth 也许并不显得有什么特别值得关注的地方。
> 
> 但对我们来说，情况不同。再看看那个 dot。那就是这里。那就是家园。那就是我们。你爱的每个人、你认识的每个人、你听说过的每个人、曾经存在过的每一个 human being，都在那里度过自己的一生。我们的 joy 和 suffering 的 aggregate，数以千计满怀确信的 religions、ideologies 和 economic doctrines，每一个 hunter 和 forager，每一个 hero 和 coward，每一个 civilization 的 creator 和 destroyer，每一个 king 和 peasant，每一对相爱的年轻伴侣，每一位母亲和父亲、满怀希望的孩子、inventor 和 explorer，每一位 morals 的 teacher，每一个 corrupt politician，每一个“superstar”、每一个“supreme leader”，我们 species 的 history 中的每一个 saint 和 sinner，都生活在那里——生活在一粒 suspended in a sunbeam 的 mote of dust 上。
> 
> Earth 是 vast cosmic arena 中一个非常 small 的舞台。想想那些 generals 和 emperors 为了在 glory 和 triumph 中成为一个 dot 的 fraction 的 momentary masters，所造成的血流成河。想想这个 pixel 某个 corner 的 inhabitants 对另一个 corner 那些 scarcely distinguishable 的 inhabitants 施加的无尽 cruelties，想想他们的 misunderstandings 多么频繁，他们多么渴望 kill one another，他们的 hatreds 又多么炽烈。
> 
> 我们的 planet 是 great enveloping cosmic dark 中一个 lonely speck。在我们的 obscurity 中，在这一切 vastness 之中，没有任何迹象表明，会有来自别处的援助，将我们从自身手中拯救出来。Earth 是迄今已知唯一 harbor life 的 world。至少在不久的将来，我们的 species 没有其他地方可以 migrate。Visit，可以。Settle，还不行。不管愿不愿意，眼下 Earth 就是我们坚守的地方。对我而言，它 underscores 了我们 preserve 和 cherish 这个 pale blue dot 的 responsibility，这是我们迄今所知唯一的家园。

虽然仍有各种小问题，但这大体是 correct 的。我仍然觉得它优美而激动人心，但必须承认：它没那么激动人心了。它不再那样充满 human meaning，不再 condemning 我们 fighting 的愚蠢，也不再 exhorting 我们保持 kindness。标准版本似乎为这些 conclusions 提供了 cosmic justifications。遗憾的是，那些 cosmic justifications 只是言辞上的障眼法。把它们剥除后，剩下的东西看起来就没有那么多 meaning 了。

完整的 PBD text 与 Abrahamic religious texts 之间，有一个引人注目的 parallel。religious texts 中的一条 implied argument 是：“God created 了你和 Universe，所以当祂说你应该这样或那样行事时，你确实应该认真听取。”这是一条从 cosmology 通向 individual action 的 argument，在这个意义上，它与 PBD text 是 similar 的。而当你意识到两者都基于 false premise 时，两者都会受损。Abrahamic 的那些 texts 是因为其 cosmology 是 wrong 的，这 undermining 了 God 决定 human behaviour 的 authority。而 PBD text 则是因为，从 cosmos 通向 human meaning 的 argument 在很大程度上是 illusory 的。

我们能否 recover PBD 试图建立的、cosmos 与 individual meaning 之间的美妙 connection？我想我们很可能可以。我不会在这里详细尝试。但我相信，关键在于将这种 big-picture cosmic view 与源于同一 underlying reality 的其他 points of view 加以 juxtapose 和 synthesize。Pale Blue Dot _以及_ tree of life _以及_ 我们 brain 中的 100 trillion connections _以及_ human experience 和 human possibility 的种种奇妙 varieties。所有这些事物都 express 着一种 single underlying unity，一种我们最终可以渴望 apprehend 的 unity，一种与 universe 的 deep communion，同时也是 self 与 humanity 的 deepening。Hume 说得对，ought 并不 entirely arise out of is，但对 is 的丰富 appreciation 无疑会使它变得深刻得多、深刻得多。

我不会在这里尝试实行这个 program。我只是在指出我认为比 PDB text 更好的 goal 和 strategy，却没有真正付出 implement 它所需的艰苦努力！不过，我确实想指出这种尝试中的几个 challenges。在 historian Edwin Burtt 的著作《The Metaphysical Foundations of Modern Science》[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn2) 中，Burtt 提出，modern science 导致了一场巨大的 transition：从 human-and-God-centered 的 conception of reality，转向 cosmos-centered 的 reality，其中 humans 是 peripheral。这场 transition（连同 economic specialization）在 human condition 中造成了巨大的 alienation。这种 alienation 体现在 physicist Steve Weinberg 等人的话语中：“universe 越显得 comprehensible，它也就越显得 pointless”[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn3)。

Sagan 努力寻求、也几乎领会到的，是一种将 cosmic 与 human、scale 与 meaning 加以 connecting 的方式。但这种 connection 不能仅仅在 size 中找到。它存在于 structure、relationship、pattern 和 depth 中。一种 healthier view 是某种 long zoom：从我们生活 intricate 的 interior 向外移动，直至 cosmos 的 vastness，将我们内在的 complexity 与外在的 complexity 加以 linking。这意味着 understanding 我们的 3 billion base pairs、我们的 100 trillion synapses，以及 human experience 的全部 variety 和 possibility；并将这些与 culture、biosphere 和 cosmos 的 layered structures 加以 connecting。Meaning 存在于这些 layers 中，也存在于它们 interconnect 的方式中。是的，你想要与 unself 达到 deep communion；但它必须 grounded in 对家人、朋友以及自己所处地方和时代的爱。你是一个 pan-African plains ape——同时也是 artist、scientist、poet 和 explorer。凭借所有这些身份，你能够 apprehend cosmos-in-the-small 和 cosmos-in-the-large。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

One of my favourite pieces of media is Carl Sagan's famous monologue about "the Pale Blue Dot" (PBD) photograph. It's wonderful in text, but is especially good in Sagan's voice, and presented alongside the photo:

I love the PBD image and text, and while I won't belabour its merits here, I've [written about them extensively elsewhere](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html). In this note I want instead to discuss two glaring issues in the text, and whether and how those issues can be addressed.

The first issue is nihilism. Sagan very nearly straight up says we human beings don't much matter. We are tiny and inconsequential. All those generals and emperors – how silly they were to fight over a fraction of a dot. He's diminishing what can be won through violence, as a way of saying people shouldn't try to win through violence. We like the conclusion, and so it's tempting to accept that line of argument. But if we take it seriously, it means little we do matters. After all, if it's silly to want to be momentary master of a fraction of a dot, how much more silly to worry about whether you have a job, or your partner's happiness, or your child's schooling, or how to cook roast beef, or much of anything else.

Of course, the mere fact I don't like the nihilistic conclusion doesn't mean it's wrong. Maybe nihilism is correct! Maybe it's true that nothing we do much matters. But, at the very least, I think this implied conclusion makes it worth considering whether there are other issues in the text.

The second issue is an internal inconsistency: having strongly implied that life doesn't much matter, Sagan finishes with: "There is perhaps no better demonstration of the folly of human conceits than this distant image of our tiny world. To me, it underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another, and to preserve and cherish the pale blue dot, the only home we’ve ever known."

This makes very little sense. Sagan effectively says both "hey, we're tiny and insignificant, so it doesn't make sense to aim at power and glory" and also uses this to say we need to be kinder to one another. He's trying to have it both ways. If winning glory doesn't matter, because it's on a very small scale, then loving each other doesn't much matter, either. Which is it?

People who love the Pale Blue Dot often don't much like this observation. They want to let Sagan off the hook. And of course you can do that if you creatively reinterpret his meaning, or engage in special pleading. But as beautiful as the Pale Blue Dot is – and I think it's magnificent – it's got some basic things wrong with it.

So, what to do? Part of me is tempted to throw my hands up in the air, say "Sagan got it wrong", and stomp off. But I think Sagan got a lot right, and it's [astonishingingly insightful](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html). It's pointing at something deep and true, and Sagan gets close to articulating it. Rather than letting him off the hook or stomping off we should instead ask: what can we save? And what's it pointing at?

Ultimately, I think the right approach is pretty obvious: to understand as deeply as possible that on the scale of the cosmos we are truly tiny creatures; and yet still retain confidence in the deep meaning in our actions. Those actions matter, both our crimes and our kindnesses. it's a mistake to think power and war don't matter. Those generals and emperors aren't wrong because they're operating in a small arena. They're wrong when they grossly violate other human beings, and it's because human beings really matter. They are in fact operating in a very important arena, the arena of human experience. The scriptures of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all have it right when they express variations on: "whoever destroys a life destroys an entire world; whoever saves a life saves an entire world".

Let's do a detailed critique of the PBD text. I realize it's presumptuous, but I want to identify what I feel is correct in it. The opening is superb. It really does relate our everyday experience – our individual lives, the totality of the 8 billion people alive today, the 110 billion who have ever lived – to that tiny point of pale light:

> From this distant vantage point, the Earth might not seem of any particular interest.
> 
> But for us, it's different. Look again at that dot. That's here. That's home. That's us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives. The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child, inventor and explorer, every teacher of morals, every corrupt politician, every “superstar,” every “supreme leader,” every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived there – on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.

I'm a little suspicious of the next paragraph. But upon reflection the words are largely correct. I will register two mild critiques. First, there is already some insinuation – later made more explicit – that the triumphs of generals and emperors don't matter. But, in fact, they do[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn1). As I said above, if they don't, then nothing we do matters. Second, and this is an aside more than a strong critique, it's incongruous to be talking about "one corner of this pixel", when what's being discussed is a very-nearly-spherical object. Still, the paragraph is overall very good.

> The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. Think of the endless cruelties visited by the inhabitants of one corner of this pixel on the scarcely distinguishable inhabitants of some other corner, how frequent their misunderstandings, how eager they are to kill one another, how fervent their hatreds.

The next paragraph has many problems:

> Our posturings, our imagined self-importance, the delusion that we have some privileged position in the Universe, are challenged by this point of pale light. Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves.

It's connected to the later comment on "the folly of human conceits". The implication is that human beings aren't important. But we _are_ important. You can both understand that we live on a "lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark" _and_ believe that human lives matter a tremendous amount. Now, there may be more and less healthy positions to take on this issue – plenty of people have little conception of _enough_, and elevated need for importance, and that's responsible for a lot of violence and pursuit of glory. But it's a terrible mistake to dismiss our importance. We can choose to believe in our own meaning.

The last sentence of the paragraph is fine – it's a bridge into the next paragraph, which is a beautiful reminder that humanity is a stewardship, somewhat apart from the rest of the text:

> The Earth is the only world known so far to harbor life. There is nowhere else, at least in the near future, to which our species could migrate. Visit, yes. Settle, not yet. Like it or not, for the moment the Earth is where we make our stand.

The final paragraph is again troubling:

> It has been said that astronomy is a humbling and character-building experience. There is perhaps no better demonstration of the folly of human conceits than this distant image of our tiny world. To me, it underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another, and to preserve and cherish the pale blue dot, the only home we’ve ever known.

The first sentence is arguably true, but it's a little too much astronomy agitprop for my taste (sorry Carl!). The second sentence is false. In fact, the folly of human conceits is better illustrated by many much more immediate and concrete errors. And nothing in the text underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another. What's the argument? Because we're tiny? Do ants have to deal kindly with one another because they're small? What about amoeba? There's no argument here, just a cleverly constructed vibe. The _conclusion_ might well be true – I certainly believe it – but this argument doesn't show it. Indeed, there is no argument.

If you restructure the text around the correct elements, you end up with:

> From this distant vantage point, the Earth might not seem of any particular interest.
> 
> But for us, it's different. Look again at that dot. That's here. That's home. That's us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives. The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child, inventor and explorer, every teacher of morals, every corrupt politician, every “superstar,” every “supreme leader,” every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived there – on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.
> 
> The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. Think of the endless cruelties visited by the inhabitants of one corner of this pixel on the scarcely distinguishable inhabitants of some other corner, how frequent their misunderstandings, how eager they are to kill one another, how fervent their hatreds.
> 
> Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves. The Earth is the only world known so far to harbor life. There is nowhere else, at least in the near future, to which our species could migrate. Visit, yes. Settle, not yet. Like it or not, for the moment the Earth is where we make our stand. To me, it underscores our responsibility to preserve and cherish the pale blue dot, the only home we’ve ever known.

While there's still various minor issues, this is largely correct. I still find it beautiful and stirring, but must admit: it's much less stirring. It's no longer as full of human meaning, no longer condemning the silliness of our fighting, or exhorting us to kindness. The standard version appears to offer cosmic justifications for those conclusions. Unfortunately, those cosmic justifications were verbal sleight-of-hand. And when stripped them out, we're left with something with much less apparent meaning.

In the full PBD text, there's a striking parallel to the Abrahamic religious texts. One implied argument in the religious texts is "God created you and the Universe, so when He says you should behave like such-and-such, you should really pay attention". It's an argument from cosmology to individual action, and in this sense is similar to the PBD text. And both suffer when you realize that they're based on a false premise. The Abrahamic ones because their cosmology is wrong, undermining God's authority to determine human behaviour. And the PBD text because the argument from cosmos to human meaning is largely illusory.

Can we recover the beautiful connection the PBD attempts, between cosmos and individual meaning? I think we likely can. I won't attempt it in detail here. But I believe the key is to juxtapose and synthesize this kind of big-picture cosmic view with other points of view that arise out of the same underlying reality. The Pale Blue Dot _and_ the tree of life _and_ the 100 trillion connections in our brain _and_ the many wondrous varieties of human experience and human possibility. All these things express a single underlying unity, a unity that ultimately we can aspire to apprehend, a deep communion with the universe, and also a deepening of self and of humanity. Hume is right that ought doesn't entirely arise out of is, but it's certainly made far, far deeper by a rich appreciation of the is.

I won't attempt to carry this program out here. I'm merely naming what I believe is a better goal and strategy than the PDB text, without actually doing the hard work to implement it! I do want to note a few challenges in such an endeavour. In historian Edwin Burtt's book "The Metaphysical Foundations of Modern Science"[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn2) Burtt makes a case that modern science caused a massive transition from a human-and-God-centered conception of reality, to a cosmos-centered reality, where humans are peripheral. This transition (together with economic specialization) has produced great alienation in the human condition. This kind of alienation is expressed through remarks like those of physicist Steve Weinberg that "the more the universe seem comprehensible the more it also seems pointless"[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/pbdredux/index.html#fn3).

What Sagan was reaching for, and nearly grasped, was a way of connecting the cosmic to the human, scale to meaning. But that connection isn't to be found merely in size. It's found in structure, in relationship, in pattern and depth. A healthier view is a kind of long zoom: moving from the intricate interior of our lives outward to the vastness of the cosmos, linking the complexity within us to the complexity beyond. That means understanding our 3 billion base pairs, our 100 trillion synapses, and all the variety and possibility of human experience; and connecting that to the layered structures of culture, biosphere, and cosmos. Meaning lives in these layers, and in the ways they interconnect. Yes, you want deep communion with the unself; but it must be grounded in loving your family, your friends, your place and time. You're a pan-African plains ape – and also an artist, a scientist, a poet, and an explorer. You can apprehend the cosmos-in-the-small and the cosmos-in-the-large, in all these capacities.
