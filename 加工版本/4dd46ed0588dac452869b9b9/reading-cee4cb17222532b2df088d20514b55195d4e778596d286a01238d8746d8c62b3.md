# New Science's NIH report: highlights

---

## 正文

New Science 刚刚发表了[一篇关于 NIH 的出色 report](https://newscience.org/nih)，对于好奇世界上首屈一指的 science funding institution 如何运作的人而言，它既是一篇很好的 primer，也是一篇充满超越表面认识的洞见的 essay。我最近几篇 essays 的一个主题，是 [tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) 在许多情境中，尤其在 science reform 中的关键重要性。我曾在别处指出，在 Azoulay（[2011](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/)）发表之前，人们普遍认为“HHMI 所 funds 的 researchers 在 in aggregate 意义上比 NIH 的更好”这一说法是 true 的[1](https://nintil.com/new-science-nih/#sidenote-1) 。因此，这篇 essay 并不是通过查阅公开资料写成的，而是通过直接与正在或曾经在这个 system 内工作的 researchers 交谈，挖掘 NIH 圈内的 common knowledge 写成的。与此相关，另一篇值得一读、提出了互补观点的文章，是最近成立的 _Good Science Project_ 所发表的 _[对 NIH 的 Peer Review 进行 Reforming](https://goodscienceproject.org/articles/reforming-peer-review-at-nih/)_。

\[1\]. 我甚至愿意说，假如 Azoulay 中的 results contradicted 了人们关于 NIH 与 HHMI 的 relative standing 的普遍认识，人们会把这视为 paper 的 methods 有问题，而不太会视为 research funding modalities 并不重要的 evidence。毕竟，一个人的 modus ponens，就是另一个人的 tollens

我赞同这篇 essay 的总体观点：NIH 目前是一个 invaluable 且 irreplaceable 的 institution。它可以也应该得到改进，而且还有空间形成一个更加 diverse 的 funding ecosystem；这会优于目前由单一 institution 在很大程度上 orchestrates 全世界 life sciences research 的局面。

这里，我想特别挑出这篇 essay 中让我觉得尤其有意思的几个具体观点：

> 在所有问题中，我从消息来源那里听到的、对 NIH 最一致的一项批评，是这个 organization 太过“conservative”。也就是说，它在 institutional 意义上太过 conservative，而不是在 ideological 意义上。
> 
> 人们认为 NIH 不够愿意 take risks。这可以从它的 consensus-based grant evaluation、对 ambitious grants 的 de facto discouragement、它逐渐偏离 basic research，以及偏向 large、established organizations 和 researchers 的 lopsided distribution of grants 中看出来。

在互联网上的许多地方都能读到这种说法（即 NIH 在 funding 上是 conservative 的）。基于相对广泛的一致意见，我们可以暂且将其视为 true。一个有意思、但这篇 essay 没有提出的后续步骤，是请 scientists 指出哪些具体种类的 research 不会 get funded。或许可以提供**一些简短的 grants examples，以及简要的 rationale，说明为什么那些 research 尽管有 putative merits，却很可能不会 get funded**。收集这些内容，有助于以更 fine-grained 的方式理解 NIH process 究竟有哪些 biases。可以邀请 senior scientists，与传统及新兴 funders 一起举办 Workshops，讨论 NIH grants 以及哪些内容应该或不应该 funded，从而帮助弥合理解上的这一差距。Open Philantrophy 本可以公布他们从 _Transformative Research Award_ 中选出的 grants，但他们没有这样做，可能是出于 confidentiality 的原因。让那些 grants 可以被查阅

> 一位受访者讲述了两次 grants 被 rejected 的经历，原因是其中涉及的 technology 尚未在其 lab 中开展，因此没有 preliminary data。在后一次经历中，这位受访者已经接受 NIH grants 超过四十年，曾担任一家 major journal 的 editor，也曾担任一家 NIH institute 的 advisor。所有这些影响力与资历，仍不足以让 grant 获得 approved。虽然这种抵御 political forces 的能力令人印象深刻，但 grant 没有获 approved 的原因，是这位受访者从未在其 applied for grant 的 field 中工作过。
> 
> 幸运的是，这项 proposal 后来引起了一家知名 non-profit 的注意。受访者 submitted 了一份一页的 application，几个月后，在获得 full approval、且 funding amount 高于预期时，受访者“差点从\[自己的\]椅子上摔下来”。此后，这个 project 已在该 field 中取得了“transformational” progress；尽管受访者总体上对 NIH 持极其积极的看法，却对 study sections 缺乏 risk-tolerance 感到担忧。

我差点就想：Ed Boyden！但不对，Ed Boyden 太年轻，不可能已经 applying for grants 四十年了。不过，这与他在 [expansion microscopy](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MSP5FgEaz66kzCKzt/ed-boyden-on-the-state-of-science) 上的经历相吻合。

> 几位 researchers 对这个 system 的一项 unintended consequence 提出了一个有意思的看法：NIH 对“super nerds”存在 biased against 的倾向。要在这个“benevolent ponzi scheme”中周旋，就需要预判 colleagues 的 judgments，知道该找哪些合适的人寻求建议，规划如何 stagger grant timing，以及如何 explain 与 official grant applications diverge 的 results。这些 skills 都与 extraversion、networking 和 sociability 存在 correlated with 的关系。它们并不是那种 socially awkward scientist 的 typical traits：这种 scientist 喜欢花上几个小时查看 data sets、讨论 abstract theories，而不是琢磨如何 game complex bureaucratic systems。
> 
> 这并不是说，researcher 不能既是优秀的 scientist，又是熟练的游戏玩家。但确实有一些 researchers 对这个 system 感到不适，希望自己能够把更多时间花在 science 上，而少花些时间琢磨如何才能有机会做 science。

早在 40-50s，Rockefeller [Foundation](https://physicstoday.scitation.org/do/10.1063/PT.6.4.20180320a/full/) 也存在这种情况。虽然我没能找到那份文档（“N.S. Notes on Officer's Techniques”），但其中有一节讨论了这个问题，我在[这里](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/) 保存了相关引文：_我知道有个人，他几乎肯定是全世界关于 oak trees 的 genetics 的最佳 expert；但他不洗澡，而且脏话连篇、措辞激烈，以至于大多数人根本不愿与他共事。结果，他过着沮丧而失意的生活；他不仅没有任何 students——甚至连工作都没有。_ Weaver 接着说，他们不会 fund 这样的人，因为 team work 在 science 中至关重要。这或许是个极端案例，但它提供了一个反例，说明“anti- super-nerd” bias 并不是最近才出现的现象。

> 另一方面，也有一些 researchers 对 grant system 中的 complexities 可能有点_过于_适应了。不论是有意为之还是偶然形成，一些 lab leaders 因极其善于获取 grants 而声名在外，以至于他们把大部分精力放在获取 resources 上，然后把实际的 science 留给 staff 去做。不过，或许一群 super nerds 为一位 master grant-getter 工作，恰好就是 ideal lab structure？

这一点连同其他几点（indirect costs 所带来的 redistribution、benevolent Ponzi scheme 等），构成了一个有意思的例子，说明事情为什么可能并不像表面看起来那么糟。它们或许并不 efficient，但整个 system 似乎已经为一些问题找到了 workarounds；否则，这些问题会让 research enterprise 比实际情况更加 inefficient。

> NIH 将全部 extramural grant money 的约 50% 分配给 2% 的 applying organizations，其中大多数是 universities 或附属于 universities 的 research facilities。[38](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:38) 排名前 10 的 NIH recipients（共 2,632 家 institutions）[39](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:39) 获得了 \$6.5 billion in 2020. This is 22% of the NIH’s total, extramural grant budget (\$29.5 billion[40](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:40)），以及 NIH 全部 budget 的 16%。
> 
> 2020 年，获得 NIH money 最多的前十家 recipients 是：[41](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:41)
> 
> 1.  Johns Hopkins University - 通过 1,452 项 awards 获得 \$807 million
> 2.  Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center - 通过 301 项 awards 获得 \$758 million
> 3.  University of California San Francisco - 通过 1,388 项 awards 获得 \$686 million
> 4.  University of California Los Angeles - 通过 884 项 awards 获得 \$673 million
> 5.  University of Michigan Ann Arbor - 通过 1,326 项 awards 获得 \$642 million
> 6.  Duke University - 通过 931 项 awards 获得 \$607 million
> 7.  University of Pennsylvania - 通过 1,267 项 awards 获得 \$594 million
> 8.  University of Pittsburgh at Pittsburgh - 通过 1,158 项 awards 获得 \$570 million
> 9.  Stanford University - 通过 1,084 项 awards 获得 \$561 million
> 10.  Columbia University Health Sciences - 通过 1,003 项 awards 获得 \$559 million

这里，如果也能看看 per-researcher figures，会很有意思；不过需要注意，如果从 PI level 来看，就不会计入 indirect costs 和 intra-university redistribution。但无论如何，什么样的 PI 会得到更多 funding，又是为了什么？这主要是由 lab size 所 driven 吗？很想知道！

这篇 essay 还讨论了 Grant Support Index。我在[这里](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/)，尤其是[这里](https://www.worksinprogress.co/issue/we-dont-know-how-to-fix-science/)，对此发表过一些评论：

> 几年前，曾有一场关于 NIH 是否应该对 individual researchers 的 funding 进行 cap 的[debate](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries)，理由是将 funding concentrating 于单一 investigator 会产生 decreasing marginal returns。Opponents argued，这样的 policy 会 unfairly penalize 那些领导 large labs、开展 highly impactful work 的 successful investigators。\[6\] 这项 proposal [最终](https://www.sciencemag.org/news/2017/06/updated-nih-abandons-controversial-plan-cap-grants-big-labs-creates-new-fund-younger)被 [scrapped](https://science.sciencemag.org/content/356/6343/1108.full)。这样的 proposal 是否会奏效，并不重要：双方都有合理的 arguments。重要的是，NIH 从未考虑过在更小的 scale 上 randomizing 或 trialing 这项 policy——他们从一开始就把它 designed 成一项影响 NIH 全部 budget 的 policy。
> 
> 这正是我们需要改变的思维方式。相反，NIH 本应考虑 selecting 一组 investigators 的 subset，并对他们 applying a cap，然后在十年后，将 results 与那些仍可 accumulate 更多 traditional funding 的 investigators 进行 compared。

我现在对于通过 RCTing 来推进 science reform 已不那么乐观，但我仍然认为，让更小 scales 的 experimentation 得以开展，会是向前迈出的良好一步。

关于 researcher age，我写过一篇[专门](https://nintil.com/age-and-science/)讨论这个问题的 essay，最后我的想法是，归根结底，相比目前的情况，更年轻的 workforce 会是好事，因此有了[我关于](https://nintil.com/new-science-models/) _Young Researchers Research Institute_ 的构想。

关于 study [sections](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lzBhKeR6VIE)，youtube 上有一些视频，展示了它们在实践中如何运作。我最初接触“study section”这个 concept 时，发现这些视频很有帮助。

> 总之，从 grant submission 到获得 funding，可能需要 _14 months_。与此同时，现有 grants 会陆续耗尽。Postdocs 会不断进出 lab。新的 discoveries 会把 research 引向一个方向，而其他 research paths 则会逐渐走不下去。PIs 在经历这个通常需要 6-14 months 才能支付款项的 process 时，还必须将所有这些 complex management 和 budgeting 纳入考虑。

这不可接受。虽然确实并非每个 grants program 都需要立志成为 _Fast Grants_，但**将所需时间至少缩短一半，似乎是有可能做到的**。system 的 responsivity 所获得的提升，很可能足以抵偿 review quality 在 accuracy 上可能发生的任何轻微变化。这篇 essay 列举了不少很好的办法，可以用来 reform 当前的 study section model。

> NIH 的 extramural grant system 有些 convoluted，但它有其 logic。它的设计旨在用 government money 来 promote private research，同时通过 subsidies 来 fostering research institutions。
> 
> 当 NIH 向 researchers awards extramural grants 时，funds 被 divided 为 direct costs 和 indirect costs。
> 
> Direct costs 是给予 researcher 的 funds。这些 funds cover 那些容易与 researcher 及其 team associated 的 costs，包括 salaries、supplies、equipment 和 lab space。

第 5 部分很好地解释了 indirect costs 如何 work，以及它们为何存在，并呈现了支持和反对 high indirects 的论据。我倾向于赞同“它们太 high 了”的立场；虽然 universities 可能抱怨钱不够，但被迫勒紧裤腰带可能会迫使它们实现 efficiencies：或许会有更多 investments 用于 reducing sequencing、imaging 或 pathology cores 的 costs，使它们更像 autonomous business units，向 researchers charge 他们想做的事情所需的 cost，并额外收费以 account for maintenance 和 new purchases。一个稍欠现实的办法是，可以鼓励 scientists 在其他 research establishments 的 shared facilities 之间货比三家，以促成一些 healthy competition。朝着 transparent pricing 和更少 cross-subsidies 迈进，似乎 on net 是好的。这里我在想：我们能否针对某所 university 做一个 case study，看看他们的 indirects 如何 work？相应的 incentives 可能并不存在。但 NIH 可以委托开展**一项关于 indirect utilizations 的 survey，其中包括对 adminstrators 和 scientists 的 interviews，以确切了解那些 indirects 究竟发生了什么，以及 reducing 它们可能会有怎样的 implications。**

_Ricón, José Luis, “New Science's NIH report: highlights”, Nintil (2022-04-25), available at https://nintil.com/new-science-nih/._

## 原文

New Science just published [an excellent report on the NIH](https://newscience.org/nih), both a good primer for those curious about how the world's premier science funding institution works as well as an essay packed with insights that go beyond the obvious. A recent theme of my latest few essays is the key importance of [tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) in many contexts, and in science reform in particular. I noted elsewhere that the claim "the HHMI funds better researchers than NIH in aggregate" was generally believed to be true before Azoulay ([2011](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/)) came out[1](https://nintil.com/new-science-nih/#sidenote-1) . Accordingly, the essay was written not by consulting public sources, but by unearthing common knowledge within the NIH world by directly talking to researchers that work or have worked within the system. Relatedly, another interesting read, making complementary points, is _[Reforming Peer Review at NIH](https://goodscienceproject.org/articles/reforming-peer-review-at-nih/)_ from the recently launched _Good Science Project_.

\[1\]. I would go as far as claiming that had the results in Azoulay contradicted popular wisdom regarding the relative standing of NIH and HHMI, it would have been taken as an issue with the methods of the paper, and not so much for evidence of research funding modalities not mattering. After all, one man's modus ponens is another's tollens

I agree with the overall point of the essay: The NIH is at the moment an invaluable and irreplaceable institution. It can and should be improved, and there is room for a more diverse funding ecosystem which would be preferrable to the current situation where a single institution, to a large extent, orchestrates the world's life sciences research.

Here I wanted to single out specific points the essay makes that I found particularly interesting:

> The single most consistent criticism of the NIH that I heard from sources, across all issues, was that the organization is too “conservative.” That is, too conservative in an institutional sense, not an ideological sense.
> 
> The NIH is considered insufficiently willing to take risks. This can be seen in its consensus-based grant evaluation, the de facto discouragement of ambitious grants, its drift away from basic research, and the lopsided distribution of grants which favor large, established organizations and researchers.

This is something that one can read in many places in the internet (that the NIH is conservative in its funding). We can tentatively take this as true based on relatively general agreement. An interesting next step, not suggested in the essay, is asking scientists for specific kinds of research that wouldn't get funded. Perhaps **brief examples of grants and a brief rationale for why that research would probably not get funded despite its putative merits**. Collecting those would help understand in a more fine-grained way exactly what sort of biases the NIH process has. Workshops with senior scientists to discuss NIH grants and what should be funded or not could be held with traditional and new funders to help bridge that gap in understanding. Open Philantrophy could have released the grants they chose from th _Transformative Research Award_ but chose not to, probablyd due to confidentiality reasons. Making those grants accessible

> One interviewee related stories of two instances when their grants were rejected because they involved technology not ongoing in their lab and, thus, there was no preliminary data. In the latter case, the interviewee had been receiving NIH grants for over forty years, they had served as an editor on a major journal, and had been an advisor for an NIH institute. All that clout and history wasn’t enough to get the grant approved. While such ability to withstand political forces is impressive, the reason the grant wasn’t approved was that the interviewee never worked in the field in which they applied for the grant.
> 
> Fortunately, the proposal later caught the attention of a prominent non-profit. The interviewee submitted a one-page application and they “nearly fell off \[their\] chair” a few months later when they got full approval at a higher funding amount than expected. Their project has since yielded “transformational” progress in the field, and though the interviewee is extremely positive about the NIH overall, they are concerned about the lack of risk-tolerance in study sections.

I almost thought: Ed Boyden! But no, Ed Boyden is too young to have been applying for grants for forty years. But this matches his story with [expansion microscopy](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MSP5FgEaz66kzCKzt/ed-boyden-on-the-state-of-science).

> A few researchers had an interesting take on an unintended consequence of this system: the NIH is biased against “super nerds.” Navigating the “benevolent ponzi scheme” requires anticipating the judgments of colleagues, knowing the right people to talk to for advice, plotting out how to stagger grant timing and explain results that diverge from official grant applications. These are all skills correlated with extraversion, networking, and sociability. They are not the typical traits of a socially awkward scientist who loves to spend hours going through data sets and discussing abstract theories, rather than figuring out how to game complex bureaucratic systems.
> 
> This is not to say that a researcher can’t be both a great scientist and a skilled player of the game. But there are certainly researchers who are uncomfortable with the system, and who wish they could spend more of their time on the science and less on figuring out how to get to do the science.

This was also the case back in the day in the Rockefeller [Foundation](https://physicstoday.scitation.org/do/10.1063/PT.6.4.20180320a/full/) in the 40-50s. Though I could not retrieve the document ("N.S. Notes on Officer's Techniques"), there is a section in there on this and I have the relevant quote [here](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/): _I know of a man who is almost surely the best expert on the genetics of oak trees in the world; but he doesn't take baths, and he swears so much and so violently that most persons just won't work with him. As a result he is living a frustrated and defeated life; and he not only doesn't have any students - he doesn't even have a job._ Weaver then goes on to say that they would't fund people like this because team work is key in science. This may be an extreme case, but it is an example against the thought that "anti- super-nerd" bias is a recent phenomenon.

> On the other hand, there are some researchers who are probably a bit _too_ comfortable with complexities embedded in the grant system. Whether by design or happenstance, some lab leaders gain reputations at being so good at getting grants that they focus most of their energy on getting resources and then leave the actual science to their staff. Then again, maybe a bunch of super nerds working for a master grant-getter is the ideal lab structure?

This, along with other points (the redistribution that occurs with indirect costs, the benevolent Ponzi scheme, etc) is an interesting example of why things may not be as bad as they seem. Maybe they are not efficient, but the system as a whole seems to have found workarounds for issues that would otherwise make the research enterprise more inefficient than it actually is.

> The NIH gives about 50% of all extramural grant money to 2% of applying organizations, most of which are universities or research facilities attached to universities. [38](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:38) The top 10 NIH recipients (out of 2,632 institutions) [39](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:39) received $6.5 billion in 2020. This is 22% of the NIH’s total, extramural grant budget ($29.5 billion[40](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:40)), and 16% of the NIH’s entire budget.
> 
> In 2020, the top ten largest recipients of NIH money were: [41](https://newscience.org/nih/#fn:41)
> 
> 1.  Johns Hopkins University - $807 million through 1,452 awards
> 2.  Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center - $758 million through 301 awards
> 3.  University of California San Francisco - $686 million through 1,388 awards
> 4.  University of California Los Angeles - $673 million through 884 awards
> 5.  University of Michigan Ann Arbor - $642 million through 1,326 awards
> 6.  Duke University - $607 million through 931 awards
> 7.  University of Pennsylvania - $594 million through 1,267 awards
> 8.  University of Pittsburgh at Pittsburgh - $570 million through 1,158 awards
> 9.  Stanford University - $561 million through 1,084 awards
> 10.  Columbia University Health Sciences - $559 million through 1,003 awards

Here it would have been interesting to look at per-researcher figures as well, with the caveat the indirect costs and intra-university redistribution would not be accounter for if looking at the PI level. But nonetheless, what kind of PI gets more funding, and for what. Is it mostly driven by lab size? Would be interesting to know!

The essay also discussed the Grant Support Index. I made some remarks on that [here](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/) and especially [here](https://www.worksinprogress.co/issue/we-dont-know-how-to-fix-science/):

> A few years ago, there was a [debate](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries) around whether NIH should cap funding for individual researchers, on the grounds that there are decreasing marginal returns to concentrating funding on a single investigator. Opponents argued that such a policy would unfairly penalize successful investigators leading large labs that are doing highly impactful work. \[6\] The proposal was [ultimately](https://www.sciencemag.org/news/2017/06/updated-nih-abandons-controversial-plan-cap-grants-big-labs-creates-new-fund-younger) [scrapped](https://science.sciencemag.org/content/356/6343/1108.full). It’s not relevant whether such a proposal would have worked: both sides had reasonable arguments. What is important is that at no point did NIH think of randomizing or trialing this policy at a smaller scale—they designed it from the outset as a policy to affect the entirety of NIH’s budget.
> 
> That is the kind of thinking that we need to change. Instead, NIH should have considered selecting a subset of investigators and applying a cap to them, and then compared results a decade into the future with those that were left to accumulate more traditional funding.

I am now less bullish on RCTing our way through science reform but I do still think that enabling experimentation at smaller scales would a good step forward.

On researcher age I wrote an essay [just](https://nintil.com/age-and-science/) on that question where I end up thinking that at the end of the day, relative to the current situation, a younger workforce would be good, hence [my idea](https://nintil.com/new-science-models/) of a _Young Researchers Research Institute_.

On study [sections](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lzBhKeR6VIE), there are videos on youtube that show how they work in practice. I found these very helpful when I first came across the "study section" concept.

> In sum, it can take _14 months_ before a grant submission results in funding. Meanwhile, existing grants will be running out. Postdocs will be coming and going from the lab. New discoveries will pull research in one direction while other research paths peter out. PIs will have to take all of this complex management and budgeting into account while going through a process which typically takes 6-14 months to pay out.

This is unacceptable and while true that not every grants program needs to aspire to be _Fast Grants_, **cutting down time by at least half seems within the bounds of possibility**. The gains in the responsivity of the system are most likely worth whatever minor accuracy in review quality could occur. The essay has a good sampling of ways to reform the current study section model.

> The NIH’s extramural grant system is somewhat convoluted, but it has a logic. It is designed to promote private research with government money while simultaneously fostering research institutions through subsidies.
> 
> When the NIH awards extramural grants to researchers, the funds are divided between direct costs and indirect costs.
> 
> Direct costs are funds given to the researcher. These funds cover costs that are easily associated with the researcher and their team, including salaries, supplies, equipment, and lab space.

Part 5 has a good explanation of how indirect costs work and why they are there, and presents the pro and con case for high indirects. I tend to side with the "they are too high" position and while universities may complain they don't have enough money, being forced to tighten their belts could force efficiencies: perhaps more investments in reducing costs in sequencing, imaging, or pathology cores, making them more like autonomous business units that charge researchers the cost of what they want to do, and extra to account for maintenance and new purchases. Somewhat less realistically, scientists could be encouraged to shop around for shared facilities at other research establishments to induce some healthy competition. A move towards transparent pricing and less cross-subsidies seems on net good. Here I think: Could we do a case study for a particular university, and see how their indirects work? The incentives might not be there. But the NIH could commission **a survey of indirect utilizations, including interviews with adminstrators and scientists, to see exactly what is happening with those indirects, and what the implications of reducing them might be.**
