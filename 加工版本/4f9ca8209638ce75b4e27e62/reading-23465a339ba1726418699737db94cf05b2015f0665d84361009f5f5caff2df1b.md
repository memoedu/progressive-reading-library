# Mini Blog Post 23: Taking Social Initiative — Neel Nanda

## 正文

### 引言

我在之前的文章中反复谈到的一个主题是 **agency**。不要 passive，而要真正 doing things。让它成为[你的 identity 的一部分](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/become-a-person-who-actually-does-things)，理解[背后的 biases](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing)，并变得能够 [take the first step](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step)。一个极其重要的例子，就是把这一点应用到 social settings 中——**taking social initiative**。我觉得，我见到的大多数人在这方面都 systematically bad，而我这些年来一直有意识地 improved，这也成了一项_极其_ valuable 的 skill。我估计，没有它，我目前至少 80% 的 friendships 要么根本不会 exist，要么会 substantially worse。这也是我找到[让我感到 excited 的事物](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)以及 [seek positive externalities](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities) 的重要 component

我写这篇文章的目标，是概述我的 model：为什么这件事很难，怎样才能做得更好，以及我具体如何将它 applied 到 friendships 和 networking 中。

**提醒：**[Law of Equal but Opposite Advice](https://web.archive.org/web/20200122231255/http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/24/should-you-reverse-any-advice-you-hear/) 在这里_非常_适用。我猜，读这篇文章的大多数人都没有足够地 take the social initiative，但肯定也有些厚脸皮的人做得太过了！我猜，大多数读者都_非常_不可能一不小心就走到那个地步。

### Taking Social Initiative 很难

人们会 [systematically underestimate 他们的 abilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_syndrome#:~:text=Impostor%20syndrome%20\(also%20known%20as,exposed%20as%20a%20%22fraud%22.)，这是一个有充分记录的 psychological phenomenon。这_就是_ insecurity。正如我在[这里](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)所说，我发现这种 mindset 对克服自己的 insecurities 很有价值。我的目标，是对自己的 abilities，以及自己是否擅长某件事，形成一个 _true_ 的看法。而且，empirically，insecurities 对我的判断存在 systematically biased——**insecurity 是一种 cognitive bias**。

当人们考虑 taking the social initiative——organising events、meeting new people、reconnecting with old friends、去参加不熟悉的 events——时，我也观察到了类似的情况。人们特别关注 downsides，想着这件事可能怎样出错，觉得别人会笑话自己，等等。落入 [doing nothing 的 default path](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing) _极其_容易。而且 empirically，我认为**这是一种 cognitive bias**。我经常看到，人们对某件事感到 insecure，稍微走出自己的 comfort zone，随后便得到了 rewarded——bravery 受到欣赏，事情进展顺利，美好的事情发生了。而我很少看到人们这样做之后结果很糟——大多数人并不是混蛋（如果他们是混蛋，那我反正也不想听他们的意见！）。

我真正想回答的问题是：“在这里 taking the initiative 是个好主意吗？”而 empirically，我的 intuitions 并不能很好地指引我找到 truth。因为我[在意拥有 true beliefs](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-13-beliefs-are-for-true-things)，所以我会越过这些 intuitions 来看问题。我有意识地在自己心中建立了一种 counter-bias：**notice 自己何时正对 taking initiative 犹豫不决**，然后**还是让自己去做** \- 因为当我的想法存在偏离 truth 的 systematic bias 时，这样粗略的 rules 会带来 systematically better answers。而且我发现，这条 rule 带来了好得多的 outcomes，也让我快乐了许多！

此外，还有其他充分的理由，让我们预期人们 take the social initiative 的频率会 systematically 低于 optimal 的频率。Taking social initiative 具有 [positive externalities](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities)——其他人和你一样会 benefit，但_他们_不承担任何 costs。它不是 default action，而且人们对 loss aversion 和 [doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing) 存在强烈的 bias——没有什么会_迫使_你 take the initiative。你很容易担心自己显得咄咄逼人，或者 failing，或者让自己难堪；但你很少会因为_没有_ taking the initiative 而受到责怪。

所以，**这个世界的 default state 是，人们没有足够地 take the social initiative——也包括你。**

### 友谊（Friendships）

现在，我要概述生活中这项 skill 对我最有价值的几个方面，并尝试给出一些 actionable examples，说明你可以怎样 apply 它。

第一个重要方面是我的 social life 和 friendships。我许多最快乐的经历，都是与自己喜欢、在乎的人待在一起；如果没有人 takes the initiative，这些事情**就根本不会发生**。成为一个能够与朋友 take the initiative 的人，会产生_重大的_ positive externalities。而且我发现，这给我带来了许多 satisfaction——看到别人享受我 organised 并促成的事情，真的很开心！

我已经在[多篇](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)[之前的](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities)[文章](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower)中，更详细地／从不同 perspectives 概述过我对此的想法，但主要的 examples 是：

-   结识新人（Meeting new people）

    -   我曾 developed 的最 valuable 的 skills 之一，就是在遇见一个新认识、有趣又合得来的人时，能够 noticing 这一点，并尝试与对方 become friends。表现出我喜欢与对方相处，邀请对方喝咖啡（或者如今约一次通话），

    -   我甚至在还没见过对方时这样做，也取得了不错的成效——请 mutual friends 帮忙 introductions，向不认识的人 cold messaging。许多人都很友善，而且喜欢

    -   **提醒**：如果对方_不_喜欢你，这件事就有 downside risk。Empirically，人们会 systematically underestimate 别人_确实_喜欢自己的频率，所以我不会太为此焦虑。我的主要 solution，是尽可能让对方容易说不，并尽可能给他们 agency——提供一个退路，比如“如果你有时间的话”，给他们发一个可以随意忽略的 Calendly link，等等

-   Organising events——throwing parties、organising group calls、提议大家以 group 的形式一起做些事情

    -   在比较正常的时期，我发现，邀请一群朋友到我的房间里来，就是一种省事得令人发笑的 throw a party 的方式

    -   我 organising [talks](https://youtube.com/channel/UCzoIa8e4vhfRIxL_Kj6yy0Q) 时，背后也有类似的 mindset

    -   一般来说，有很多事情应该存在，却没有人 organises 它们——这是 practice social initiative 的绝佳方式！例如，如果有一门很难的 course，让大家学得很吃力，就 organise 一个 peer-support group

-   保持联系（Keeping in touch）

    -   与朋友 lose touch 真的很容易，尤其是你们不住在彼此附近时（或者双方都在 social distancing 时）

    -   而如果你们都喜欢与彼此相处，keeping in touch 就是一种 mutual win！

    -   但我发现，如果我不投入 effort，往往就会与朋友 lose touch，所以我建立了一些 systems，确保自己定期记得 reach out 联系别人，并 schedule 一次通话或 meet-up

    -   一个难点——我发现，最耗费 energy 的部分，是让 reaching out 显得 _justified_。如果我有一阵子没和某位朋友说话了，我就会对 reaching out 感到更大的 resistance，因为之前一直没有联系过，现在再联系就感觉很奇怪。这显然很蠢，却又很难单靠 force of will 来克服

        -   我的主要 solution，是创造一个 justification——即使只有一个牵强的 justification，也能让这件事容易得多。COVID 就是一个极好的 justification——如今一切都变成了 remote，与远方的人 reconnecting 便完全顺理成章了！

        -   甚至只说“我刚才想起了老朋友，才意识到咱们有一阵子没聊了，要不要叙叙旧？”也管用！

-   把朋友介绍给彼此（Introducing friends to each other）

    -   meeting new people 最难的部分之一是 filtering。但_你_很了解自己的朋友。所以，如果你觉得他们会合得来，把朋友 introducing 给彼此，就是你能带来的一件很 valuable 的事！

    -   好的 friendships _极其_ valuable，所以我会特意去做这件事——我认为，这是让朋友们的生活变得更好的最 effective 的方式之一

        -   注意——有些人对 forming more friends 并不太感兴趣，或者非常忙，因此这个 advice 并不 universally apply。先问问！

    -   如果你足够了解我，知道我可能与什么人合得来，而且你觉得我会和你的某位朋友投缘，请告诉我！

-   （一个不那么核心、但也重要的 example）Putting yourself out there——除了 organising events，还可以做些能为别人 add value 的事！

    -   开一个 [blog](https://neelnanda.io/)！这是 clarify 自己对有趣 ideas 的 thoughts，并真正做出让自己自豪的东西的好方式。

    -   把你做的 [notes](https://dynalist.io/d/ToOhEKlz9qC2PmpjgyTpXQJz) 或 [flashcards](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/65202941) 进行 Publish——它们不必是 high-quality，这件事也值得做

    -   我发现，这是一个强大的 motivation 来源！

    -   做了这样的事情后，要告诉别人，很容易令人 anxious——害怕 judgement，对 self-promotion 感到 anxiety。

    -   一个有用的 mindset——把东西放出来，**strictly better than 不这样做**——你只是在给别人提供他们可以随意忽略的 opportunities。而 self-promotion 也可以是 valuable 的——大多数人可以忽略它，少数喜欢它的人则会大大 benefit。如果你做的东西获得了 positive feedback，那么 self-promotion + publishing 就确实是 altruistic 的，而不是 selfish 的！

        -   注意——在这件事上，完全有可能做得太过，但我猜，大多数人都过于偏向不这样做，这也是我给出这个 advice 的原因。如果你得到的 feedback 是你这样做得太多，我建议听取这个 feedback。

所有这些事情中的一个核心难点，是事情都具有 **uncertain** 的性质，而且 **have downside risk**。在这种情况下，_不_ taking action 有看不见的 opportunity costs，而 _taking_ action 的 costs 则让人感受强烈、心生恐惧。但这是关于[我的 mind、而不是世界](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)的事实。我发现，想象一个 action _确实_取得良好回报的世界很有价值——这能让 opportunity costs 更 concrete，也有助于打破 [doing nothing 的 illusion](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing)。

总的来说，好的 friendships 和我在乎的人，是我 life happiness 和 mental health 中_极其_重要的 component。而培养 creating 和 maintaining 它们的 skill，是我有史以来最 valuable 的 effort investments 之一——擅长 taking initiative，直接促成了我目前绝大多数 friendships 的 existence。

### Networking（建立人际联系）

_（_**_注意：_** _我的 networking advice 很大一部分基于我与_ [_Effective Altruists_](https://www.effectivealtruism.org/) _进行 networking 的经历，他们是一群格外友善的人——这些经验未必能 perfectly generalise）_

我觉得 networking 是一个挺有意思的 concept，它给我的生活增添了相当多的 value。我交谈过的大多数人都觉得这个词有些不光彩——它让人联想到 LinkedIn profiles、investment bankers，以及卑劣、不真诚的人。我非常反对这种糟糕的 networking！不过，我起初是带着反讽意味，用 networking 这个词来描述自己实际在做的事，后来却不知不觉不再是在反讽了。所以，如今我对它已经有了相当 positive 的 association。这也是我试图为这个词正名的一次尝试！而且我发现，take social initiative 的 ability 是其中一项相当关键的 sub-skill。

我对 networking 的大致 defining 是：去认识那些拥有我觉得有意思、可以从中学习的 skills 和 experience 的人，那些能够提供 help、分享 connections 的人。

Sincerity（真诚）

我觉得，自己在 networking 上最重要的 perspective shift，就是要 **be sincere**。尝试与有意思的人交谈，_因为_我喜欢和他们交谈，而不是因为想从他们那里得到什么。也努力让自己成为有意思、令人愉快的人。与人 Building relationships，是因为这些 relationships 的存在让我开心，而不是因为我觉得它们对我有用。恰好，那些最值得认识的人，往往也正是我 intrinsically _想要_认识的人！

这是一个重要而微妙的 point——这种 mindset 不是因为表现得 sincere 是 achieve your goals 的最佳方式，就_试图_显得 sincere，而是要 **genuinely being sincere**。人很容易陷入一个误区，以为自己的 decision making process 与自己做出的 decisions 是 completely separate 的，[但这很少是真的](http://mindingourway.com/newcomblike-problems-are-the-norm/)。人类是 social creatures，我们有非常发达的 senses，能够 inferring 与我们交谈的人的 intentions。如果与你交谈的人有 ulterior motive，这通常不难察觉，而和这样的人交谈也远没有那么愉快。

而且，你_也_可以有 ulterior motivates！想认识很棒的人，因为和他们相处很开心，_也_因为认识他们有用，这没有问题，也很少会因此让人反感。不过，ultimate goal 是让认识有意思的人成为一件[你会为之兴奋的事](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)。

围绕你真正觉得有意思的事 Ask questions！与那些你喜欢相处的人 Keep in touch！分享你觉得很棒的事，聊聊自己的经历，不是因为你想炫耀或给人留下深刻印象，而是因为这能让 conversation 更好。要热情、兴奋，而不是一味追求 professional。主动提出 do favours，做一个 helpful 的人，不是因为你期待 reciprocation，而是因为**你就是会这样对待自己喜欢的人**。

我发现，underlying intent 会 _massively_ shifts interaction 的样貌，影响远比直觉上应有的程度更大。而且，这样也有趣得多——我觉得，人很容易陷入一种误区，以为要 network 就_必须_无聊、毫无生气，因为大家“都是这么做的”——如果你这么做，谁都不会开心。而说到底，遵循这种 strategy，让我拥有了一个相当棒的 network，其中都是我认识的很棒的人。这固然有用，但它_本身也_很美好！大多数人并不是混蛋，也愿意帮助那些友善、相处起来令人愉快的人。

让帮助你成为一件愉快的事

另一项 sub-skill，是尽量让帮助你这件事既愉快又容易：

-   培养 [skill of asking good questions](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations)——沿着你最感兴趣的事探索，努力学习，而不是努力提出那些你“觉得自己应该问”的 questions

    -   提前弄清楚自己究竟希望在哪些事情上获得 help，并为此想好 questions

        -   想清楚该说什么，以及如何 structure advice，是一种 **cognitive labour**。如果你尽可能替对方完成这些事，对方就会觉得更愉快

        -   这些不必做到 perfectly comprehensive 和 specific，但你能做的每一点都有帮助！例如：“我对这个 area 挺困惑的”，或者“我对这个 topic 理解得还不太好。我目前的 understanding 是 \_\_\_，这里面有没有遗漏什么重要的东西？”

        -   总体而言，要 signal 你希望从 conversation 中获得什么——specific career advice、introductions、general knowledge、life advice 等。人们喜欢能帮上忙，所以尽量让这件事容易一些！

    -   这也能让 conversations 更加多样，从而让对方觉得更有意思！如果他们与属于你这个 reference class 的人总是进行相同的 conversations，那么不一样的 conversation 会让大家都更开心。被问到真正好的 questions，也能 clarify _他们的_ thoughts，这是一个值得追求的 goal。

-   成为一个相处起来有趣的人

    -   要有礼貌，但把对方当作普通人对待，而不是过分拘谨，或一心想着 status

    -   给予 sincere compliments

        -   注意——**sincere** 很重要，flattery 通常很容易被看穿

        -   如果对方说的话有帮助，就 Explicitly 说出来——要 specific，并说明它是如何帮助你的

    -   如果他们说的话后来帮助了你，给他们发条消息表示感谢！尽量 specific、concrete——相比 flattery，这更能成为 sincerity 的一个好 signal，因为它更难伪装。收到这样的消息_真的_会让人很满足，尤其是当你能指出自己作为 direct consequence 而采取的 concrete actions 或取得的 successes 时

-   Giving advice 是件有趣的事！觉得自己正在帮助别人，这种感觉很好。所以，尽量清楚地说明，这对你究竟有什么帮助！

-   尊重他们的时间，尽量给他们提供方便——我喜欢给对方发一个 Calendly link，并在其他方面保持灵活。

-   做一个能帮上忙的人

    -   想想你能为他们提供什么——你认识、可以介绍给他们的人，好的 resources 和 articles，有意思的 insights 和 perspectives。

        -   你认识的人越多，这件事就越容易！

        -   注意——如果做得不对，这可能会令人尴尬。始终以提问的方式开口，例如“你听说过 \_\_\_ 吗”，或者“你认识 \_\_\_ 吗”。最初的问题要简短，这样，如果对方_确实_已经知道，就不会尴尬

Present opportunities（提供机会）

另一种有帮助的 mindset：**人们完全可以 say no**。因此，除了 saying no 的 cost，reaching out 就是在 **presenting them with an opportunity**。而 receiving opportunities 是件令人愉快的事！这给了他们更多 option value。我发现，这种 mindset 对于 overcoming insecurity _特别_有用，无论这种 insecurity 是源于担心打扰别人，还是怕自己显得傻。

如果你在找 job 或 internship，而这件事成功了，_所有人_都 wins。对所有参与者而言，这都是一个很好的 opportunity！因此，向别人 presenting 这样的事，几乎算不上 high cost。

如果你想向某个人寻求 advice，给予 advice 往往也能让对方感到愉快！一个 opportunity 不一定非要 _directly_ benefit 他们。我知道，过去我自己从别人那里获得过_大量_有用的 advice，而我几乎无法回报_他们_。所以，能够 pay it forwards，向那些像过去的我一样的人 give advice，会让人很满足。

而且，帮助别人 succeed 也会令人满足！尤其是在 Effective Altruism community 内——如果你的 goal 是让世界变得更好，**人们会 actively 想要 help you succeed**。因为你们都在同一个 team——如果你能 do more good，那么所有人都 wins。

Minimise costs（尽量降低成本）

你 reaching out 给对方带来的主要 cost，在于他们可能不得不 say no——这是一种 emotional labour。我会尽量确保给他们留出退路，例如“如果你很忙也没关系”。有时，如果我想 signal“只有你觉得有趣才答应”，我就会发一条明显是复制粘贴的消息

此外，**要能够坦然接受听到 no**。这不是 rejection，也不表示你打扰了别人——你只是向他们 presented 一个他们不感兴趣的 opportunity。他们仍然从 option value 中 benefited，这也不是对你的 personal attack——很多时候，人们只是很忙！我把它看作一个非常 stochastic process——你 contact 很多人，其中少数人感兴趣。如果在那几次 connections 中，每个人都 benefits，那么最初的 opportunity 就是有 valuable 的！

一个有用的 framing——一个人越忙、status 越 higher，就越擅长 saying no！如果不培养 prioritising 和 saying no 的 skill，你根本_不可能_同时既忙又 high-status，因为要做的事实在太多了。因此，对方的 status 越 higher，reaching out 的 cost 反而越 _lower_——我觉得这个 framing 相当有启发性，也很 unintuitive。而当你 reaching out 的对象是一家 _company_，而不是某个具体的人时，就更是如此——这没那么 personal，因此所需的 emotional effort 也就更 lower。

Taking action（采取行动）

那么，究竟该如何把这些付诸行动？与大多数 taking the initiative 的形式一样，这件事很难，也很 subjective，而且会在很大程度上取决于你的个人情况。以下是一些想法：

-   如果你已经认识某个能帮上忙的人，就克服自己的不情愿，开口去问

-   如果你在 events 上遇到了有意思的人，就 keep in touch！

-   如果你和某个人略有交情，而他们能在某件事上帮到你，就 reach out，开口去问！

-   向朋友寻求 introductions——即使你心中只是大致想到某一类人，也问问朋友是否认识能在这方面帮上忙的人

    -   如果你的朋友需要 advice，也看看你能把他们 connect 给谁！对于这样的消息，人们通常会有相当好的回应：“我的一个朋友在 \_\_\_ 方面需要 help，你有兴趣和他们聊聊吗？”

-   在 LinkedIn 上向别人发送 Cold message

    -   我的一个朋友会给自己想进入的 fields 中的人发消息，寻求 advice 和 mentorship，这种做法取得了多得令人惊讶的成功。

-   向那些你欣赏其 work 的人发送 Cold email——例如写出有意思的 papers 的 academics，或制作了你喜欢的 content 的人

    -   带着针对 papers 的 specific comments 和 thoughts 去 Reaching out 给 academics，可能会非常受欢迎！看到别人 meaningfully engage with 自己的 work，看到他们对自己 sincerely interested，而不是只在 blindly spamming 别人，是件令人愉快的事。

### 结论（Conclusion）

基于以上所有理由，我认为 taking the social initiative 是好的，而且人们应该更经常这样做。我也认为，这是一项在日常生活中非常广泛 applicable 的 skill。我发现，设定一条更经常 systematically 这样做的 rule，已经给我的生活带来了 _重大_ 改善。当然，这是一个 empirical claim，并不 _obviously_ 是 true。而且，这件事很 _难_。所以我想，读到这里的不少人可能会有些认同我的 arguments，但在直觉上仍不觉得信服，于是把它存放为“一个我永远不会付诸行动的有趣想法”。

那种感觉就是 **uncertainty** 的感觉。这是一个重要的 question，也是一个重要的 empirical claim。如果你 systematically biased against taking the initiative，systematically 错失 valuable opportunities，而且能够学着更好地做到这一点，那么这就是 _极其_ valuable information。那份 information 的 value _远远_ outweighs 尝试几次的 costs，以及 potential failure。而当你缺少 valuable information 时，solution 就是 [run an experiment 并试一试](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/the-value-of-experiments)！从这个 perspective 来看，failure 不再是一个 meaningful concept——无论发生什么，都会 reduce 你的 uncertainty，而 _那_ 才是真正的胜利。

此外，**taking the initiative 是 habit forming 的**。一旦你尝试过几次，而且进展顺利，所需的 activation energy 就会 _大幅_ goes down。随着时间推移，taking the social initiative 可以成为你 identity 的一部分，也成为[让你感到兴奋的事情](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)。大部分 energy 都用在 overcoming 你的 intuitions 上，而 intuitions 来自你的 experiences。所以，如果你读到这里，觉得这些话很有说服力，却又有点 outside your comfort zone，我强烈建议你想一想，自己可以如何 [take the first step](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step)。因为 first step 远比后面的每一步都难。

有哪些事情是你想做，却还没有足够勇气开始的？你目前正在错失哪些 opportunities？你可以做些什么，作为 **成为你想成为的** **那种人** 的 first step？

### Introduction

A theme I’ve touched on pretty heavily in previous posts is **agency**. Not being passive, and actually doing things. Making it [part of your identity](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/become-a-person-who-actually-does-things), understanding [the underlying biases](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing) and becoming able to [take the first step](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step). One extremely important instance is applying this to social settings - **taking social initiative**. I think this is something that most people I see are systematically bad at, and something I’ve deliberately improved at over time, and has been an _insanely_ valuable skill. I estimate that at least 80% of my current friendships either wouldn’t exist or would be substantially worse without this. And this is an important component of how I find [things I’m excited about](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about) and [seek positive externalities](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities)

My goal in this post is to outline my model for why this is hard, how to become better at it, and how I’ve specifically applied this to friendships and to networking.

**Health warning:** The [Law of Equal but Opposite Advice](https://web.archive.org/web/20200122231255/http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/24/should-you-reverse-any-advice-you-hear/) applies _massively_ here. My guess is that most people reading this don’t take the social initiative enough, but there are definitely shameless people who take it far too much! My guess is that most readers are _very_ unlikely to reach that point by accident.

### Taking Social Initiative is Hard

It’s a well-documented psychological phenomenon that people [systematically underestimate their abilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_syndrome#:~:text=Impostor%20syndrome%20\(also%20known%20as,exposed%20as%20a%20%22fraud%22.). This is what an insecurity _is_. And as I talk about [here](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory), I’ve found this mindset valuable for getting over my own insecurities. My goal is to have a _true_ view of my abilities, and whether I’m good at something. And, empirically, insecurities are systematically biased against me - **insecurity is a cognitive bias**.

And I observe a similar thing when people consider taking the social initiative - organising events, meeting new people, reconnecting with old friends, going to unfamiliar events. People focus strongly on the downsides, ways this could go wrong, think people will laugh at them, etc. It’s _extremely_ easy to fall into the [default path of doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing). And empirically, I think **this is a cognitive bias**. I have frequently observed people being insecure about something, going a bit outside their comfort zone, and being rewarded for it - bravery is admired, things go well, awesome things happen. And I have rarely observed people doing this and it going badly - most people aren’t jerks (and if they are jerks, those aren’t opinions I want to be listening to anyway!).

The true question I want to be answering is “is taking the initiative a good idea here?”, and empirically, my intuitions are not a good guide to the truth. And because I [care about having true beliefs](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-13-beliefs-are-for-true-things), I look past this. I’ve deliberately created a counter-bias within myself to **notice when I’m borderline on taking initiative**, and to **make myself do it anyway** \- because when my thoughts have a systematic bias away from truth, crude rules like this will lead to systematically better answers. And I’ve found that this rule has resulted in much better outcomes, and made me a lot happier!

Further, there are other good reasons to expect people to take the social initiative systematically less often than would be optimal. Taking social initiative has [positive externalities](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities) - other people benefit, as well as you, while _they_ incur none of the costs. It’s not the default action, and there’s a strong bias towards loss aversion and [doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing) - nothing _forces_ you to take the initiative. It’s easy to fear coming across as pushy, or failing, or embarrassing yourself, while you rarely get blamed for _not_ taking the initiative.

Thus, **the default state of the world is that people do not take the social initiative enough - including you.**

### Friendships

I’m now going to outline the areas of my life where this skill has been most valuable to me, and to try to give actionable examples for how you could apply this.

The first important area is with my social life, and friendships. A lot of my happiest experiences involve being around people I like and care about, and these things **just won’t happen** if nobody takes the initiative. There are _major_ positive externalities to being the kind of person who can take the initiative with friends. And I’ve found that this is something that’s give me a lot of satisfaction - it’s really fun to see other people enjoy something I organised, and made happen!

I’ve outlined my thoughts on this over [various](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about) [previous](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities) [posts](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower) in more detail/from different perspectives, but the main examples are:

-   Meeting new people

    -   One of the most valuable skills I’ve ever developed, is the skill of noticing when I meet somebody new and interesting who I get on with, and trying to become friends with them. Showing that I enjoy their company, inviting them to coffee (or nowadays to have a call),

    -   I’ve even had good success with doing this without meeting them first - asking mutual friends for introductions, cold messaging people I don’t know. A lot of people are friendly, and enjoy

    -   **Health warning**: This one has downside risk, if the other person _doesn’t_ like you. Empirically, people systematically underestimate how often other people _do_ like you, so I wouldn’t stress too much about this. My main solution is to make it as easy as possible for the other person to say no, and to give them as much agency as possible - offer an out like “if you have the time”, send them a Calendly link that they’re free to ignore, etc

-   Organising events - throwing parties, organising group calls, suggesting you do things as a group

    -   During more normal times, I find that inviting a bunch of my friends to my room is a hilariously low effort way to throw a party

    -   I have a similar mindset behind organising [talks](https://youtube.com/channel/UCzoIa8e4vhfRIxL_Kj6yy0Q)

    -   Generally, there are a lot of things that should exist, but nobody organises them - this is a great way to practice social initiative! Eg, If there’s a hard course people are struggling with, organise a peer-support group

-   Keeping in touch

    -   It’s really easy to lose touch with friends, especially if you aren’t near each other (or are both social distancing)

    -   And if you both enjoy each other’s company, keeping in touch is a mutual win!

    -   But I find that often I just lose touch with friends if I don’t put effort in, so I have systems set up to ensure that I regularly remember to reach out to people and schedule a call, or meet-up

    -   One point of difficulty - I find the bit that takes the most energy is making reaching out feel _justified_. If I haven’t spoken to a friend in a while, I feel more resistance to reaching out, because it feels weird that I hadn’t reached out before. This is very obviously dumb, but difficult to overcome with sheer force of will

        -   My main solution is to create a justification - even having a tenuous one makes this much easier. COVID was an excellent justification - now everything was remote, reconnecting with people far away makes perfect sense!

        -   Even just “I was thinking about old friends, and realised we haven’t spoken in a while, want to catch up?” works!

-   Introducing friends to each other

    -   One of the hardest parts of meeting new people is filtering. But _you_ know your friends well. And so a really valuable thing you can add is introducing friends to each other, if you think they’d get on!

    -   Good friendships are _incredibly_ valuable, so I try to make a point of doing this - I think it’s one of the most effective ways to make the lives of my friends better

        -   Note - Some people aren’t super interested in forming more friends, or are very busy, so this advice won’t apply universally. Check first!

    -   If you know me well enough to know who I might get on with, and you think I’d click with a friend of your’s, please let me know about it!

-   (Less central, but important example) Putting yourself out there - beyond just organising events, do things that can add value to other's!

    -   Start a [blog](https://neelnanda.io/)! Great way to clarify your thoughts on interesting ideas, and to actually make something you’re proud of.

    -   Publish [notes](https://dynalist.io/d/ToOhEKlz9qC2PmpjgyTpXQJz) or [flashcards](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/65202941) you make - they don’t have to be high-quality for this to be worthwhile

    -   I find this a powerful source of motivation!

    -   It’s easy to get anxious about telling other people when you do things like this - fearing judgement, anxiety about self-promotion.

    -   A useful mindset - putting things out there is **strictly better than not** - you’re just giving other people opportunities that they’re free to ignore. And self-promotion can be valuable - most people can ignore it, the few who enjoy it will benefit a lot. If you get positive feedback on what you make, self-promotion + publishing is genuinely altruistic, not selfish!

        -   Note - it’s totally possible to go too far here, but my guess is most people err way too hard against, which is why I’m giving this advice. If you get feedback that you do this too much, I recommend listening to that.

A core difficulty in all of these is that things are **uncertain** and **have downside risk**. In situations like this, _not_ taking action has invisible opportunity costs, while _taking_ action has visceral and scary costs. But this is a fact of [my mind, not of the world](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory). I find it valuable to imagine a world where the action _does_ pay off well - this makes the opportunity costs more concrete, and helps to break [the illusion of doing nothing](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-15-the-illusion-of-doing-nothing).

Overall, good friendships and people I care about are an _incredibly_ important component of my life happiness and mental health. And cultivating the skill of creating and maintaining these is one of the most valuable investments of effort I’ve ever made - being good at taking initiative directly led to existence of the vast majority of my current friendships.

### Networking

_(_**_Note:_** _Much of my networking advice is based on my experiences networking with_ [_Effective Altruists_](https://www.effectivealtruism.org/)_, who are unusually nice people - this may not perfectly generalise)_

I find networking a pretty interesting concept, and it’s added quite a lot of value to my life. Most people I talk to consider it a bit of a dirty word - associated with LinkedIn profiles, investment bankers, and scummy, insincere people. I am pretty strongly opposed to this kind of shitty networking! But I started using the word networking ironically to describe what I actually do, and it accidentally stopped being ironic. So I now have a pretty positive association with it now. And this forms my attempt to reclaim that word! And I’ve found that the ability to take social initiative is a pretty key sub-skill here.

I’m roughly defining networking as getting to know people who have skills and experience that I find interesting and could learn from, people with help they could give, connections they could share.

Sincerity

I think my most important perspective shift with networking is to **be sincere**. Try to talk to interesting people _because_ I enjoy talking to them, not because I want something from them. Try to be interesting and pleasant myself. Building relationships with people, because it makes me happy that those relationships exist, not because I think it’ll be useful to me. And conveniently, the people most worth knowing also tend to be the people I intrinsically _want_ to know!

This is an important and subtle point - the mindset is not about _trying_ to seem sincere because it’s the best way to achieve your goals, it’s about **genuinely being sincere**. It’s easy to fall into the trap of thinking that your decision making process is completely separate from the decisions you make, [but this is rarely true](http://mindingourway.com/newcomblike-problems-are-the-norm/). Humans are social creatures, and we have very developed senses for inferring the intentions of the people we talk to. It’s not hard to tell when the person you’re talking to has an ulterior motive, and it’s far less fun to talk to someone like that.

And you can _also_ have ulterior motivates! Wanting to know cool people because they’re fun to be around _and_ useful to know is fine, and rarely the kind of thing that bothers people. But the ultimate goal is for knowing interesting people to be something [you feel excited about](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about).

Ask questions about the things you find genuinely interesting! Keep in touch with the people whose company you enjoy! Share things you find cool and talk about your experiences, not because you’re trying to show-off or impress, but because it makes for a better conversation. Be enthusiastic and excited, rather than striving to be professional. Offer to do favours and be helpful, not because you expect reciprocation, but because **that’s what you do for people you like**.

I’ve found that the underlying intent _massively_ shifts what the interaction is like, far more than it seems like it should. And it’s far more fun - I think it’s easy to fall into the trap of thinking you _need_ to be boring and soulless to network, because that’s the “done thing” - if you do this, nobody is having a good time. And, at the end of the day, by following this strategy, I have a pretty great network of cool people I know. And this is useful, but it’s _also_ awesome for its own sake! Most people aren’t jerks, and are happy to help out others who are nice and pleasant to be around.

Be pleasant to help

Another sub-skill is trying to make helping you as pleasant and easy as possible:

-   Develop the [skill of asking good questions](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) - follow the things that most interest you and try to learn, rather trying to ask the questions you “think you should”

    -   Figure out exactly what you want help with in advance, and come up with questions to that end

        -   Figuring out what to say and how to structure advice is **cognitive labour**. Things are more fun for the other person if you do as much of that for them as possible

        -   These don’t have to be perfectly comprehensive and specific, but everything you can do helps! Eg “I’m pretty confused about this area” or “I don’t understand this topic very well. My current understanding is \_\_\_ is that missing anything important?”

        -   Generally signal what you want from the conversation - specific career advice, introductions, general knowledge, life advice, etc. People like being helpful, so make it as easy as possible!

    -   This also makes the conversations more varied, and thus more interesting for the other person! If they always have the same conversations with people in your reference class, everyone has more fun if it’s unusual. Being asked really good questions can clarify _their_ thoughts, and is a good goal to aim for.

-   Be a fun person to be around

    -   Be polite, but treat them like a normal person, rather than being highly formal, or caught up with awareness of status

    -   Give sincere compliments

        -   Note - **sincere** is important, flattery tends to be easy to see through

        -   Explicitly say when things they say are helpful - be specific, and say how it helped you

    -   If what they said helped you later on, send them a message of thanks! Be as specific and concrete as possible - this is a good signal of sincerity over flattery because it’s harder to fake. It’s _really_ satisfying to receive these, especially if you can point to concrete actions or successes you had as a direct consequence

-   Giving advice is fun! It’s nice to feel as though you’re helping somebody else. So try to be clear about exactly how this is helpful to you!

-   Respect their time, and be as convenient for them as possible - I like to send people a Calendly link and be otherwise flexible.

-   Be helpful

    -   Think about things you could offer them - people you know and could introduce them to, good resources and articles, interesting insights and perspectives.

        -   This one gets easier the more people you know!

        -   Note - this can be embarrassing if done wrong. Always broach it as a question, eg “have you heard of \_\_\_”, or “do you know \_\_\_”. Keep the initial question short, so that if they _do_ already know about this, it’s not awkward

Present opportunities

Another helpful mindset: **people can just say no**. Thus, other than the cost of saying no, reaching out is **presenting them with an opportunity**. And receiving opportunities is pleasant! It gives them more option value. I find this mindset _super_ useful for overcoming insecurity about bothering people, or looking silly.

If you’re looking for a job or internship, if that’s successful, _everyone_ wins. That’s a great opportunity for all involved! Thus presenting people with something like that is hardly high cost.

If you want advice from somebody, that can often be fun to give! An opportunity doesn’t have to _directly_ benefit them. I know I personally have gotten a _ton_ of useful advice from people in the past, and I can hardly pay _them_ back. So it’s satisfying to be able to pay it forwards, and give advice to people like my past self.

And it’s satisfying to help somebody else succeed! Especially within the Effective Altruism community - if your goal is to make the world a better place, **people actively want to help you succeed**. Because you’re all on the same team - if you can do more good, then everyone wins.

Minimise costs

The main cost to them of you reaching out is if they have to say no - this is emotional labour. I try to ensure I give them out’s eg “no worries if you’re busy”. Sometimes I send an obviously copied and pasted message, if I want to signal “only say yes to this if it feels fun to you”

Further, **be OK with hearing a no**. This isn’t a rejection, or a sign that you’ve bothered somebody - you’ve presented them with an opportunity they weren’t interested in. They still benefited from the option value, and this isn’t a personal attack on you - often people are just busy! I see this as a very stochastic process - you contact a lot of people, and a few are interested. And if everyone benefits in those few connections, then the original opportunity was valuable!

A useful framing - the busier and higher status somebody is, the better they are at saying no to things! You fundamentally _can’t_ be busy and high-status without developing the skill of prioritising and saying no, there are just too many things to do. And thus, the higher status they are, the _lower_ the cost of reaching out - I find this framing pretty insightful and unintuitive. And this goes all the more for reaching out to a _company_ rather than a specific person - it’s less personal and thus even lower emotional effort.

Taking action

So, how to actually act upon all this? As with most forms of taking the initiative, this is hard, and subjective, and will depend heavily on your personal circumstances. A few thoughts:

-   If you already know somebody who could help you, get over your reluctance and ask

-   If you meet interesting people at events, keep in touch!

-   If you vaguely know somebody, and they could help you with something, reach out and ask!

-   Ask friends for introductions - even if you just have a general kind of person in mind, ask if your friends know anyone who could help with that

    -   And if your friends are in need of advice, see who you might be able to connect them with! People respond pretty well to messages like “a friend of mine needs help with \_\_\_, would you be interested in chatting with them?”

-   Cold message people on LinkedIn

    -   A friend of mine has had a surprising amount of success with messaging people in fields he wants to enter, and asking for advice and mentorship.

-   Cold email people whose work you admire - academics with interesting papers, people who make content you enjoy

    -   Reaching out to academics with specific comments and thoughts on their papers can go down super well! It’s nice to see other people meaningfully engage with your work, and to see that they’re sincerely interested in you, rather than just blindly spamming people.

### Conclusion

For all these reasons, I think taking the social initiative is good and something people should do more often. And that it’s a skill which is very widely applicable in daily life. I’ve found that setting a rule of systematically doing it more often has been a _major_ life upgrade. Of course, this is an empirical claim, and isn’t _obviously_ true. And it’s _hard_. So I imagine a good amount of those reading this will kinda buy my arguments, but not feel convinced on a gut level, and be storing this as “an interesting idea I’ll never act upon”.

That feeling is the feeling of **uncertainty.** This is an important question, and an important empirical claim. If you are systematically biased against taking the initiative, are systematically missing out on valuable opportunities, and could learn to be better at it, then that’s _incredibly_ valuable information. The value of that information _vastly_ outweighs the costs of trying it a few times, and potential failure. And when there’s valuable information you lack, the solution is to [run an experiment and try it](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/the-value-of-experiments)! From that perspective, failure ceases to be a meaningful concept - whatever happens will reduce your uncertainty, and _that_ is the true victory.

Further, **taking the initiative is habit forming**. The activation energy required goes down _massively_ once you’ve tried it a few times and it’s gone well. And with time, taking the social initiative can become part of your identity, and [something you feel excited about](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about). Most of the energy goes to overcoming your intuitions, and intuitions come from your experiences. So if you’re reading all this, and this feels compelling, but a bit outside your comfort zone, I urge you to think about how you could [take the first step](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step). Because the first step is by far the hardest.

What are the things you want to do, but aren’t brave enough to start? What opportunities are you currently missing out on? What could you do, as the first step to **becoming the** **kind of person you want to be**?
