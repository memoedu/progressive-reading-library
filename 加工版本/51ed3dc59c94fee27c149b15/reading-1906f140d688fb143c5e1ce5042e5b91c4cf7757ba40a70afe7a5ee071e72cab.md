## 引言

那些伟大的 religions 长存至今。在今天活着的八十亿人中，有六十亿人将自己认同为 Christian、Islamic、Hindu 或 Buddhist[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn1)。这些 religions 中最年轻的 Islam，也已有大约 1,400 年的历史。尽管这些 religions 的 collective influence 有过起落，它们依然极具 influential 的力量。为什么这种 influence 能持续如此之久？

这种延续令人困惑的一个原因是，在许多 core issues 上——比如如何 live well、如何 organize 一个 society、humanity 如何 originated——我们今天的理解远远超过这些 religions 形成时的理解。遗憾的是，古老的 religions 只是缓慢而且有些生硬地 integrated 了这种更深入的理解。想想它们对 Galileo 和 Darwin 的 ideas 的 opposition，更不用说更现代的 ideas 了。我相信，如果 religion 能彻底 integrated 这种现代的理解，就会得到很大的 improved。遗憾的是，尽管这些 religions 的许多 members 都在真诚地努力，要 change core texts 来 integrate 那些 ideas，往往几乎是不可能的。因此，虽然古老的 religions 确实有所衰落，但更令我惊讶的是，它们竟然没有完全消失，没有被某种 secular tradition，或被能够 integrate 现代的理解并持续与之同步的新的 dominant religions 所取代。

在这些粗略的笔记中，我围绕这些 religions 为何依然如此 influential 随意展开思考。我不会回答这个问题——我知道的还不够多，无法作答。相反，我会按照自己的兴趣探索，关注这个问题中我感兴趣的几个方面，而略去其他许多方面。这番探索带有游戏性质，而且坦白说，与总的问题只有松散的联系——随着探索的推进，这些笔记逐渐至少同样多地、甚至更多地转向了把 religious texts 作为 literary forms 来理解[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn2)。探索的三组主要问题是：

1.  我们能否利用 story，让几乎所有 human beings 都对我们最深刻的现代 scientific ideas 获得强烈的 felt experience，并对它们如何 inform 我们在世界中的 understanding 和 action 形成 integrated 的感受？
2.  那些 influential 的 religious texts 讲述了哪些 grand overarching stories？myth 的 role 是什么？讲述这些 stories 的现代版本存在哪些 barriers？
3.  在 religion 中，是否可能存在某种关于“progress”的 notion？为什么古老的 religions 一直如此 resistant to change？是否有可能 design 能够 change 和 improve 的 religions？

这些问题与古老的 religions 为何依然如此 influential 有什么关系，或许并不明显。但这些问题的共同之处在于，它们都在 identifying：要 construct 具备我们可能期望现代 religion 具有的 qualities 的 religions，究竟在哪些方面出人意料地_困难_。这或许能部分解释为什么古老的 religions 依然存在：它们为 human beings 做了数量惊人的好事，而这些做法比乍看之下更难 improve upon。

## 我们能否 fuse story 与 science？

古老的 religious texts 中有许多精彩的 stories。我想简要思考一下 story，以便更好地理解，它如何同时充当 ideas 和人们对世界的 experience 的 carrier。stories 的一大 benefit 是，它们不只是 describe，还帮助我们 empathize。Roger Ebert 曾将 movies 描述为“generating empathy 的 machines”，而 stories 总体上也是如此。它们让我们 share 别人的 experience：我们看到他们所见的事物；感受到他们的感受；知道他们面临什么问题；什么让他们快乐、悲伤、愤怒、欣喜；他们如何理解世界；以及他们为什么以那样的方式行动。philosopher Thomas Nagel 指出，无论我们把一只 bat describe 得多么详尽——它的 physiology、behaviour、neuroanatomy 等等——都无法 convey _成为_一只 bat 是什么样的感受。description 与 experience 之间存在 gap。不过，尽管 pure description 无论多好，都无法 convey 成为他者是什么样的感受，出色的 storytelling 却能让我们对一个与自己非常不同的 protagonist 产生 emphathize。storyteller 越出色，其 art 越深厚，我们就越能对与自己不同的 beings 产生 empathize，理解_成为_他们意味着什么。好的 stories 会 change 并 expand 我们的 consciousness[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn3)。

一个著名的 example 是 Vladimir Nabokov 的“Lolita”。其中的 point of view character——rapist pedophile Humbert Humbert——让我们感到恐惧。但这种恐惧的一部分，恰恰在于发现我们至少能对他的 personality 的某些部分产生一点 empathize。在生活中，我们常常通过彻底排斥 evil 来应对它——我们把世界上那些 Ted Bundy's、Adolph Hitler's 和 Humbert Humberts' 妖魔化。发现他们在某些方面与我们颇为相似，令人不快，也令人不安。第二个 example 甚至比“Lolita”更有雄心（！），那就是 Ted Chiang 的短篇“Story of Your Life”。它让我们以令人信服的方式，experience 一个能够看穿时间的人的 consciousness。我觉得它如此引人入胜，以至于阅读时有时会感到相当恍惚。实在非同寻常。

当然，story 不只是为我们提供 empathetic experience。当一个 character 理解了强大的 ideas，story 就能让我们获得一个拥有那种 understanding 的人的 experience。伟大的 religious texts 反复利用这种可能性，有时用得极为出色。仅举一个 example，Christian Bible 不只是 abstract 地向我们劝说为他人 sacrifice 的 value，或提供有关 sacrifice 为什么以及何时可能是好事的 descriptions。不，它直接向我们展示人们 making sacrifices 的 experience：他们为什么这么做，何时这么做，是什么感受，以及 consequences 是什么。最有力量的是，我们看到了 Jesus 在接近 death 时的 fear 和 pain，也看到了他内在的 motives：对 humanity 的 love 和对 God 的 obedience；以及由此带来的 redemption。你完全可以合理地质疑这些 ideas 是否说得通——关于 Crucifixion 的 game theory 和 political economy，值得写一篇出色的 critical essay——但在 emotionally 的层面，passion of Jesus 的 story 远比 purely descriptive content 更有力量，同时仍承载着嵌入其中的 descriptive content。

我们如何、又在什么情况下，才能以这种 narrative form 提供对我们最好的现代 ideas 的 experience？在某些 domains，这已经常常做得很好。例如，我们的伟大 writers 不仅极其出色地展现 psychology，还原创了许多最深刻的 insights。在 explaining human experience 方面，Shakespeare、Austen、Tolstoy 及其同行往往不仅超越了古老的 religious texts，也超越了现代的 psychology texts。我们能否在所有 domains 中，都提供同样深刻的、理解我们最好的现代 ideas 的 experiences？仅举一个 example：能否讲述一个出色的 story，不只是 descriptively 地 explain relativity，而是真正让我们像 Einstein（及其 successors）那样看待和 experience 世界？这或许要求太高了：拥有 Einstein 那种 capability 的 mind 的人实在太少！但问一问我们究竟能深入到什么程度，仍然很有意思。你更愿意听 Albert Einstein 的 ideas？还是拥有 Einstein 的 experience，甚至至少在某种微小的程度上成为 Albert Einstein？

如果 Jesus、Buddha 或 Muhammad 深入理解了 Einstein、Darwin、Adam Smith 的 ideas——事实上，理解了现代 economics、sociology、biology、cosmology、behavioural genetics、history、evolutionary psychology、neuroscience 等等领域中所有最好的 ideas——religion 会发生怎样的 changed？我确实知道，我会非常想听听，他们在深入理解这些 ideas 中的任何一种，或（尤其是）全部之后，会作何回应。我猜他们[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn4)会说出一些非常令人惊讶的话。我也想知道，religious texts——以及它们帮助催生的 religions——会发生怎样的 changed。我猜它们会得到很大的 improved。我们许多最好的 ideas，都与古老的 religious texts 处于极强的 tension 之中。我常常想，如果 Bible 明确反对 slavery 和 racism，abolitionism 会提前多久扎根。或者，考虑一些更微妙的问题，一些我们至今仍在苦苦思索的问题。许多 religions 都有某种 personal responsibility 和 reward for merit 的 notion——现代 behavioural genetics 是否应该促使这些 notion 被 modified？再过一个世纪，当我们能更详尽地说明你的 genome——你没有选择、而是被赋予你的东西——如何 influences 你的 behaviour 时，又会怎样？我们永远不会知道，Jesus、Buddha 或 Muhammad 会如何看待 IVF、behavioural genetics、human rights law 或 Big Bang cosmology。我真希望我们能够知道。

遗憾的是，把 stories 用作 ideas 的 carriers 时，完全可能用得很差[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn5)。挑战在于 uncover 有力量的 stories，让它们_同时_提供一种引人入胜的 experience，使人理解深刻的 ideas 及其 implications。人们常常讨论 combining science 与 religion，或 combining science 与 art 的“问题”。但我认为，一个更紧迫的问题是 fusing story 与 science。（我在这里以非常宽泛的意思使用“science”，指的几乎是对世界的任何 descriptive understanding。）我猜，这样的 fusion 能让我们写出 stories，为这样的问题提供有力量的 answers：“理解 Einstein 的 ideas 是什么样的感受？不只是理解一点点；甚至不只是 surface-level symbolic understanding；而是真正地、深入地、emotionally 地、发自肺腑地理解，并与自己对世界的全部 experience integrated 在一起，这是什么样的感受？”“理解 Charles Darwin 的 ideas 是什么样的感受？深入理解 humanity 的 animal origins 及其对我们的 behaviour 的 influence，是什么样的感受？理解 genetic influences 对我们生活的作用，是什么样的感受？它如何 change 你对其他人的 actions 的 judgements？又如何 change 你对自己的 actions 的 judgements？”“理解 mathematics 是什么样的感受？”“自己的 theory 被 experiment refuted，是什么样的感受？”“前往 stars 是什么样的感受？”用 story 以引人入胜的方式回答这些问题，会 expand 大多数人的 consciousness。然而，fusing story 与 science 的这个问题，还没有得到很好的解决。因此，这些 ideas 还没有很好地 integrated 到我们 shared 的 humanity 的 story 之中。话虽如此，Chiang 和 Nabokov 当时也在解决极其困难的 narrative problems！他们所 conveying 的 consciousnesses 非常陌生——诚然，没有 bat 的那么陌生，但依然非常陌生，在某些方面或许与 Einstein 和 Darwin 的 consciousnesses 一样陌生。因此，story 与 science 的_良好_ fusion 或许是可能的。

对于这个问题，我还没有经过充分检验的 solution。不过，我曾尝试过一种相关的 literary form：_[discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html)_。在 discovery fiction 中，writer constructs 一段 narrative，讲述我们原本可能如何 discovered 某个 complex idea 或多个 ideas：“一条由小的 questions 和 ideas、false starts 和 backtracking 构成的 arc，经过 incremental steps，最终通向 \[the target idea\]。这些东西都不应凭空出现：它们都应该是 simple、几乎显而易见的 steps。”例如，我写过一篇关于如何 [discover cryptocurrency](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/) 的 discovery fiction；[这里](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/df.html)还有更多 discovery fiction 的 examples。

Discovery fiction 给我们一种 idea 如何产生的 experience。它的好处在于，它从 reader 所处的位置、从他们已经 understand 的内容出发，然后逐渐 changes 他们的 consciousness，使其成为一个能够看出这个 idea _why_ 会是如此的人的 consciousness。但 powerful ideas 不只是 stand alone，它们还会 remake 我们对 world 的 experience。假设你刚刚 understood 某个 powerful idea——为了具体一些，就选 comparative advantage 这个 idea——也许是通过 discovery fiction。但理想情况下，你不会只是 abstractly understand 这个 idea。你会学会看到 comparative advantage 在 world 中 arises 的种种不同地方，也会学会 infer consequences。因此，_well-fortified discovery fiction_ 展示的不仅是对一个 idea 的 basic form 的 discovery，还包括学习在 world 中看到这个 idea，以及学习看到许多 consequences 的 process。并不是所有 consequences，因为我们永远不会完全完成将 deep ideas integrating 到我们的 experience 之中。但至少要对这个 idea 可能如何 remake 我们对 world 的 experience 有所感受。

Discovery fiction 通常并不具有 story（或 non-fiction）在通常意义上的 beautiful。这样的 beauty 通常产生于 text 以 vividness 和 clarity 揭示 powerful 而 surprising 的 relationships 之时。相比之下，discovery fiction 涉及大量 detailed routine analysis 和 wrong turns。只有经过那种朴实无华的 exploration process 之后，我们才能将 understanding boil down，展示我们的 exploration 已经 uncovered 的 surprising relationships。这个 limitation 似乎是 discovery fiction 所 inherent 的。另一方面，逐渐 understand 的 experience 也有它自身的 beauty。而 discovery fiction 至少可以在一个 idea 已经被 understood 之后，包含对它的 beautiful distillations。

暂且离开 discovery fiction，当 understanding scientific ideas 确实是 story 所迫使的事情时，conventional stories 有时也能 fuse story 和 science。"Story of Your Life" 在一定程度上做到了这一点；一个更有力的 example 是 Carl Sagan 的 novel "Contact"。"Contact" 的 protagonist 是 astronomer Ellie Arroway，她与一个 alien civilization 进行了 first contact。随着阅读，我们逐渐 understand 她对 world 的许多 experience：universe 有多么 large；humanity 有多么 tiny；还有多少事物仍是 unknown，仍有待 discovered。她并不只是把 mathematics 和 astronomy 当作枯燥的 fact 来 understand。它们以与 story directly relevant 的方式，deeply permeate 她的整个生活。因此，随着阅读，我们更好地 understand 拥有这样的 understanding 会是怎样的；它 expands 我们的 consciousnes，使其 encompass 那些 ideas。

当然，以这种方式使用 conventional fiction 有着严格的 limits。在 conventional fiction 中，一个我们能够 empathize with 的 protagonist 面临着一个（believable、interesting 的）problem。Explaining 他们如何 understand world 并不是 story 的 primary purpose；我们想看的其实是 protagonist 如何 overcomes story 中的 problems。试图将 explanation of ideas 放在中心位置的 Authors 会毁掉自己的 story。不过，当 author 确实找到了一个值得讲述的好 story，就像 "Contact" 那样，这可以成为 fusing story 和 science 的一种 powerful approach。相比之下，discovery fiction 既更 flexible，也能够 sustain 深得多的 involvement，因为它一开始的 purpose 就是 discover 某个 interesting idea 或一组 ideas——例如 comparative advantage、quantum mechanics 或 behavioural genetics。为此 motivate reader，可能需要相当用心。但一旦 reader 真正投入其中，它就确实有了力量。

fusing story 和 science 的第三种 approach 是 narrative non-fiction，由 Tom Wolfe、John McPhee 和 Joan Didion 等 writers 开创。这类 works 往往讲述某位 world-leading expert 及其工作的 story——例如 McPhee 的 "The Curve of Binding Energy" 中的 nuclear physicist 与 nuclear weaponry，或 Wolfe 的 "The Right Stuff" 中最勇敢的 fighter pilots 和 astronauts。这是 discovery fiction 的一种 embodied form，在其中，我们不是 discovering 一个 idea，而是 discover 一个人。那个人的 deep expertise 常常让他们起初显得 strange 而 foreign，但随着我们逐渐 understand 这个人，我们也逐渐 understand 那些 underpinning 他们对 world 的 experience 的 ideas。

总之：stories 提供一种 being 的 experience。一种 understand 某些事物，并通过那种 lens 去 experience 整个 world 的 experience。这与 traditional descriptive non-fiction explanation 非常不同，而且可能深刻得多。这一点很重要，因为自古以来，我们已经如此 profoundly deepened 了对 world 的 understanding，但这些 deepened understanding 中的许多内容，并未被 incorporated 到 humanity 的 grand narrative stories 之中。如果 Jesus 或 Buddha 已经 deeply understood humanity 在 animal kingdom 中的 origins，他们还会是原来的样子吗？如果是 modern cosmology 呢？或 modern theory of justice 呢？或 abundance 从何而来呢？我怀疑他们会 profoundly different。因此，ancient religions persist 的一个原因，是我们还没有找到将 improved understanding narrativize 的方式；我们还没有 fully solved fusing story 和 science 这个 problem。但 discovery fiction、narrative non-fiction，甚至 conventional fiction（尽管有相当大的 limitations）这样的 approaches，似乎是实现这一点的有希望的方式。

## 将 Religious texts 视为 grand stories；将 religious texts 视为 myth；是什么使 myth 难以 construct？

religious canon 不只是围绕几个重要 themes organized 起来的一组好 stories。我最熟悉的 religious text——Christian Bible——可以被视为一个单一的 grand story，讲述 humanity 如何从其 original sin 中被 saved。这个 story 包含许多 sub-stories，而它们本身也是 grand stories：humanity 的 story；成为 enlightened 是怎样一种感受的 story；humanity 与 God 的 relationship 的 story；universe 的 story，以及 humans 如何 relate 到它。而在叙述过程中讲述的所有更小的 stories，都服务于那些（overlapping 的）grand overarching stories；反过来，overarching stories 又赋予 individual stories 多得多的 meaning。一般来说，不同 religions 往往以不同 proportions mix 不同的 grand stories，当然，在某些 texts 中，story 只起 subsidary role。一些常见的 grand stories 如下：

-   你的 story：如何 live well，如何 choose well
-   你的 family 的 story
-   你的 people 的 story
-   humanity 的 story
-   human origins 的 story
-   那位 enlightened one 的 story[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn6)
-   future 的 story
-   humanity 的 end 的 story
-   universe 及其 origins 的 story
-   humanity 与 universe 的 relationship 的 story
-   God 的 story
-   humanity 与 God 的 relationship 的 story

我所说的 "grand story"（大致）是指：(a) 一个通过 smaller stories 和其他 commentary 组成的 mosaic 来展开的 overarching story；以及 (b) 一个探讨具有 deep、widespread，而且可以说是 intrinsic interest 的 problem 的 story。如果我与一个 uncontacted tribe 进行了 first contact，发现他们拥有上述所有 types 的 stories，我不会感到惊讶。事实上，如果 alien intelligences 也有这样的 stories，我同样不会惊讶！不是 _所有_ alien intelligences。但有些会有。因此，对 grand story 的一个带点玩笑意味的 definition 是：一个探讨具有 broad sentient interest 的 problem 的 story[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn7)。

除了 Religious texts，我最喜欢的许多 books（以及其他 media）也是 grand stories。Carl Sagan 的 "Cosmos" 是 universe 的 story，是 humanity 在其中的 place 的 story，也是我们如何逐渐认识它的 story。Olaf Stapledon 的 "Last and First Men" 是 humanity 的 future 的 story。Stapledon 的 "Starmaker" 是 universe 的 story。Melodysheep（John Boswell）制作的 videos 往往也是 grand stories——universe 的 past 的 history；universe 的 future 的 history；各种 alien civilizations 的 history。我在这里说的是 "story"，而有一个耐人寻味的事实：它们没有一部是 classic narrative pieces，即使是通常被归入 fiction 的 Stapledon 的作品也不例外。然而，每一部首先都是一段 emotional journey，关注 deeply personal questions，而 descriptive element 则居于次要位置。也许正因如此，我觉得“……的 story”是最贴切的描述。看看 "Cosmos" 第二章开头的几行：

> 我一生都在思索别处存在 life 的 possibility。它会是什么样子？它会由什么 made？我们 planet 上所有 living things 都是由 organic molecules constructed 的——它们是 complex microscopic architectures，carbon atom 在其中 plays a central role。曾经有过一个尚无 life 的时代，那时 Earth 荒芜而全然死寂。如今，我们的 world 充满了 life。它是如何产生的？在没有 life 的情况下，carbon-based organic molecules 是如何 made 的？最初的 living things 是如何 arise 的？life 如何 evolve，produce 出像我们这样 elaborate 而 complex、能够 explore 自身 origins 之 mystery 的 beings？而在无数其他可能 circle 其他 suns 的 planets 上，是否也有 life？如果 extraterrestrial life 确实 exists，它是否和 Earth 上的 life 一样 based on 相同的 organic molecules？其他 worlds 的 beings 看起来是否很像 Earth 上的 life？还是说，它们 stunningly different——是对其他 environments 的其他 adaptations？还有什么是 possible 的？Earth 上 life 的 nature 与对别处 life 的 search，是同一个 question 的两个方面——对我们是谁的 search。

这些并不是枯燥的、descriptive 的 textbook questions。这些是 deeply personal questions，为 writer 和 reader 同样铺设了一个 mystery 和一段 emotional journey。Sagan 的 form 常常让人想到 non-fiction，但他的 text 真正 conveys 的是“一段个人旅程”（这也是他所选的 subtitle），一部记录他 emotional understanding 如何改变、同时也带着 reader 一同前行的 memoir。

在含糊地 defined 了 grand story 之后，让我转向另一个难以 define 的 term：myth。我并不自称 understand myth 是什么。但有一种有用且 plausible 的 meaning：myth 是一种帮助一个 people define shared identity 的 grand story。从这个意义上说，所有这些 ancient religions 都具有 myth 或 myths 的 collections 的 character[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn8)[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn9)[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn10)。

这与 ancient religions 为什么 persist 有什么关系？一个 speculative answer 是：也许今天 constructing 好的 myths 存在严重障碍。当然，许多单独的 grand stories 都已出版：self-help genre 中有数千本关于你的 story 的书；现代 pop cosmology 全都围绕 universe 及其 origins 的 story；诸如此类。但今天很少有书包含由 grand stories 构成的 overlapping mosaic，足以支撑一个 well-formed shared identity。大多数作者缺乏这样写作所需的广博见识：能写你的 story 的人，很可能无法写 humanity 的 origins 的 story，或 humans 与 universe 之间 relationship 的 story。（我欣赏 Sagan 的 "Cosmos" 的一点是，单个段落便能无缝地从 science 穿行至 arts、religion、history、psychology，再转到 science 的另一个 branch；很少有作者具备做到这一点所需的广博理解。）相比之下，古代 religious texts 的作者发展出了由 overlapping grand stories 构成的惊人 mosaic；他们的一些 ideas 如今显得 naive；但我们许多人宽容这些疏漏，欣赏他们以 unified 的方式探讨重大问题。换句话说：我怀疑，今天 myth 对理解的要求就是高得多。古代 religious texts 得以享有既往地位带来的豁免：当 text 富有洞见时（它们往往如此），religion 的成员便给予极高评价；当它们并非如此时，也会予以放过。但那些想为 humanity 创造新 myths 的人，却得不到同样的宽容。

## religious progress 是否可能？如何 design 能够 change 的 religions？

humanity 最伟大的 inventions 之一，是那些_为 radical change 而 designed_的 institutions。Democracies 让 overthrow of power 不再罕见且令人痛苦，而是 routine 且 orderly，按既定时间进行。Capitalism 围绕 competition 和 creative destruction 建立，由 startups replacing incumbents，或 originating 全新的 product categories。Science 围绕 competition[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn11) 和 ideas 的 creative destruction 建立，由 improved ideas replacing incumbents，或 originating 全新的 fields。在每种情况下，相关 institutions 都围绕某种 replacement 的 process 建立。我说的是 "institutions"，但不是指单一的 centralized organization，那可能 bottleneck change；而是指一套 norms、processes 和 organizations，共同帮助 decentralized change 发生。这些 institutions 并不 work perfectly。但从长远来看，它们在 enabling human progress 方面都取得了非凡成效。

相比之下，古代 religions 并非为 change 而 designed，而是为 stasis 而 designed。从历史上看，这并不令人意外：这些 religions 起源时，change 往往以 war、famine 或 disease 的形式出现。现代 progress 的 notion——change 带来 improvement——当时尚处于 nascent 阶段。因此，尽管古代 religions 当然_确实_ changed，它们往往极不情愿地才作出那些 changes。一个 emblematic example 是：直到 1992(!)，Catholic Church 才为以 heresy 罪名 trying Galileo 并 imprisoning 他而道歉。在 Islam 中，religion 中的 innovation 被称为 bid'ah，被视为一种 sin[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn12)。仅仅是 heresy 和 bid'ah 这些 notions 的存在，就暗示了一种对 change 默认持怀疑态度的立场。据我理解，Hinduism 和 Buddhism 稍微不那么 rigid，但仍然相当紧密地遵循古代 doctrine。相比之下，science、capitalism 和 democracy 都推崇各自的 innovators，至少在 theory 上如此，在 practice 中也常常如此。

哪些 elements 能让 system 具备 change 的能力？让我提出两个 observations。其一，system 需要某种 mechanism，用来 identifying 和 amplifying improvements。identification 和 amplification 越好，就越 effective；反过来，如果 identification 或 amplification 较弱，就不会很 effective。在 democracy 中，这种 mechanism 是 voting；在 capitalism 中，是 market 和 consumer demand；在 science 中，是 experiment 的 test，以及更广泛的 scientific community 中某种 ideas 的 market。religion 的 progress 所能采用的 analogous mechanism 是什么，并不那么显而易见。但思考可能的 mechanisms 很有趣！我稍后会回到这个问题。

第二，促成 stasis 的一种 force，是围绕某个或某些 fixed canonical text 的强烈 orientation。我有时把 Christianity 想成一大群人一起非常深入地阅读 Bible。是的，它也是 communities 和 institutions 的庞大集合——但这种 collective reading 才是其核心。我不是说每个人都参与阅读，而是有一个核心群体确实读得非常深入，而且是一起读[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn13)。虽然这是一个美好的画面和 idea，但那种 centralized focus 也 inhibits change。尽管 science、capitalism 或 democracy 也有 canonical books——比如 Hobbes 的 "Leviathan"，或 Schumpeter 的 "Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy"——但它们在 community 中远没有那么 central。阅读并相信 Hobbes 或 Schumpeter，并不是你作为 democracy 中的 citizen 或作为 capitalist 的 identity 的核心。即使最伟大的 science 书籍——比如 Newton 的 "Principia" 或 Darwin 的 "Origin of Speces"——最终也往往会被 superseded。相比之下，religions 对 fixed、central canonical texts 的 orientation 是一股巨大的 inertial force，preventing change。

我们能 design dynamic religious systems 吗？或者，退一步说：对于这样的问题又如何——lead a good life 意味着什么？当然，许多 communities 确实尝试以某种 dynamic、decentralized 的方式回答后一个问题。positive psychology、developmental economics、effective altruism (EA)、moral philosophy，以及整个 political sphere 中的很大一部分，都致力于为这个问题提供 answers："lead a good life 意味着什么？" 的确，我曾在[别处](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/)指出："在某种意义上，EA 是一种尝试，希望为‘什么是 good？’这个问题做到 science 为‘world 如何 work？’这个问题所做到的事情。它不是提供一个 answer，而是在 developing 一个旨在 continually improve 这个 answer 的 community。" 以一种不同（但 overlapping）的方式，positive psychology 和 developmental economics 也是如此。每个 community 都可以 split 成两个 parts：一部分关注 developing 关于 good 的 abstract notion（happiness scales、DALYs 和 QALYs、discounted future utility 等）；另一部分则使用那些 measures，来 study 不同 actions 的 "goodness"。在某种意义上，这种 division 让它能够尽可能将 ought reduce 为 is，第二部分主要关注 "is" questions。而对于 _is_ 的 questions，我们已有相对不错的 decentralized approaches 来实现 progress。online forums 和 academic literature 中相当 robust 的 discussion 对此有所帮助。不幸的是，这种讨论仍然受到 charisma，以及 money 和 power 在少数 organizations 中的 centralization 所造成的显著 distortions。那些 organizations 和 communities 究竟会有多 dynamic，仍有待观察。

By Michael Nielsen

## Introduction

The great religions endure. Of the eight billion people alive today, six billion identity as Christian, Islamic, Hindu, or Buddhist[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn1). The youngest of those religions, Islam, is roughly 1,400 years old. While the religions' collective influence has waxed and waned, they remain extremely influential. Why has this influence persisted for so long?

One reason the persistence is puzzling is that on many core issues – things like how to live well, how to organize a society, how humanity originated – we understand far more today than when these religions were developed. Unfortunately, the ancient religions have only slowly and somewhat awkwardly integrated that improved understanding. Think of the opposition to the ideas of Galileo and Darwin, not to mention more modern ideas. I believe religion would be much improved if it thoroughly integrated this modern understanding. Unfortunately, despite sincere work by many members of these religions, it is often next-to-impossible to change core texts to integrate those ideas. So while the ancient religions have indeed somewhat faded, what I find much more surprising is that they haven't disappeared entirely, replaced either by some secular tradition or by new dominant religions that integrate and remain current with modern understanding.

In these rough notes I riff on why these religions continue to be so influential. I won't answer the question – I don't know enough to do so. Rather, I explore idiosyncratically, focusing on a few aspects of the question of interest to me, while omitting many other aspects. The exploration is playful, and frankly only loosely related to the overall question – as I explored the notes gradually became at least as much or more about understanding religious texts as literary forms[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn2). The three main groups of questions explored are:

1.  Can we use story to give nearly all human beings a strong felt experience of our deepest modern scientific ideas, an integrated sense of how they inform our understanding and action in the world?
2.  What are the grand overarching stories told by influential religious texts? What is the role of myth? What barriers are there to telling modern versions of these stories?
3.  Is there some notion of "progress" possible in religion? Why have the ancient religions been so resistant to change? Is it possible to design religions to change and improve?

It's perhaps not obvious what these questions have to do with why the ancient religions continue to be so influential. But what these questions have in common is that all are about identifying ways in which it's surprisingly _hard_ to construct religions with the qualities we might desire in a modern religion. That perhaps goes part of the way to explaining why the ancient religions are still around: they do a surprisingly large number of good things for human beings, in ways harder to improve upon than might at first appear.

## Can we fuse story and science?

There are many great stories in the ancient religious texts. I want to briefly reflect on story, to better understand how it works as a carrier both of ideas and of people's experience of the world. One big benefit of stories is that they don't just describe, they also help us empathize. Roger Ebert once described movies as "machines for generating empathy", and this is true of stories in general. They let us share other people's experience: we see what they see; what they feel; what their problems are; what makes them happy, sad, angry, elated; how they understand the world; and why they act the way they act. The philosopher Thomas Nagel has pointed out that no matter how well we describe a bat – its physiology, behaviour, neuroanatomy, and so on – it won't convey what it is it like to _be_ a bat. There is a gap between description and experience. But while pure description, no matter how good, doesn't convey what it is like to be the other, great storytelling enables us to emphathize with a protagonist who is very different to ourselves. The better the storyteller, and the deeper their art, the more we can empathize with beings unlike ourselves, to understand what it is to _be_ them. Good stories change and expand our consciousness[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn3).

A famous example is Vladimir Nabokov's "Lolita". We are horrified by the point of view character, the rapist pedophile Humbert Humbert. But part of the horror is finding we can empathize at least a little with parts of his personality. Often in life we cope with evil by rejecting it completely – we demonize the Ted Bundy's and Adolph Hitler's and Humbert Humberts' of the world. Finding that they are in some ways quite similar to us is unpleasant and troubling. A second example, even more ambitious (!) than "Lolita", is Ted Chiang's short "Story of Your Life". This gives us a convincing experience of the consciousness of someone who can see through time. I find it so compelling that at times I become quite disoriented as I read. It's extraordinary.

Of course, story isn't just about giving us an empathetic experience. When a character understands powerful ideas, story can provide us with the experience of someone with that understanding. The great religious texts use this possibility repeatedly, sometimes brilliantly. As just one example, the Christian Bible doesn't merely advise us in the abstract of the value of sacrifice for others, or offer descriptions of why and when sacrifice can be a good thing. No, it directly shows us the experience of people making sacrifices: why they do it, when they do it, what it feels like, and what the consequences are. Most powerfully, we see Jesus's fear and pain as he approaches his death, as well as his underlying motives of love for humanity and obedience to God; and the redemption that results in. You can reasonably question whether the ideas make sense – there's a good critical essay to be written about the game theory and political economy of the Crucifixion – but emotionally the story of the passion of Jesus is far more powerful than purely descriptive content, while still carrying the descriptive content embedded.

How and when can we provide an experience of our best modern ideas in such a narrative form? In certain domains this is already often done well. For instance, our great writers not only show psychology superbly well, they've originated many of the deepest insights. Shakespeare, Austen, Tolstoy, and their colleagues often exceed not just ancient religious texts but also modern psychology texts in explaining human experience. Can we provide similarly deep experiences of understanding our best modern ideas across all domains? To pick just one example: is it possible to tell a superb story that doesn't merely explain relativity descriptively, but actually enables us to see and experience the world as Einstein (and his successors) saw it? This is perhaps too much to ask: very few people have a mind of Einstein's capability! But it is interesting to ask how deeply we can go? Would you rather hear Albert Einstein's ideas? Or have Einstein's experience, to even be Albert Einstein, at least in some small measure?

How would religion would have been changed if Jesus or the Buddha or Muhammad had deeply understood the ideas of Einstein, Darwin, Adam Smith – indeed, all the best ideas of modern economics, sociology, biology, cosmology, behavioural genetics, history, evolutionary psychology, neuroscience, and so on? I do know I would have loved to hear their response to deeply understanding any or (especially) all of these. I suspect they[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn4) would have had very surprising things to say. And I wonder how the religious texts – and the religions they helped spawn – would have been changed. I suspect they would have been much improved. So many of our best ideas are in very strong tension with the ancient religious texts. I often wonder how much sooner abolitionism would have taken hold if the Bible had taken a stand against slavery and racism. Or, consider more subtle questions, questions we're still struggling with. Many of the religions have some notion of personal responsibility and reward for merit – should those be modified by modern behavioural genetics? What about in another century, when we can say far more still about how your genome – something you didn't choose, but which was given to you – influences your behaviour. We'll never know how Jesus or the Buddha or Muhammad would have thought about IVF or behavioural genetics or human rights law or Big Bang cosmology. I wish we did.

It's unfortunately certainly possible to use stories poorly as carriers for ideas[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn5). The challenge is to uncover powerful stories that _also_ provide a compelling experience of understanding deep ideas and their implications. People often discuss the "problems" of combining science and religion, or of combining science and art. But I think a more urgent problem is fusing story and science. (I'm using "science" here very broadly, to mean pretty much any descriptive understanding of the world.) I suspect such a fusion would let us write stories to give powerful answers to questions like: "What is it like to understand Einstein's ideas? Not just a little; not even merely a surface-level symbolic understanding; but truly, deeply, emotionally, into your gut, in a way integrated with all your experience of the world?" "What is it like to understand Charles Darwin's ideas? What is it like to understand deeply the animal origins of humanity, and its influence on our behaviour? What is it like to understand genetic influences on our lives? How does it change your judgements of other people's actions? Of your own?" "What is it like to understand mathematics?" "What is it like to have your theory refuted by experiment?" "What is it like to go to the stars?" Using story to answer such questions in a compelling way would expand most people's consciousness. Yet this problem of fusing story and science hasn't yet been well-solved. And so these ideas aren't well integrated into our shared story of humanity. That said: Chiang and Nabokov were also solving extremely difficult narrative problems! They were conveying very foreign consciousnesses – not as foreign as a bat, true, but very foreign, perhaps as foreign in some regards as Einstein's and Darwin's. So perhaps a _good_ fusion of story and science is possible.

I don't have a well-tested solution to this problem. However, a related literary form I have experimented with is _[discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html)_. In discovery fiction the writer constructs a narrative of how we could have discovered some complex idea or ideas: "an arc of small questions and ideas, false starts and backtracking, incremental steps eventually leading to \[the target idea\]. None of these things should come out of thin air: they should all be simple, almost-obvious steps." For instance, I've written a piece of discovery fiction about how to [discover cryptocurrency](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/); there are many more examples of discovery fiction [here](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/df.html).

Discovery fiction gives us an experience of how an idea comes about. The benefit is that it starts where the reader is, with what they already understand, and then gradually changes their consciousness, to that of a person who can see _why_ the idea is the way it is. But powerful ideas don't just stand alone, they remake our experience of the world. Suppose you've just understood some powerful idea – let us pick the idea of comparative advantage, just for concreteness – perhaps through discovery fiction. But ideally you won't just understand the idea abstractly. You'll learn to see the many varied places comparative advantage arises in the world, and you'll learn to infer consequences. And so _well-fortified discovery fiction_ shows not merely the discovery of the basic form of an idea, but also this process of learning to see the idea in the world, and of learning to see many of the consequences. Not all the consequences, since we are never fully done integrating deep ideas into our experience. But of at least getting a sense of how the idea may remake our experience of the world.

Discovery fiction is not usually beautiful in the conventional sense a story (or non-fiction) is beautiful. Such beauty usually arises when a text reveals powerful and surprising relationships with vividness and clarity. By contrast, discovery fiction involves lots of detailed routine analysis and wrong turns. It is only after that meat-and-potatoes process of exploration that we can boil understanding down, showing the surprising relationships our exploration has uncovered. This limitation seems to be inherent in discovery fiction. On the other hand, the experience of coming to understand has its own beauty. And discovery fiction can at the least include beautiful distillations of an an idea, once it has been understood.

Moving away from discovery fiction, conventional stories can sometimes fuse story and science when understanding the scientific ideas is truly forced by the story. This happens to some extent in "Story of Your Life"; a stronger example is Carl Sagan's novel "Contact". The protagonist of "Contact" is an astronomer, Ellie Arroway, who makes first contact with an alien civilization. As we read we come to understand much of her experience of the world: how large the universe is; how tiny humanity is; how much remains unknown, still to be discovered. She doesn't just understand mathematics and astronomy as dry fact. They deeply permeate her entire life in ways directly relevant to the story. And so as we read we understand better what having such an understanding would be like; it expands our consciousnes to encompass those ideas.

Of course, there are tight limits on the use of conventional fiction in this way. In conventional fiction there is a (believable, interesting) problem facing a protagonist who we empathize with. Explaining how they understand the world is not the primary purpose of the story; rather, we're trying to see how the protagonist overcomes the problems of the story. Authors who try to center explanation of ideas will ruin their own story. Still, when the author has identified a genuinely good story to tell, as in "Contact", this can be a powerful approach to fusing story and science. By contrast, discovery fiction is both more flexible and also able to sustain a much deeper involvement, since the up-front purpose is to discover some interesting idea or set of ideas – something like comparative advantage, or quantum mechanics, or behavioural genetics. This can require some considerable care to motivate. But once a reader is bought in, there is real force to it.

A third approach to fusing story and science is narrative non-fiction, pioneered by writers such as Tom Wolfe, John McPhee, and Joan Didion. Such works often tell a story of some world-leading expert and their work – a nuclear physicist and nuclear weaponry, in McPhee's "The Curve of Binding Energy", or the most daring fighter pilots and astronauts, in Wolfe's "The Right Stuff". It's an embodied form of discovery fiction, where instead of discovering an idea we discover a person. That person's deep expertise often makes them strange and foreign at first, but as we gradually come to understand the person, we also come to understand the ideas underpinning their experience of the world.

Summing up: stories provide an experience of being. Of what it is like to understand certain things, and to experience the entire world through that lens. This is very different, and can be far deeper, than a traditional descriptive non-fiction explanation. This matters because we've so profoundly deepened our understanding of the world since ancient times, but much of that deepened understanding isn't incorporated into the grand narrative stories of humanity. Would Jesus or the Buddha have been the same if they'd deeply understood the origins of humanity in the animal kingdom? Or modern cosmology? Or the modern theory of justice? Or where abundance comes from? I suspect they would have been profoundly different. And so one reason the ancient religions persist is because we haven't yet found ways to narrativize our improved understanding; we haven't yet fully solved the problem of fusing story and science. But approaches like discovery fiction, narrative non-fiction, and even conventional fiction (with considerable limitations), seem promising ways of doing so.

## Religious texts as grand stories; religious texts as myth; what makes myth hard to construct?

A religious canon isn't just a collection of good stories organized around a few important themes. The Christian Bible, the religious text I know best, may be viewed as a single grand story of how humanity was saved from its original sin. This story has many sub-stories which are themselves grand stories: a story of humanity; a story of what it is like to be enlightened; a story of humanity's relationship to God; a story of the universe, and how humans relate to it. And all the still smaller stories told along the way serve those (overlapping) grand overarching stories; the overarching stories, in turn, give far more meaning to the individual stories. In general, the different religions tend to mix different grand stories in different proportions, and of course in some of the texts story only plays a subsidary role. A few commonly-occurring grand stories:

-   The story of you: how to live well, how to choose well
-   The story of your family
-   The story of your people
-   The story of humanity
-   The story of human origins
-   The story of the enlightened one[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn6)
-   The story of the future
-   The story of the end of humanity
-   The story of the universe and its origins
-   The story of humanity's relationship to the universe
-   The story of God
-   The story of humanity's relationship to God

By "grand story" I mean (roughly): (a) an overarching story which is addressed through a mosaic of smaller stories and other commentary; and (b) a story addressing a problem of deep widespread and arguably intrinsic interest. If I made first contact with an uncontacted tribe, I would not be surprised if they had stories of all the types listed above. Inded, I would not be surprised if alien intelligences had such stories! Not _all_ alien intelligences. But some. And so a playful definition of a grand story is a story addressing a problem of broad sentient interest[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn7).

Religious texts aside, many of my favorite books (and other media) are grand stories. Carl Sagan's "Cosmos" is the story of the universe, of humanity's place within it, and how we've come to know it. Olaf Stapledon's "Last and First Men" is the story of the future of humanity. Stapledon's "Starmaker" is the story of the universe. Melodysheep (John Boswell) creates videos that are often grand stories – a history of the past of the universe; of the future of the universe; of the variety of alien civilizations. I say "story" here, and it's a curious fact: none of these are classic narrative pieces, even Stapledon's work, which is usually shelved as fiction. And yet each is principally an emotional journey, focused on deeply personal questions, with the descriptive element secondary. It is perhaps for this reason that I feel that "The story of…" is the most apt description. Consider the open lines of the second chapter of "Cosmos":

> All my life I have wondered about the possibility of life elsewhere. What would it be like? Of what would it be made? All living things on our planet are constructed of organic molecules—complex microscopic architectures in which the carbon atom plays a central role. There was once a time before life, when the Earth was barren and utterly desolate. Our world is now overflowing with life. How did it come about? How, in the absence of life, were carbon-based organic molecules made? How did the first living things arise? How did life evolve to produce beings as elaborate and complex as we, able to explore the mystery of our own origins? And on the countless other planets that may circle other suns, is there life also? Is extraterrestrial life, if it exists, based on the same organic molecules as life on Earth? Do the beings of other worlds look much like life on Earth? Or are they stunningly different—other adaptations to other environments? What else is possible? The nature of life on Earth and the search for life elsewhere are two sides of the same question—the search for who we are.

These are not dry, descriptive, textbook questions. These are deeply personal questions, setting up a mystery and emotional journey for the writer and the reader alike. Sagan's form often suggests non-fiction, but his text really conveys "a personal voyage" (also his chosen subtitle), a memoir of his changed emotional understanding which carries the reader along too.

Having nebulously defined grand story, let me switch to another hard-to-define term: myth. I don't claim to understand what myth is. But one useful and plausible meaning is that a myth is a grand story which helps define a shared identity for a people. In this sense, all these ancient religions have the character of myth or collections of myths[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn8)[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn9)[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn10).

What's this got to do with why ancient religions persist? A speculative answer: maybe there are serious obstructions to constructing good myths today. Of course, many individual grand stories are published: the self-help genre contains thousands of books on the story of you; modern pop cosmology is all about the story of the universe and its origins; and so on. But few books today contain an overlapping mosaic of grand stories sufficient for a well-formed shared identity. Most writers lack the breadth to write in such a way: someone who can write the story of you likely can't write the story of the origins of humanity or the story of the relationship between humans and the universe. (Something I admire about Sagan's "Cosmos" is that a single paragraph will seamlessly weave through science to the arts to religion to history to psychology and then to another branch of science; few writers have the catholicity of understanding required to pull this off.) By contrast, the writers of the ancient religious texts developed an amazing mosaic of overlapping grand stories; some of their ideas now appear naive; but many of us are forgiving of such lapses, appreciating that they engage with the big questions in a unified way. Put another way: the bar of understanding for myth today is, I suspect, simply much higher. The ancient religious texts get grandfathered in: members of a religion give the text tremendous credit when it is insightful (as they often are), and give them a pass when they are not. But would-be makers of new myths for humanity aren't extended the same leniency.

## Is religious progress possible? How to design religions for change?

One of humanity's greatest inventions is institutions _designed for radical change_. Democracies make the overthrow of power not rare and traumatic, but routine and orderly, done on a schedule. Capitalism is built around competition and creative destruction, with startups replacing incumbents, or originating entirely new product categories. Science is built around competition[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn11) and the creative destruction of ideas, with improved ideas replacing incumbents, or originating entirely new fields. In each case, the relevant institutions are built around some process of replacement. I say "institutions", but I don't mean a single, centralized organization, which may bottleneck change; rather a set of norms and processes and organizations that collectively help decentralized change happen. These institutions don't work perfectly. But over the long run all have done a remarkable job of enabling human progress.

By contrast, the ancient religions are not designed for change, they are designed for stasis. This is historically unsuprising: when the religions originated, change was often in the form of war, famine, or disease. The modern notion of progress – change leading to improvement – was still nascent. And so while the ancient religions of course _have_ changed, they've often made those changes kicking and screaming. An emblematic example: it wasn't until 1992(!) that the Catholic Church apologized for trying Galileo on charges of heresy, and imprisoning him. In Islam, innovation in religion, known as bid'ah, is considered a sin[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn12). The mere fact notions such as heresy and bid'ah exist is suggestive of a default suspicious stance toward change. My understanding is that Hinduism and Buddhism are somewhat less rigid, but still hew pretty closely to ancient doctrine. By contrast, science, capitalism, and democracy all celebrate their innovators, at least in theory, and often in practice.

What elements make a system able to change? Let me make two observations. One is that the system needs some mechanism for identifying and amplifying improvements. The better the identification and the amplification, the more effective; conversely, if the identification or amplification is weak, it won't be very effective. In democracy the mechanism is voting; in capitalism it's the market and consumer demand; in science it's the test of experiment and a sort of market in ideas among the broader scientific community. It's not so obvious what the analogous mechanism could be for progress in religion. But it's fun to think about potential mechanisms! I'll come back to this shortly.

Second, one force for stasis is the strong orientation around a fixed canonical text or texts. I sometimes think of Christianity as a large group of people reading the Bible really deeply together. Yes, it's also a large collection of communities and institutions – but that collective reading is at the core of it. I don't mean that every person participates in the reading, but a core of people do read very deeply, and together[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/persistence/index.html#fn13). And while this is a lovely image and idea, that centralized focus also inhibits change. Although there are canonical books about science or capitalism or democracy — think of Hobbes's "Leviathan", say, or Schumpeter's "Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy" – they are far less central to the community. Reading and believing in Hobbes or Schumpeter isn't central to your identity as a citizen in a democracy or as a capitalist. And even the greatest books of science – say, Newton's "Principia", or Darwin's "Origin of Speces" – tend to eventually be superseded. By contrast, the orientation of religions toward fixed, central canonical texts is a large inertial force, preventing change.

Can we design dynamic religious systems? Or, as a less ambitious version: what about for questions like: what does it mean to lead a good life? Of course, many communities do try to answer this latter question in some dynamic, decentralized way. Efforts such as positive psychology, developmental economics, effective altruism (EA), moral philosophy, and much of the entire political sphere, are oriented around providing answers to the question: "what does it mean to lead a good life?" Indeed, I have noted [elsewhere](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/) that "EA is, in some sense, an attempt to do for the question 'what is the good?' what science has done for the question 'how does the world work?'. Instead of providing an answer it is developing a community that aims to continually improve the answer." In a different (but overlapping) way this is also true of positive psychology and developmental economics. Each community may be split into two parts: a part that is concerned with developing an abstract notion of good (happiness scales, DALYs and QALYs, discounted future utility, and similar); and a part that then uses those measures to study the "goodness" of different actions. In some sense, this division enables it to reduce ought as much as possible to is, with the second part being mostly concerend with "is" questions. And on questions of _is_ we have relatively good decentralized approaches to progress. This is aided by a reasonably robust discussion in both online forums, and the academic literature. Unfortunately, that conversation is still subject to considerable distortions by charisma, and by centralization of money and power in a small number of organizations. It remains to be seen how dynamic those organizations and those communities will be.
