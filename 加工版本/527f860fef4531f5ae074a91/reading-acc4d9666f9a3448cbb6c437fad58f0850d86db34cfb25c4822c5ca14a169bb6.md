# Keju：1300 年的 Studying

---

## 正文

为什么 East Asian countries 的 students 在 school 中 perform 得这么好？

[data 是 clear 的](http://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en/full-report/how-did-countries-perform-in-pisa_dc514907.html)。以下是 [PISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programme_for_International_Student_Assessment) 的 mathematics rankings，这个 organization 在 internationally 范围内 compares educational performance：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/pisa-top-10.jpg)

我们必须沿着这份名单看到第七名，才会遇到一个 Western nation。而且这个 ranking 甚至不包括 China。虽然 China 不在这个 data set 中，但在 2018 年，China 的 major metropolitan areas——Beijing、Shanghai、Jiangsu 和 Zhejiang（BSJZ）——实际上 ranked higher than Singapore。

East Asian countries 在 reading 方面也 score high，占据了 top 5 spots 中的 4 个。考虑到基于 characters 的 writing systems，例如 Japan 和 China 使用的那些，比大多数 European languages 使用的 alphabetic systems 更难 learn，这就更令人印象深刻。

 对于 East Asian scholastic performance 这个问题，显而易见的答案是 culture。

在 PISA 的 mathematics rankings 中 top 的六个 East Asian countries 里，除了 Japan，其余国家的 classwork 都 exceed 了 [OECD 的 weekly hours average](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2017/06/do-students-spend-enough-time-learning_e1a6c6ea/744d881a-en.pdf)；如果把合并统计的 Chinese cities BSJZ 也算在内，其中三个在 total hours studying 方面位居 worldwide 的 top seven。这些 high-performing countries 的许多 students，也会在当天的 regular classes 结束_之后_，attend 晚上的“cram” schools。

 所有这些 studying 究竟是不是好事，是一个复杂的问题。一方面，competitive、academic culture 可能会减少孩子们 play 和 socialize 的机会。如果 academic knowledge 对许多 jobs 并不 useful，那么这种 approach 可能是一种给 society 带来拖累的 [net-negative](https://www.amazon.ca/Case-against-Education-System-Waste/dp/0691174652)。

但是，看看 East Asian economies 令人难以置信的 performance，例如 Hong Kong、Singapore、South Korea 和 Taiwan 这 [Four Asian Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Asian_Tigers)，尤其是它们在 high-technology manufacturing 方面的表现，就很难相信所有这些对 math 和 science 的重视没有带来一些 payoff。

讨论不同 educational cultures 的 relative merits 或许很有趣（也包括 East Asian approaches 内部的一些 differences，尤其是 Japan 的 outlier status：以 comparatively fewer hours of studying 获得 high grades），但我更感兴趣的是这些 cultures 本身的 origins。East Asian countries 最初为什么会如此强调 educational attainment？

## Keju：1300 年的 Studying

对于 culture 中强调 education 的现象，有一种 explanation 与 Chinese civil service exams，也就是 keju（科举）的 long-lasting influence 有关。当 Europe 刚刚开始 invent feudalism 时，Chinese state 的人员已经由 meritocratic bureaucracy 构成，而这些人选自世界有史以来一些最 competitive exams 的 graduates。

为了更好地理解这一点，我最近读了 Ichisada Miyazaki 引人入胜的著作 [China’s Examination Hell](https://www.amazon.ca/Chinas-Examination-Hell-Examinations-Imperial/dp/0300026390)，以探究这个 system 带来的 benefits（以及 costs）。

civil service examinations 始于 Sui dynasty（605 CE），最初的设计目的是绕过那些对 imperial government 拥有相当大 control 的 powerful aristocratic families。

在 theory 上，exams 向所有男性开放。[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/09/01/book-review-chinas-examination-hell/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18654 "女性被 excluded 在 exams 之外。这些 exams 并不是 Gender egalitarian 的。") 任何男性，从 noble family 的儿子到 peasant commoner，都可以 attend。当然，在 practice 中，多年 study 的 financial burdens 意味着，除了那些已经 well off 的人之外，很少有人能够认真 pursue 它。

尽管如此，这个 system 仍允许一定程度的 social mobility，帮助 legitimize imperial rule，并起到了 centralize power 的作用。由于这些原因，尽管经历了 dynastic changes，甚至 foreign conquest，这个 system 仍 persisted 了十三个世纪。直到 Qing 的末期，它才终于被 abolished。

即使是 [Hong Xiuquan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Xiuquan)，在多次未能成为 licentiate 后发生 nervous breakdown，相信自己是 Jesus Christ 的弟弟，并发动了一场造成数千万人死亡的 war，却仍然在他 controlled 的 territories 中建立了自己版本的 examination system。即便对于那些亲身在 exams 下受苦的人来说，exams 也是 essential 的。

## 参加 Exams 是什么样的体验？

Miyazaki 的书之所以有趣，不仅在于它描述了这个 system——其中有许多层 examinations 和 re-examinations，以 memorizing [Chinese classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Books_and_Five_Classics) 为中心——也在于他记述了 students 亲身经历时是什么样的。

到了 Qing dynasty 晚期，exams 的 complexity 达到顶峰，分为三个 major divisions：provincial、metropolitan 和 palace examinations。

“每年”举行——实际上每三年举行一次——的 provincial examinations，会把 exam takers 连续三天锁在庞大、labyrinthian exam halls 中的狭小 cells 里。Exam takers 会领到三块木板：一块当床，一块当凳子，一块用作 writing surface。这些 halls 由于使用得如此不频繁，往往破败不堪，students 不得不保护自己珍贵的 exam answers，免遭漏进来的雨水浸湿。

参加 provincial exams 的人不仅面临 harsh physical conditions，还面临 supernatural 的威胁。人们普遍相信，在这个 process 的这一 stage，ghosts 会 punished 那些犯有 unseen crimes 的人。Seducers 和 swindlers 可以预料自己会被 haunted；一些 anecdotes 讲述了 guilty consciences 如何在三天 confinement 期间导致 suicide 或 nervous breakdown。

在另一些情况下，karma 则更为 indirect。过去的 transgression 可能导致灯油意外洒到自己的 answer sheets 上，而这种 violation 总会导致 exams 的 failure。人们普遍相信，exam results 中任何 apparent unfairness，都必定是由于 concealed crimes 或 evil deeds 正受到 heaven 的 judged，而不是某种倒霉的意外。

少数 succeeded 的人会踏上前往 capital 的漫长（而且昂贵）的旅途，参加 metropolitan examinations。在 keju 的早期，这些 examinations 是 scholar 历程中的 final stage；此后，他几乎可以保证在 Chinese bureaucracy 中获得一席之地。

然而，test-takers 对 examiners 所负有的 loyalty，在 government system 内部造成了 factions。就像今天的 academics 常常把自己的 careers 归功于 eminent doctoral advisors 一样，scholars 也对自己的 examiner 负有 loyalty。为了防止这 undermining imperial loyalty，由 emperor 评判的 palace exams 被 instituted。[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/09/01/book-review-chinas-examination-hell/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18654 "在 practice 中，emperor 的选择受到负责 sorted 和 presented exams 的 ministers 引导。事实上，一些特别 skillful 的 orators 经常左右 emperor 对 best answers 的选择，以至于许多 examiners 都祈祷自己的 answers 能由这些 ministers 朗读出来。") 人们设想，这样一来，graduates 感激的就不会是某位 examiner，而是 emperor 本人。

考虑到这个 system 中 corruption 的 incentives 如此巨大，为 combat cheating 和 favoritism 而采取的众多措施令人印象深刻。Exam takers 会受到彻底搜查，以查出任何夹带的 writing。Examiners 只能看到 students 的 seat number，而看不到他们的姓名；在 higher exams 中，甚至连 answer sheets 都由 scribes meticulously hand-copied，以免 handwriting 中的 distinguishing marks reveal test-taker 的 identity。

Scandals 一旦出现，就会引发如此强烈的 public backlash，以至于人们有强烈的 motivation，至少维持 integrity 的表象。被发现参与 undermining 这个 process 的 schemes 的 Examiners，往往会被 exiled 或 executed。

exam process 本身是 intensely competitive 的。对于参加 provincial exam 的人来说，首先必须 passed 一个 local exam，才能进入这个 system。那个 exam 的 pass rate 大约为 1 to 2%。Provincial exams 的 pass rate 大约为 5%，metropolitan exams 的 pass rate 则为 15 to 20%。虽然很少有人 failed palace exams——它往往更像一种 formality，而不是 substantive test——但一个人在其中获得的 ranking，对其 career opportunities 有着重要 impact。总的来说，在十万名 potential applicants 中，或许只有十来个人最终能实现梦想，成为 palace exams 的 graduate（jinshi）。

与 modern university system 不同，后者的 key academic bottlenecks 出现在一个人的青年时期，而 studying 和参加 imperial exams 的 process 可能持续多年。新 graduates 是白发苍苍的老人，并不罕见。在某些情况下，elderly exam-takers 会被自动 passed，让他们获得 jinshi status，因为人们知道他们已经超过了 government 中 official positions 的 retirement 年龄。

## Imperial Examination System 的 Legacy

很难确切知道，China 的 examination system 对如今 East Asian countries 的 academic achievement 究竟产生了多大的 impact。   一篇[巧妙的 paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueaa043)利用了 China 内部 exam attendance 的 intensity 所呈现的 natural variation，以每 10,000 人口中 jinshi 的 percentage 进行 measured，发现一个 region 的 jinshi doubling 与如今 educational attainment 的 8.5 percent increase 存在 corresponds with 的关系。

对于我们能否直接 trace 这些 effects，我有些怀疑。几个世纪以来，China 对其邻国 exerted 了巨大的 cultural influence，因此，即使是 Japan 这样从未拥有自己版本的 imperial examination system 的国家，也仍然 adopted 了 Chinese culture 的许多 aspects，从 writing 中 Chinese characters 的 use，到 Zen Buddhism。很难相信，对 learning 的 attitudes 没有同样 diffuse，即便不存在 formal examination system。

但即使我们无法 ascertain keju 对 modern attitudes towards education 的确切 impact，我认为，其 legacy 的问题仍值得思考。在超过一千年的时间里，examination system 形成了对 education 的 cultural emphasis，而 Europe 至少在 Renaissance 之前都没有与之相当的事物。

尽管存在 flaws，keju 在当时似乎是格外 progressive 的。根据 merit 而不是 birthright 来 Giving government positions，这在 Western nations 直到十九世纪都几乎闻所未闻。exam system 在 Europe 的 feudalism 刚刚 arising 时便 emerged，并一直 endured 到上个世纪初，这一点非同寻常。

keju 的 cultural impact 不仅限于 education。其中一个 effect 似乎是对 military 的 suppression。虽然 military exams 确实与 civilian 的考试 in parallel 进行，但在其中取得 success，既不会带来 society 中的 great respect，也不会带来 government 中的 high office。同样，battlefield generals 通常能够 advance 到什么程度也受到 limited，从而带来了对 military 更强的 civilian control。

与此同时，虽然 examination system 所促成的 military 的 suppression 和 power 的 centralization，可能使 China 比 Europe 及其他地方的 polities 更 peaceful、更 stable，但[有人曾 argued](https://www.amazon.ca/Guns-Germs-Steel-Jared-Diamond/dp/0393317552)，state 的 centralization 也可能 prevented 那些在 Europe spurred Industrial Revolution 的 disruptive innovations。

此外，还有 exams 本身的 content。虽然 studying Chinese Classics 原本应当 impart wise judgement 和 upright conduct，但它在这些方面是否 successful，并不清楚。而从 modern educational perspective 来看，这个 system 将重点放在远离 effective governance 所需的 skills 的那些 skills 上，似乎格外 wasteful。

最后，还有 exams 本身的 intensity，以及这种 competition 对 culture 和 society 的 impact。虽然可以说，这样的 system 是 superior to 同一时期大部分时间里 West 所 practiced 的 hereditary aristocracy 的，但花费几十年 studying for exams 是否是对 best minds 的 best use，仍不清楚。

也许今天也可以提出类似的 argument，因为 credential inflation 的 prevalence 不一定会 resulting in 大幅增加的 human capital。future societies 会不会因为我们重视 grades 和 degrees 胜过 useful skills，而 judge 我们自己的 culture？还是说，在 educated 程度高得多的后代眼中，我们会被视为 philistines？

一种 culture 所包含的，不仅是它的 values，还有 measuring 那些 values 的 yardstick，这使得它们之间的 comparison 成了一项 unworkable 的任务。未来几代人会将 keju 视为 meritocratic、knowledge-based society 不可或缺的 forerunner，还是一场极度 wasteful 的 scholastic competition，他们的看法既会反映当时存在的这个 system，也同样会反映 education 在未来 culture 中的 role。

无论如何，imperial examination system 都令我着迷，既因为它作为一种 institution 所具有的惊人 persistence，也因为它提供了一个窗口，让我们看到 education 如今在 societies 中继续发挥的 role。

## 原文

Why do students in East Asian countries perform so well in school?

The [data are clear](http://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en/full-report/how-did-countries-perform-in-pisa_dc514907.html). Here are the rankings for mathematics for [PISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programme_for_International_Student_Assessment), an organization which compares educational performance internationally:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/pisa-top-10.jpg)

We have to go down the list to number seven before we reach a Western nation. And this ranking doesn’t even include China. While China wasn’t in this data set, in 2018 China’s major metropolitan areas of Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu and Zhejiang (BSJZ) actually ranked higher than Singapore.

East Asian countries also score high in reading, claiming 4 of the top 5 spots. This is even more impressive when you account for the fact that writing systems based on characters, such as those used in Japan and China, are much harder to learn than alphabetic systems used in most European languages.

 The obvious answer to the question of East Asian scholastic performance is culture.

Of the six East Asian countries that top the PISA rankings in mathematics, all but Japan exceed the [OECD average for weekly hours](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2017/06/do-students-spend-enough-time-learning_e1a6c6ea/744d881a-en.pdf) of classwork, and including the combined Chinese cities of BSJZ, three were in the top seven worldwide for total hours studying. Many students in these high-performing countries also attend evening “cram” schools _after_ their regular classes conclude for the day.

 Whether all this studying is a good thing (or not) is a complicated question. On the one hand, a competitive, academic culture may remove opportunities for kids to play and socialize. To the extent that the academic knowledge is not useful for many jobs, this approach may be a [net-negative](https://www.amazon.ca/Case-against-Education-System-Waste/dp/0691174652) that puts a drag on society.  

But, it’s hard not to look at the incredible performance of East Asian economies, such as the [Four Asian Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Asian_Tigers) of Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea and Taiwan, particularly in high-technology manufacturing, and believe that all this focus on math and science isn’t having some payoff.

While it might be fun to debate the relative merits of different educational cultures (including some of the differences within East Asian approaches, especially Japan’s outlier status in terms of high grades with comparatively fewer hours of studying), I’m more interested in the origins of those cultures themselves. Why is there such a strong emphasis on educational attainment in East Asian countries in the first place?

## The Keju: 1300 Years of Studying

One explanation for the cultural emphasis on education has to do with the long-lasting influence of the Chinese civil service exams, or the keju (科举). While Europe was just beginning to invent feudalism, the Chinese state was staffed by a meritocratic bureaucracy drawn from graduates of some of the most competitive exams the world has ever known.

To try to understand this better, I recently read Ichisada Miyazaki’s fascinating book, [China’s Examination Hell](https://www.amazon.ca/Chinas-Examination-Hell-Examinations-Imperial/dp/0300026390), to look into the benefits (and costs) created by this system.

Initiated in the Sui dynasty (605 CE), the civil service examinations were originally designed to get around the powerful aristocratic families that wielded considerable control over the imperial government.

In theory, the exams were open to all men.[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/09/01/book-review-chinas-examination-hell/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18654 "Women were excluded from the exams. Gender egalitarian, the exams were not.") Any man, from the son of a noble family down to a peasant commoner, could attend. In practice, of course, the financial burdens of years of study meant that few outside of the already well off could seriously pursue it.

Still, the system allowed for a degree of social mobility, helped legitimize imperial rule, and worked to centralize power. For these reasons, despite dynastic changes and even foreign conquest, the system persisted for thirteen centuries. It was finally abolished in the waning days of the Qing.

Even [Hong Xiuquan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Xiuquan) who, after failing multiple times to become a licentiate, had a nervous breakdown, believed himself to be the younger brother of Jesus Christ, and launched a war that killed tens of millions, nonetheless set up his own version of the examination system in the territories he controlled. The exams, even for those who personally suffered under them, were essential.

## What Was it Like Taking the Exams?

Miyazaki’s book is interesting not only for its description of the system, with its many layers of examinations and re-examinations centered on memorizing the [Chinese classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Books_and_Five_Classics), but also for his accounts of what it was like for the students themselves.

At the height of their complexity, in the late Qing dynasty, the exams had three major divisions: provincial, metropolitan and palace examinations.

The provincial examinations, held “annually”—every three years—had exam takers locked into tiny cells in large, labyrinthian exam halls for three days straight. Exam takers were provided with three wooden boards: one for a bed, one for a bench and one for a writing surface. The halls, used so infrequently, were often dilapidated, with students sheltering their precious exam answers from leaking rain.

Those taking the provincial exams faced not only harsh physical conditions, but also threats from the supernatural. It was widely believed that ghosts punished those guilty of unseen crimes at this stage of the process. Seducers and swindlers could expect to be haunted, with some anecdotes of guilty consciences leading to suicide or nervous breakdown during the three days of confinement.

In other cases, karma was more indirect. A past transgression might result in lamp oil accidentally spilling on one’s answer sheets, a violation that led invariably to failure at the exams. It was widely believed that any apparent unfairness in the exam results must be due to concealed crimes or evil deeds being judged from heaven rather than some unlucky accident.

Of the few who succeeded, they made the long (and expensive) journey to the capital for the metropolitan examinations. In the early years of the keju, these were the final stage in a scholar’s journey, after which he would have a near-guaranteed place in the Chinese bureaucracy.

Yet, the loyalty test-takers owed to their examiners created factions within the government system. Much as academics today often owe their careers to eminent doctoral advisors, scholars owed loyalty to their examiner. To prevent this from undermining imperial loyalty, palace exams, judged by the emperor, were instituted.[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/09/01/book-review-chinas-examination-hell/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18654 "In practice, the emperor’s choices were guided by ministers who sorted and presented the exams. Indeed, some particularly skillful orators often swayed the emperor’s choice of the best answers, so that many examiners prayed their answers would be read aloud by these ministers.") This way, it was supposed, graduates would owe their gratitude not to an examiner, but to the emperor himself.

Given the enormous incentives for corruption in this system, it was impressive how many steps were taken to combat cheating and favoritism. Exam takers were thoroughly searched for any smuggled writing. Examiners only had access to students’ seat number, not their names, and, in the higher exams, even the answer sheets were meticulously hand-copied by scribes so as not to reveal a test-taker’s identity through distinguishing marks in handwriting.

Scandals, when they did emerge, created such public backlash that that there was a strong motivation to maintain at least a facade of integrity. Examiners found to engage in schemes to undermine the process were often exiled or executed.

The exam process itself was intensely competitive. Of those entering the provincial exam, first a local exam had to be passed just to get into the system. That exam had a roughly 1 to 2% pass rate. Provincial exams had a roughly 5% pass rate, and metropolitan exams had a 15 to 20% pass rate. Although few failed the palace exams, which tended to be more of a formality than a substantive test, the ranking one achieved there had an important impact on one’s career opportunities. In all, from a hundred thousand potential applicants, perhaps a dozen would go on to see their dream realized by becoming a graduate of the palace exams (jinshi).

Unlike the modern university system, where key academic bottlenecks occur in one’s youth, the process of studying and taking the imperial exams could go on for years. It wasn’t uncommon for new graduates to be grey-haired old men. In some cases, elderly exam-takers were passed automatically, allowing them to earn their jinshi status, knowing that they had already passed the age of retirement from official positions in government.

## Legacy of the Imperial Examination System

It’s hard to know exactly how big an impact China’s examination system has had on academic achievement in East Asian countries today.   One [clever paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueaa043) used the natural variation in the intensity of exam attendance within China, as measured by the percentage of jinshi per 10,000 population, finding that a doubling of jinshi in a region corresponds with an 8.5 percent increase in educational attainment today.

I’m somewhat skeptical that we can directly trace these effects. For centuries, China exerted enormous cultural influence on its neighbors, so even countries like Japan, which never had its own version of an imperial examination system, nonetheless adopted many aspects of Chinese culture, from the use of Chinese characters in writing to Zen Buddhism. It’s hard to believe that attitudes towards learning did not similarly diffuse, even absent a formal examination system. 

But even if we can’t ascertain the exact impact of the keju on modern attitudes towards education, I think the question of its legacy is still worth considering. For more than a millennium, the examination system created a cultural emphasis on education that had no parallel in Europe, at least until the Renaissance.

Despite its flaws, the keju seems remarkably progressive for its time. Giving government positions based on merit, not birthright, was practically unknown in Western nations until the nineteenth century. The fact that the exam system emerged when feudalism was just arising in Europe and endured until the beginning of the last century is remarkable.

The cultural impact of the keju extends beyond education. One effect seems to have been a suppression of the military. While military exams did proceed in parallel to civilian ones, success in them led neither to great respect in society nor high office in government. Similarly, battlefield generals were limited in how far they could typically advance, leading to stronger civilian control over the military.

At the same time, while the suppression of the military and centralization of power assisted by the examination system may have made China more peaceful and stable than polities in Europe and elsewhere, [some have argued](https://www.amazon.ca/Guns-Germs-Steel-Jared-Diamond/dp/0393317552) that the centralization of the state may have prevented the disruptive innovations that spurred the Industrial Revolution in Europe.

And then there was the content of the exams themselves. While studying the Chinese Classics was supposed to impart wise judgement and upright conduct, it’s not clear that it was successful on these fronts. And from a modern educational perspective, the system seems extraordinarily wasteful in terms of its focus on skills far removed from those needed for effective governance.

Finally, there’s the intensity of the exams themselves and the impact of that competition on culture and society. While such a system was arguably superior to the hereditary aristocracy practiced in the West for much of the same time period, it’s unclear that decades spent studying for exams were the best use of the best minds.

Perhaps a similar argument could be made today with the prevalence of credential inflation not necessarily resulting in vastly greater human capital. Will future societies judge our own culture for our focus on grades and degrees over useful skills? Or will we be viewed as philistines by much more educated descendants?

A culture includes within it, not only its values, but the yardstick for measuring those values, making comparison between them an unworkable task. Whether future generations will see the keju as an essential forerunner to a meritocratic, knowledge-based society, or an exorbitantly wasteful scholastic competition will say just as much about the role of education in the culture of the future as it does about the system as it existed at the time.

Regardless, the imperial examination system fascinates me both for its incredible persistence as an institution, and as a window into the role education continues to play in societies today.
