# Productivity：Tasks 与 Priorities

---

## 正文

我对 self-assessment 很着迷：我们如何看待自己，与我们实际上是什么样的人，两者有何不同。

举个例子，我第一次开办 [Foundations](https://join.ingeniumcourses.com/foundations/) 课程时，我们从 fitness 开始。竟然有那么多人声称自己已经有了 solid exercise habit，这让我印象深刻。

考虑到我吸引的读者类型，这可能是 sampling bias 所致（你们真的都这么 fit 吗？）。但也可能是因为，总体而言，人们会 overestimate 自己实际进行的 exercise 有多少。在 surveys 中，大多数人都说自己 meet recommended exercise guidelines，但实际情况是，[真正做到的人还不到百分之十](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797\(11\)00012-2/abstract)。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-1.jpg)

Fitness 似乎是一个我们会作出 overly-rosy self-assessment 的领域。

现在，把它与课程第二个月的主题 productivity 比较一下。这里没有 optimism，median response 反而是在说他们的 productivity 有多么 dismal。而且，由于这是一门付费课程，许多学员都是 high-earning knowledge workers；至少按照 [productivity 的 economic definition](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2024/06/11/overlooked-productivity/)，他们是地球上最 productive 的一群人之一。

为什么会有这种 disconnect？为什么我们在 estimation 自己的 exercise 时如此乐观，可一谈到自己正在做的工作，却又对自己如此苛刻？

## Productivity：Tasks 与 Priorities

我认为，部分原因在于 productivity advice 这一整类建议从一开始就是如何被推销的：大多数建议侧重于掌控 tasks，而大多数人更为 priorities 所困扰。

Task-focused advice 讨论的是如何把一切做完：planners、calendars、to-do lists、batching、[Kanban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanban)、[Pomodoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique)。有无数 methods 旨在让你在处理我们所有人都有的那份 infinite 的 aspirations 和 responsibilities 清单时，变得更加 efficient 和 effective。

但我们不可能把一切都做完。无论你 check off 多少 tasks，一份 infinite to-do list 上仍然会有 infinite number 的事情。我在课程中遇到的那些 high-performing productivity pessimists，正在用一个不可能达到的标准评判自己。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-2.jpg)

最近重读 Stephen Covey 的 [First Things First](https://www.amazon.ca/First-Things-Stephen-R-Covey/dp/0684802031) 时，我一直在想这个谜题：那些客观上最 productive 的人，反而觉得自己最不 productive。我隐约记得，这本书讲的是 weekly reviews 和他的 productivity system。在我的记忆里，它主要讨论的是按周 scheduling activities，以及 prioritizing 那些 important 但不 urgent 的 activities。

重读之后，我意识到自己记错了。实际上，这本书的大部分内容从根本上就在反对 task-focused productivity approach。weekly review 的主要内容并不是 scheduling 你一周所有的 tasks，而是问自己：我应该努力过怎样的生活？

## 指南针与时钟

在所有 80 年代末的 self-help gurus 中，我最喜欢 Stephen Covey。那些朴实的故事和格言，如今看来有点老套（他甚至连脏话都不说！）。但与许多同时代的人相比，Covey 既睿智，又有深度。

Covey 常用的一个比喻，是将时钟与指南针对照。时钟把 time 变成一种 measurable quantity，你可以对它进行 slice up、allocate，并用它来 plan——它成了你可以 manage 的东西。相比之下，指南针不会给你一个 plan；它所能做的，只是告诉你是否正朝着正确的方向前进。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-3.jpg)

归根结底，productivity 的问题不在于我们没有做完所有想做的事（我们从来都做不完），也不在于我们不可能更好地利用自己的 time 和 energy（我们总能做得更好）。相反，问题在于，我们希望对自己利用有限 time 的方式感到满意。

productivity 的目标不应该是 obsessively 地担忧自己做完了多少事情。相反，它应该是让我们在一天结束时觉得：即使把自己的 time 花在任何其他事情上，也不会更好。

归根结底，我们需要为自己的人生 create 一个 vision，并 regularly 问自己是否真的在践行它。Tasks、projects 和 purposes 需要多久才能取得成果，就让它们花多久。我们唯一要做的，就是问自己什么才真正重要，并坚定地坚持下去。

## 每周的 Review

 在我人生中的大部分时间里，我都做 weekly reviews，但它们基本上是 task oriented 的：我的 schedule 上有什么？我想做完什么？什么要放进本周的 to-do list？什么要 delayed 到以后？

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-4.jpg)

这一部分很有帮助。生活很少按 plan 发展，但 plans 本身仍然无比宝贵。  不过，我现在明白，这其实只是整个 method 的一半，而且还是较不重要的那一半。

 weekly review 的重点不是 logistics。它的目的不是提前 schedule 你一周的每一刻，也不是精确 determine 你需要 accomplish 哪些 to-do list items。相反，这是一个机会，让你花几分钟审视人生中什么对你真正重要，然后确保在接下来的一周里，尽自己最大的努力，按照你的答案去生活。

我来举个例子：如果深入反思，孩子和家庭对我来说比工作更重要。我的孩子正处于照料起来可能很棘手的年龄。这常常导致我请假，或做完的事情更少。当下，我的第一反应往往是责备自己不够 productive——看看自己的 to-do list，我做完的事情肯定比单身且没有孩子的时候少。

但如果退一步看，我就会意识到，这并不是 productivity 的失败，反而是我在成为最 productive 的自己。在人生的这个阶段，我宁愿少做完一些事情，让工作服从于家庭。一次为我重新确认这一点的 weekly review，会把被 interrupted 时的挫败感，转变为一种认识：恰恰是这些 interruptions，才是我人生中真正的 priority。

再举一个例子：我经常做大量的 reading 和 research。筹备[我的上一本书](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/getbetter/)，是一段历时多年的 research 漫长旅程。如果把自己与其他作者比较，显然，我想要 drill down into academic research 的愿望多少是个 handicap。如果我专注于吸引人的故事和朗朗上口的短句，而不是 complicated science，我或许能卖出更多书（也能写得更快）。

但再次退一步看，我意识到，理解世界是驱动我的 values 之一。花“太多时间 researching”并不是一个错误，因为对我来说，努力应对生活的 complexity，比 producing 令人安心的 simplifications 更重要。

## 构建你的 Compass

这些听起来都很好，但如果你不确定自己的 values 是什么呢？你究竟该如何作出那些艰难的 decisions，对生活的不同部分进行 trade-off，并对这些 choices 自然带来的任何 consequences 感到满意？

在这一点上，我与 Stephen Covey 的看法略有不同。他非常强调拥有一份 personal mission statement，但我一直觉得，这些陈述往往要么过于 generic，成了 platitude（例如，“我将服务于人类。”），要么过于 specific，以至于过度 constrain 你未来的生活（例如，“我将通过在 refrigerator repair industry 提供 exceptional quality assurance 来服务于人类。”）。

相反，我认为，关于 values 的实情是：你已经拥有它们。即使你无法用语言表达，你也早已深切地知道，在生活中，哪些事情对你而言是真正 important 的，哪些只是你迫于压力而觉得应该 achieve 的。

你需要的不是想出一份自己从未表述过的 mission statement，而只是不断问自己为什么要做这些事，直到得到让你觉得 self-evident 的答案。

 拿一张纸，逐项查看你的 to-do list。开始针对 agenda 上的每个 item 问“为什么”。不断追问，直到你找到一个对你而言 intrinsically compelling 的 motivating reason，或者意识到自己并没有充分的 reason 去做这件事。（如果是后者，就把它从 to-do list 上划掉，或者想办法尽早不再做它。）

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-5.jpg)

这不必局限于那些令人振奋的事情。“把家里漏水的地方修好”也许不会给你的生活带来喜悦，但你很容易把这个 task 与关乎家庭和家人 material well-being 的更深层 motivations 联系起来。同样，如果你一直履行某项乏味的 obligation，只是因为自己答应过，那么，以做一个信守承诺的人为依据，就很容易 justify 这件事（而且，今后再答应类似的事情前，你或许会三思）。

这样的 questioning 所做的，是 identify 哪些事情是你出于更 superficial 的 reasons 而受到 motivated 去做的，哪些事情才真正与你产生共鸣（例如，“我想要 six-pack，这样别人就会觉得我很性感。”，与“我想保持 healthy，这样到了晚年仍能好好生活。”之间的区别）。不仅如此，它还帮助你认清那些对你真正重要、却并不算“productive”，因而通常不会被列入 task-oriented 的 to-do list 的事情，例如 building relationships、deep thinking 和 kindness。

完成这一步后，weekly review 就成了你每周重新审视这一 vision 的机会。不只是 plan out 何时才能把所有事情做完（提醒一下：你做不完），而是确保自己把时间用在做正确的事情上。

如果你能做到这一点，那么无论 to-do list 上有多少事情被 checked off，你都可以在一周结束时对自己的 productivity 感到满意。

## 原文

I’m fascinated by self-assessment: how we view ourselves versus how we actually are.

As an example, when I ran the first session of my [Foundations](https://join.ingeniumcourses.com/foundations/) course, we started with fitness. I was impressed by how many people claimed to already have a solid exercise habit.

Now, that could be due to sampling bias given the sort of readers I attract (are you all really so fit?). But it could also be because people, in general, overestimate how much exercise they actually do. In surveys most people say they meet recommended exercise guidelines, but the reality is that [fewer than ten percent actually do](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797\(11\)00012-2/abstract).

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-1.jpg)

Fitness seems to be an area where we have an overly-rosy self-assessment.

Now compare that to the second month in the course, productivity. Instead of optimism, here the median response was about how dismal their productivity was. And, since this is a paid course, many of the students are high-earning knowledge workers who, at least according to the [economic definition of productivity](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2024/06/11/overlooked-productivity/), are some of the most productive people on Earth.

Why the disconnect? Why are we so cheerful in our estimation of our exercise, yet so hard on ourselves when it comes to the work we’re doing?

## Productivity: Tasks and Priorities

I think part of it has to do with how the entire genre of productivity advice has been sold in the first place: most advice focuses on getting a handle on tasks, whereas most people struggle more with priorities.

Task-focused advice is about how to get it all done: planners, calendars, to-do lists, batching, [Kanban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanban), [Pomodoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique). There are countless methods designed to make you more efficient and effective at tackling the infinite list of aspirations and responsibilities we all have.

But we can’t get it all done. An infinite to-do list will still have an infinite number of things on it, no matter how many tasks you check off. The high-performing productivity pessimists I encountered in the course were judging themselves by a standard that is impossible to reach.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-2.jpg)

This puzzle, that the most objectively productive people feel the least productive, was on my mind when I recently reread Stephen Covey’s book, [First Things First](https://www.amazon.ca/First-Things-Stephen-R-Covey/dp/0684802031). I vaguely recalled this book being about weekly reviews and his productivity system. In my memory, it was mostly about scheduling activities on a weekly basis and prioritizing activities that were important but not urgent.

Upon re-reading, I realized that my recollection was mistaken. Most of the book actually argues against the task-focused productivity approach in the first place. The weekly review isn’t mostly about scheduling all your tasks for the week, rather it’s about asking yourself: What sort of life should I be trying to live?

## The Compass and the Clock

Of all the late-80s self-help gurus, I like Stephen Covey the best. The folksy stories and aphorisms seem a little hokey today (he doesn’t even use the F-word!). But, compared to a lot of his contemporaries, Covey is wise and thoughtful.

One metaphor Covey relies on is contrasting a clock with a compass. A clock turns time into a measurable quantity that you can slice up, allocate and plan with—something you can manage. In contrast, a compass doesn’t give you a plan; all it can do is show you whether or not you’re headed in the right direction.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-3.jpg)

The problem of productivity, ultimately, isn’t that we’re not doing everything we would like to (we never are), or that we couldn’t possibly use our time and energy better (we always could). Instead, it’s wanting to feel satisfied with how we use what limited time we have available.

The aim of productivity should not be to worry obsessively about how much we get done. Instead, it should be to feel, at the end of the day, that our time wouldn’t have been better spent doing anything else.

Ultimately, we need to create a vision for our lives and regularly ask ourselves whether we’re actually living up to it. Tasks, projects and purposes will take as long as they need to reach fruition. Our only job is to ask ourselves what really matters and have the conviction to stick with that.

## The Weekly Review

 I’ve done weekly reviews for much of my life, but they have been largely task oriented: What’s on my schedule? What would I like to get done? What goes on the to-do list for this week? What gets delayed to the future?

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-4.jpg)

That part is helpful. Life rarely goes according to plan, but the plans themselves are still invaluable.   However, I now see that this is really only half of the method, and the less-important half at that.

 The point of the weekly review is not logistics. It’s purpose is not to schedule every moment of your week in advance or determine exactly which to-do list items you need to accomplish. Instead, it’s an opportunity to take a few minutes to examine what actually matters to you in life, and then ensure that the week that follows is your best effort to live according to how you answer.

I’ll give an example: If I reflect deeply, my kids and family matter more to me than my work. My kids are at an age when childcare can be tricky. This often results in my taking time off or getting less done. In the moment, my first response is often to chastise myself for not being more productive—looking at my to-do list, I definitely get less done than I did when I was single and child-free.

But if I step back, I realize that this isn’t a failure of productivity, instead it’s me being my most productive self. At this stage of my life, I’d rather get less done and have work subordinated to family. A weekly review that reaffirms this for me transforms the frustration of being interrupted with recognition that those very interruptions are my true priority in life.

Here’s another example: I often do a lot of reading and research. Prepping [my last book](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/getbetter/) was a multiyear research odyssey. If I compare myself with other authors, it’s clear my desire to drill down into academic research is a bit of a handicap. I could probably sell more books (and write them faster) if I focused on catchy stories and soundbites rather than complicated science.

But, stepping back again, I realize that understanding the world is one of my driving values. Spending “too much time researching” isn’t a mistake given that trying to grapple with the complexity of life is more important to me than producing soothing simplifications.

## Building Your Compass

This all sounds good, but what if you aren’t sure what your values are? How do you actually make those difficult decisions to trade-off various parts of your life—and be satisfied with whatever consequences those choices naturally lead to?

Here I part ways a bit with Stephen Covey. He puts a lot of emphasis on having a personal mission statement, but I’ve always found these tend toward either being so generic as to be a platitude (e.g., “I will serve mankind.”) or so specific that they overly constrain your future life (e.g., “I will serve mankind through delivering exceptional quality assurance in the refrigerator repair industry.”).

Instead, I think the truth about values is that you already have them. Even if you can’t put it into words, you already have a deep sense of what’s truly important to you in life compared to what you merely feel pressured to achieve.

What’s needed isn’t to come up with a mission statement you’ve never articulated before, but simply to keep asking yourself why you’re doing things until you have answers that feel self-evident.

 Get a piece of paper and go through your to-do list. Start asking “why” of each of the items on your agenda. Keep asking until you either get to a motivating reason that is intrinsically compelling to you or you realize that there isn’t a good reason for you to be doing it. (In which case, strike it off your to-do list or find a way to get out of doing it at the earliest opportunity.)

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Underrate-productivity-5.jpg)

This doesn’t have to be limited to inspiring stuff. “Get the leak fixed in my house” may not spark joy in your life, but it’s pretty easy to connect this task to deeper motivations about the material well-being of your home and family. Similarly, a tedious obligation you’re sticking to because you agreed to do it is easy to justify on the basis of being the kind of person who keeps their promises (and perhaps you will think twice before promising something like that in the future).

What this questioning does is identify the things you’re being motivated to do by more superficial reasons and those that truly resonate with you (e.g., “I want a six-pack so people will think I’m hot.” versus “I want to be healthy so I can continue to live well into old age.”). Even more, it also helps you recognize the things that truly matter to you that aren’t “productive” and so don’t normally fit into a task-oriented to-do list, such as building relationships, deep thinking and kindness.

Once you’ve done that, the weekly review is a chance to check in with that vision every week. Not just to plan out when you’ll get it all done (hint: you won’t), but so you make sure you spend your time doing the right things.

If you can do that, you can end the week satisfied with your productivity, no matter how much of your to-do list got checked off.
