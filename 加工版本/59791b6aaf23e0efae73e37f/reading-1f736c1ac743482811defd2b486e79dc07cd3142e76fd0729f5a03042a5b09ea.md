## 第 2 部分 - Howard Nielsen 访谈

## 祖父为 Indigenous Students 挺身而出

**Michael Nielsen:** 好，今天是录制的第二天。昨天我点了关闭之后，你说了一件很有意思的事。不知道你能不能给正在看这段视频的人讲讲这个故事。是你无意中听到你爸爸对 principal 说的一番话。

**Howard Robert Nielsen:** 对，对。其实这是 Sharon 的故事。Sharon 是几年前告诉我的。有一天放学后，她在 school 等爸爸，爸爸当时跟 principal 开了个会。我们知道的就这么多。她无意中听到爸爸告诉 George Milne，他对 school 里 indigenous kids 的那种做法不合适，应该停止那样做。

**Michael:** 这真的很了不起。

**Howard:** 是啊。我想爸爸当时大概是 P&C 的成员，或者类似的。他平时不会主动制造有争议的局面，但显然那次就是这么回事。Sharon 说不出 George 回了什么之类的，因为他们在另一个房间，但她确实听到了那一段。

**Michael:** 你爸爸似乎一直很热心公益，而且大多是以一种非常和善的方式。他通常会用幽默来表达自己的意思。但他既然会那么直接，想必对这件事看得相当重。

**Howard:** 是啊，我会说他是那种会挑选值得争辩的事情的人。有时候你跟人聊天，对有些事未必那么赞同，但那些事也无关紧要。而他基本上会选择值得争的一仗，如果他认为有人行为不妥，需要跟他们争一争的话。

**Michael:** 几十年后，我成长过程中留下的印象是，\[European\] Australia 与 indigenous people 的 relationship 仍然很难处理，而且往往并不怎么 positive。人们往往相当 racist。所以在我看来，他选择为这件事据理力争，真的很有意思，也很令人敬佩。

**Howard:** 嗯，我觉得还有一点，就是他如果跟某个人有分歧，会更倾向于私下谈。所以他不太愿意在一大群人面前这么做，那种场合容易让人产生戒心。如果说了什么、是对谁说的，以及因此应该由谁来回应，都清清楚楚，他就更有可能得到积极的回应。但不会有旁人在场，也没有人会觉得自己被逼到了墙角。因为人在受到斥责或批评时，有时会做出蠢事。是啊，爸爸很擅长处理这些事情。

那些年，我跟他参加过不少 community meetings 和 tennis meetings。我不记得发生过什么——他确实会就人们应该怎么做，讨论各种不同的事情，是的，我觉得他的 social consciousness 相当强。而且正如你说的，在那个年代，这并不是流行的做法。那大概是 60 年前了，超过 60 年了。当时提出那种主张并不时髦，虽然显然 school 里大概也不怎么谈 social justice，但有一条不言而喻的道理：不管是不是 indigenous，人都应该受到善待。显然那一次，要么爸爸亲眼看到了什么，要么是别人告诉了他什么，而他在替他们出面。我不知道究竟是哪一种。

**Michael:** 这真挺了不起。Sharon 把这件事告诉了你，而你又记住了，也很有意思。

**Howard:** 是啊，我想当时一定是在跟她聊我正在写的那本小书，想找一些发生过的、值得一提的故事。我想这就是其中一个。

## 开始在 Glossop High School 上学

**Michael:** 昨天我们讲到了你 primary school 生涯的结束。听起来这段经历里有扔土，赢过一些 races，打了不少 tennis，而且最后似乎还错失了一次 Dux 的机会。所以那之后，你就去了 Glossop High School，对吗？

**Howard:** 对，Glossop High School。离家几 kilometers。那时候，到了 year seven，星期五下午我们就能去 Glossop High School 做 woodwork。我们这些男孩子做 woodwork，女孩子学 domestic science。所以我们就骑自行车来回。那挺好玩的。所以我们对 high school 已经有了一些了解。

那所 high school——你得做一次 test，看看你会进哪个 year level。全都是 streamed。当然，我们当时并不知道。我们完全不清楚那是怎么回事。总之，你做一次 test，然后就进 1A、1B、1C、1D 或 1E。这个 system 肯定有相当大的 flawed 之处，因为我居然进了 1A。

**Michael:** 哈！

**Howard:** 所以，我是我们 primary school 唯一进了 1A 的人。这有不少坏处，其中之一就是我必须学 Latin。这本来就没让多少人感兴趣，反正肯定没让我感兴趣。所以尝试学了几年 Latin 之后，我改学了 modern history，那是进 1B 的人可以选的。不过，在 English、maths 和 science 这些课上，我仍然留在 1A 这个 class group 里。但 Latin 那部分，最后我跟 Miss Moore 谈了谈，她既是 Latin teacher，也是我的 English teacher——我想部分原因是，不知道为什么，她有点偏爱我——于是她允许我留在她的 class 里，但单独学 history。

**Michael:** 这真有意思。不得不说，在很多方面，modern history 似乎确实比 Latin 更有用，除非你打算主持 Latin mass 之类的。

**Howard:** 没错，是的。当然，当初要求我们学它，是因为它有助于我们 understand English language。

## Religion 与 Sunday School（宗教与主日学）

**Michael:** 说到 Latin masses，你小时候，religion 有没有在生活中起过什么作用？

**Howard:** 有，其实这个问题很有意思。昨天说到学校时，我就在想这件事。小时候，我们星期天早上会去当地的 Church of England 上 Sunday school。我们的 Sunday school teacher 是 Hallam 太太。她那时大概只有 55 岁，但我们觉得她像是有 85 岁了。她人很好。

那段时间，我还真的读完了整本 Bible。那本书只比一副扑克牌稍大一点。字很小。要让我 recite 里面太多内容，我就有些吃力了，不过我觉得挺有意思，也很高兴自己读过。那是一次 educational experience，尽管里面用的是 older English，所以读起来颇费了些工夫。

Sharon 应该也读过，Vicki 大概也读过。我不记得我们上了多少年 Sunday school。并不是全年每周都要上——我想他们还是放了我们一马——不过感觉每年大约要上半年，可能持续了三年，那时我们大概是 10 岁、11 岁左右。

**Michael:** 可能是 King James Bible 之类的版本。那本书一定很小。

**Howard:** 说实话，我不知道是什么版本。我的理解是，King James Bible 出得更晚一些，语言也稍微容易理解一点。不过这些又都有不同的 versions。

总之，妈妈原本来自 Bundaberg，有 German background。他们信奉 Apostolic。但 Monash 没有 Apostolic church，倒是有一座 Church of England church，属于 Protestant，所以看起来也算是同一边的。

我们以前每天晚上在餐桌前都会 say grace。“对于我们即将领受的一切，请让我们真心感恩。Amen。”

还有，说来也有趣，你应该会对这件事感兴趣——妈妈教过我们一段晚上念的 prayer。而我至今每晚都会念一段 prayer。

**Michael:** 我还不知道呢。是什么 prayer？你小时候念的是什么 prayer，现在念的又是什么 prayer？

**Howard:** 小时候那段我记不得了，实在太久远了。现在这段其实只是我私人的事情。基本上，更多是在提醒自己积极、感恩、善待别人。妈妈教我们的 prayer，并不是那种让你因为畏惧 God 什么的而心生恐惧的内容。完全不是那样。不过，如果要给 Protestant church 找一个定位，它还是相当 right wing 的。

所以，我想我的 religious experience 差不多就是这些。不过说到底，它还是以某种形式存在于我每天的生活里。

**Michael:** 这是多年来慢慢变成现在这样的，还是你在某个时候作了决定？

**Howard:** 嗯，不是突然决定的，现在大概已经是第 10 版之类的了。它会变。不过更多是想着照顾我珍爱的人。你们大家都会出现在里面，家里人都在。里面其实并没有 God 这个词。所以大概到了这个地步，我其实并不 believe in God，本来也从来没有真正 believe in God。但我对此有个古怪的小玩笑：God 不过是按照典型的 Australian 习惯，把“good”缩短了而已。

所以我一直坚持这个说法。每当有人问我——我知道 Cooper 问过，我想 Bowen 也问过——“你 believe in God 吗？”我就说，嗯，我相信 good，那不过是 God 的另一个版本。因为真有一个 actual being 的想法，有点……我不太能接受。但我能感觉到，重要的是积极地生活。

**Michael:** 你爸爸有 religious 的一面吗？

**Howard:** 不怎么有。至少不是 church religious 那种。有意思的是，我也想过好几次，就他的行为而言，他可能比我认识的大多数人都更 Christ-like。我倒不是说他是另一个 Christ 什么的，但就有点像……究竟要看你说了什么，还是看你做了什么？

**Michael:** 他为社区和别人做了很多事。

**Howard:** 对，而且待人公道。我记得一件事——其实和 religion 无关，说的是他的态度，还有他帮助别人的能力。有一天，我和他在一块 fruit blocks 里摘 grapes，或者是在干别的什么。距离我们大约 100 meters 的旁边那条路上，一辆车尖叫着停下来，一个人冲出车门，大声向爸爸求助，问爸爸能不能帮他处理一件事。那人原本来自 Yugoslavia，讲着 broken English，所以我实在听不明白他在说什么。

后来才知道，那人以为自己正在被 Stasi tracked，也就是类似 Gestapo 或 military 里什么人的组织，而且他觉得自己会被赶出 Australia，因为他显然是个 migrant。

总之，爸爸把事情弄清楚了。原来，在那场 war 期间，也就是仅仅 10 或 15 年前，Australia 的 ASIO spy network 一直在 trailing 很多人，认为他们可能是 Russian spies。而这个人曾经参加过 Queensland 的 Mackay 的一场舞会，和一位他们已知是 spy 的女人跳过舞。所以他们有一张他和她跳舞的照片。基本上，他们只是在 checking him out。

当然，因为他的出身，他来自的国家里，police 并不像我们在 Australia 所认识的那样——在 Yugoslavia 或 Europe，police 和 army 人员往往被认为极其恶劣、咄咄逼人，朝你开枪和向你问话一样随意——所以他担心自己也会遇上这种事。

所以爸爸帮了他。我不知道具体怎么帮的，但那人后来没那么脆弱不安了，也确实留在了 Australia，继续工作，过得挺开心。这就是那种大家会找爸爸帮忙的事情，尽管那并不是他的工作。

## Glossop High School 的 Sports

**Michael:** 我们还是按时间顺序继续吧。你当时在 Glossop，不喜欢 Latin。

**Howard:** 嗯，我在学校打过一些 cricket。有意思的是，学校早上上课前和午餐时的活动时间是这样安排的：所有人——至少所有男生——都会到 field 上，比如到了 cricket 的时候，大约会有六个 cricket balls 从一头飞到另一头。batsmen 就在那里，没有 pads，什么都没有。基本上 cricket 一直打个不停。

我记得入学第一天还是第二天，我就在那儿，周围全是 17 岁的大孩子，而我才 11 岁。总之，ball 朝我飞来，我 caught 住了，显然，一个 first year 居然能 catch a ball，是件不寻常的事。于是轮到我 batting。我在那里，面对一个 year 11 学生投来的 ball，其他孩子全围着我——差不多有 20 个 slips，都觉得我会栽跟头。

我心想，好吧，这看起来不太妙。不过我想，第一个 ball 过来，我就来一下 slash。幸好，他们把 ball 投到右侧，正适合打一个 square cut。于是我就 square cut 过去，ball 一路打中了三个孩子，最后到了 boundary。

整件事最重要的结果是，第二个 ball bowled 过来时，我周围那圈人已经向外退了大约 five meters。谁也不想被打中。于是，我就这样进入了那些熬过第一关的 first year cricketers 的名人堂。

我们参加了很多 inter-school sport 和 athletic days。直到 15、16 和 open，我才开始 win age cups。不过在那之前，我也 won 过几场 races。这些我都很喜欢。同时我也在打 tennis。

学校每年都会举办一场大型 tennis tournament，那里有些很厉害的 tennis players。当然，first years 本来是不该过 first round 的，但我设法打进了 semi-final。我和一个叫 Phil Trelaw 的人 played，他大约 six foot six，而我当时大约 five foot six。最后我在 quarterfinal 里 beating 了他，然后在 semi-final 里被最终 won 的那个人 beaten。那场 game 是在 open court 上 played 的，所以有很多人在看。

后来，我还代表 open football team，迎战 Mount Gambier High School。能 picked 进那支 team，我确实挺意外，因为我才 14 岁，个头也不算特别大。我算是比较高，不过大概也够 quick。那场 match，我从 half forward flank kicked 了两个 goals，所以我特别开心。

那是我在 Glossop High School 的最后一年，因为第二年我去了 Adelaide Boys High School，差不多就是为了打 tennis。

**Michael:** 我知道你转过学，也大致知道是什么时候，不过要问你当时几岁，我未必答得出来。我知道大概是最后两三年的时候。

**Howard:** 我当时 15 岁。我在 14 岁那年年底转学，真正到 Adelaide High 报到是在 15 岁那年年初。

**Michael:** 昨天我也注意到一点——即使小时候，你对 sports 也相当 competitive。你仍然记得很多细节，和谁 played，甚至大概的 score。我想，这些对你相当重要。

**Howard:** 对，sport 在 Riverland 和我们家里都很重要。事情大概就是这样。不过也不是事事都轻松愉快。我确实记得，自己 winning 过 14-year-old sprint race，还 beating 了 previous record，那是一个叫 Robin Greenwood 的人创下的相当出色的 record。

第二年，在对 Renmark High 的 inter-school match 里，我决定不打 tennis，而是试着参加同时进行比赛的 cricket team。结果我们 12 个人走向 field 时，我才发现自己会被 selected 为 12th man，可真是谢谢了。这让我很意外，因为我以为自己能进 team。总之，我有点失望，不过还是得继续往前走。

match 开始后，我确实有几次机会去 field，但等轮到我们 bat 时——他们 hit 了 80 左右——Renmark 的那个 fast bowler 正在 running through 我们的 team。我想有一阵子我们是 eight for 12，他已经 taken 了一次 hat-trick，而且可能本来还应该再有两次 hat-tricks。

于是，负责的老师说，看我们打得这么不好，你也上去 bat 一下吧。总之，我出去 bat，知道自己肯定要挨收拾，不过他 bowled 的第一个 ball，位置正适合 cover drive，于是我 cover drove 出了 four。我们 team 响起了一阵掌声，因为那是我们第一次 scored 超过 one 的 runs。

不过，wicket keeper 对我说：“没有人敢这么对 Jono，还能全身而退。”

果然，下一个 ball 我 clean missed——它离 stumps 大概只差几厘米。再下一个 ball 差不多又在同一个位置，我又把它 clunked 出了 four。我就说：“你怎么知道我的名字叫‘没有人’？”

不过这也没持续多久，因为下一个 ball 我就被 bowled 了，所以最后是 out for eight。wicket keeper 觉得挺好笑。bowler 对一个 over 里被 hit 出 two fours 非常不高兴。这种事以前不会发生。我想他前一年好像还在 State School Boys primary school team 里。

所以我只是想说，并不是每次交手我都大获全胜。不过，撇开 putting the bat on the ball 不说，考虑到我们 team 当时的情况，我想那也算得上有点成功。

**Michael:** 离开家是什么感觉？你肯定得住在别人家里吧。

**Howard:** 我确实想家人。幸好，我要住的是一户认识的人家，所以从这个角度来说，那是段挺好的经历。不过只住了一年，之后就有好有坏了。

我知道妈妈和家里人都想我。尤其是 Robyn 大概特别想我，因为她还小，我以前总会陪她玩，逗她开心。多年后她说过，我走的时候她特别想我，因为那时她大概才九岁。从我 10 岁或 12 岁、她还是两三岁的小不点时，我就一直陪她玩。

**Michael:** 一个 12 岁的男孩——通常满脑子都是男孩子的事，喜欢和一群男孩跑出去玩——能这样关心一个小孩子，真是件很好的事。

**Howard:** 是啊。问题是，不幸的是，像那样离开家，你就进入了一个新环境，根本没有时间停下来，为这事那事发愁。你忙着做自己答应要做、也想做的事。要是当时没有这些事，我大概就会有更多时间回想，也会更想念大家。

我和 Vicki 也相处得很好，和 Sharon 也是。她们常被我缠着，要在后院玩 football 和 cricket，不过她们自己也都挺 athletic 的。当然，我们也不是一直都在玩。

**Michael:** 你父母有车吗？

**Howard:** 有，我们家有车。我记得的第一辆车是一辆旧 Essex，就像《The Untouchables》里的警车。后来我们有过 Holdens 和 Falcons。Falcon station wagon。

**Michael:** 那是你妈妈开车带你们到处去，还是你们坐公交车？

**Howard:** 嗯，没有公交车，只有校车。没有其他公共交通。所以以前都是妈妈开车送我们，或者我们自己骑自行车。我们其实住在 Nielsen Road，那条路是以我们家族命名的。从那里到学校或 Scouts 活动的地方，大约有一公里半。我参加过 Cubs 和 Scouts。那些活动我也参加过，晚上参加完 Scouts，再摸黑骑车回来。

**Michael:** 你说你的这些朋友住得很分散，我觉得挺有意思。这让我想到，要和朋友一起做些什么的时候，你们是怎么来回的。

**Howard:** 是啊，我们其实不怎么见面。大多数孩子要到 17 或 16 岁才有驾照，而且那些车都只是校车。我不记得自己曾经像那样去哪个朋友家过夜。我们其实就是学校里的朋友，还有一起运动的朋友。

不过，我们在 Adelaide 算是有过一次小聚。Sharon 和我去看 Gather Round，那是把所有 AFL matches 集中在一个周末、在 Adelaide 进行的活动。我和之前说的那四个朋友聚了聚，John Dolman 的哥哥或弟弟也来了。大家叙旧，聊得很开心。

还有，大约 15 年前，Adelaide High 百年校庆的时候，我见到了以前 football team 和 tennis team 的一些人。正式聚会之前，我们大约十来个人先在当地的酒馆碰了面，聊了好多事情。那次很开心。

## 童年朋友

**Howard:** 你提到朋友，这挺有意思。我以前还真没想过，除了在一起运动，我们其实没怎么一起待过。而且我们各自玩的 sports 也不一样。大概唯一共同玩的就是 football。

我在 Glossop High School 的时候，妈妈以前会开车带我去 Adelaide 接受 tennis coaching。那是每两周一次，所以隔周我就会代表 Obama and Monash Junior Colts 的 under 14s 玩 football。我们大多数时候都会 get beaten，不过那一年我最后 winning 了 Best and Fairest，而且我们和一些 top teams 有过几场不错的 matches。

有意思的是，以前经常和我 play against 的人里，有个来自 Cobdogla 的小伙子——这个地名我跟你说过。他叫 Steve McFarlane。他是个非常出色的 sports person，尤其擅长 cricket，tennis 和 football 也都很棒。我们以前经常 play against each other。

他最后始终没能在 tennis 上 beating 我，尽管有一次他本该赢的。在 Tintra 的一次 tournament 中，他在 net 处有一个 sitter of a shot。他当时肯定两眼放光，结果 hit 得太远——hit it out 了大约 20 厘米。最后他 losing 给了我。

不过，作为 footballer，他 won 了整个 comp 的 Best and Fairest。同一年，我其实是整个 comp 中仅次于他的 runner up。不过我想他应该拿到了 30 votes，平均下来相当于每场 match 都差不多是 second best on the ground。

Steve 是个好人，性格很安静。他是个很好的 sports person，我是说，他很懂得如何坦然面对 win 和 loss。不幸的是，他后来出了事。那时他应该才 30 出头——他在河里淹死了。他的朋友们出去钓鱼，他不知在哪儿掉进了河里，双脚被树根缠住。我想他们其实一直没找到他的遗体。大约过了五年，我才听说这件事。要是知道，我会去参加葬礼的。那时我在 Brisbane。

所以，是啊，Steve。很难过，因为他真是个好人。在彼此 competition 的时候，他和我大概跟任何人一样 competitive，但不会 overly competitive。并不是非拼个你死我活不可。

## 坦然面对 Winning 和 Losing

**Michael:** 你说到坦然面对 winning 和 losing。这是什么意思？又是从哪里学来的？是来自你爸爸、你妈妈，还是别的什么？

**Howard:** 那大概更可能是来自我爸爸。还有另一件与之相伴的事——我奶奶曾在我的签名纪念册上写过一句话。我们以前都有签名纪念册，会请别人在上面留言。她在我的那本上写的是：“好、更好、最好，永不止步，直到好变成更好，更好变成最好。”

这基本上就是说，要努力，尽自己所能。最后那部分——“永不止步，直到好变成更好，更好变成最好”——我稍微改了改，因为即使你已经做到了自己所能做到的最好，也未必就会是最好的那个人。你不可能 win 每一场 match。所以，更重要的是努力、尽自己所能，这和成为最好的那个人是不一样的。

我也说不准，可能更多是一种自然而然的事。大概是受爸爸影响。有时候，这些东西从某个地方而来，后来你已经不记得来源了，但它们当时对你的影响很深。

对我来说，不管是在学校还是 playing tennis，最重要的事情之一，就是给自己这样一个挑战：以一种让旁人看不出我是在 winning 还是 losing 的方式去 play。

**Michael:** 我明白你的意思了，是的。

**Howard:** 说起来挺有意思，因为有一天，我在这方面得到了一些很好的反馈。我当时在 Gold Coast playing seniors tennis，刚以 6-4、6-2 之类的比分 beating 了一个人。之后我们一群人在聊天，其中一个人说：“你的问题是，我们想知道你的 score，却没法从你的样子看出来。”所以我们得开口问。

我就想，这很好。因为我觉得，什么 intimidating opponents，还有那些做法——说真的，有什么意义呢？所以，那算是一次积极的反馈。等了大约 50 年才等来，因为以前从来没有人对我说过这话。

**Michael:** 我最近看了 Andy Murray 的一次采访。他说，他很佩服自己那些伟大的 rivals——尤其是 Federer 和 Nadal，Djokovic 稍微差一点——他们都特别能保持 unflappable。即使处于 down 的局面，也会继续坚持。

他说 retirement 之后，他去看女儿参加 cross country，她在 300 人里 place 19th。他高兴极了——她表现得太棒了。后来他反思起来，说自己要是 lost 一场 Grand Slam final，就会非常沮丧。他意识到，自己其实整个 career 都一直在苛责自己。

他说，他真的很后悔，自己从来没学会单纯享受 playing。他非得 winning 不可，哪怕是世界 second best，也觉得糟透了。我觉得很有意思的是，如果能改变自己 career 中的一件事，他就会改变这一点——学着更享受一点过程，也更客观地看待这件事：一年里在两项 Grand Slams 中 coming runner-up，其实并不代表你是个糟糕的人。

**Howard:** 是啊，成熟一点。有多少人从来没能参加 Grand Slam？嗯，实际上有几百万人。

## 认真对待 tennis

**Michael:** 那你当时一定相当认真地对待 tennis，毕竟你去 Adelaide High、离开家，部分原因就是这个。

**Howard:** 是啊，我想是的。我记得去之前的那一年，和爸妈聊过这件事。他们说：“你现在 14，快 15 了。如果你想 play tennis seriously，就得去 Adelaide，接受 proper coaching，并有 continuous play。”我们都觉得这是个好主意。

我并没有觉得自己是被逼着去的。基本上就是聊了这么一回。接着就是：“那你去哪儿，具体怎么安排呢？”于是就由妈妈来安排。

实际上，我住在一位 tennis players 的家里，他叫 John James，和他爸妈一起住。第二年，Sharon 也来了 Adelaide，因为她要上 Teachers College。于是她和我一起住在 Houston 小姐家。她人挺好，不过住在那里大概算不上多有意思。但她人很好。到了第三年，我们住在 Clyde 太太家。她更健谈一些。

不过，说到选哪所学校，当然是要花钱的。要么是有人找过我，要么就是 King's College 找过我，提出给我 sports scholarship，让我去 play tennis，还有别的什么。King's College 那所学校的男生都戴那种 boater hats，基本上看着挺难看的。和 Brisbane Boys College 的帽子一样。

我说，我不去那所学校。我才不戴那种帽子。

**Michael:** 穿着上的罪过让你受不了了！

**Howard:** 是啊，“你们可以再多掏点钱。”反正去那里也是要花钱的，而在 Adelaide High 就不用冒戴那种帽子的风险。

后来有了 Football 这条路，大概是在那里读到一半的时候，我意识到，自己其实并不觉得接下来人生的 10 或 15 年都会 playing tennis。到处跑是要花钱的，大概负担不起。

我的 football 表现确实很好。我大概是 South Australia 的 high school football team 里最好的 player。我们参加的是 highest grade schools comp，我在那支 team 里担任 centre half forward。拿到了最多的 votes，还有 player 方面的那些荣誉。所以我本来很可能会在 South Australia 打 top level football，很可能是加入 North Adelaide；我 16 岁时，他们就邀请我和他们一起 train 过。就那么一次，大概是“看看这孩子怎么样”。

离开学校时，我想过再去他们那里 trial，这也是他们希望的。不过我没有去，因为我们来了 Queensland。我本来可以选择留在那里，但我觉得——我想我爸妈——结果我并没有回 Bundaberg 的家，反正是去上 college 了。

我发现自己在 football 上有些不太一样的 strengths，这很好。我为自己有这些 strengths 感到高兴。不过到最后，sport 不再是第一位了。上了 college 后，我意识到自己对 teaching、educating、developing 别人有一种使命感。Playing football 和 tennis 很好，可以继续，但不是作为 vocation。我后来又回去为 Mayne play football，不过那其实是 semi-professional。

## 父母送你去 Adelaide

**Michael:** 稍微往回说一点，听起来，是你爸妈向你指出，如果你想认真对待 tennis，就需要去 Adelaide。我只是确认一下，自己有没有理解对。

**Howard:** 是的。

**Michael:** 他们能这么做，挺有意思的。我只能想象，送你离开时，他们大概很难过。这么做真是非常支持你。

**Howard:** 嗯，我同意。当时还是个青少年的我，大概并没有像应该的那样充分体会到这一点。他们并不是叫我走，而是请我考虑去不去。我想他们只是想确认，我是不是会认真对待这件事。

到后来，对于 pursuing 我的 tennis career 这件事，我大概有些内疚，因为他们为了让我上学，牺牲了时间、精力和金钱。但他们从来、从来没有提过这些。

**Michael:** 那时候又不是签字让自己坐一辈子牢。更像是在问，这个机会适合我吗？

**Howard:** 嗯，没错，所以你得多参加 tournaments 之类的，才能知道。我也确实这么做了，去 Melbourne、Sydney、Brisbane 和 Adelaide 参加 state titles，也参加 national titles，也就是 Australian Open。参加过 Juniors、Intermediates 和 Men's Open titles。在 Australia 的 Juniors 里，我大概排在 top 20。还有些人大概不在那个 top 20 里，后来却在 professional tennis 上做出了点成绩，主要是 playing doubles。

所以是有可能的，不过到最后，我大概是在说，人生不只有 tennis，我要去做那些别的事，尽管还不知道具体会是什么。

有意思的是，Bowen 也有过类似的经历——过了一两年，他决定自己不想 play competitively 了。

**Michael:** 我上次回去时，他跟我说过类似的话。我当时就觉得，和你刚才说的很像。

**Howard:** 是啊。

**Michael:** 后来成为著名作家的 David Foster Wallace，青少年时期其实也是一位非常认真的 tennis player，后来基本上也有了同样的认识。他成年后写过一些 essays，谈 top players——谈 Tracy Austin、Roger Federer，还有另外几个人。

他那篇谈 Tracy Austin 的 essay 里，有一点很有意思：看过她的许多访谈后，他最终意识到，她并不是一个很有趣的人。而这也是她成为如此出色的 tennis player 的部分原因。她就是非常、非常想 win。没有什么外部因素把事情变得复杂。这也是她成为极其出色的 tennis player 的部分原因。

这个看法挺有意思。和你青少年时期得出的结论很相似。

**Howard:** 是啊，我能理解。肯定得这样——而且她当时应该也还是个青少年，她 winning Grand Slams 时，才 14 或 15 岁。

**Michael:** 没错。她是 US Open 有史以来最年轻的 winner。或者说，无论如何，她很长时间里都是。

## 澳大利亚网球公开赛（The Australian Open）

**Michael:** 所以你参加了 Australian Open，然后就在你爸妈搬到 Bundaberg 的同时，你搬到了 Brisbane，是这样吗？你有没有考虑过去 Bundaberg？

**Howard:** 没有，他们当时已经在那里住了一年了。

**Michael:** 哦，明白了，所以过了一年，你才从 Adelaide 搬过去。

**Howard:** 对。所以我直接去了 Brisbane 的 Teachers College。我在 Adelaide 拿到了去那里就读的 scholarship，后来又拿到了去 Brisbane 的 scholarship，所以我就去了 Brisbane。那也差不多是我从 tennis 转向 football 的转折点。那个假期里，我放下了 tennis racket，拿起了 footy，去参加 Mayne 的 training。

**Michael:** 我记得你跟我说过，你大概在 1968 或 69 年左右参加过 Australian Open。

**Howard:** 我想是 68 年。那是最后一届只有 amateurs 才能参加的 Australian Open。因为早在 10 年前，就出现了那个 breakaway group，Laver、Hoad、Rosewall，还有那些人都离开了，去参加他们自己的 professional tour。所以那些 players 里面最好的都走了。

1968 Australian Open 的 field 大概是历来最弱的。因此，他们给了一些崭露头角的 juniors 参加 men's 的机会。我对阵的那个人，我记得当时在 Australia 的 junior 中排 number three。Billy Lloyd。我记得比分是 6-4, 7-5, 8-6，差不多是这样。所以我还是有些机会的。

**Michael:** 挺不错的。

**Howard:** 不过，我跟别人解释时一直很谨慎，我并不是说自己达到了当时 Australian Open men's 的 standard。如果当时 professionals 都在，我们肯定不可能参赛。

**Michael:** 不过，能参加还是很让人兴奋吧？是在 Melbourne 吗？

**Howard:** 是，很兴奋。对，在 Melbourne。在迁到现在的地方之前，当时是在 Flinders Park。

前一周是 Linton Cup，也就是当时规模很大的 Junior Interstate Competition。所以前一周，我们对阵的队伍里有 Phil Dent、John Alexander，还有其他几个后来 played professionally 的 juniors。还有 Jeff Masters。

**Michael:** 那些人确实都是顶尖的。都是很出色的 tennis players。

**Howard:** 对。我跟他们中的几个人交过手。在 Sydney 对阵过 Jeff Masters——他以 6-3, 6-3 击败了我。后来我又对阵 Phil Dent。我们当时在 Adelaide 的 Memorial Drive 比赛。有一次我真的很恼火，因为我们正打着，大家都觉得他会击败我。我想他当时在 Australia 的 junior 中排 number one。

我们打到 four all，接着争夺 5-4 的那个 game 非常接近，我输了，然后他就以 6-4 赢了。但在这期间，有三次他 served 时 hit a let cord，我 called let，而且顺便说一句，我也 returned the ball 了。他却觉得自己没听见 let，所以基本上就是在指责我 cheating。

我说，嗯，我听见了。而且我实际上也已经 returned the ball back into play 了。又不是你 aced me。于是他说，那我找别人来。我要找个 umpire。我说，请便，这样正好能把这个问题说清楚。如果再有 lets，就会有人 called。

不过，后来当然再也没有 lets 了，所以他以 6-4, 6-1 击败了我。但他指责我 cheating，真的让我很难受。我说，我这辈子从没拿过任何不属于我的 point。现在也不会开这个头。

所以那件事很烦人。不过，这多少也说明了一点——跟他，还有 Jeff Masters 打，6-3, 6-3 是个相当普通的 score，但毕竟还是 competitive 的。

可能我对自己的 level of play 并没有很准确的判断。我想，我上场时大概并不总觉得自己应该 win，或者能够 win。我也不是抱着应该 lose 的想法上场，但在 winning 那些 winnable 的 points 和 important points 这方面，我可能不够 aggressive。

我想，这让我意识到，作为 tennis player，我确实不够 aggressive，没办法一直在 tour 上打下去，因为我心里还会想着其他事情。当时我有点不想跟别人说，因为听起来——我的想法是，生活里不只有 tennis，所以我要去做其他事情，虽然我也并不清楚那些事情究竟会是什么。

## 俱乐部风波（The Incident at the Club）

**Michael:** 所以你爸妈搬到了 Bundaberg。这些年我听你提起过好几次这件事，但一直没弄清楚完整的来龙去脉。我记得我也确实听你爸爸非常含蓄地提起过一两次。感觉这对他来说，大概仍然是个相当敏感的话题。所以，当地那家 hotel，或者说 club，当时究竟发生了什么？

**Howard:** 对，是 club。这让妈妈很难过，爸爸大概也一样。

事情应该发生在我 high school 的倒数第二年和最后一年。当时我并不知道，爸爸在 club 的工作是 manager。所以他雇人照看那里、担任 barman，处理诸如此类的事情。

有一段时间，其中一个叫 Lehman 的人，一直在偷拿 club 的 till 里的钱。起初偷得不多，后来越来越厉害。爸爸一开始不确定是谁干的，也不清楚是怎么回事。

但长话短说，他后来发现，有人在肆无忌惮地拿钱。于是他真的自掏腰包，补上了一部分损失。他没有当面处理、sacking 那个人，尽管他本来可以这么做——他最后之所以知道是谁，是因为这个 Lehman 去度了两周假，而那段时间就没有钱不翼而飞了。

但这让家里的气氛很紧张，因为——妈妈想知道，凭什么要用他们自己的钱去填那个窟窿。所以家里为这些事争吵过几次。爸爸只是想再给那个人一次机会。但那个人以前就惹过法律上的麻烦。他是当地人——在上一个工作地方就偷过钱。

所以，爸爸所在 club 的 management committee 最后因为他没有采取 appropriate managerial action，把爸爸 sacking 了。有些人觉得，这件事本身就有点过分了——不如现在把那个偷钱的人 sacking，然后重新开始。

爸爸多少参与了 politics，因为他是 Labour Party candidate，而当地 community 里有相当一部分人并不是 Labour Party supporters，这也成了他的一处不利因素。当时的说法是，那些人因为不满爸爸是 Labour 的人，就拿这件事当借口。

话虽如此，也有人支持他，只是人数不够，改变不了结果。所以他最后丢了那里的工作，还是回到果园干活。但我想，在一个发生过这种事的 community 里继续生活，让他们感到压力太大了。而爸爸可能也为此感到内疚。

他确实告诉过我，他很后悔没有做自己本该做的事。

**Michael:** 他觉得自己本该怎么做？直接 sack 那个人吗？

**Howard:** 对。给过他一两次机会之后就该这么做。而且，用家里的钱去弥补别人的不当行为，是不合适的。

那件事很不好。他跟我提过——我们在 Brisbane 的时候，有一次他为 club 那些事，算是向我道了歉。我说，我们不会去想，不会再去计较你在那件事里做了什么。你已经说了自己本该怎么做，但其他人本来应该把这件事处理得好得多。

我很确定，如果我当时知道这件事——直到他被辞退之后，我才知道此前发生的这一切——我肯定会找那个 management group 里的某些人说上几句。我知道爸爸在各种事情上帮助过他们，而他们却说了对他不利的话。

总之，这就是背后的原因。当然，这可能也算是顺势而为。我想妈妈受到的影响大概比爸爸更大。而且，我觉得她本来可能也在推动到 Bundaberg 接手一家 hotel，回到那里熟悉的环境。所以，这么做并不奇怪，但如果没有那件事，他们肯定不会这样做。

所以我不太确定当时自己多大。15、16——事情发生的时候，大概是 16 岁。

**Michael:** 如果你妈妈觉得自己被当众抛下，独自承受难堪，你有没有一点这样的感觉？还是说，你根本没往心里去？

**Howard:** 没有，我没有。这也是我对爸爸说的话。我说，爸爸，我不觉得这是我的问题，也不觉得是你的问题。这是他们的问题。你几乎没做错什么。而且，这个 punishment 肯定比 crime 严重得多。

我只是对他说，不，这绝对没有对我们的生活造成任何负面影响。正面的影响是，你在那种情况下信任别人时会更加谨慎一点，也会及早采取行动，而不是等到为时已晚。我没有把这番话对爸爸说，但这就是我从中学到的东西。

倒不是说，从那以后，我生活里真的发生过什么需要我担心这种问题的事，但这仍然是个教训——人并不总是好人。显然，爸爸的做法并不是 illegal。他又不是在 breaking the law。他是想帮助别人，而且用的是自己的钱。你愿意的话，可以说这是 mismanagement，但它并不是 unlawful。

没有人说爸爸拿了钱，或者有过类似的说法。这一点大家都很清楚。他知道，一直搞鬼的是那个 Lehman。另一个 barmen 也认为是那个人干的。而那个 barmen 很担心，因为他觉得自己可能会被怀疑——其实除了爸爸以外，总共也只有两个 barmen。

不过，妈妈确实一直放不下这件事，我想甚至到了让爸爸很难应对的地步，她总是纠结于其中不对的地方。但那确实是 injustice。我也有同样的感受。Injustice 已经发生了，而那些人本来应该做得更好。他们原本有机会把事情处理得更好。

**Michael:** 后来那些年，你爸妈经常回 Monash 吗？

**Howard:** 我们确实回去过几次。有一次是 Stuart 参加 football 的那段时间。你还记得吗？

**Michael:** 记得，我记得很清楚。

**Howard:** 我们往南走，途经 Monash，就去了 club 喝了一杯。那里已经换了个人接手——多年前发生的那些事，大家都已经放下了。

后来，我们还在 pub 见到了爸爸以前的一些 tennis 伙伴——Fundaks、Vineys 和 Harrises 那几家人，大家聊得很开心。但爸爸那时确实不太像平常的自己。大家都盼着他简单讲几句之类的。他却把这件事交给了我。我当时应该三十五六到快四十岁了。所以我就简单说了几句，表达我们有多么感激那些长年与爸爸交好的朋友，也说事情最终没有完全按照他们最初期待的那样发展，实在令人遗憾。

不过，那是个很愉快的夜晚。但有件事很有意思——我们前面说过，我对 playing tennis professionally 的想法，以及后来是怎么改变的——那天晚上在 club，有个人，其实就是 John Hinwood，跟我在一旁聊了几句，他说：“我很惊讶，你居然没有打 professional tennis。你比某某、某某都强。”他提了几个人的名字。

我说，好吧。其实很简单，从他的角度看，他就是那样想的。我并不是那样想的。倒不是说，我当时在把自己跟其他 players 作比较，因为你就是上场去打。不过有意思的是，在他的印象里，那是我会去做的事。而且，他觉得我有能力做到。

## Adelaide High School——一个不同的世界

**Michael:** 我刚才在想——你妈妈真是特别、特别、特别为你骄傲。我 8、9 岁的时候，她会拿出以前的剪贴簿，主要给我和 Stuart 看——Kate 那时应该还太小——里面都是你们以前的东西，尤其是你和 Sharon 的，也有一些 Vicki 和 Robyn 的。你们做过的事、参加过的 races，还有所有那些 tennis 的事。

我刚才把这些和他们送你去 Adelaide Boys High 这件事联系起来了。那可能真的不只是为了你的 tennis。她和你爸爸大概是这样想的——他很多方面都很出色，而那是一个稍微大一些的世界，也许他可以出去看看其他的可能性，而这些在 Monash 未必能看得到。

**Howard:** 对，很有可能。我不记得有哪次谈话具体这样说过，但我能想象他们可能说过。比如，“那里还会有别的你喜欢的事”，而我也确实找到了。

对我来说，Adelaide High 的环境很不一样，因为我原先所在的 Glossop High School 几乎全是 white Anglo-Saxon，而我刚到 Adelaide High 时所在的班里有 29 个孩子，来自 17 个不同的 nationalities。因为学校在城市里——Adelaide——所以有 Italians、Greeks、Yugoslavs、Czechs。他们要么已经是下一代，也就是说不是当地的 first generation，要么可能出生在海外，很小就来到了 Adelaide。

所以，我确实接触到了一种完全不同的 culture。那段日子很有意思。

说来有意思，我其实在 high school 的第三年 failed 了。也就是我的 intermediate year。

**Michael:** 那是在 Glossop。

**Howard:** 对，所以从 Glossop 到 Adelaide High 之间——当时按理说，如果你所有科目都拿到 good marks，那就没问题，但如果 English 没拿到 pass mark，你那一整年就会 fail。

所以我到现在还是想不通怎么会这样，因为我的 English 根本没那么差，所以我肯定是哪里搞砸了，却自己都不知道。不管怎样，妈妈去找 high school 替我争取，说我不应该在 Adelaide High 继续读 intermediate，而应该进入第四年，也就是 leaving year。我也确实进去了。然后那一年我又 repeated 了一次。

因为我一直比大多数人小一岁半——至少小一岁——也许有些人和我年龄差不多，但我没遇到几个。所以我不得不 repeat year 11。我倒不介意，觉得也还好，因为那时我才 16 岁。不过，和你那时候的年龄差不多。你上学时，five 岁就读 year one，16 岁就 finished year 12，17 岁开始上 uni。

**Michael:** 没错。

**Howard:** 总之，结果看来，我并不是一个很好的 student。就算我有什么聪明之处，也不在 physics、chemistry、maths 1、maths 2 这些方面——尽管我还是相当奇迹般地 got through 了。

**Michael:** 所以你的心思在别的地方。然后你爸妈就搬去了 Bundaberg——

**Howard:** 嗯。

**Michael:** 我看一下时间。我们已经聊了一个小时 20 分钟，另外还花了些时间做准备。

**Howard:** 对。嗯，我也准备今天就到这里了。

**Michael:** That's really remarkable.

**Howard:** Yeah. I'm sure Dad was probably on the P&C or something like that. It wouldn't have been his normal form to create a controversial situation, but apparently that's what happened. **Michael:** My impression growing up, several decades after that, was that \[European\] Australia still had a difficult relationship with its indigenous people and often not a particularly positive one. People were often pretty racist. It just seems like a very interesting and admirable fight to be picking on his part.

He's more likely to get a positive response where there was no doubt about what the comment was and no doubt about who it was directed to and therefore no doubt about who should respond. It wouldn't have been fashionable to be making that argument, even though obviously social justice was probably not talked about much at the school, but it was implied that people should be treated well, indigenous or not. Obviously in this case either Dad had observed something or had been told something by somebody else and he was acting on their behalf. I don't know which it might have been.

**Michael:** That's pretty remarkable. Also really interesting that Sharon reported it to you and that you remembered.

**Howard:** Yeah, I think I must have been chatting to her about the little book that I was writing and looking for stories about things that happened that were of some note. And I guess that was one of them.

## Starting at Glossop High School

**Michael:** Yesterday we got up to the end of your primary school career. It seems to have involved throwing of dirt, a certain amount of winning of races, quite a bit of tennis, and apparently a lost Dux opportunity at the end. So after that, you went to Glossop High School, is that right?

**Howard:** Yeah, Glossop High School. It was a few kilometers away. In those days, when you got to year seven, on Friday afternoons we got to do woodwork over at the Glossop High School. Those of us boys would do woodwork and the girls would do domestic science. So we rode our bikes over and back. That was good fun. So we already had some experience with the high school.

The high school—you had to do a test to see what year level you're going to be in. It was all streamed. We didn't know that, of course. We had no idea what all that was about. So you did a test, and then you got into 1A or 1B or 1C or 1D or 1E. The system must have been fairly flawed because I got into 1A.

**Michael:** Ha!

**Howard:** So I was the only one from my primary school that got into 1A. The downside to that, of which there were plenty, was that I had to do Latin, and that didn't really excite many people but certainly didn't excite me. ## Religion and Sunday School

**Michael:** Speaking of Latin masses, did religion play any role at all as a kid?

**Howard:** Yeah, actually interesting question. I was thinking of it yesterday when I was talking about school. As kids, we went down to the local Church of England on Sunday morning to do Sunday school. We had Mrs. Hallam as our Sunday school teacher. She was probably only about 55, but we thought of her as about 85. She was a nice lady.

During that time, I actually read the whole Bible. It was like slightly larger than a pack of cards. Small print. Ask me to recite too many things from it and I'd be struggling, but I found it quite interesting and I'm glad I did it. It's an educational experience, even though the language was older English, so it was a bit of a thing to wade through.

Sharon would have done that as well, and Vicki probably would have done it. I don't know how many years we did Sunday school. It wasn't on every week of the year—I think they spared us from that—but it felt like about half the year, maybe three years, when we were 10 and 11 or something like that.

**Michael:** It was probably a King James Bible or something like that. It must have been a very small one.

**Howard:** I don't know what it was to be honest. My understanding is that the King James Bible came out later and was a bit more accessible language-wise. But then there are different versions of all that.

Anyway, Mum was originally from Bundaberg and from a German background. They were Apostolic. But there was no Apostolic church in Monash, but there was a Church of England church which was Protestant, so it sort of belonged, apparently.

And we used to say grace at the table every night. "For what we are about to receive, make us truly thankful. Amen."

And then, funnily enough, you'll find this of interest—Mum taught us a little prayer to say at night. And I still say a prayer every night.

**Michael:** I didn't know that. What's the prayer? What was the prayer you said as a kid and what's the prayer you say now?

**Howard:** I can't remember the one from childhood, that's going back a bit too far. The current one is just a private thing really. It's more about being positive and thankful and being good to people, essentially. It wasn't like a prayer that Mum taught us that was striking fear into you out of fear from God or whatever. It was quite not that. But the Protestant church was fairly right wing if you're going to choose a reference to it.

So that's about the sum total, I think, of my religious experience. But when it comes down to it, it's a daily presence of some sort.

**Michael:** Did this gradually evolve over the years or did you decide at some point?

**Howard:** Yeah, no, it's about version 10 or something like that now. It changes. But it's more about looking after people that are dear to me. You guys all feature in it, the family. The word God doesn't actually appear in it. So it's probably where I got to the point where I don't actually and never did ever actually believe in God as such. But my quirky bit of humor about it is that God is just, in typical Australian fashion, a shortened version of "good."

So I've stuck to that. Whenever anybody asks me—I know Cooper's asked me that, and I think Bowen has asked me—"Do you believe in God?" I say, well, I believe in good, which is just a different version of God. Because the idea of there being an actual being doesn't sort of… I couldn't quite accept that one. But I could sense that things are about being positive.

**Michael:** Was your dad religious at all?

**Howard:** Not really. Not church religious anyway. The interesting thing is, and I've thought about this a few times, in terms of the way he acted he was probably more Christ-like than most other people that I know. I'm not prepared to say he was another Christ or anything, but it's sort of like… is it in what you say or is it in what you do?

**Michael:** A lot of service to community and other people.

**Howard:** Yeah, and just being fair to other people. One thing I remember—an event that was not about religion really, about his attitude and his ability to help people. One day, he and I were out on one of the fruit blocks picking grapes or doing whatever we were doing. This car screeched up in the road alongside us about 100 meters away and a guy raced out and was yelling out to Dad about helping, could Dad help him with something. The guy was originally from Yugoslavia, so he had broken English and I couldn't really understand what he was talking about.

It turns out the guy thought he was being tracked by the Stasi, which is like the Gestapo or somebody from military, and that he was going to be sent from Australia because he was obviously a migrant.

Anyway, Dad got to the bottom of it. What the bottom of it was that during the war, which was only like 10 or 15 years prior to that, the ASIO spy network in Australia was trailing a whole lot of people that they thought might be Russian spies. And this guy had been at a dance in Mackay, Queensland, and danced with a woman who they knew was a spy. So they had a photo of him dancing with her. They're just checking him out, basically.

And of course, because of his background coming from a country where police weren't the way we see them in Australia—the police and army folks in Yugoslavia or Europe often were seen as extremely negative and aggressive and just as likely to shoot you as ask you a question—so he was worried that might happen to him.

So Dad helped him. I don't know how exactly, but the guy became less fragile and did stay in Australia and did keep working and was happy. That's where people did go to Dad to get help even though that's not what his job was.

## Sports at Glossop High School

**Michael:** Maybe we'll keep going chronologically. You were at Glossop, not enjoying Latin.

**Howard:** Well, I played a bit of cricket at the school and it was interesting that the way play times were set up before school and at lunchtime, everybody—every guy anyway—was out on the field, say cricket time, with about six cricket balls going from one end to the other. And batsmen were in, no pads, no nothing. So there was continuous cricket basically.

I remember in my first or second day of schooling, I was out there with all these big 17 year old kids and I'm the 11 year old. Anyway, the ball came to me and I caught it, which apparently was unusual for a first year to actually catch a ball. So I was in batting. I'm there facing up to the ball from some year 11 kid, and all the kids were surrounding me—like about 20 slips thinking that I would crash.

I thought, okay, this doesn't look too good. But I thought I'll have a slash at the first ball that comes to me. Fortunately, they put the ball right out on the right side for a square cut. So I square cut it and hit three kids on the way through and the ball went to the boundary.

The big thing out of all this was that on the second ball that was bowled to me, the circle around me expanded out by about five meters. Nobody wanted to get hit. So that was my induction into the Hall of Fame of first year cricketers who made it past the first thing.

We played lot of inter-school sport and athletic days. And I didn't win age cups until you got to 15, 16 and open. But I won a few races along the way. Enjoyed all that. And in the meantime I was playing tennis.

And then I was playing in the open football team against the Mount Gambier High School. That was where I did get surprised that I got picked in that team because I was only 14 and not all that big really. I was reasonably tall but I was probably quick enough. And I kicked a couple of goals in that match from a half forward flank, so I was very happy with that.

That was my last year at Glossop High School, because the next year I went down to Adelaide Boys High School to play tennis, more or less.

**Michael:** I was aware of the transfer and roughly of when it occurred, but I'm not sure I could have said how old you were when you made the switch. I knew it was like the last two or three years.

**Howard:** I was 15. I made the switch at the end of the year that I was 14 and the beginning of the year of 15 is when I actually rolled up at Adelaide High.

**Michael:** Something I noticed yesterday as well—even as a kid, you were pretty darn competitive about sports. You still remember a lot of detail about who you played, even roughly what the score was. I guess that it mattered quite a bit to you.

**Howard:** Yeah, well, sport was big in the Riverland and in our family. That's sort of what happened. But it wasn't all beer and skittles. I do remember winning the 14-year-old sprint race and beating the previous record which was a really good record from a guy called Robin Greenwood.

And in my second year, instead of playing tennis in the inter-school match against Renmark High, I decided to opt to try and play in the cricket team which was played at the same time. Anyway, turns out as we were going out to the field with the 12 of us, I was going to be selected as the 12th man, thanks very much. Which surprised me because I thought I might have made the team. Anyway, I was a bit disappointed, but onward and upward.

The match was played and I did get to field a bit a couple of times, but then when it was our turn to bat—they hit 80 or something—this fast bowler from Renmark was running through our team. I think we were eight for 12 at some point and he'd taken a hat-trick and should have had two more hat-tricks probably.

So the teacher who was running the thing said you might have a bat seeing as we're not doing so good. Anyway, I went out to bat and I knew I was going to get clunked, but the first ball he bowled he put it in the right place for a cover drive, so I cover drove it for four. Lot of applause from our team because that was the first runs we scored more than one.

But the wicket keeper says to me, "Nobody does that to Jono and gets away with it."

Sure enough, the next ball I clean missed—just missed the stumps by probably a few centimeters. Then the next ball was more or less in the same spot and I clunked that for four. And I said, "How did you know my name was Nobody?"

But it was short lived because the next ball I was bowled, so I was out for eight. The wicket keeper thought it was pretty funny. The bowler was very unhappy about getting hit for two fours in one over. That didn't happen. I think he was in the State School Boys primary school team a year before or something.

So just to say that not all my interactions were triumphs. But apart from putting the bat on the ball, I suppose that's somewhat triumphant given the nature of our team at the time.

**Michael:** What was it like moving away from home? You must have boarded.

**Howard:** I did miss my family. Fortunately I was going to stay with a family that I knew so it was quite a positive experience from that point of view. But that was just for one year and then after that it was a bit of a mixed bag.

I know my mother and the family missed me. And Robyn probably in particular missed me because she was young and she and I used to play with her and entertain her. She's said years gone by she really missed me when I left because she was nine or something like that. From the time I was 10 or 12 and she was just a little bub like two or three.

**Michael:** It's a nice thing for a 12 year old boy—usually pretty oriented towards boy things and going off with boy gangs—to be oriented that way toward a little kid.

**Howard:** Yeah. The thing is, unfortunately, when you go away from home like that, you're going into a new environment where you don't have any time really to stop and worry about stuff. You're so busy doing what it is that you have agreed to do and wanted to do. Probably if I hadn't had that, you'd have had more time to reflect and miss people.

Vicki and I got along well as well and so did Sharon and I. They were harassed by me wanting to play football and cricket in the backyard a lot, but they were quite athletic themselves anyway. And of course we didn't do it all the time.

**Michael:** Did your parents have a car?

**Howard:** Yeah, we had cars. The first car I remember is an old Essex, like the police car in "The Untouchables". Eventually we had Holdens and Falcons. Falcon station wagon.

**Michael:** So was your mum driving you around or were you catching the bus?

**Howard:** Well, there was no bus, only a school bus. There was no other public transport. So Mum used to drive us, or we used to ride our bikes. We actually lived on Nielsen Road, which was named after our family. That was about one and a half kilometers to school or to Scouts. I was part of the Cubs and Scouts. Did that sort of stuff as well, riding in the dark from nighttime Scouts.

**Michael:** I was interested in you talking about these friends of yours who are all spread out. It made me think about how did you get around if you're going to do stuff with your friends.

**Howard:** Yeah, we didn't really see each other. Most kids didn't have a license until they were 17 or 16, and the buses were just school buses. I don't remember ever actually staying at any one of a mate's places like that. We were just school friends really, and sports friends.

But we had a little reunion of sorts in Adelaide. Sharon and I went down to watch the Gather Round where all the AFL matches were played in Adelaide in one weekend. And I had a reunion with those four friends I was talking about and John Dolman's brother as well. We had some good times catching up.

And about 15 years ago at the hundredth anniversary of Adelaide High, I caught up with people who were in the football team and tennis team. About a dozen of us met up in the local pub before the formal reunion and had chats about a whole lot of things. That was good fun.

## Childhood Friends

**Howard:** It's interesting you talk about friends. I hadn't really thought about the fact that we didn't spend much time together other than if we were playing sport. And the sports we played were different from each other. Probably the only one in common was football.

While I was at Glossop High School, Mum used to drive me down to Adelaide for tennis coaching. It was only once a fortnight, so I used to play football on the other fortnight for Obama and Monash Junior Colts, under 14s. We used to get beaten most times, but I ended up winning the Best and Fairest that year and we had some good matches against some top teams.

It's interesting because one of the guys I used to play a lot against was a young kid from Cobdogla—that name I've given you. Steve McFarlane was his name. He was a very good sports person, particularly good at cricket, also very good at tennis and football. We used to play against each other a lot.

He never ended up beating me in tennis, although he should have done once. He had a sitter of a shot on the net in a tournament in Tintra. His eyes must have lit up and he hit it too far—hit it out by about 20 centimeters. And he ended up losing to me.

But as a footballer, he won Best and Fairest for the whole comp. And that's in the same year that I was actually runner up in that whole comp to him. But I think he would have gotten 30 votes, which would have averaged about second best on the ground every match.

Steve was a good guy, really quiet sort of guy. A very good sports person as in he knew how to take a win and a loss quite well. Unfortunately something happened to him later in life. He must have been in his early 30s—he drowned in the river. His friends were out fishing and somewhere he fell in and got his feet tangled up in a root system. I don't think they ever found his body actually. I didn't hear about it for about five years. I would have gone to a funeral. I was in Brisbane at the time.

So yeah, Steve. Sad because he was such a good guy. He and I were probably as competitive as anybody else in terms of being in competition with each other without being overly competitive. It wasn't a do or die effort.

## Winning and Losing Well

**Michael:** You're talking about winning and losing well. What does that mean? And where did it come from? Was it from your dad or from your mum or something else?

**Howard:** That's probably more likely from my dad. There's another thing that sat alongside of that—my grandma wrote in my autograph book. We had autograph books that we got comments from people about. And she wrote in mine: "Good, better, best, never let it rest, till your good is better and your better best."

Which basically interprets as just try hard and do your best. The end bit—"never let it rest till your good is better and your better best"—I kind of changed that up a bit because if you're doing as well as you possibly could, you weren't necessarily going to be the best. You weren't going to win every match. So the more important thing was to try hard and do your best, which is different from being the best.

I don't know, it's probably a bit more of a natural thing. It probably came from Dad. Sometimes these things come from places where you don't remember them afterwards, but they have a strong impact on you at the time.

For me, one of the most important things, whether at school or playing tennis, was a challenge to play in such a way that nobody knew whether I was winning or losing while I was playing.

**Michael:** I see what you mean, yeah.

**Howard:** Apparently it was quite funny because I got some good feedback on that one day. I was playing seniors tennis down at the Gold Coast and I'd just come off from beating a guy 6-4, 6-2 or whatever. We were chatting afterwards with a group of people and one of the guys said, "The problem with you is we want to know what your score is but we can't tell by looking at you." So we have to ask.

And I thought, that's good. Because I feel like a lot about intimidating opponents and doing stuff—what's the point of that, really? So that was a bit of positive feedback. It took about 50 years to come because nobody had ever said that to me before.

**Michael:** I saw Andy Murray interviewed recently. He was saying that he quite admired the fact that all of his great rivals—particularly Federer and Nadal and a little bit less Djokovic—they were just very good at being unflappable. They'd be down and they'd just keep going.

He said after his retirement, he'd gone to see his daughter place 19th out of 300 in the cross country. And he was over the moon—she had done so awesome. And then he was reflecting later, he said he would be so down if he lost a Grand Slam final. And he realized that he had just been beating up on himself really for his whole career.

He said he really regrets that he never learned how to just enjoy playing. He had to be winning, and being the second best in the world was dreadful. I thought it was really interesting that if he could change one thing about his career, that's what he would change—he'd learn to just enjoy the process a little bit more and have a bit more perspective on the fact that coming runner-up at two Grand Slams in a year isn't actually a sign that you're a terrible person.

**Howard:** Yeah, be a bit more mature. How many people never got to a Grand Slam? Well, millions actually.

## Serious About Tennis

**Michael:** So you must have been pretty darn serious about tennis if you were going off to Adelaide High and leaving home partially because of that.

**Howard:** Yeah, I guess I was. I remember having a chat with Mum and Dad about it the year before I went. They were saying, "You're 14 coming on 15 now. Actually, I lived with the family of one of the tennis players, John James, and his Mum and Dad. Then the following year, Sharon came down to Adelaide because she was going to Teachers College. **Howard:** Yeah, "you can cough up some more money." It was going to cost money to go there anyway, so there were no hat risks at Adelaide High.

We played in the highest grade schools comp and I was the centre half forward in that team. Got most votes and players stuff. When I went to college, I realized that I had a calling for teaching, educating, developing other people. Playing football and tennis was good, keep doing that, but not as a vocation. I got back to play football for Mayne, but it was semi-professional really.

## Your Parents Sending You to Adelaide

**Michael:** Just to go back a little bit, it sounds like it was your Mum and Dad who pointed out to you that if you wanted to take tennis seriously, you needed to go to Adelaide. Just checking if I got that right.

**Howard:** Yeah.

**Michael:** It's an interesting thing for them to have done. I can only imagine they were probably very sad to send you off. It's a very supportive thing to have done.

**Howard:** Well, I agree with that. At the time, I don't think I really appreciated that as much as I should have as a teenager. It wasn't like they were telling me to go. They were inviting me to think about going. I guess they were just checking that I would be serious about that.

## The Australian Open

**Michael:** So you played the Australian Open and then you moved up to Brisbane at the same time as your Mum and Dad were moving to Bundaberg, is that right? Did you consider going to Bundaberg?

**Howard:** No, they'd been up there for a year.

**Michael:** Oh okay, so you moved up from Adelaide after a year.

**Howard:** Yeah. So I went straight to Teachers College in Brisbane. I got a scholarship in Adelaide to go there and then got a scholarship to go to Brisbane as well, so I went to Brisbane. And that's more or less the turnover point from tennis to football as well. I put my tennis racket down over that holiday break and picked up a footy and went to training with Mayne.

**Michael:** I think you've told me you played in the Australian Open around 1968 or 69 or something.

**Howard:** 68 I think it was. It was the last Australian Open that was held when only amateurs were able to play in it. Because there'd been the breakaway group 10 years earlier when Laver and Hoad and Rosewall and all those guys took off on their own professional tour. So the best of all those players left.

The 1968 Australian Open was probably the weakest field ever. And so they gave some up-and-coming juniors an opportunity to play in the men's. I played a guy who was, I think, number three in Australia as a junior. Billy Lloyd. I think it was 6-4, 7-5, 8-6 or something like that. So I had some chances there.

**Michael:** Pretty good.

**Howard:** Anyway, I'm careful to explain to people that I'm not claiming that I'm the standard of Australian Open men's at the time. We definitely wouldn't have been playing if it had the professionals around.

**Michael:** Was it exciting still to do it? Was it in Melbourne?

**Howard:** Yes, it was exciting. Melbourne, yeah. It was Flinders Park before it changed over to where it is now.

The week before was the Linton Cup, which was the Junior Interstate Competition that was very big at the time. So the week before we played against teams like Phil Dent and John Alexander and a few other juniors that played professionally afterwards. Jeff Masters.

**Michael:** Those are top guys really. Great tennis players.

**Howard:** Yeah. I played a couple of them. Played Jeff Masters in Sydney—he beat me 6-3, 6-3. Then I played Phil Dent. We were playing in Adelaide at Memorial Drive. One time I really got very annoyed because we were playing away and he was expected to beat me. I think he was number one in Australia as a junior at the time.

We were four all and then had a very close game for 5-4, which I lost, and then he won 6-4. But in between time, there'd been three times when he'd served and hit a let cord, and I called let and returned the ball by the way. And he thought he couldn't hear the let, so he accused me of cheating basically.

I said, well, I've heard this. I've actually returned the ball back into play anyway. It's not like you aced me. So I'm getting somebody else. I'm going to get an umpire. I said, be my guest, it'll raise the point. If there are some more lets, they'll be called.

Anyway, of course there were no more lets after that, so he beat me 6-4, 6-1. But I got really upset that he was accusing me of cheating. I said, I've never taken a point in my life that wasn't mine. I wasn't about to start.

So that was annoying. But it did sort of prove—him and Jeff Masters, 6-3, 6-3 is a pretty average score, but then again it was competitive.

Probably I didn't really have a good gauge on my own level of play. I think I probably didn't always go out feeling that I should win or could win. I didn't go out feeling that I should lose either, but probably not aggressive enough in terms of winning points that were winnable and important points.

I think that helped me realize that I wasn't really aggressive enough as a tennis player to play on the tour continuously because I would have other things on my mind. I kind of didn't feel like telling people at the time because it sounds—my thinking was there's more things to life than tennis and so I'll go and do those, without knowing exactly what they were going to be.

## The Incident at the Club

**Michael:** So your Mum and Dad moved up to Bundaberg. I've heard you mention this a number of times over the years without ever quite getting the full story. I think I actually heard your dad mention it once or twice, very indirectly. It seemed like it was probably still quite a touchy subject for him. So what actually happened at the local hotel or the club?

**Howard:** Yeah, the club. It was very upsetting for Mum and probably for Dad too.

What happened was, it would have been during my second last year and last year at high school. Unbeknownst to me, Dad's job at the club was to be the manager. So he employed people to look after the place and to be the barman and all that sort of stuff.

Over a period of time, one of those people—a guy called Lehman—was pinching money out of the till at the club. That started off fairly low key and then upgraded. Dad wasn't sure initially who it was or what it was.

But the short story is that he realized there was money being taken ruthlessly. And so he actually replaced some of that lost money out of his own pocket. So instead of fronting up and sacking the guy, which he could have done—the way he ended up knowing who it was, was this guy Lehman went on holidays for two weeks and during that time there was no money disappearing.

But it caused a lot of tension at home because—Mum wanted to know why their money should be used to prop that up. So there were a few arguments at home about all that. Dad just wanted to give this guy another chance. But the guy had previously been in trouble with the law. He's a local guy—he'd pinched money from the last place he'd worked at.

So Dad's management committee at the club ended up sacking Dad for not taking appropriate managerial action. And that in itself was something that some people felt was just a bit over the top—let's sack this guy now anyway and then we'll start all over.

One of the downfalls of Dad being involved a bit in politics, because he was a Labour Party candidate, is that there were a goodly number of people from the local community who were not Labour Party supporters. And the word was that they used this as an excuse because of their crankiness at Dad being a Labour guy.

Having said that, he had people who were supporting him, but not enough to make a difference. So eventually he lost his job there, went back to still working on the block anyway. But I think they found it too stressful to be in a community where that had happened. And Dad probably felt guilty about it.

He did tell me that he regretted not doing what he should have done.

**Michael:** What did he think he should have done? Just sack the guy?

**Howard:** Yeah. After giving him a chance or two. And it's not appropriate for the family money to be spent on somebody else's indiscretions.

That was not good. He mentioned that to me—he sort of apologized at one point when we were up in Brisbane about the club and whatever. I said, we don't think, give a second thought to what your role was in that. You've said what you should have done but people should have been much better about it than that.

I'm pretty sure that if I'd known about it—I didn't know any of this was going on until after he got the sack—I certainly would have had my piece to say to some of the people on that management group who I know he helped for various things and who spoke against him.

Anyway, so that's the reason behind that. And of course, it was probably a bit convenient. I think Mum would have been more affected by it than Dad. And I think she was probably pushing to get a hotel in Bundaberg and be back in familiar territory there anyway. So it wasn't an unnatural thing to do, but it certainly wasn't something they would have done otherwise.

So I'm not sure how old I would have been. 15, 16—16 probably when that happened.

**Michael:** If your mum was feeling held out publicly to dry, did you feel that way at all? Or was it just water off a duck's back?

**Howard:** No, I didn't. That's what I said to Dad. I said, Dad, I don't see it as my problem or your problem. It's their problem. You've done very little wrong. And certainly punishment's worse than the crime.

I just said to him, no, absolutely no way that it's had any negative effect on our lives. The positive effect is that you're just a bit more wary about trusting people in those sort of situations and take early action rather than when it's too late. I didn't say that to Dad, but that's what my learning was out of it.

Not that that's ever really occurred in my life since then that I've had to worry about that, but it's still a lesson—that people aren't always good people. Obviously, what Dad did wasn't illegal. It wasn't like he was breaking the law. He was trying to support somebody else and doing it through his own means. It was mismanagement if you like, but it wasn't unlawful.

There was no talk about Dad taking the money or anything like that. That was all well known. He knew that was the guy, Lehman, who had been doing the trick. And one of the other barmen had thought that was the case as well. And he was worried because he thought he might be thought of—and there were only actually two barmen apart from Dad.

But yeah, Mum got quite obsessed by it really, to the point where I think it became quite difficult for Dad to handle Mum's obsession with what was wrong. But it was an injustice. I felt the same way. Injustice had been perpetrated and people should have done better. They had a chance to do something better than they did.

**Michael:** Did your parents go back much to Monash in later years?

**Howard:** We did go a couple of times. We went during that time when Stuart's football was on. Do you remember?

**Michael:** Yeah, I remember that well.

**Howard:** We came back down through to Monash and we went to the club and had a drink. There was a new guy taking over—all that sort of stuff that had happened years before, everybody had moved on.

And then some of Dad's old tennis mates—the Fundaks and the Vineys and the Harrises—we met down at the pub and had good chats about stuff. But Dad was not quite himself then really. People were expecting him to make a little speech or something like that. He deferred to me actually. I must have been like mid to late 30s. So I made a little speech just to say how appreciative we were of those people who had been good friends of Dad's for a long time, and how it was just a pity that things hadn't worked out quite the way they expected originally.

But that was a good night. It was interesting though—when we talked earlier about my approach to playing tennis professionally and how that changed—on that night at the club, one of the guys, John Hinwood actually, we were having a little chat on the side and he said, "I'm surprised you didn't actually play professional tennis. You're better than so and so and so and so," who he mentioned.

I said, okay, it was just quite simply from his point of view, he saw it that way. I didn't see it that way. Not that I was comparing myself to other players at the time because you just go out and play. But it was interesting that he had a perception that that's something that I would have done. And that he thought I was capable of doing that.
