## 正文

Michael Nielsen

Astera Institute

2023年5月14日

_迄今为止，AI 在 science 中最大的成功是 AlphaFold 2 system。这是一个 deep learning system，它在一个 fundamental scientific problem 上取得了重大进展：如何根据构成一个 protein 的 amino acids 的 sequence 来 predict 该 protein 的 3-dimensional structure。这一突破帮助引发了 molecular biology 中正在进行的 deep learning revolution。虽然这显然令 molecular biologists 感兴趣，但我相信，作为 artificial intelligence 可能如何 impact discovery 的一个具体 prototype，它对于整个 science 都有更广泛的意义。在这场简短的 survey talk 中，我简要讨论的问题包括：这样的 systems 可以如何被 validated？它们能否被用来 identify 人类 scientists 可以从中学习的 general principles？而我们究竟应该期待一个好的 theory 或 explanation 提供什么？这场演讲关注的是现有 results 和近期，而不是更长远的未来。不过，我希望它能帮助我们扎实地理解那个更长远的未来，以及 AI systems 的 benefits 和 risks。_

_这是2023年5月在华盛顿特区的 [Metascience 2023 Conference](https://metascience.info/) 上所作演讲的文字稿。_

2020年，deep learning system AlphaFold 2[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn1) 令 biologists 感到惊讶：它被证明能够经常对 protein structure 作出 correct、near atomic-precision 的 predictions。也就是说，仅凭构成一个 protein 的 amino acids 的 linear sequence，AlphaFold 就能够 predict 该 protein 中 atoms 的 positions。这些 results 并非经过 cherrypicked，而是来自一场与一百多个其他 modeling groups 共同参加的 adversarial、blind competition。在少数情况下，AlphaFold[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn2) 甚至 exceeded experimental accuracy，促使已有 experimental results 被 re-evaluated 和 improved。

虽然 AlphaFold 令人印象深刻，但它也远未完成：它实际上是一座通往新时代的桥梁，开启了许多 scientific 和 metascientific questions。这些问题包括：我们期待一个好的 theory 或 explanation 提供什么；validate 这种 understanding 意味着什么；以及我们人类可以从这些 systems 中学到什么。最重要的是：作为一种 systemic intervention，AI systems 是否可能以及如何 impact 整个 science 的 progress。在我的演讲中，我会把 AlphaFold 视为一个具体 prototype，用以讨论 AI 可以如何被用于 science 的各个领域。出于这些原因，即便你此前对 proteins 乃至 biology 都没有兴趣，metascientists 也值得关注 AlphaFold 及其 successor systems。

这场演讲是一篇 survey。我不是 molecular biologist，因此，如果在这方面出现任何错误，请大家谅解。不过，了解 proteins 是一件愉快、且时常令人深受启发的事。对于那些和我一样并非 biologists 的听众，请允许我花几分钟回顾一些背景。Molecular biology 给人的感觉，有点像走进一间巨大的工坊，里面满是奇妙而各式各样的机器。UniProt 这样的 large databases 包含数以亿计的 proteins 的 amino acid sequences。例如，有 kinesin proteins，它们在 cell 的 interior 四处 transport large molecules。有 haemoglobin，它在你的 blood 中 carries oxygen，并帮助为你的 metabolism 提供动力。还有 green fluorescent protein，它在 exposed to ultraviolet light 时会 emits green light，并且可以被用来 tag 和 track 其他 biomolecules。所有这些以及更多的 molecular machines，都是由 evolution by natural selection 这道严苛的筛网 created 和 sorted 出来的。每一台机器都可能值得用一生去研究。例如，关于 kinesin superfamily 已有数千篇 papers，但我们实际上才刚刚开始了解它。不过，尽管如此丰富的 biological machines 令人惊叹，我们并不 _a priori_ 知道这些机器做什么，或者如何做到。我们没有说明书，只能努力弄清楚。

事实上，对于数以亿计的已知 proteins[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn3) 中的绝大多数，我们能够轻易直接 determine 的只有基本蓝图：利用 genome sequencing，我们可以找到 form 该 protein 的 amino acids 的 linear sequence，费用不过几美分。但 proteins 是微小的 3-dimensional structures，通常宽度只有 nanometers，因此要 directly image 它们极为困难。对于单个 protein，experimentally determine 相应的 3-dimensional structure 通常需要工作 _数月_，一般使用 x-ray crystallography、cryo-electron microscopy 或 NMR。

这种差距确实很重要。它之所以重要，是因为理解 shape 对于理解以下这类问题至关重要：

-   作为 immune response 的一部分，一个 antibody protein 可以 bind to 哪些 antigens？
-   这个 protein 可以 carry 什么？\[例如，haemoglobin carries oxygen 的方式\]
-   proteins 如何 form larger complexes？\[例如，ribosome\]

理解一个 protein 的 shape，并不能告诉你有关其 function 的一切。但这对于理解一个 protein 能做什么、以及它如何做到，是 fundamental 的。

理想情况下，我们仅凭 amino acid sequence 就能 determine 其 shape。chemists 和 biologists 有理由期待，这往往是可能的[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn4)；在1970年代，scientists 开始进行 physics simulations，尝试 determine 一个 protein 将会 fold into 的 shape。此后，人们采用了许多 techniques（kinetics、thermodynamics、evolutionary、……）。很长一段时间里，这些 techniques 取得的 progress 都非常缓慢。即使对于这些 progress，人们也不得不怀疑 modelers 是否在 cherrypicking，哪怕他们抱着最好的意愿。例如，1990年代，Johns Hopkins 曾声称已在很大程度上“solved”了 protein structure prediction problem[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn5)。

为了以公平而严格的方式 assess progress，一项名为 [CASP](https://predictioncenter.org/)（Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction）的 competition 于1994年启动。CASP 每两年举行一次，要求 modelers 对 protein structure 作出 _blind predictions_。也就是说，他们需要为一些 proteins predict structures：这些 proteins 的 amino acid sequence 已知，但 biologists 仍在努力 experimentally determining 其 three-dimensional structure，并预计会在 competition 结束后不久得知该 structure，以便用它来 score predictions。对于任何给定 model，其 score（非常粗略地说）就是根据某个严格的 threshold，amino acids positions 被 predicted correctly 的 percentage[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn6)。如你所见，在整个2010年代，winner 的 score 通常落在30到50的 range 内[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn7)：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/scores_GDT_partial.jpg)

也就是说，winner 会（大致）将约一半的 amino acids place 得 near perfectly。随后，在2018年和2020年，DeepMind 携 AlphaFold 和 AlphaFold 2 参赛：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/scores_GDT.jpg)

我将重点讨论2020年的 CASP 14，当时 AlphaFold 2 取得了87的 score，也就是说，大约87 percent 的 amino acids 位于一个严格的 threshold 之内。这是 AlphaFold 在要求最严格的 category，即 free modeling category 中的 score；在这个 category 中，contestants 被要求 predict 一些 proteins 的 structure，而此前已知的 similar proteins 很少。在所有 categories 中，AlphaFold 对 backbone 中 alpha-carbon atoms 的 position 所作的 predicted，其 root mean square distance 为0.96 Angstroms[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn8)。次佳 technique 的 accurate 程度大约只有它的三分之一，root mean square distance 为2.8 Angstroms[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn9)：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/rmsd.jpg)

作为比较，一个 carbon atom 的 van der Waals diameter 大约为1.4 Angstroms。AlphaFold 的 accuracy 往往 comparable to experimental accuracy。CASP 的共同创始人、University of Maryland 的 John Moult 说[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn10)：

> 我们在这一个问题——proteins 如何 fold up——上已经停滞了近50年。我亲自在这个问题上工作了这么久，经历了这么多次停顿和重新起步，也曾怀疑我们究竟能否抵达目标；如今看到 DeepMind 为它 produce 一个 solution，真是一个非常特别的时刻。

这是一番非常有力的话，值得深入探究：AlphaFold 在什么意义上 solves 了 protein structure prediction problem，以及还有什么尚待理解。不过，就连 AlphaFold 的 competitors 也赞不绝口。以下是 Columbia University 的 Mohammed AlQuraishi 的说法[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn11)：

> 这是否构成了 static protein structure prediction problem 的一个 solution？我认为是的，但其中还有种种复杂之处。诚实且深思熟虑的人在这里可以持不同意见，归根结底，取决于一个人如何 definition “solution”这个词究竟意味着什么……scientific problem 的大部分已经 solved；现在剩下的是 execution。

那是两年半以前。我认为，今天一个合理的总体看法是：AlphaFold 是一次巨大的飞跃，但即使在 basic problem 上，仍有许多工作要做，而现在也可以着手处理许多重大的新 problems。

要描述 AlphaFold 的 high-level architecture，需要几个小时。今天我只想强调几点：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/architecture.png)

它是一个 deep neural network，也就是一个 hierarchical model，拥有通过 training 而 learned 的93 million parameters。一个 amino acid sequence 被 input，一个 3-dimensional structure 被 output，同时还有一些 error estimates，用来 quantifying AlphaFold 对每个 amino acid 的 placement 有多 confident。基本 training data 来自 protein data bank（PDB），这是人类保存自1970年代以来 experimentally determined 的 protein structures 的 repository。在 training 时，其中共有170,000个 protein structures（不过，出于 technical reasons，一小部分被 omitted）。AlphaFold network 中的 parameters 通过 gradient descent 被 adjusted，以确保在给定 input 时，network 会 outputs correct structure。

这就是 network 如何 learned to predict structures 的一个（非常！）概括的图景。还有许多其他重要的想法。其中一个巧妙而重要（尽管已有）的想法，是找到一种从数以亿计的 proteins 的已知 sequences 中 learn 的方式。其思路是找到许多其他 proteins，它们的 amino acid sequences 与 input protein 的相似，这意味着它们很可能是 evolutionarily related。例如，也许它本质上是同一个 protein，只不过存在于其他 species 中。AlphaFold 尝试找到许多这样的 similar proteins，并从中 learn。为了直观地理解它可能如何做到这一点，假设通过查看许多 similar proteins，AlphaFold 发现某一特定 pair 的 amino acids 在 linear chain 中相距很远，但其中一个 amino acid 的 changes 似乎与另一个的 changes 是 correlated。如果你看到了这种情况，可能会怀疑这些 amino acids 在 space 中很可能 close together，并且在一起 co-evolving，以 preserve 该 protein 的 shape。事实上，这确实经常发生，而且它提供了一种方式，让 AlphaFold 既能从 _known structural information_（在 PDB 中）中 learn，也能从 _known evolutionary information_（在 protein sequence databases 中）中 learn。AlphaFold paper 的 lead author John Jumper 在[一场演讲](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EjZMxq4oEt4&t=443s&ab_channel=RCSBProteinDataBank)中对此有一个很好的表述：physics informs AlphaFold 对 evolutionary history 的 understanding，而 evolutionary history informs AlphaFold 对 physics 的 understanding。

## Generalization 与 reliability（泛化与可靠性）

你可能会问：AlphaFold 只是在 memorizing 它的 training data，还是能够 generalize？CASP 提供了一种 basic validation：在 AlphaFold predicted 那些 competition structures 时，它们尚不在 PDB 中。此外，CASP 在某种意义上是一个“natural” sample：structural biology community 更愿意 solve 那些 biologically important 的 structure[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn12)。因此，这表明它具有一定的能力，能够 generalize 到广大 biologists 感兴趣的 proteins。

如果我们用 deep learning 来 study 那些并不 occur in nature 的 proteins，例如 mutations 的结果，或 protein design 的一部分，又会怎样？目前有大量工作正在进行，坦率地说，眼下这是一个令人兴奋、却又纷繁杂乱的局面，各个方向都有探索——任何像样的概述，即使不涵盖数百篇 papers，也会涉及数十篇。有些 papers 说，AlphaFold 对某某 type 的 mutation works _badly_，对另某某 kind 的 mutation 则 works _well_，还有针对 protein design 的各种 deep learning fixes，等等。我的体会是：要弄清这些 shortcomings，并 improving 这些 systems 来 address 这些 shortcomings，足以让 biologists 忙上好多年。

OpenFold[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn13) 对 deep learning 的 generalize 能力做了一些有趣的 tests；它是 AlphaFold 的一个 open source near-clone。例如，他们直接从 training set 中 removed 了 topology 的大多数 broad classes，然后 retrained OpenFold。为此，他们采用了一种 standard classification scheme，即针对 protein topology 的 CATH classification。他们从 training set 中 removed 了 95% (!!!) 的 topologies，然后从头 retrained OpenFold。即使绝大多数 topologies 已被 removed，其 performance 仍与 AlphaFold 1 相近[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn14)：就在 AlphaFold 2 出现前的两三年，它还会是 state-of-the-art。

在这一思路的另一种 variation 中，他们从 protein data bank 的小得多的 subsamples 出发，retrained OpenFold。他们没有使用 170,000 个 structures，而是用小到只有 1,000 个 structures 的 training sets 来 retrained，这些 structures 是 chosen at random 的。即使 training set 如此小，他们仍取得了与 AlphaFold 1 comparable 的 performance（事实上还 slightly better than 它）。而仅用 10,000 个 structures 作为 training data，他们就取得了与完整 OpenFold comparable 的 performance。

从实际应用来看，AlphaFold generalization 这个问题之所以重要，部分原因是 DeepMind 已经 released 了 AlphaFold DB：一个包含 215 million 个 protein structures 的 database，其中包括 48 个 species 的（almost-complete）proteomes，涵盖 humans、mice 和 fruit flies。这些 structures 的获得方式，是取出 UniProt 中的 genetic sequences，再用 AlphaFold 来 predict 对应的 structure。你可以把整个 process 看作：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/AFDB.png)

这是一次令人惊叹的 generalization。如果我们拥有一个 perfectly reliable 的 model，它就会把我们对 protein structures 的理解 extend 大约 three orders of magnitude。值得强调的是，AlphaFold 并未做任何额外的 experiments；也没有获取任何额外的 data。然而，仅仅通过“思考”，就有可能获得数量极其庞大的 predictions，而且人们预期它们会非常好。我听过好几位 biologists 表达类似的感受：“没有人会仅凭一个 AlphaFold prediction 就认定它是 true；但它是极其有用的起点，能为你省下好几个月的工作。”

随着 models 进一步变好，我预期 model 与 experiment 之间的界线会变得模糊。这听起来可能有些奇怪，但事实上，传统的“experimentally-determined structures”本来就需要极其 complicated 的 theories，才能从 data 走向 structure。如果你认为 AlphaFold（或其后继者）提供了一个更强的 theory，你最终可能会更相信 deep learning system 给出的 predictions，而不是（今天的）“experimental results”。已经有一些早期迹象表明，这种情况正在开始发生。在 CASP assessment 中，AlphaFold 在几个使用 NMR spectroscopy 来 determined 的 structures 上 performed poorly。一篇 2022 年的 paper[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn15) examined 了“904 个同时具有 Alpha-Fold 和 NMR structures 的 human proteins”，并得出结论：“Alpha-Fold predictions 通常比 NMR structures 更 accurate。”其中一位作者 Mike Williamson，实际上还曾帮助开创将 NMR 用于 structural biology 的做法。

用一个更简单的情境来说明同一点：我们如何 interpret 来自 telescope 的 images，取决于我们的 optics theory；如果我们 change 或 improve 自己的 optics theory，我们对 telescope 所得的所谓“raw data”的理解也会改变。事实上，在我们对 gravitation 引起的 bending of light 的理解中，确实发生过相关的事情。在那个例子中，我们改变了对 light 如何 travels through space 的理解，而这又影响了我们 interpret experimental data 的方式，尤其是在理解 distant galaxies 的 gravitational lensing 这类 phenomena 时。

类似地，“experimental” protein structure determination 也强烈依赖 theory。你可以从 x-ray crystallography 中大致体会这一点，它需要许多困难的 steps：protein sample 的 purification；proteins 的 crystallization (!)；通过 x-ray diffraction 来 obtain two-dimensional images；用于 invert 并 solve for three-dimensional structure 的 procedures；判断 inversion 何时足够好的 criteria。这涉及大量 theory！事实上，inversion procedure 通常需要从一个好的“guess”入手，也就是一个 candidate search structure。人们经常使用 related proteins，但有时找不到好的 search structure，而这可能导致无法找到 solution。AlphaFold 已被用于寻找好的 search structures，帮助针对特别棘手的 structures 来 invert data。因此，theory 与 experiment 之间的界线早已模糊。我预期，在未来几年里，弄清如何 validate AI 对 problems 给出的“solutions”，将成为一个备受 scientific 和 metascientific 关注的重大 topic。

## 是否可能存在一组 simple 的 protein structure principles？AI 或许能帮助我们 discover 它吗？

任何具有 93 million 个 learned parameters 的 model 都是 complicated 的。它并不是通常意义上的“theory”或“explanation”。你可能会想：能否利用 AlphaFold（或其后继系统）帮助 discover 这样的 theory，即便它只是 partial 的？比如，是否可能存在一组 simple 的 protein structure prediction principles？而 AlphaFold 2 究竟在 learning 什么？为免大家失望，我先说明：我们还不知道这些问题的答案。但追寻这些问题，是思考 AI 在 science 中的作用的一种有用的 intuition pump。

寻找这类 principles 的一种 approach 是“behaviorist artificial psychology”：通过 observing system 的 behavior 来 inferring high-level principles。当然，biologists 已经广泛使用 AlphaFold 的 detailed predictions，例如 discovering proteins 上新的 binding sites。但这虽然非常有用，却不等同于 inferring 关于 protein structure 的 novel high-level principles。不过，在其他一些重要的 deep learning systems 中，人们已经通过 observing behavior 找到了一些有意思的新 high-level principles。

例如，通过 observing AlphaZero chess system[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn16)，人们 inferred 出了一些 violated 传统 chess grandmaster wisdom 的 behaviors。这些 behaviors 于 2018 年 12 月[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn17)和 2019 年 1 月[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn18)公布。最近的一篇 paper[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn19) examined 了这些 behaviors，并试图 determine human players 如何（以及是否）因这个 system 而发生了改变。他们发现，2019 年 top ten players 中的变化很少，只有一个例外：当时和现在的世界 top player，Magnus Carlsen。他们 identify 出 Carlsen 在 2019 年显著改变其 play 的多个方面，而这些改变很可能受到了 AlphaZero 的影响。

这些改变包括：Carlsen 在 game 早期 advanced 其 h pawn[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn20)的频率大幅提高（执 white 时 increase 了 333%，执 black 时 increase 了 175%）。他改变了执 white 和 black 时的 opening strategies。事实上，他在 2019 年执 white 时最常用的两种 opening strategies（Queen's Gambit declined 和 Grunfeld Defense 的 variations），都是他在 2018 年完全没有 play 过的 openings。而执 black 时，他在 2018 年 played Sicilian 的比例不到 10%，2019 年却达到了 45%。他们还 observed 到，Carlsen 更愿意 sacrifice material。所有这些改变都与从 AlphaZero 中 learning 相符。Carlsen 在 2019 年没有 lost 任何 classical games，而且尽管已经是 top-rated player，他仍是唯一一位 Elo rating 显著提高的 top grandmaster：他增加了 37 rating points。相比之下，top 10 players 中有 6 人实际上 lost points，其余 3 人中也没有谁 gained 超过 6 points。当然，我们不知道其中有多少归因于 Carlsen 从 AlphaZero 中 learning，但这篇 paper 提出了可信的论证，表明 Carlsen 从 AlphaZero 中 learned 了很多。

这样的 behavioral observation 很有意思，却也令人沮丧：它并没有告诉我们这些 behaviors _为什么_会发生。我们或许知道 AlphaZero 喜欢在早期 advance h pawn，但我们真正想要的是理解为什么。我们能否转而查看 neural networks 的内部，理解它们是如何做到这些事情的？据我所知，针对 AlphaFold，这类 investigation 还只得到过一些随意的尝试。但对于 simpler neural nets，人们正在 discovering 有意思的 structure。例如，去年 Neel Nanda 和 Tom Lieberum[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn21) trained 了一个 single-layer transformer neural network，让它对两个 integers 进行 modulo 113 的 add。起初，这个 network 只是 memorized 了 training set 中的所有 examples。它能很好地对这些 examples 进行 add，但在其他地方的 performance 糟糕透顶（这并不令人意外）。然而，随着他们用长得多的时间 trained 这个 network，它 transitioned 到了在 held-out examples 上 performing vastly better 的状态。不知怎么，在没有额外 training data 的情况下，它正在 learning 如何 add。

Nanda 和 Lieberum 花了几周时间查看 network 内部，以理解发生了什么变化。通过 reverse engineering 这个 network，他们发现它已经 learned 了一种非同寻常的 add 方法。大意是：给定 numbers x 和 y，这个 network computed 一个 wave e^{2\\pi i kx/113}，然后进行 phase shift，得到 e^{2\\pi i k x/ 113} \\times e^{2\\pi i ky/113}，最后试图找到一个 canceling phase shift z，使 wave e^{2 \\pi i k(x+y-z)/113} 具有尽可能 flattest 的“tone”。这是一位 radio frequency engineer 或 group representation theorist 进行 addition 的方式。这让 Nanda 和 Lieberum 相当惊讶。正如 Nanda 所说：

> 我要强调，这个 algorithm 完全是通过 gradient descent 来 learned 的！我事先既没有 predict 也没有 understand 这个 algorithm，也没有做任何事情来鼓励 model learn 这种进行 modular addition 的方式。我只是通过 reverse engineering weights 才 discovered 了它。

第一次听说这一连串事情时，我非常困惑：为什么这个 network 会 switch 到这个 more general algorithm？毕竟，它起初已经 memorized 了 training data，并提供了 excellent performance：为什么还要改变？答案在于，在 training 期间，neural network 会为 more complex models 付出 cost：loss function 的选择使得 gradient descent 更偏好 lower-weight models。而这个 wave algorithm 实际上就是 lower-weight 的。这是一种 Occam's razor 的 mechanical implementation，偏好 simpler、并且在这个例子中也 more general 的 approach。事实上，通过 varying loss function，你有可能以许多不同方式 impose Occam's razor。一个引人遐想的问题是：将来有一天，我们能否对 AlphaFold 这样的 systems 做类似的事情，或许 discovering 关于 protein structure 的新 basic principles？

## 最后的一点思考

我希望大家喜欢这次对 AlphaFold 的简短介绍，以及对 AI 在 science 中应用的思考。当然，还有远多于此的内容可以谈。但我相信，很明显，AI systems 提出了许多 fundamental scientific 和 metascientific questions。最重要的是：随着它们越来越擅长不同类型的 cognitive operation，越来越能够在 environment 中 sense 和 actuate，它们将如何 impact 整个 science？AI systems 最终会 systematically change 整个 practice of science 吗？它们或许会大幅 speed up scientific discovery 吗？如果会，这又会带来什么 risks 和 benefits？

## 后记：对于更 messier、可供 learn 的 good data 更少的 problems，又会怎样？

演讲结束时，听众提出了许多有趣的问题。其中一个引人深思的问题来自 Evan Miyazono。大意是：“作为 AI 的一个 target，protein structure prediction 受益于拥有大量非常 clean 的 prior data，system 可以从中 learn。对于没有大量 clean prior data 可供 learn 的 problems，你认为 AI 会有多大用处？”

这是个很好的问题。大约一年前，我会说，确实，当你处理的是更 messier、metrics of success 不那么 clear 的 problems 时，apply AI 似乎颇具挑战。但这一年来，随着我对 foundation models、transfer learning 和 zero-shot learning 有了更好的理解，我改变了看法。

为了解释我为什么改变看法，我先提供两方面的背景。首先，是一些非正式的传闻：许多人观察到，ChatGPT 在 coding 方面比 GitHub Copilot 好得多。我并不确切知道原因。不过，我从知情人士那里听到的一种常见猜测是，在大量 text（以及 code）上进行 training，会 substantially improves model 的 code 能力。不知为何，text 中的 regularities 似乎也在 improving ChatGPT 理解 code 的能力。

第二方面的背景是：AlphaFold 也使用了类似的想法。为了解释这一点，我需要介绍 AlphaFold 的 training 中我在演讲正文里没有讨论的一部分：它实际上 implicitly includes 一个 large language model，把 amino acids 的 sequence 当作一种有待填补的“text”。要理解这一点，请回想一下，给定 amino acids 的一个 sequence，AlphaFold 会在已有的 genetic databases 中 looks up similar sequences。而在 training 期间，就像 language model 一样，AlphaFold 会将那些 sequences 中的一些 individual amino acids 进行 masks out（或 mutates），并尝试 predict missing values。这样做迫使 network 更多地了解 evolutionary information 的 structure；然后，它利用这种被迫获得的理解，在 protein structure prediction 上做得更好。通过这种方式，AlphaFold 受益于来自一个 related domain（genetics）的 transfer learning，那里有多得多的 information 可用。

回到 science 的更广泛背景，我预计 large multi-modal foundation models 将会：(a) 逐渐开始 outperform special-purpose systems，正如 large language models 经常 outperform 更 specialized 的 natural language algorithms；以及 (b) 它们将 exhibit zero-shot 或 few-shot learning。这些 large multi-modal models 不仅会在 text 上接受 trained，还会在 images、actions、code、genetic data、geographic data，以及各种 sensor 和 actuator data 上接受 trained。而且，正如 language models 中的情况一样，它们会利用自己在一个 domain 中理解的东西，来帮助指导其在 adjacent、往往更 messier 的 domains 中的工作，而 model 对这些 domains 掌握的 information 或许有限。事实上，已经有迹象表明 protein language models 正朝这个方向发展。

当然，这一切还只是一个故事和一种直觉。除了非常 nascent 的形式之外，它尚未发生，而我也可能完全错了！我当然预计，要使它良好运作，还需要新的想法。不过，希望这些话概括了我的直觉在过去一年里如何发生变化：我认为，未来几年，AI models 在仅有少量 prior data 的情况下 addressing messy problems 的表现，会好得令人惊讶。而它们将通过 multi-model foundation models、transfer learning 以及 zero- and few-shot learning 来做到这一点。

Michael Nielsen

Astera Institute

May 14, 2023

_The biggest success of AI in science so far is the AlphaFold 2 system. This is a deep learning system which has made large strides on a fundamental scientific problem: how to predict the 3-dimensional structure of a protein from the sequence of amino acids making up that protein. This breakthrough has helped set off an ongoing deep learning revolution in molecular biology. While obviously of interest to molecular biologists, I believe this is of much broader interest for science as a whole, as a concrete prototype for how artificial intelligence may impact discovery. In this short survey talk I briefly discuss questions including: how can such systems be validated? Can they be used to identify general principles that human scientists can learn from? And what should we expect a good theory or explanation to provide anyway? The focus of the talk is extant results and the near term, not the longer-term future. However, I hope it may help in grounding an understanding of that longer-term future, and of the benefits and risks of AI systems._

_The text for a talk given at the [Metascience 2023 Conference](https://metascience.info/) in Washington, D.C., May 2023._

In 2020 the deep learning system AlphaFold 2[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn1) surprised biologists when it was shown to routinely make correct near atomic-precision predictions for protein structure. That is, using just the linear sequence of amino acids making up a protein, AlphaFold was able to predict the positions of the atoms in the protein. These results were not cherrypicked, but rather emerged from an adversarial, blind competition with over a hundred other modeling groups. In a few cases, AlphaFold[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn2) has even exceeded experimental accuracy, causing existing experimental results to be re-evaluated and improved.

While AlphaFold is impressive, it's also far from complete: it's really a bridge to a new era, opening up many scientific and metascientific questions. These include: what we expect a good theory or explanation to provide; what it means to validate that understanding; and what we humans can learn from these systems. Most of all: whether and how AI systems may impact the progress of science as a whole, as a systemic intervention. In my talk, I'll treat AlphaFold as a concrete prototype for how AI may be used across science. For these reasons, it's valuable for metascientists to engage with AlphaFold and successor systems, even if you have no prior interest in proteins or even in biology.

The talk is a survey. I am not a molecular biologist, so my apologies for any errors on that front. That said, it's been enjoyable and often inspiring to learn about proteins. Let me take a few minutes to remind you of some background for those of you (like me) who aren't biologists. Molecular biology feels a bit like wandering into an immense workshop full of wonderful and varied machines. Large databases like UniProt contain the amino acid sequences for hundreds of millions of proteins. You have, for example, kinesin proteins, which transport large molecules around the interior of the cell. You have haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in your blood, and helps power your metabolism. You have green fluorescent protein, which emits green light when exposed to ultraviolet light, and which can be used to tag and track other biomolecules. All these and many more molecular machines, created and sorted by the demanding sieve of evolution by natural selection. Every individual machine could be the subject of a lifetime's study. There are, for instance, thousands of papers about the kinesin superfamily, and yet we're really just beginning to understand it. But while this wealth of biological machines is astonishing, we don't _a priori_ know what those machines do, or how they do it. We have no instruction manual, and we're trying to figure it out.

In fact, for the vast majority of the hundreds of millions of proteins known[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn3), all we can easily directly determine is the basic blueprint: using genome sequencing we can find the linear sequence of amino acids that form the protein, at a cost of no more than cents. But proteins are tiny 3-dimensional structures, typically nanometers across, making it extremely difficult to directly image them. For a single protein it will routinely take _months_ of work to experimentally determine the corresponding 3-dimensional structure, usually using x-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy or NMR.

This discrepancy really matters. It matters because understanding the shape is crucial for understanding questions like:

-   What antigens can an antibody protein bind to, as part of an immune response?
-   What can the protein carry around? \[E.g., the way haemoglobin carries oxygen\]
-   How do proteins form larger complexes? \[E.g., the ribosome\]

Understanding the shape of a protein doesn't tell you everything about its function. But it is fundamental to understanding what a protein can do, and how it does it.

Ideally, we'd be able to determine the shape from the amino acid sequence alone. There are reasons chemists and biologists expect this to often be possible[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn4), and in the 1970s, scientists began doing physics simulations to attempt to determine the shape a protein will fold into. Many techniques have been adopted since (kinetics, thermodynamics, evolutionary, ….) For a long time these made only very slow progress. And even for that progress, one must wonder if modelers are cherrypicking, even with the best will in the world. There were, for instance, claims in the 1990s from Johns Hopkins to have largely "solved" the protein structure prediction problem[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn5).

To assess progress in a fair but demanding way, in 1994 a competition named [CASP](https://predictioncenter.org/) (Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction) was begun. Running every two years, CASP asks modelers to do _blind predictions_ of protein structure. That is, they're asked to predict structures for proteins whose amino acid sequence is known, but where biologists are still working on experimentally determining the three-dimensional structure, and expect to know it shortly after the competition is over, so it can be used to score predictions. The score for any given model is (very roughly) what percentage of amino acids positions are predicted correctly, according to some demanding threshold[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn6). As you can see, through the 2010s the winner would typically score in the range 30 to 50[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn7):

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/scores_GDT_partial.jpg)

That is, the winner would (roughly) place somewhat than half of amino acids near perfectly. Then, in 2018 and 2020, DeepMind entered with AlphaFold and AlphaFold 2:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/scores_GDT.jpg)

I'll focus on CASP 14 in 2020, when AlphaFold 2 achieved of score of 87, so roughly 87 percent of amino acids within a demanding threshold. This was AlphaFold's score in the most demanding category, the free modeling category, where contestants are asked to predict the structure of proteins for which few similar proteins were previously known. Across all categories, AlphaFold predicted the position of the alpha-carbon atoms in the backbone with a root mean square distance of 0.96 Angstroms[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn8). The next-best technique was roughly one third as accurate, with a root mean square distance of 2.8 Angstroms[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn9):

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/rmsd.jpg)

For comparison, the van der Waals diameter of a carbon atom is roughly 1.4 Angstroms. AlphaFold's accuracy is often comparable to experimental accuracy. The co-founder of CASP, John Moult of the University of Maryland, said[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn10):

> We have been stuck on this one problem – how do proteins fold up – for nearly 50 years. To see DeepMind produce a solution for this, having worked personally on this problem for so long and after so many stops and starts, wondering if we’d ever get there, is a very special moment.

That is a very strong statement, and it's worth digging into in what sense AlphaFold solves the protein structure prediction problem, and what remains to be understood. However, even AlphaFold's competitors were laudatory. Here's Mohammed AlQuraishi of Columbia University[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn11):

> Does this constitute a solution of the static protein structure prediction problem? I think so but there are all these wrinkles. Honest, thoughtful people can disagree here and it comes down to one’s definition of what the word “solution” really means… the bulk of the scientific problem is solved; what’s left now is execution.

That was two-and-a-half years ago. I think a reasonable broad view today is: AlphaFold is a huge leap, but much remains to be done even on the basic problem, and many enormous new problems can now be attacked.

AlphaFold's high-level architecture would take several hours to describe. I want to emphasize just a few points today:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/architecture.png)

It's a deep neural network, meaning a hierarchical model, with 93 million parameters learned through training. An amino acid sequence is input, and a 3-dimensional structure is output, together with some error estimates quantifying how confident AlphaFold is in the placement of each amino acid. The basic training data is the protein data bank (PDB), humanity's repository of the protein structures experimentally determined since the 1970s. At training time, that was 170,000 protein structures (though a small fraction were omitted for technical reasons). The parameters in the AlphaFold network are adjusted using gradient descent to ensure the network outputs the correct structure, given the input.

That's a (very!) broad picture of how the network learned to predict structures. Many more ideas were important. One clever and important (albeit existing) idea was a way to learn from the hundreds of millions of known sequences for proteins. The idea is to find many other proteins whose amino acid sequences are similar to the input protein, meaning they're likely to be evolutionarily related. Maybe, for instance, it's essentially the same protein, but in some other species. AlphaFold tries to find many such similar proteins, and to learn from them. To get an intuition for how it might do that, suppose by looking at many similar proteins AlphaFold finds that there is some particular pair of amino acids which are a long way apart in the linear chain, but where changes in one amino acid seem to be correlated to changes in the other. If you saw that you might suspect these amino acids are likely to be close together in space, and are co-evolving together to preserve the shape of the protein. In fact, this often does happen, and it provides a way AlphaFold can learn both from _known structural information_ (in the PDB), and from _known evolutionary information_ (in protein sequence databases). A nice way of putting it, in [a talk from John Jumper](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EjZMxq4oEt4&t=443s&ab_channel=RCSBProteinDataBank), lead author on the AlphaFold paper, is that the physics informs AlphaFold's understanding of the evolutionary history, and the evolutionary history informs AlphaFold's understanding of the physics.

## Generalization and reliability

You might wonder: is AlphaFold just memorizing its training data, or can it generalize? CASP provides a basic validation: the competition structures were not in the PDB at the time AlphaFold predicted them. Furthermore, CASP is in some sense a "natural" sample: the structural biology community prefers to solve structure which are biologically important[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn12). So it suggests some ability to generalize to proteins of interest to biologists at large.

What if we use deep learning to study proteins which don't occur in nature, maybe as the result of mutations, or as part of protein design? There's a huge amount of work going on, and frankly it's an exciting mess right now, all over the place – any reasonable overview would cover dozens if not hundreds of papers. There are papers saying AlphaFold works _badly_ for such-and-such a type of mutation, it works _well_ for such-and-such a kind of mutation, all sorts of deep learning fixes for protein design, and so on. My takeaway: it's going to keep biologists busy for years figuring out the shortcomings, and improving the systems to address those shortcomings.

Some interesting tests of deep learning's ability to generalize were done by OpenFold[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn13), an open source near-clone of AlphaFold. For instance, they retrained OpenFold with most broad classes of topology simply removed from the training set. To do this, they used a standard classification scheme, the CATH classification of protein topology. They removed 95% (!!!) of the topologies from the training set, and then retrained OpenFold from scratch. Even with the great majority of topologies removed, performance was similar to AlphaFold 1[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn14): just a couple of years prior to AlphaFold 2 it would have been state-of-the-art.

In another variation on this idea, they retrained OpenFold starting from much smaller subsamples of the protein data bank. Instead of using 170,000 structures, they retrained with training sets as small as 1,000 structures, chosen at random. Even with such a tiny training set, they achieved a performance comparable to (in fact, slightly better than) AlphaFold 1. And with just 10,000 structures as training data they achieved a performance comparable to the full OpenFold.

As a practical matter, the question of AlphaFold generalization matters in part because DeepMind has released AlphaFold DB, a database of 215 million protein structures, including the (almost-complete) proteomes of 48 species, including humans, mice, and fruit flies. These were obtained by taking genetic sequences in UniProt, and then using AlphaFold to predict the structure. You can view the whole process as:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/assets/AFDB.png)

It's an astonishing act of generalization. If we had a perfectly reliable model, it would extend our understanding of protein structures by roughly three orders of magnitude. It's worth emphasizing that no additional experiments were done by AlphaFold; no additional data were taken. And yet by "just thinking" it was possible to obtain a very large number of predictions that people expect to be very good. I've heard from several biologists variations on the sentiment: "No-one would take an AlphaFold prediction as true on its own; but it’s an extremely helpful starting point, and can save you months of work".

As the models get better still, I expect the line between model and experiment to become blurry. That may sound strange, but in fact traditional "experimentally-determined structures" actually require immensely-complicated theories to go from data to structure. If you believe AlphaFold (or a successor) offered a stronger theory, you might end up believing the predictions from the deep learning system more than you believe (today's) "experimental results". There are early hints of this beginning to happen. In the CASP assessment, AlphaFold performed poorly on several structures which had been determined using NMR spectroscopy. A 2022 paper[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn15) examined "904 human proteins with both Alpha-Fold and NMR structures" and concluded that "Alpha-Fold predictions are usually more accurate than NMR structures". One of the authors, Mike Williamson, actually helped pioneer the use of NMR for structural biology.

To make the same point in a simpler setting: how we interpret the images from a telescope depends upon our theory of optics; if we were to change or improve our theory of optics, our understanding of the so-called "raw data" from the telescope would change. Indeed, something related has really happened in our understanding of the bending of light by gravitation. In that case, we've changed our understanding of the way light travels through space, and that has affected the way we interpret experimental data, particularly in understanding phenomena like gravitational lensing of distant galaxies.

In a similar way, "experimental" protein structure determination depends strongly on theory. You can get a gist for this in x-ray crystallography, which requires many difficult steps: purification of protein sample; crystallization (!) of the proteins; x-ray diffraction to obtain two-dimensional images; procedures to invert and solve for the three-dimensional structure; criteria for when the inversion is good enough. A lot of theory is involved! Indeed, the inversion procedure typically involves starting with a good "guess", a candidate search structure. Often people use related proteins, but sometimes they can't find a good search structure, and this can prevent a solution being found. AlphaFold has been used to find good search structures to help invert data for particularly challenging structures. So there is already a blurry line between theory and experiment. I expect figuring out how to validate AI "solutions" to problems will be a major topic of scientific and metascientific interest in the years to come.

## Is a simple set of principles for protein structure possible? Might AI help us discover it?

Any model with 93 million learned parameters is complicated. It's not a "theory" or "explanation" in the conventional sense. You might wonder: can AlphaFold (or a successor) be used to help discover such a theory, even if only partial? Might, for instance, a simple set of principles for protein structure prediction be possible? And what, exactly, is AlphaFold 2 learning? To avoid disappointment, let me say: we don't yet know the answers to such questions. But pursuing them is a useful intuition pump for thinking about the role of AI in science.

One approach to finding such principles is "behaviorist artificial psychology": inferring high-level principles by observing the behavior of the system. Of course, AlphaFold's detailed predictions have already been used widely by biologists, for things like discovering new binding sites on proteins. But while this is very useful, it isn't the same as inferring novel high-level principles about protein structure. However, there are other important deep learning systems in which interesting new high-level principles have been found by observing behavior.

For instance, by observing the AlphaZero chess system[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn16) behaviors have been inferred which violated conventional chess grandmaster wisdom. These behaviors were announced in December 2018[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn17) and January 2019[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn18). A recent paper[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn19) examined these behaviors, and tried to determine how (and whether) human players had changed in response to the system. They found few changes in the top ten players in 2019, with one exception: the world's top player then and now, Magnus Carlsen. They identify multiple ways in which Carlsen changed his play significantly in 2019, plausibly influenced by AlphaZero.

Among the changes: Carlsen advanced his h pawn early in the game[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn20) much more frequently (a 333% increase with white, a 175% increase with black). He changed his opening strategies for both white and black. Indeed, his two most common 2019 opening strategies with white (variations of the Queen's Gambit declined and of the Grunfeld Defense) were openings he didn't play at all in 2018. And with black he played the Sicilian less than 10% of the time in 2018, but 45% of the time in 2019. They also observed an increase in Carlsen's willingness to sacrifice material. All these changes were consistent with learning from AlphaZero. Carlsen lost no classical games in 2019, and was the only top grandmaster to considerably improve his Elo rating, despite already being the top-rated player: he increased by 37 rating points. By contrast, 6 of the top 10 players actually lost points, and none of the other 3 gained more than 6 points. Of course, we do not know how much of this was due to Carlsen learning from AlphaZero, but the paper makes a plausible case that Carlsen learned much from AlphaZero.

Such behavioral observation is interesting but frustrating: it doesn't tell us _why_ these behaviors occur. We may know that AlphaZero likes to advance the h pawn early, but what we'd really like is to understand why. Can we instead look inside the neural networks, and understand how they do what they do? As far as I know this kind of investigation has only been done casually for AlphaFold. But for simpler neural nets people are discovering interesting structure. Last year, for example, Neel Nanda and Tom Lieberum[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html#fn21) trained a single-layer transformer neural network to add two integers modulo 113. At first, the network simply memorized all the examples in the training set. It could add those well, but (unsurprisingly) gave terrible performance elsewhere. But as they trained the network for much longer, it transitioned to performing vastly better on held-out examples. Somehow, with no additional training data, it was learning to add.

Nanda and Lieberum spent weeks looking inside the network to understand what had changed. By reverse engineering the network they discovered it had learned to add in a remarkable way. The rough gist is: given numbers x and y the network computed a wave e^{2\\pi i kx/113}, then did a phase shift to e^{2\\pi i k x/ 113} \\times e^{2\\pi i ky/113}, and finally tried to find a canceling phase shift z which would result in the wave e^{2 \\pi i k(x+y-z)/113} having the flattest possible "tone". It's a radio frequency engineer or group representation theorist's way of doing addition. This came as quite a surprise to Nanda and Lieberum. As Nanda said:

> To emphasize, this algorithm was purely learned by gradient descent! I did not predict or understand this algorithm in advance and did nothing to encourage the model to learn this way of doing modular addition. I only discovered it by reverse engineering the weights.

This sequence of events greatly puzzled me when first I heard it: why did the network switch to this more general algorithm? After all, it initially memorized the training data, and provided excellent performance: why change? The answer is that during training the neural network pays a cost for more complex models: the loss function is chosen so gradient descent prefers lower-weight models. And the wave algorithm is actually lower-weight. It's a kind of mechanical implementation of Occam's razor, preferring an approach which is simpler and, in this case, more general. Indeed, by varying the loss function you can potentially impose Occam's razor in many different ways. It's intriguing to wonder: will one day we be able to do similar things for systems like AlphaFold, perhaps discovering new basic principles of protein structure?

## Concluding thought

I hope you've enjoyed this brief look at AlphaFold and reflections on the use of AI in science. Of course, there's far more to say. But I believe it's clear there are many fundamental scientific and metascientific questions posed by AI systems. Most of all: as they get better at different types of cognitive operation, and as they become more able to sense and actuate in the environment, how will they impact science as a whole? Will AI systems eventually systematically change the entire practice of science? Will they perhaps greatly speed up scientific discovery? And if so, what risks and benefits does that carry?

## Afterword: what about messier problems, with less good data to learn from?

At the end of my talk many interesting questions were asked by audience members. One striking question came from Evan Miyazono. It was, roughly: "As a target for AI, protein structure prediction benefits from having a lot of very clean prior data which the system can learn from. How useful do you think AI will be for problems that don't have lots of clean prior data to learn from?"

It's a great question. A year or so ago I would have said that yes, it seems challenging to apply AI when you're working with messier problems with less clear metrics of success. But I've changed my mind over the year, as I've better understood foundation models, transfer learning, and zero-shot learning.

To explain why I've changed my mind, let me give two pieces of context. First, some informal gossip: many people have observed that ChatGPT is much better at coding than GitHub Copilot. I don't know the reason for sure. But a common speculation I've heard from informed people is that training on lots of text (as well as code) substantially improves the model's ability to code. Somehow, it seems the regularities in the text are improving ChatGPT's ability to understand code as well.

The second piece of context: a similar idea is used with AlphaFold. To explain this I need to explain a piece of AlphaFold's training that I didn't discuss in the body of the talk: it actually implicitly includes a large language model, treating the sequence of amino acids as a kind of "text" to be filled in. To understand this, recall that given a sequence of amino acids, AlphaFold looks up similar sequences in existing genetic databases. And during training, just like a language model, AlphaFold masks out (or mutates) some of the individual amino acids in those sequences, and attempts to predict the missing values. Doing this forces the network to learn more about the structure of the evolutionary information; it then takes advantage of that forced understanding to do better at protein structure prediction. In this way, AlphaFold benefits from transfer learning from a related domain (genetics) where far more information is available.

Returning to the broader context of science, I expect large multi-modal foundation models will: (a) gradually begin to outperform special-purpose systems, just as large language models often outperform more specialized natural language algorithms; and (b) they will exhibit zero-shot or few-shot learning. These large multi-modal models will be trained not just on text, but also on images and actions and code and genetic data and geographic data and all sorts of sensor and actuator data. And, just as in the language models, they will use what they understand in one domain to help inform their work in adjacent, often messier domains, which the model may perhaps have limited information about. Indeed, there are already hints of protein language models moving in this direction.

Of course, all this is merely a story and an intuition. It hasn't happened yet, except in very nascent ways, and I may be quite wrong! I certainly expect new ideas will be needed to make this work well. But hopefully that sums up how my intuition has changed over the last year: I think AI models will, in the next few years, be surprisingly good at addressing messy problems with only a little prior data. And they'll do it using multi-model foundation models, transfer learning, and zero- and few-shot learning.
