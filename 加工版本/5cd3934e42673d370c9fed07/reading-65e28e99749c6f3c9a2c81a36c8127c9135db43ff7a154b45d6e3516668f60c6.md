# 个体很重要｜Individuals matter

---

## 正文

我看到人们在查看 data 时最常犯的错误之一，就是 incorrectly using 一个 overly simplified model。这个错误有一种具体变体，曾让我要看过的大多数 work roadmaps 偏离轨道：把人当作 interchangeable，仿佛谁在做什么并不重要，仿佛 individuals 并不重要。

Individuals 很重要。

在 roadmap creation 和 review process 中，我反复见到的一种 pattern 是：人们会 plan out 接下来几个 quarters 的 work，然后为其 assign 一定数量的人，一个 project 配一个人做一个 quarter，另一个配两个人做三个 quarters，等等。名义上，这个 process 能让 teams 了解其他 teams 正在做什么，并适当地 plan。我从未在这样的 organization 工作过：这种做法确实奏效，确实让 teams 能在对其他 teams 有 dependencies 的情况下 effectively execute。

我看到的实际情况却是，当 projects 开始 work 时，人们会问谁在负责这个 project，然后根据最终负责这个 project 的是谁，猜测这个 project 能否按时 completed、能否以 effective 的方式 completed，甚至究竟能不能 completed。“哦，Joe 要接手 feature X？他从来都不 ships 任何 reasonable 的东西。看来我们不能 depend on 它了，因为那根本不会 work。我们做 Y 而不是 Z 吧，因为那样就不需要 X 真正 work。”roadmap creation 和 review process 维持着人是 interchangeable 这种礼貌的虚构，但大家都知道这不是真的；那些 effective、想要按时 ship 的 teams，到了动真格的时候就不能再配合演戏，哪怕他们平时会配合 managers、directors 和 VPs，而这些人在 create roadmaps 时，仿佛可以对人进行 generically abstracted over。

人的 non-fungibility 还会在另一个地方造成 predictable problems：managers 如何 operate teams。想要 create effective teams 的 Managers[1](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:P)，最终不得不为此对抗 system。Non-engineering orgs 大多把人当作 fungible，而我工作过的多家公司的 finance org 会要求 engineering org 按 headcount 来 budget，从而迫使其把人当作 fungible。当然，公司花的是钱，不是“heads”，但 internal bookkeeping 是按“heads”进行的，因此，对某个 team 而言，$X 的 budget 会被换算成类似“三个 staff-level heads”的东西。没有办法把它换成“两个更 effective、better-paid 的 staff level heads”[2](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:E)。如果你 hire 两个 staff engineers，而不 hire 第三个，那么这个“head”及其 associated budget 最终就会被挪到别处。

我反复见到的一件事是，hiring manager 会[想要 hire 一个他们认为会 highly effective 的人，甚至只是一个具备 specialized skills 的人，却无法 hire，因为公司把 budget 换算成“heads”时采用的 rate，不允许 hiring 某类 heads](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1452701799417143296)。HR 中会有一个“comp team”或其他 group 出面反对，因为 comp team 没有“一个 effective engineer”或“一个很难 hire 到人的 specialty”这样的概念；对一个人而言，role、level 和 location 就 defines 了他，因此，某人的报酬相对于其 role 和 level 太高，就意味着这是一次 bad hire。如果有任何讲道理的人对这个 process 拥有 power，并且愿意使用它，这种事就不会发生；但 bureaucracy 在设计上就是让很少有人拥有 power[3](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:B)。

retention 上也会发生类似的事。我认识一位出色的 engineer，每年经常为公司创造 $x0M/yr[4](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:Q) 的 additional profit。他想搬回 Portugal 的家，公司便把他的 cash comp 削减到原来的四分之一。公司还提出，如果他搬到 Spain 而不是 Portugal，就只把他的 cash comp 削减到原来的一半。他离开了，去了没有 location-based pay 的公司。这件事曾 escalated 到 director level，但仍不足以 override HR，所以他还是走了。HR 并不在乎这个人为公司赚的钱，比 HR 对所有 international employees 进行 location adjustments 所节省的总额还多，因为这家公司的 HR 根本没有 employee 的 value 这个概念，只有 cost、title、level 和 location[5](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:L)。

与此相关，我见过两次这样的“操作”，一次是远远旁观，一次是近距离亲历：HR 决定 [attrition 太低了](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1277591470162104321)。其中一次，head of HR 认为公司的 ~5% attrition 是“unhealthy”的，因为它太低；另一次，HR 认为公司略低于 10% 的 attrition 太低。在这两种情况下，公司都采取了一些举措，让 attrition 上升到 HR 认为“healthy”的 level。在我远远旁观的那次，我认识的公司员工都认同：公司最好的 engineers 中，大多数在接下来的一年里离开了，许多人仅过了几个月就走了。在我近距离亲历的那次，我列出了一份我所知道的最 effective 的 engineers 名单（例如上文提到的那个人，他在 paternity leave 期间让公司的 revenue increased 了 0.7%）；当公司成功把整体 attrition 推到超过 10% 时，最 effective 的 engineers 却以超过该 rate 两倍的速度离开（这还低估了其 impact，因为他们往往是 long-tenured 的 senior engineers，这类人的 normal expected attrition 本应不到公司 average attrition 的一半）。

有些人似乎把公司看成一局 SimCity：如果想要更多的钱，就可以拧一下旋钮，increase taxes，然后得到更多的钱，对城市产生 uniform 的影响。但公司不是一局 SimCity。如果你想要更多 attrition，便拧动旋钮让它上升，得到的 additional attrition 并不是 sampled uniformly at random 的。不能把人作为一个整体，当成一种 abstraction，仿佛 company leadership 采取的 actions 会以同样的方式 impacts 每个人。如果你拧动一个导致 increased attrition 的旋钮，最 effective 的那些人离开的可能性就会 disproportionately 高。

到目前为止，我们谈的是把 individual people 当作 fungible，为何对 corporations 行不通；当然，一般而言，这样做也行不通。例如，我的一位朋友在 Africa 做过相当多“实地”的 development work，她抱怨说，许多打算 donate 的人希望有 clear、simple 的 criteria 来指导自己的 donations（例如，一项 RCT 表明某个 intervention 是 highly effective 的）。但许多 effective interventions 的 impact 无法在 ex ante 以任何 simple 的方式 demonstrated，原因之一是，implementing 这个 intervention 的 team 的 composition 很重要；因此，一项 randomized trial 或其他 experiment，除了对 trial 中的那些 teams 在 trial 期间所处的 context 适用外，并不 applicable 于其他 implementing 这个 intervention 的 team。

一个例子是他们参与的一项 intervention，它的成果之一，是帮助在一个国家 wipe out guinea worm。Ex post，我们可以说那是一项 highly effective intervention，因为那是一个三人 team，在相对较短的一段时间内，以 $12/(person-day)[6](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:T) 的 budget 开展工作，因此它是一项 high ROI intervention；但在 ex ante，没有办法为这项 intervention 提出 quantitative case，而且似乎也不太可能存在一组 randomized trials 或 experiments，可以为这项 intervention 提供依据。

他们的 intervention 并不是 wiping out guinea worm，那只是一个 side effect。这项 intervention 基本上是在全国各地奔走，进驻 regional government offices，以了解它们的 problems，然后 advise/facilitate 更好的 decision making。在与人交谈并提出 improvements/changes 的过程中，他们意识到 guinea worm 可以通过更好的 clean water distribution（guinea worm 可能来自饮用 unfiltered water；为人们提供 clean water 可以 solve 这个 problem），而且流入该国、专门用于 water-related projects（例如 building wells）的 aid money，本来就已经 sufficient，前提是把它 distributed 到该国因 contaminated water 而有 high rates of guinea worm 的地方，而不是流向 aid money 当时正在流向的地方（那些地方由于各种原因，已经有大量 aid money 流入，例如靠近一家开展大量 charity work 的当地“办事处”）。这个 team 为帮助 wipe out guinea worm 具体做的事情，是向 government officials 做 powerpoint presentations，说明 government 可以如何 advise 接收 aid money 的 organizations，让这些 organizations 能 more efficiently place wells。At the margin，在一个国家 wiping out guinea worm，可能就足以让这项 intervention 成为 high ROI，但这只是这个三人 team 所带来的“return”中非常小的一部分。我之所以只提这件事，是因为它是一项 self-contained、easily-quantifiable 的 change。在 regional government offices 中“leveling up” decision making 所带来的大部分 value 都非常难以 quantify（而且，即便能够 quantified，仍会有非常大的 error bars）。

许多在 ex ante 看起来相同的 interventions，甚至很可能是其中大多数，产生的 impact 都微乎其微，乃至没有。对于那些派出大量人员到各处做听起来类似的 work、却 produce 很少 value 的 organizations，例如 Peace Corps，我的朋友有很多话可说。

我朋友的 team 与大多数 teams 之间，一个主要 difference 是：她的 team 由一些在各种 contexts 中都有 highly effective 的 track record 的人组成。在早先的一份工作中，我的朋友入职了一家规模不小（$5B/yr revenue）的 government-run utility company，随即被 assigned 一个 problem；她当时并不知道，这已经是一个存在多年的 open problem，被认为是 unsolvable 的。没人愿意碰这个 problem，所以他们 hired 她，是想找一个替罪羊，等 problem 爆发时就归咎于她并将她 fire。然而，她不但 solved 了 assigned 给她的 problem，还 solved 了若干其他被认为 unsolvable 的 problems。由三个这样的人组成的 team，能从那些大多数 teams 无法 succeed at 的 potentially high ROI interventions 中取得很大成效，例如去一个外国，improving 全国各地 regional offices 的 governmental decision making，改善到足以让 government 能够 solve 那些困扰该国数十年的 serious open problems。

许多 highest ROI interventions 同样是 skill intensive 的，无法用 simple back-of-the-envelope calculations 来处理；但我所看到的关于这个话题的大多数讨论，无论是当面还是网上，都严重依赖 simplistic 却 irrelevant 的 back-of-the-envelope calculations。这不只是局限于酒会闲谈的一个 problem。我朋友的 intervention 差点被她工作的 organization 扼杀，因为那个 organization 充斥着她所谓的“overly simplistic [EA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism) thinking”，导致 organization 的 leadership 试图把 resources 转向那些 expected return 的 computation 更 simple 的 projects，因为那些 projects 被认为有 higher impact，尽管从 ex post 来看，它们的 impact 更低。当然，我们不应该根据 interventions 在 ex post 的表现来 judge 它们，因为那会过度偏向 high variance interventions；但我认为，一个愿意认真思考、运用自己的 judgement，而不是把 judgement 外包给一个 simple metric 的人，能够而且应该说，当时那项 intervention 在 ex ante 是一个好的 choice。

更 [legible](https://amzn.to/3qGiucN) 的 projects 获得更多 funding，这个问题既存在于 organizations 之间，也存在于它们内部。例如，我的朋友说，当初 GiveWell 主要或只 recommending 那些具有可简单 quantifiable 的 return 的 charities 时，她基本上无法让在其他 fields 工作的朋友把 resources 投入那些没有得到 GiveWell endorsed 的 efforts。当她建议把 resources 投入某个具体的 cause、project 或 organization 时，不了解她的 aid background 的人就会说：“你没听说过 GiveWell 吗？”

我和一位当时在 GiveWell 工作的朋友谈过这件事。据他说，GiveWell 最初 focused on 那些具有容易 quantifiable 的 value 的 charities，并不是因为他们认为那些是 impact 最高的 charities。而是因为，作为一个刚起步的 organization，他们需要具有可信度，而对于 value 容易 quantifiable 的 charities，更容易提出可信的论证。他不会赞同 donors 把所有 resources 都 funnel 到 GiveWell endorsed 的 charities，并 neglect 其他 improve 世界的方式；他认为 GiveWell 也不会赞同。但许多人希望世界是简单的，于是 apply 这个 algorithm：“GiveWell list 上的 charity = good；不在 GiveWell list 上 = bad”，因为这让他们眼中的世界变得简单。

不幸的是，无论对这些人还是对世界而言，世界都不简单。

回到 tech company 的 examples，Laurence Tratt 指出了我也观察到的一件事：

> 在 large organisations 中，我发现一件很有意思的事：当它们意识到需要做些不同的事情时（也就是说，它们正在缓慢 failing，想要扭转局面），显而易见的做法是让一个 small team take risks，因为他们可能 win big。可它们往往反而成立没完没了的 committees，而这些 committees 只会 perpetuate 当初导致它们成立的那种 drift！我认为，这是因为它们实在很难不把人看作 fungible，即使它们真的想这样做：突破自身的 organisational mould 几乎超出了它们的能力，哪怕这意味着 long-term doom。

我们可以用来理解眼前情况的一个 lens 是 [legibility](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2010/07/26/a-big-little-idea-called-legibility/)。当你面对一个 complex system 时，无论它是拥有数千名 engineers 的 company，还是有数十亿美元投入 aid work 的世界，这个 system 都过于 complex，任何 decision maker 都无法真正理解，无论那是 company 的 exec，还是试图弄清自己的钱应当投向何处的 potential donor。解决这个问题的一种方式，是通过想象 individuals 是 fungible 来 reducing 问题的 perceived complexity，让 system 更 legible。这会产生相对 inefficient 的 outcomes，但与[尝试理解眼前的 issues](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1458948982059593730)不同，它是 highly scalable 的。如果说 tech companies 喜欢什么，那就是做能够 scale 的事情，而把 complex system 当作 SimCity 或 Civilization 来对待，就是 highly scalable 的。当 returns 是相对 evenly distributed 的时候，为了 legibility 而放弃 potential outlier returns，是一个不错的 trade-off。但当 ROI 是 heavy-tailed distribution，当合适的人能够在 paternity leave 期间 increase 一家 giant tech company 的 company revenue 达 0.7%，而在 full-time 从事这项工作时还能增加更多，那么，为了 improve legibility 而 severely tamping down 曲线的 right side，就会非常 costly，可能让你失去 potential returns 的大部分。

感谢 Laurence Tratt、Pam Wolf、Ben Kuhn、Peter Bhat Harkins、John Hergenroeder、Andrey Mishchenko、Joseph Kaptur 和 Sophia Wisdom 提供的 comments/corrections/discussion。

### 附录：re-orgs

一位朋友最近给我讲了一家时髦的 tech company 的故事：他们试图把六个人 move 到另一个 project，而这些人既不想 work on 那个 project，也觉得它并没有什么道理。结果是，两名 senior devs quit，EM retired，一名 PM 被 fired（说来话长），还有三个人离开了 team。无论是原来的 project 还是新的 project，其 team 都不得不从头 re-created。

情况还可能糟糕得多。在那个例子中，至少还有一些人没有离开 company。我曾经问过某个人，为什么已经公开承诺的 feature X 还没有 implemented，而且整个 sub-product 也处于 broken 状态。得到的回答是，在工作了大约一年、大家认为距离 shipping 这个 feature 只剩几周的时候，leadership 决定，这个先前被视为 top priority 的 feature 不再是 priority，应当 abandoned。team 据理力争，说这个 feature 已经非常接近完成，他们只是希望有足够的 runway 来 finish 这个 feature。这个请求遭到拒绝后，整个 team 都 quit 了，此后 sub-product 就逐渐 decayed。许多年后，曾经有人尝试 reboot 这个 team，但由于一些超出这个故事范围的原因，当时是由一位新 manager managing new grads，并没有真正 re-create 原先 team 的能力。

正如我们之前看到的，[effective team 很难 create，因为 team 中存在 institutional knowledge](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/)，还有 [team 的 culture](https://danluu.com/culture/)，但 destroying 一个 team 却非常容易。

我觉得很有意思的是，如此多身处 senior management roles 的人，始终认为自己可以 re-direct 人员，就像在 Civilization 中打开 city view 并 assigning workers 从一个 task switch 到另一个 task 那么容易，而与我交谈的 senior ICs 却能以很高的 accuracy predicting 这类 moves 什么时候不会奏效。

1.  另一方面，也有 managers 想要 maximize 自己 career 的 return。在我工作过的每一家非 startup 的 company，这都涉及 moving up the ladder，而最容易的办法就是尽可能多地收拢人员。我曾经工作过的一家 company，其明确列出的 promo criteria 基本上就是“有多少人 report up to 这个人”。

    如果你认为人基本上是 fungible 的，那么把 promotions 和 compensation 与 managed 的人数 Tying 起来或许有道理；否则，这显然是一个愚蠢的想法。

2.  如果考虑到 retention budgets（从一个不属于 org 的 normal budget 的 pool 中拨出的资金，通常用于 match 即将离开的人的 offers）等因素，事情并没有这么简单，但加上这一细节并不会真正改变根本论点。[\[返回\]](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fnref:E)
3.  让人没有 power 的 system 有一些 advantages，例如 mitigating abuses of power、各种 biases、nepotism 等。你可以主张，通过让人 powerless 来 reducing outcomes 的 variance 是更理想的 result，但在 winner-take-most markets 中——许多 tech markets 正是如此——强迫所有人停留在 lowest-common-denominator effectiveness，只会让你沦为陪跑者。

    一个具体的、小规模的 example，是[那些没有针对 technical blog posts 的 bureaucratic comms/PR approval process 的 companies 所拥有](https://danluu.com/corp-eng-blogs/)的巨大 advantage。大多数 companies 设置 onerous process，其背后的 theory 是保护 company 免受糟糕 blog post 的 downside risk，但那些本可以通过 onerous process 得到 mitigated 的糟糕 engineering blog posts 的 examples 少之又少；相比之下，拥有良好 writing publicly 的 processes 的 companies，却能获得大量显而易见的 value。

    一个更大规模的 example 是，如今规模已达 >= $500B 的 large companies，都做出了其 bureaucracy 缠身的 competitors 不可能做出的 aggressive moves，这让它们得以彻底击败 competitors。当然，许多其他下了 serious bets 而不是求稳的 companies，比那些试图求稳的 companies 更快 failed，但至少那些 companies 还有机会，不像一味求稳的 companies。

    [\[返回\]](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fnref:B)
4.  我通常对这类 claims 持怀疑态度。在我工作过的多家 companies，如果你 tally up 那些声称取得的 revenue 或 user growth wins，再将它们与 actual revenue 或 user growth compare，就会发现其中有些蹊跷，因为 total claimed wins 远远大于 observed total。

    只是因为[我通常对 measurements 很好奇](https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/)，我有时会对人们 claimed wins 做自己的 analysis，而我得出的 estimate 几乎总是远低于 original estimate。当然，我通常不会在内部 publish 这些 results，因为一般来说，那样只会树敌无数，却不会带来任何改变。在一个极端的例子中，我发现整个 org 使用的 experimental methodology 都是 broken 的，导致他们在 A/B tests 中得到 spurious wins。我私下告知了他们，但他们没有采取任何行动；对他们而言，这是唯一合理的做法，因为让 experiments 在实际并不存在 improvement 时 systematically showed improvement，是 org 获得更多 power 的一种 cheap 且 effective 的方式：让其人员得到 promoted，并让更多 headcount 被 allocated 给它。而如果任何对 bureaucracy 拥有 power 的人在意 results 的 accuracy，claimed wins 与 actual results 之间如此巨大的 discrepancy 从一开始就不可能存在。

    总之，尽管我通常对 claimed wins 持怀疑态度，但在亲自 checking 之后，我发现这个人的 claimed wins 非常可信。他们在 paternity leave 期间完成的一个 project（之所以在休假期间做，是因为他们的 manager，实际上还有 organization 和 company，都不支持他们所做的那类工作）让 company 的 revenue increased 了 0.7%；这一结果是 robust 的，而且在 long-term holdback 中，其 value 实际上还在 increasing。在 leadership 因为难堪而允许他们从事有 valuable 的工作之后，他们还能 produce 同等 magnitude 的 wins。

    附言：如果你也想在家试试，在弄清哪些 teams 和 orgs hit 了自己的 roadmap goals 之后，还有另一个有趣的游戏可以玩。想要加分的话，可以 plot 一个 team hits 的 roadmap goals 的 percentage 与其 headcount growth 的关系，以及在不同 teams 中，hitting 上个 quarter 的 goals 对 hitting 下个 quarter 的 goals 有多大的 predictive 作用。

    [\[返回\]](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fnref:Q)
5.  我见过不少人在疫情期间因为 location adjustments 而离开 employers。在一个例子中，HR 坚称那个人实际上得到了非常优厚的 compensated，因为，尽管这个人的薪酬 significantly less than 许多比其低一个 level 的人，看起来似乎并非 highly paid，但根据 HR 的 formula——其中包含 location-based pay adjustment——按 normalized pay 来说，这个人是整个 company 中同一 level 里 highest paid 的人之一。先撇开关于 fairness 的 abstract considerations，[对于 employee 而言](https://yosefk.com/blog/compensation-rationality-and-the-projectperson-fit.html)，HR 告诉他们，考虑到其 location，他们已经是 highly paid，就像 HR 用一个根据身高支付薪酬的 formula，告诉 employee 按其身高而言，薪酬已经很优厚。这按 HR 的任何 formula 来说或许是对的，但实际上，对 employee 而言毫无意义，因为他们可以去一个 height-based pay adjustment 更小的地方工作。

    在 pandemic 之前，Companies 可以实行 severe location-based pay adjustments，而自身无需承担任何 cost。但自 pandemic 以来，许多 companies 已加大 remote hiring 的力度，其中一些 companies 的 location-based pay adjustments 相对较小，这使它们能够从那些仍维持 severe location-based pay adjustments 的 companies 中 disproportionately hire away 它们选中的人。

## 原文

One of the most common mistakes I see people make when looking at data is incorrectly using an overly simplified model. A specific variant of this that has derailed the majority of work roadmaps I've looked at is treating people as interchangeable, as if it doesn't matter who is doing what, as if individuals don't matter.

Individuals matter.

A pattern I've repeatedly seen during the roadmap creation and review process is that people will plan out the next few quarters of work and then assign some number of people to it, one person for one quarter to a project, two people for three quarters to another, etc. Nominally, this process enables teams to understand what other teams are doing and plan appropriately. I've never worked in an organization where this actually worked, where this actually enabled teams to effectively execute with dependencies on other teams.

What I've seen happen instead is, when work starts on the projects, people will ask who's working the project and then will make a guess at whether or not the project will be completed on time or in an effective way or even be completed at all based on who ends up working on the project. "Oh, Joe is taking feature X? He never ships anything reasonable. Looks like we can't depend on it because that's never going to work. Let's do Y instead of Z since that won't require X to actually work". The roadmap creation and review process maintains the polite fiction that people are interchangeable, but everyone knows this isn't true and teams that are effective and want to ship on time can't play along when the rubber hits the road even if they play along with the managers, directors, and VPs, who create roadmaps as if people can be generically abstracted over.

Another place the non-fungibility of people causes predictable problems is with how managers operate teams. Managers who want to create effective teams[1](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:P) end up fighting the system in order to do so. Non-engineering orgs mostly treat people as fungible, and the finance org at a number of companies I've worked for forces the engineering org to treat people as fungible by requiring the org to budget in terms of headcount. The company, of course, spends money and not "heads", but internal bookkeeping is done in terms of "heads", so $X of budget will be, for some team, translated into something like "three staff-level heads". There's no way to convert that into "two more effective and better-paid staff level heads"[2](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:E). If you hire two staff engineers and not a third, the "head" and the associated budget will eventually get moved somewhere else.

One thing I've repeatedly seen is that a hiring manager will [want to hire someone who they think will be highly effective or even just someone who has specialized skills and then not be able to hire because the company has translated budget into "heads" at a rate that doesn't allow for hiring some kind of heads](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1452701799417143296). There will be a "comp team" or other group in HR that will object because the comp team has no concept of "an effective engineer" or "a specialty that's hard to hire for"; for a person, role, level, and location defines them and someone who's paid too much for their role and level is therefore a bad hire. If anyone reasonable had power over the process that they were willing to use, this wouldn't happen but, by design, the bureaucracy is set up so that few people have power[3](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:B).

A similar thing happens with retention. A great engineer I know who was regularly creating $x0M/yr[4](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:Q) of additional profit for the company per year wanted to move home to Portugal, so the company cut the person's cash comp by a factor of four. The company also offered to only cut his cash comp by a factor of two if he moved to Spain instead of Portugal. He left for a company that doesn't have location-based pay. This was escalated up to the director level, but that wasn't sufficient to override HR, so they left. HR didn't care that the person made the company more money than HR saves by doing location adjustments for all international employees combined because HR at the company had no notion of the value of an employee, only the cost, title, level, and location[5](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:L).

Relatedly, a "move" I've seen twice, once from a distance and once from up close, is when HR decides [attrition is too low](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1277591470162104321). In one case, the head of HR thought that the company's ~5% attrition was "unhealthy" because it was too low and in another, HR thought that the company's attrition sitting at a bit under 10% was too low. In both cases, the company made some moves that resulted in attrition moving up to what HR thought was a "healthy" level. In the case I saw from a distance, folks I know at the company agree that the majority of the company's best engineers left over the next year, many after only a few months. In the case I saw up close, I made a list of the most effective engineers I was aware of (like the person mentioned above who increased the company's revenue by 0.7% on his paternity leave) and, when the company successfully pushed attrition to over 10% overall, the most effective engineers left at over double that rate (which understates the impact of this because they tended to be long-tenured and senior engineers, where the normal expected attrition would be less than half the average company attrition).

Some people seem to view companies like a game of SimCity, where if you want more money, you can turn a knob, increase taxes, and get more money, uniformly impacting the city. But companies are not a game of SimCity. If you want more attrition and turn a knob that cranks that up, you don't get additional attrition that's sampled uniformly at random. People, as a whole, cannot be treated as an abstraction where the actions company leadership takes impacts everyone in the same way. The people who are most effective will be disproportionately likely to leave if you turn a knob that leads to increased attrition.

So far, we've talked about how treating individual people as fungible doesn't work for corporations but, of course, it also doesn't work in general. For example, a complaint from a friend of mine who's done a fair amount of "on the ground" development work in Africa is that a lot of people who are looking to donate want, clear, simple criteria to guide their donations (e.g., an RCT showed that the intervention was highly effective). But many effective interventions cannot have their impact demonstrated ex ante in any simple way because, among other reasons, the composition of the team implementing the intervention is important, resulting in a randomized trial or other experiment not being applicable to team implementing the intervention other than the teams from the trial in the context they were operating in during the trial.

An example of this would be an intervention they worked on that, among other things, helped wipe out guinea worm in a country. Ex post, we can say that was a highly effective intervention since it was a team of three people operating on a budget of $12/(person-day)[6](https://danluu.com/people-matter/#fn:T) for a relatively short time period, making it a high ROI intervention, but there was no way to make a quantitative case for the intervention ex ante, nor does it seem plausible that there could've been a set of randomized trials or experiments that would've justified the intervention.

Their intervention wasn't wiping out guinea worm, that was just a side effect. The intervention was, basically, travelling around the country and embedding in regional government offices in order to understand their problems and then advise/facilitate better decision making. In the course of talking to people and suggesting improvements/changes, they realized that guinea worm could with better distribution of clean water (guinea worm can come from drinking unfiltered water; giving people clean water can solve that problem) and that aid money flowing into the country specifically for water-related projects, like building wells, was already sufficient if the it was distributed to places in the country that had high rates of guinea worm due to contaminated water instead of to the places aid money was flowing to (which were locations that had a lot of aid money flowing to them for a variety of reasons, such as being near a local "office" that was doing a lot of charity work). The specific thing this team did to help wipe out guinea worm was to give powerpoint presentations to government officials on how the government could advise organizations receiving aid money on how those organizations could more efficiently place wells. At the margin, wiping out guinea worm in a country would probably be sufficient for the intervention to be high ROI, but that's a very small fraction of the "return" from this three person team. I only mention it because it's a self-contained easily-quantifiable change. Most of the value of "leveling up" decision making in regional government offices is very difficult to quantify (and, to the extent that it can be quantified, will still have very large error bars).

Many interventions that seem the same ex ante, probably even most, produce little to no impact. My friend has a lot of comments on organizations that send a lot of people around to do similar sounding work but that produce little value, such as the Peace Corps.

A major difference between my friend's team and most teams is that my friend's team was composed of people who had a track record of being highly effective across a variety of contexts. In an earlier job, my friend started a job at a large-ish ($5B/yr revenue) government-run utility company and was immediately assigned a problem that, unbeknownst to her, had been an open problem for years that was considered to be unsolvable. No one was willing to touch the problem, so they hired her because they wanted a scapegoat to blame and fire when the problem blew up. Instead, she solved the problem she was assigned to as well as a number of other problems that were considered unsolvable. A team of three such people will be able to get a lot of mileage out of potentially high ROI interventions that most teams would not succeed at, such as going to a foreign country and improving governmental decision making in regional offices across the country enough that the government is able to solve serious open problems that had been plaguing the country for decades.

Many of the highest ROI interventions are similarly skill intensive and not amenable to simple back-of-the-envelope calculations, but most discussions I see on the topic, both in person and online, rely heavily on simplistic but irrelevant back-of-the-envelope calculations. This is not just a problem limited to cocktail-party conversations. My friend's intervention was almost killed by the organization she worked for because the organization was infested with what she thinks of "overly simplistic [EA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism) thinking", which caused leadership in the organization to try to redirect resources to projects where the computation of expected return was simpler because those projects were thought to be higher impact even though they were, ex post, lower impact. Of course, we shouldn't judge interventions on how they performed ex post since that will overly favor high variance interventions, but I think that someone thinking it through, who was willing to exercise their judgement instead of outsourcing their judgement to a simple metric, could and should say that the intervention in question was a good choice ex ante.

This issue of projects which are more [legible](https://amzn.to/3qGiucN) getting more funding is an issue across organizations as well as within them. For example, my friend says that, back when GiveWell was mainly or only recommending charities that had simply quantifiable return, she basically couldn't get her friends who worked in other fields to put resources towards efforts that weren't endorsed by GiveWell. People who didn't know about her aid background would say things like "haven't you heard of GiveWell?" when she suggested putting resources towards any particular cause, project, or organization.

I talked to a friend of mine who worked at GiveWell during that time period about this and, according to him, the reason GiveWell initially focused on charities that had easily quantifiable value wasn't that they thought those were the highest impact charities. Instead, it was because, as a young organization, they needed to be credible and it's easier to make a credible case for charities whose value is easily quantifiable. He would not, and he thinks GiveWell would not, endorse donors funnelling all resources into charities endorsed by GiveWell and neglecting other ways to improve the world. But many people want the world to be simple and apply the algorithm "charity on GiveWell list = good; not on GiveWell list = bad" because it makes the world simple for them.

Unfortunately for those people, as well as for the world, the world is not simple.

Coming back to the tech company examples, Laurence Tratt notes something that I've also observed:

> One thing I've found very interesting in large organisations is when they realise that they need to do something different (i.e. they're slowly failing and want to turn the ship around). The obvious thing is to let a small team take risks on the basis that they might win big. Instead they tend to form endless committees which just perpetuate the drift that caused the committees to be formed in the first place! I think this is because they really struggle to see people as anything other than fungible, even if they really want to: it's almost beyond their ability to break out of their organisational mould, even when it spells long-term doom.

One lens we can use to look at what's going on is [legibility](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2010/07/26/a-big-little-idea-called-legibility/). When you have a complex system, whether that's a company with thousands of engineers or a world with many billions of dollars going to aid work, the system is too complex for any decision maker to really understand, whether that's an exec at a company or a potential donor trying to understand where their money should go. One way to address this problem is by reducing the perceived complexity of the problem via imagining that individuals are fungible, making the system more legible. That produces relatively inefficient outcomes but, unlike [trying to understand the issues at hand](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1458948982059593730), it's highly scalable, and if there's one thing that tech companies like, it's doing things that scale, and treating a complex system like it's SimCity or Civilization is highly scalable. When returns are relatively evenly distributed, losing out on potential outlier returns in the name of legibility is a good trade-off. But when ROI is a heavy-tailed distribution, when the right person can, on their paternity leave, increase company revenue of a giant tech company by 0.7% and then much more when they work on that full-time, then severely tamping down on the right side of the curve to improve legibility is very costly and can cost you the majority of your potential returns.

### Appendix: re-orgs

A friend of mine recently told me a story about a trendy tech company where they tried to move six people to another project, one that the people didn't want to work on that they thought didn't really make sense. The result was that two senior devs quit, the EM retired, one PM was fired (long story), and three people left the team. The team for both the old project and the new project had to be re-created from scratch.

It could be much worse. In that case, at least there were some people who didn't leave the company. I once asked someone why feature X, which had been publicly promised, hadn't been implemented yet and also the entire sub-product was broken. The answer was that, after about a year of work, when shipping the feature was thought to be weeks away, leadership decided that the feature, which was previously considered a top priority, was no longer a priority and should be abandoned. The team argued that the feature was very close to being done and they just wanted enough runway to finish the feature. When that was denied, the entire team quit and the sub-product has slowly decayed since then. After many years, there was one attempted reboot of the team but, for reasons beyond the scope of this story, it was done with a new manager managing new grads and didn't really re-create what the old team was capable of.

As we've previously seen, [an effective team is difficult to create, due to the institutional knowledge that exists on a team](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/), as well as [the team's culture](https://danluu.com/culture/), but destroying a team is very easy.

I find it interesting that so many people in senior management roles persist in thinking that they can re-direct people as easily as opening up the city view in Civilization and assigning workers to switch from one task to another when the senior ICs I talk to have high accuracy in predicting when these kinds of moves won't work out.
