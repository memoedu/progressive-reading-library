# 你在 supporting 这个 system！

---

## 正文

最近，Washington Post 的 _Wonkblog_ 对一群正在 earning to give 的人作了一些深入的[报道](http://www.washingtonpost.com/blogs/wonkblog/wp/2013/05/31/join-wall-street-save-the-world/?tid=ts_carousel)。

看到 effective altruism 的 ideas 进入主流，真让人兴奋。尤其是这篇文章，还有一个很有意思的新特点：一个很大的评论区，里面满是不曾认同我们的人。

那里的讨论水平相当低，但如果我们希望 effective altruism 背后的 ideas 得到充分传播，它们就必须能在那种环境中生存。因此，我决定忍着不适读一读评论区，试着从中汲取教训，看看怎样才能为更广泛的读者 refine 我们的 ideas；这或许能带来一些启发。

通读这些负面回应后，可以看出 objections 大致有五个主要 components。我看到的所有针对 earning to give 的攻击，都可以由这些 components 的某种 combination 来解释（我读完了截至 June 1st 的 12 PM 的整个讨论串，尽管中间可能有几次看得两眼发直）。以下就是五种主要类型：

## 你在 supporting 这个 system！

一种常见的回应是，participate 在 Wall Street 的这个“system”之中，仍然是不可以的。

> 如果你通过 trading 和 excessive speculation 使 commodities 变得更 expensive，然后再把你的一部分 profits donate 给挨饿的人，这就有点 disingenuous，因为他们的 food 的 price 高到他们负担不起，你也是其中的一部分原因。—**mhdrgdajveeah**

> 胡说八道。Wall Street 正在毁掉这个国家。肮脏、腐朽、greedy 的人渣。他们全都是。就算他们把自己的一些钱送出去，那又怎样？那些被 Wall Street 毁掉的辛勤劳动的人呢？—**dubhlaoich**

> 你会碰到的问题是——

> 如果你是通过 financing 一家 company 来赚钱，而它正在 doing 的 harm，比你可能从自己的 profit 中 do 的 good 还要多。

> 例如，一家 company 可能想为 tobacco products 去 exploit emerging markets。—**Mark in Colorado**

正是这类反驳，使得提及 [replaceability](http://80000hours.org/blog/35-the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1) _非常重要_：也就是说，如果你不在一个 unethical industry 中工作，就会有另一个人 ~（这个人很可能更加 unethical）~[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/etg-objections/#fn:1) 来接替你的位置。许多人听过 replaceability argument 之后，回头看会觉得它太 obvious 了，以至于他们不会提起它，除非意识到别人还没有想明白，才把它作为 counter-argument 提出来。

但事实上，replaceability（以及更一般地说，考虑 marginal 而不是 total 的 effects）对大多数人来说似乎很不 obvious。因此，我认为我们需要更多地谈论 replaceability，尤其是在主流来源中。

## 那 big problem _x_ 呢？

这类评论抱怨说，那些正在 earning to give 的人，应该去处理某个 bigger problem 的一部分。在这里，主要目标是“fixing 这个 system”：

> 我想，在 greedy 的人自己的游戏里与他们较量，是 noble 的，但我更希望这个‘游戏’根本不存在。或者，干脆对 trades assessing 一个 fair tax rate，以 support 我们的政府在 eradicate malaria 等许多事情上的努力，怎么样？—**naomi94112**

> 还有，那些 stocks 正为 profit 而被 blindly manipulated 的 companies，究竟在 supported 或 expanded 什么 ecocide？比如，通过 changing US 对 environment 的 policy 来尝试真正改变世界，难道不是更好吗？—**getsmart4**

> 我们需要那些 smart、dedicated、想做正确的事的人，把自己的精力和才华直接投入 reforming 一个正在拖累人们的 system，或者至少不要 enabling 它。—**jgunne**

我们需要更多地强调，_我们在意这些问题，只是我们对它们无能为力的地方很多。_ 以 financial reform 为例。financial industry 目前每年记录的 profits 约为 \$120 billion a year ([source](http://blogs.reuters.com/felix-salmon/2011/03/30/chart-of-the-day-us-financial-profits/)). If proposed reforms halve those profits, the industry should be willing to spend up to \$60 billion，用于 lobbying 以阻止这样的 reform，这意味着我们得集结同样规模的 resources，才能通过这样的 reform。这大约相当于每年都要付出到 2020 年 curing malaria 的 total estimated cost（[来源](http://www.rbm.who.int/gmap/2-5.html)）。所以，仅仅因为某件事是一个 big problem，并不意味着它就是最值得着手处理的问题。

## 你应该 attack causes，而不是 symptoms。

另一个常见的 argument 是，solving disease 是在 curing 一个 symptom，而不是一个 cause。

> 我并不反对 charity，但认为这种 charity 会“拯救世界”的 belief 是 naive 的，而且 ignores 了那些 perpetuate poverty 的 structural conditions。只要 property 和 wealth 继续 concentrated 在少数人手中——Wall Street 正是这一点的典型象征——就会继续有人被 deprived of basic necessities of life。—**GeorgeStevens**

> 我确实觉得，这番关于 saving lives 的话冷漠得令人奇怪。这有点像 pro-lifers 强迫 women 给自己的 fetuses 进行 give birth，结果他们（那些 pro-lifers）却丢下出生的 infant，走开时回头喊道：“现在你只能靠自己了！”这些好心人不过是把问题往后又拖了一点。—**barnesgene**

我对 development economics 了解不多，但我认为这里有一个相当清楚的回应：这不是 cause/symptom relationship，而是一个 vicious cycle。Poor countries 没有 health infrstructure，所以它们有 high disease burd en，所以它们是 economically disadvantaged，所以它们无法 build health infrastructure。Attacking 这个 loop 的任何部分都有帮助，而 disease 恰好是最容易从外部 solve 的部分。

## 但我们永远无法 _know_ 自己正在 doing 多少 good！

对我个人而言，最恼人的倾向是坚持认为，既然我们不能确切地知道事情如何，就什么也不该做。

> 以一只 dog 的钱可以买到 thousands 张 nets 为 premise，选择 fund mosquito nets 而不是一只 seeing eye dog，真的 ethical（或 smart）吗？他们真的能 per-emptively \[sic\] know，这些 nets 的 distribution 与一只 service dog 的 placement 相比，会带来怎样的 value 吗？如果他们确实 know，他们用来 justify 自己的 decision 的 criteria 又是什么？assume mosquito netting 会对 African society 作出一只 seeing eye dog 无法对这个国家的某个人作出的 contribution，这 reasonable 吗？—**cleverrevolution**

> 许多评论都源于 Singer 提出的那类 Utilitarian arguments 所面临的根本问题。由于世界及我们的 consequences 的 actions 有着惊人的 complexity，尝试处理 minimal global goods 的 maximization，往往最终陷入 absurdity 或 obscurity。考虑到任何 action 都有 infinitude 的 unforeseeable consequences，哪种 action 才会 maximize goods？不幸的是，这远比只是说‘救救这个女孩！’难以回答。然后，你究竟要做到什么程度？我在这方面见过的最好的 objection 是 Parfit 的 repugnant conclusion。有兴趣的人可以看：[http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/repugnant-conclu…](http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/repugnant-conclusion/) —**Calicles**

唯一真正涉及 philosophy 的 objecter 归入了这一 category，这并非偶然：counter-argument 要更 nuanced 一些，也更难解释。不幸的是，我完全不知道怎样把它提炼成一种能在评论串中 survive 和 replicate 的形式——不过，我认为想出点办法相当重要。

无论如何，counter-argument 的基本形式是：没错，我们不知道最终结果会是什么，但我们可以作出非常好的 guess。它好到足以让采用这类 estimates 所得到的结果，远远好于凭 intuitively right 的感觉行事，或根据你用来 picking actions 的任何其他 rule 行事。我最近读了很多 Slate Star Codex，所以，有兴趣了解对这个问题更详细的 analysis 的读者，我会推荐他们去读 [Scott Alexander](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/02/if-its-worth-doing-its-worth-doing-with-made-up-statistics/)。

## 但还有 deontology/virtue ethics 呢！

我们从这些评论中能学到的最重要的事情，可能是许多人根本就不是 consequentialists——这甚至不是因为他们像 Calicles 那样持有 philosophical objections，而是因为大多数人成长过程中接触的 default morality 并不是 consequentialism。

> 我对 hedge funds 一无所知，但那通常不都是 unethical 的吗？？ends 并不能 justify means。—**GerriM**

> 噢，拜托。拯救世界是 Jesus 的职责。在 Wall Street 工作只会让你成为 financial industry employee，而不是什么救世主。如果你想为世界做些好事，那就像个男人一样行事。还记得 manhood 吗？还记得 value 吗？还记得不去 speculating 和 gambling，而是 building 和 constructing 吗？别再用 fake math 去 ripping people off 了。实话实说吧。

> 让一个男人焕发阳光般光彩的，不是 money，而是 merit。

> 想明白吧，孩子。—**agx48**

> Trigg 的 mistake 就在于 dicounting 那个让他的钱能够发挥作用的 human element。没有 human element，钱就不能 make a difference。真是个任性的孩子。—**Frazil**

我认为，这可能是我们最需要学会更好地回答的 objection——不仅是为了让人们接受 earning to give，也是为了更广泛地 raise the sanity waterline。我希望在以后的文章中进一步谈谈，这些评论者究竟是怎么回事。但关键在于，让 consequentialism 对人们而言更加 viscerally appealing，是一件非常重要的事。

## 结论（Conclusion）

本文只是想作为回应主流社会对 earning to give 的 views 这一过程中的一步。对于如何让 effective-altruism memes 更能 resilient 地应对这类 objections，我目前还没有太多想法。我主要只是想做些基础工作，对最常见的 objections 进行 categorizing，好让比我更有技巧的 rhetoricians 想出如何 spread 能够回答它们的 memes。

## 原文

Recently the Washington Post’s _Wonkblog_ had some in-depth [coverage](http://www.washingtonpost.com/blogs/wonkblog/wp/2013/05/31/join-wall-street-save-the-world/?tid=ts_carousel) of a bunch of people who are earning to give.

It’s really exciting to see effective altruism’s ideas hitting the mainstream. This article in particular also has a very interesting new feature: a large comments section full of people who don’t already agree with us.

The level of discourse there is pretty low, but if we want the ideas behind effective altruism to spread thoroughly, they have to be able to survive in that kind of environment. So I decided it might be instructive to hold my nose, read the comments section and try to draw lessons about how we can refine our ideas for a wider audience.

Reading through the negative responses, there are roughly five principal components of the objections. Some combination of these components accounted for all the attacks against earning to give that I saw (I read through the whole thread up to 12 PM on June 1st, although my eyes may have glazed over a couple of times). So here are the five main strains:

## You’re supporting the system!

One common response is that it’s still not OK to participate in the Wall Street “system”.

> If you make commodities more expensive through trading and excessive speculation, than donate your a portion of your profits to starving people, it’s a bit disingenuous, because you are part of the reason that the price of their food is beyond their reach. —**mhdrgdajveeah**

> Nonsense. Wall Street is ruining this country. Dirty, rotten, greedy scum. All of them. So what if they give away some of their money. How about the hard working people who have been ruined by Wall Street? —**dubhlaoich**

> The problem you run into –

> If you are making the money by financing a company that is doing more harm than you can possibly do good from your profit.

> For instance a company may want to exploit emerging markets for tobacco products. —**Mark in Colorado**

These kind of retorts are why it’s _very important_ to mention [replaceability](http://80000hours.org/blog/35-the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1): that if you don’t work in an unethical industry, someone else ~(who’s probably more unethical)~[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/etg-objections/#fn:1) will take your place. For a lot of people, after they hear the replaceability argument, it seems so obvious in retrospect that they don’t mention it except as a counter-argument when they realize that other people haven’t figured it out.

But in fact, it seems replaceability (and more generally, thinking about marginal, rather than total, effects) is highly non-obvious to most people. So I think we need to talk about replaceability a lot more, especially in mainstream sources.

## What about big problem _x_?

This type of comment complains that those who are earning to give should be working on some piece of a bigger problem. In this case the main target is “fixing the system”:

> I suppose that’s noble to play against the greedy at their own game, but I’d rather see the ‘game’ simply not exist. Or how about simply assessing a fair tax rate on trades, to support our government’s efforts to eradicate malaria, among many other things? —**naomi94112**

> and, just what ecocide is being supported or expanded by the companies whose stocks are being blindly manipulated for profit? Wouldn’t it be better to attempt to truly change the world by changing US policy toward the environment, for example? —**getsmart4**

> We need the smart, dedicated people who want to do the right thing to put their energies and talents directly into reforming a system that is dragging people down, or at least not enabling it. —**jgunne**

We need to emphasize more that _we care about these problems, we just can’t do much about them._ Take financial reform. The financial industry currently records profits of about $120 billion a year ([source](http://blogs.reuters.com/felix-salmon/2011/03/30/chart-of-the-day-us-financial-profits/)). If proposed reforms halve those profits, the industry should be willing to spend up to $60 billion a year on lobbying to prevent such reform, which means we’d need to muster the same kind of resources to pass such reform. That’s about the total estimated cost of curing malaria by 2020 ([source](http://www.rbm.who.int/gmap/2-5.html)), every year. So just because something is a big problem, doesn’t mean it’s the best problem to work on.

## You should attack the causes, not the symptoms.

Another common argument is that solving disease is curing a symptom, not a cause.

> I’ve nothing against charity, but the belief that this sort of charity will “save the world” is naive and ignores the structural conditions that perpetuate poverty. So long as property and wealth continues to be concentrated in a small number of hands — Wall Street being emblematic of this — there will continue to be those deprived of the basic necessities of life. —**GeorgeStevens**

> I do find this talk about saving lives curiously bloodless. It’s a little like pro-lifers forcing women to give birth to their fetuses, only for them (the pro-lifers) to walk away from the resulting infant and call back to them, “You’re on your own now!” These do-gooders are just kicking the can a little further down the road. —**barnesgene**

I don’t know too much about development economics, but I think there’s a fairly clear response here: it’s not a cause/symptom relationship, it’s a vicious cycle. Poor countries have no health infrstructure, so they have high disease burd en, so they’re economically disadvantaged, so they can’t build health infrastructure. Attacking any portion of the loop is helpful, and disease happens to be the easiest to solve from outside.

## But we can’t ever _know_ how much good we’re doing!

The trend that is most annoying to me personally is the insistence that since we don’t know things for sure, we shouldn’t do anything.

> Is it really ethical (or smart) to fund mosquito nets in lieu of a seeing eye dog on the premise that thousands of nets can be bought for one dog? Can they really know per-emptively \[sic\] the value will come from the distribution of those nets versus the placement of one service dog? And if they do know, what are the criteria that they use to justify their decision? Is it reasonable to assume that mosquito netting will make a contribution to African society in way that one seeing eye dog cannot make too somebody in this country? —**cleverrevolution**

> A lot of the comments stem from the ultimate problem with the sort of Utilitarian arguments Singer presents. Because of the incredible complexity of the world and the actions of our consequences, attempts to treat the maximization of minimal global goods often end in absurdity or obscurity. Which action, given the infinitude of unforeseeable consequences of any action, will maximize goods? Unfortunately, much more difficult to say than just ‘save this girl!’ Then, how far do you go? The best objection I’ve seen in this vane is Parfit’s repugnant conclusion. For those interested: [http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/repugnant-conclu…](http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/repugnant-conclusion/) —**Calicles**

It’s not an accident that the only objecter actually to engage with philosophy fell under this category: the counter-argument is a bit more nuanced and difficult to explain. Unfortunately, I have no idea how to boil it down into a form that can survive and replicate in a comment thread—though I think it’s pretty important that we figure something out.

Anyway, the basic form of the counter-argument is that, sure, we don’t know what the end result will be, but we can make a really good guess. Good enough that using such estimates gets way, way better results than doing what feels intuitively right, or whatever other rule you’re picking actions by. I’ve been reading a lot of Slate Star Codex recently, so I’ll refer interested readers to [Scott Alexander](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/02/if-its-worth-doing-its-worth-doing-with-made-up-statistics/) for a more detailed analysis of this problem.

## But deontology/virtue ethics!

Possibly the most important thing we can learn from these comments is that lots of people just aren’t consequentialists—not even because of philosophical objections like Calicles, but because the default morality that most people grow up with isn’t consequentialism.

> I don’t know anything about hedge funds but isn’t that generally unethical?? The ends do not justify the means. —**GerriM**

> Oh, please. Saving the world is Jesus’ job. Working on Wall Street makes you a financial industry employee, not a savior of anything. If you want to do the world some good, then act like a man. Remember manhood? Remember value? Remember not speculating and gambling, but building and constructing? Stop ripping people off with fake math. Call it like it is.

> It’s not the money, but the merit, that gives a man the glow of sunshine.

> Figure it out, kid. —**agx48**

> Trigg’s mistake is dicounting the human element that makes his money able to do something. Without the human element the money doesn’t make a difference. Such a petulant child. —**Frazil**

I think this might be the most important objection to get better at answering—not only to get people to accept earning to give, but to raise the sanity waterline more generally. I hope to touch more on what exactly is going on with these commenters in a future post. But the upshot is that making consequentialism more viscerally appealing to people is a really important thing to do.

## Conclusion

This is only intended to be one step in the process of responding to mainstream views of earning to give. I don’t have very many thoughts yet about how to make effective-altruism memes more resilient to objections like these. I mostly just want to do the legwork of categorizing the most common objections, so that more skilled rhetoricians than I can figure out how to spread memes that answer them.
