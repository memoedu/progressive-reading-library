# Nintil - Ad hoc explanations

---

## 正文

几天前，我写了[这篇相当长的](https://nintil.com/2018/01/16/this-review-is-not-about-reviewing-the-elephant-in-the-brain/)关于 The Elephant in the Brain 的评论，Robin Hanson 在[这里](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2018/01/a-long-review-of-elephant-in-the-brain.html)作了回复。看完这篇回复，我觉得我们的一致之处比我起初想的更多，这是件好事，但他的回复中仍有几点我不同意。

我先记下一件让我极其惊讶的事，尽管它只是讨论中的一个小点：

>  （请注意，我们的书从未使用“hypocrisy”。）

这只是 Hanson 顺带说的一句话。起初，我以为自己的记忆又出了问题（我承认自己的记性不好），因为我在评论中写到 hypocrisy 时，并没有实际核查这个词的使用情况。但随后，Overcoming Bias 上的一位评论者指出，书中确实[使用了它](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2018/01/a-long-review-of-elephant-in-the-brain.html#comment-3722576756)。几天后，Bryan Caplan 也提出了[同样的说法](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2018/01/elephant_in_the.html)——也许他们午饭时讨论了我的评论 :-)——但他指出，这个 concept 本身贯穿全书。它不仅贯穿全书，而且这个词在书中还出现了几次，与 hypocrite 一起用于这里所说的 sense（不计 references 或 quotes）。书中使用这个词并不是什么坏事，因为它讨论的恰恰就是这个问题，但 Bryan 和 Robin 说书中严格避免使用这个词，这让我困惑。也许他们原本想避免这个词，以免让 arguments 带上 moralising 的语气：hypocrisy 听起来像件坏事，但它不知怎么还是溜进了书里。毕竟，Hanson 写[人们是 hypocrites](https://www.google.co.uk/search?ei=KpRrWsP8J6KKgAaorqGwAQ&q=robin+hanson+hypocrisy&oq=robin+hanson+hypocrisy&gs_l=psy-ab.3..35i39k1.2360.2746.0.2987.5.5.0.0.0.0.155.272.0j2.2.0....0...1c.1.64.psy-ab..3.2.272....0.sSr74iEXj4U)已经写了很久。

接下来说些更切题的事情，

> 1.  其他 explanations 可以 account for 我们考虑的每一个 puzzling patterns。
> 2.  我们不应把 hidden purposes 称为“motives”，也不应把对它们的 purposeful ignorance 称为“self-deception”。

我的评论与这些说法相近，但并不完全相同：我说，是的，其他一些 explanations 可以 account for 这些 puzzling patterns；我也否认其中一些 puzzles 甚至存在。我先讨论第二点。

> 我以前说过，但请容我再说一次：我们在这本书中关注的是**那些重大的 puzzling patterns of behavior，它们并不 fit with 人们通常在最公开的场合中所说的 usual purposes。我们指出了其他 purposes，人们通过这些 behaviors 能更好地 achieve 这些 purposes。** $$...$$ 我们把这些 purposes 称为“motives”，并指出，人们似乎对自己的 behaviors achieve 这些 purposes 一事表现出令人怀疑的 unaware，尽管这些都是熟悉的 purposes，与 behavior 有着 simple connections。**这种有意造成的 lack of awareness，我们称为“self-deception”。** 我们没有澄清人们在多大程度上 lie，或在多大程度上对这些 motives 是 unconscious，也没有澄清 behaviors 在 particular circumstances 下有多大程度的 adaptive。这些情况因 culture、person 和 context 而有很大差异，而能把这么多内容放进一本书已经很不容易。**我们关注的是 distal 而非 proximate 的 causes。** $$...$$ Kel 提到，我们敬重的一位知名作者（Trivers）偏好一个与我们的 usage 相容的“self-deception” definition。**但 Kel 仍抱怨说，按照他偏好的 usage，我们的 claims 是 doubtful 的。他还说，即使他接受我们在这里的 usage，按照他对“aware”、“motive”和“selfish”等 terms 偏好的 usage，其他 claims 仍会是 doubtful 的。**（请注意，我们的书从未使用“hypocrisy”。）你看，他偏好的这些 terms 的 usage，依赖于关于 lying、awareness、consciousness 和 adaptiveness 的 distinctions。由于我们不作这些 distinctions，我们的 claims 就根本不可能正确使用这些 terms。因此我们就是错了，就是错了，承认错了吧！$$...$$ 当然，**我们尚未 proven 我们所 postulate 的 hidden motives 的 centrality**，尤其是如果你要求我们先 disprove 每一种 behavior pattern 的所有 possible ad hoc explanations。

上面引用的最后一段让我意识到，我开始写评论时作出的一个 assumption 是错的：我高估了作者对书中那些 claims 的信心。从这个角度看，在他们眼中，我这篇长达 21k 词的评论想必显得用力过猛。我原以为这些 claims 意在表达比实际更 solid 的结论，因为尽管作者承认书中会有错误，但它由一家大型 academic publisher 出版，加上我读到的一些我非常尊重的人的反馈，让我觉得，这本书确实提出了极其 solid、具有很高 probability of being true 的 claims，而不只是提出一个值得进一步 investigation 的有趣 theory。

我认为，前面几段 misrepresent 了我关于“conceptual confusion”所提出的观点。我个人认为，只要一个人清楚地 defines 自己的 terms，在那个特定的 argumentative framework 内接受它们，就没有什么问题，或者说几乎没有问题。

使用这些词或那些词，唯一的麻烦在于，如果说得不够清楚，它们可能让人对所讨论的话题产生 confusion。也许是我太迟钝，或理解得太字面化，但对我来说，那些说法不够清楚；不过，我绝不是想暗示作者故意让自己想说的话变得晦涩！

然而，我提出的那些观点，本来就是打算放在作者偏好的用词方式下讨论，以便交流。归根结底，我们脑中有一些想要传达的 meanings，并为此使用词语，但不同的人可能用相同的词来表达或暗示不同的事情。

例如，我说“adaptive self-deception”的 evidence 很少时，这个 claim 是在他们所讨论的 framework 内提出的；在其中，他们提出了大致这样的 claims：“brain 中存在不同的 modules，其中的 press secretary 就是你的 conscious awareness；有很多事情正在发生，而你并不 aware；你的 brain 向你 hides things，同时又依据 true information 行动。”

因此，很明显，作者在这里使用 self-deception，指的是我和 Pinker 认可的“two inconsistent representations of knowledge”这一 meaning。

而这个 meaning 恰好涵盖在 Trivers 的 usage 中，因此，我的 self-deception concept 被 subsumed 于 Trivers 的 concept。正如我在上一篇评论中所说，这完全没有问题。但这样一来，使用这个 concept 时就必须说清楚，因为这个词现在指向了不同的事情。按照我那篇评论之后的情况，cognitive bias 的 evidence 很多，而 adaptive SD 的 evidence 很少。因此，声称我们 engage in self-deception，就 collapses into 说我们的 cognition 是 biased 的。这就是我的观点。因此，如果我们所说的是“cognitive biases 是 pervasive 的”，那么我承认“self-deception 是 pervasive 的”。

说到这里，有人可能觉得我只是某种吹毛求疵的词语纳粹，但不是这样；我真正在意的只有 clarity，并且明确不考虑词语有什么 intrinsic meaning 之类的东西。

如果上面说得还不清楚，设想我们正在考虑 drinking 对 health 的 effects。我们发现，heavy drinking 会 worsens health。因此，我接受“Heavy drinking causes worse health”。这时有人说：“对我来说，drinking 是一个更 inclusive 的 concept，我们还必须把 drinking water 和 alcohol 都 include 在内。”这个人接着说：“Drinking（其 definition 是 drinking water and/or alcohol）causes worse health，但在 low-alcohol drinking samples 的 results 中存在一定的 heterogeneity。”我会接受这个 claim。但在这里，我觉得把 water 的 drinking 与 alcohol 的 drinking 分开 study 会更清楚。adaptive self-deception 和其他 biases 也是如此。

Hanson 回复中的第一点：

> 对于几乎任何 particular observed pattern，通常都可以 construct 一个 ad hoc explanation。问题就在这里：这太容易了。因此，我们给自己定了更高的标准，尝试在每个 area 提出**一个 single main purpose，它可以 explain 尽可能多的 behavior puzzles。不过，我们也提到了另外几个 plausible purposes。** 考虑这样一个 example：把 medicine 的 over-consumption explain 为对 optimal spending level 的 ignorance。这是 plausible 的，但如果我们所知道的仅仅是人们 ignorant，那么 predict under-consumption 就会与 predict over-consumption 一样容易。因此，要 explain over-consumption，我们就必须加入一个关于 mistake 的 typical direction 的 auxiliary assumption。我们还需要另一个 auxiliary assumption，来 explain 这种 mistake 跨越 time 和 space 的 strong correlation。每个 auxiliary assumption 都可能是 plausible 的，但它并不 obvious。因此，你作出的这类 assumptions 越多，你的 theory 在 prior probability 方面就越不利。毕竟，你必须把每个 assumptions 的 prior 进行 multiply together，才能得到那个 explains 世界的 total model 的 prior。相比之下，尽管为所有 puzzles 提供一个 single main explanation 通常也需要几个 auxiliary assumptions，但它需要的更少。这就是为什么，当一个 simpler explanation 同样 fits the data 时，Bayesian analysis 往往会 favor 它。

我同意 Robin，在 competing theories 之间进行 arbitering，Bayesian approach 是正确的方法；但我提到的 explanations 并不是 ad hoc，我认为它们依赖的是 general well established principles。比如，我的一个 assumptions 是“人们 care about 自己的 health”，或“人们 care about 自己的家人和朋友”（不只是想表现出来，而是真正在意！），或“human body 是一个极其 complex、很难从 first principles 来 understand 的 system”。这些 claims 或许并非以 probability one 为 true，但也很接近。此外，这些 claims 并不是新的：它们早已存在，我们也早已相信它们，我只是把它们 connecting 起来，并 drawing out 它们的 implications。相比之下，Hanson 的 theory 虽然确实雄心勃勃，却是一个新的 theory，会因其 novelty 而获得低得多的 initial prior probability。

再重述一次，这本书的 core thesis 是：“我们对自己 motives 的 key aspects 是 strategically blind 的”（而这 explains 了一些 puzzles）。这些 key aspects 与 selfishness 有关。

在整篇评论中，我对其中一些 puzzles 提出了 rejections（因此，如果这些 rejections 成立，它们就会成为这样的 examples：theory predicts 了某种并未 observed 的事情，从而 counting against 它）。例如，关于 body language，我 argued，我们对它的 workings 的 aware 程度远高于该章所说，而它没有被 taught formally，恰恰是因为我们对它有很好的 intuitive grasp。我并不是 claiming 一个人不能在这方面做得更好，而是说，对大多数实际用途而言，我们的 intuition 已经 suffices。类似地，关于 charity，作者 argued，人们说 charity 是为了 maximising good done，但如果真是如此，人们的 behave 就会不同（也就是说，他们会是 EAs）。相反，我说，人们在 donating money 时 aim for 的并不是这个。另一个是 status 与 health 之间由 stress 所 mediated 的 causal link。但正如我在上一篇文章中提到的，情况似乎也并非如此。

我还 identified 了至少一些书中没有提及的 cases：theory 会 predict 某种我们并未 observe 到的事情。如果我们用 healthcare 来 show that we care，难道我们不应该 observe 到 healthcare 被用作 gift 吗？这听起来像是 model 的一个隐约 plausible 的 implication，但它并不是 Hanson 会接受的 implication（我想）。

对于其他问题，我确实提出了 alternate explanation；我想在这里谈谈 medicine，因为考虑到涉及的 resources 数量，它可以说是那些弄清楚真相尤为重要的 topics 之一。

我对 medicine 的 explanation 涉及以下几点：

1.  人们 care about 自己的 health
2.  人们 care about 他人的 welfare，主要是朋友和家人
3.  human body 是一个 highly complex system
4.  human body 是少数已经存在数千年、而 humans 一直不得不应对的 complex systems 之一
5.  出于 ethical reasons，人们不愿对 human bodies 进行 do experiments（过去甚至因 religious beliefs 而不愿用 dead bodies 进行 do experiments）
6.  Good health 通常是 good life 的 prerequisite
7.  对于 complex systems，Trial-and-error learning 是 extremely hard 的
8.  没有现代的 tools of science，6\. 就更 harder 了
9.  Humans 有一种 tendency，会在没有 patterns 的地方 see patterns

我不认为这些是 ad-hoc，而是将它们视为 general truths，并非 specific to medicine；而且，我再次强调，我认为它们是 extremely plausible 的。

考虑 architecture 和 engineering：Cathedrals 或 roman engineering 与它们那个时代的 medicine 相比，都是顶尖的。为什么？因为与世界上的大多数事物一样，classical mechanics 或对它的 intuitive understanding 让事情变得容易。Classical mechanics 是 based on simple principles 的，而纵观 human history，我们遇到或建造的大多数 systems，仍然都可以用这些 simple principles 来 explainable。

对于 human body，有些事情似乎很容易：(许多种类的) Surgery、dentistry 或 ophtamology，通过 classical mechanics 更容易 understand。这些取得了最大的进展，但 medicine 的许多 aspects 并非如此：Cancer、heart diseases、infections 或 diabetes 很难通过这种方式 understand，因此人们花了更长时间才 understand 并 properly cure 它们。

这并没有阻止人们尝试。但考虑到我们往往会 heal naturally，任何 random treatment（herbs、magic rituals）似乎都能 cure 你。这些 anecdotes 随后成为 folk knowledge，而没有 science，这就是你能做到的最好程度。

我们的 expectations 对 perception 的 effect（对 pain 的 perception，以及 placebo effect），也使人们难以找到“really work”的 remedies，因为那些“并不 really work”的 remedies 实际上也 work！（意思是，它们确实 relieve pain，但并不是出于你以为的那个 reason）

我的 theory predicts 许多事情：在那些更像 mechanics 的 fields 中，你会看到更多 progress 和更好的 treatments；随着时间推移，medicine effectiveness 将 increase，而 supply 将 match demand。因此，我 predict，我们看到的所有 waste 都将随着 healthcare 的 knowledge improves 而 disappear——尤其是在由 out of pocket payments dominated、使 prices visible 的 healthcare systems 中，不过这些大概并不是大多数 healthcare systems——。而且，即使 general population 或 clinicians 的 subpopulation 并未 aware of“healthcare 是为了 show that we care”这个 theory，这也会发生。（我还 predict，他们不会 aware of 它）。在大多数 developed countries，Healthcare spending 占 GDP 的 % 将 go down。

然而，如果 healthcare——部分地——是为了 show that we care，而人们 subconsciously 想这么做，我们应该不会看到这种情况发生。Waste 将永远伴随我们，除非人们关注这个 theory，而我们开展某种巧妙的 Hansonian institutional design，以 cater to 那些 hidden motives。或者，也许仍然有人会认为，是的，长期而言 knowledge 将获胜，但考虑到当前的 lack of knowledge，用于 show that we care 的 healthcare 能 explains overconsumption。正如 Robin 所说：

> 考虑这个 example：将 medicine 的 over-consumption explaining 为对 optimal spending level 的 ignorance 所致。这是 plausible 的，**但如果我们所知道的仅仅是人们是 ignorant 的**，我们 predict under-consumption 就会与 predict over-consumption 一样容易。因此，要 explain over-consumption，我们必须添加一个关于 mistake 的 typical direction 的 auxiliary assumption。我们还需要另一个 auxiliary assumption，来 explain 这种 mistake 在 time 和 space 上的 strong correlation。

我想你会同意我，medicine 和 healthcare 比 classical mechanics 更 harder，也更 complex。这一点，加上上面的一些其他 claims，explain 了为什么我们在这里知道的没有在其他 fields 中那么多。但正如 Hanson 指出的，这并不能 explain 为什么是 over 而不是 underconsumption。

那么，我们应该去问那些为 patients 安排 treatment 的 clinicians，以及要求 treatment 的 patients。他们怎么说？首先，他们 aware 到所提供的 care 中有 ~20% 是 unnecessary 的，但仍会 recommend 它，以免因 malpractice 被起诉，也因为 patients 向他们施压，要求他们这样做。([Lyu et al, 2017](http://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0181970))。至少在英国、意大利、日本和澳大利亚，情况也类似([Ortashi et al. 2013](https://bmcmedethics.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1472-6939-14-42))。这个 motive 很可能超出了 economic issues：其中一篇 articles 指出，doctors 可能愿意 overtreat，以免不得不面对因未提供 treatment 而导致 patient 的 health worsening 所带来的 negative thoughts。

除此之外，physicians ([Hoffman & Del Mar, 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28097303)) 和 patients ([Hoffman & Del Mar., 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25531451)) 都会 overestimate on net treatment 有多好，并 underestimate treatment 可能带来的 harms。相反 direction 的 Errors 也很 prevalent，但占上风的是 optimistic bias。

如果我们沿着这些 papers 继续追查，再问为什么这两个 groups 都会 overestimate on net，最终就会找到一[组 cognitive biases](https://www.minnpost.com/second-opinion/2016/03/why-we-need-be-aware-therapeutic-illusion)：[illusion of control](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4488611/) 和 confirmation bias，而这两者都是在许多 domains 中被 observed 的 general phenomena，不只是在 healthcare 中。也许还有 complexity bias。

这两者大概也可以被 leveraged 来 explain finance 中的 puzzles：为什么人们支付如此高的 fees，却从 financial advisors 那里得到糟糕的 funds。它们也可能 explain 其他类似的事情，比如 dowsing（信不信由你，英国一些自来水公司的员工现在还这样做）、education（“看，这个学生因为我才大有成就，education 是 works 的”），或者 [nutrition science](https://www.vox.com/2016/1/14/10760622/nutrition-science-complicated)（这里与 medicine 的 parallels 更加显著）

（也许我应该写一本“cognitive biases 如何 rule the world”的书，而不是评论别人的书？或者至少写一篇像样的 paper，这样我也能成为 review 的对象？）

综合考虑，并 thinking bayesianly，哪种更 likely，是这些 biases，还是 hidden motives？当然，我们不必二选一，两者都可能存在，但我认为 evidence 的 weight 更倾向于支持这样的判断：我所说的因素远大于 hidden motives（在 healthcare 中）。

理想情况下，我们应该就此打赌：赌 $200，在 5（或者 7？10？）年后，evidence 对我的 explanation 的支持会比对基于 hidden motives 的 explanation 的支持更强（除非我们想将上述 biases 称为 hidden motives！但那样我们就 reject 了将 healthcare 视为 showing care 的说法……）。由于我认为在 even odds 下，这个 bet 是 biased in my favor 的（对 health 中 hidden motives 的 Research 可能需要时间，而 Hanson 关于这个问题的 paper 差不多二十年前就发表了，至今却很少有人跟进），如果我赢了，我拿 200；如果我输了，我付 400，或类似的数额。我提名 Bryan Caplan 和 [RCAFDM](https://twitter.com/RCAFDM) 担任 arbiters。

补记：我在 Hanson 的原帖下收到了一条简短回复。下面是我的回复，回复的是他对我的回复的回复，而我的回复回应的是他对我那篇书评的回复：

> 我相当确信，a) 很少有人 aware，即使是亲密的朋友，也会 use status motives 来 negotiate 一个 non-equal relative status；b) 大多数人会说，他们的 charity 的目的是 help others。（几乎从来没有人谈论“maximizing”任何东西。）

我说人们 aware of body language、无需 study 它，意思是我们知道一个人什么时候 happy 或 sad，什么时候有人 looks down，什么时候有人在 fidgeting with their hair，以及那意味着什么；知道 touch signals closeness，having the arms crossed 意味着什么，或者知道，在某种 hierarchy 中，looking into the eyes of a superior 比 looking into the eyes of an inferior 更 intimidating。我们或许无法 verbalise 为什么，也不会仅凭 intuition 就了解有关 body language 的一切，但我的 claim 是，我们知道的已经足够应付日常交往，因此不必专门 learn 它。如果与一组 peers 相比，body language awareness 的 training 对任何 success-related outcome 都有很大的 effect (Cohen's d>0.2)，我会感到惊讶。我试着找过这方面的 studies，但没有找到。

对于 charity，他们会这么说，而且他们说得没错。他们 help others，但不是任意 random others；只要继续问“为什么是这些 others，而不是那些 others”，他们就会 confirm。

> 各处的大多数 ad hoc explanations 都是 based on 合理且由来已久的 assumptions。但它们仍然需要 topic-specific auxiliary assumptions。例如，Kel 援引“biases”来 explain 为什么我们对 medicine 是 over- 而不是 under-consume：_再问为什么这两个 groups 都会 overestimate on net，最终就会找到一组 cognitive biases：illusion of control 和 confirmation bias，而这两者都是在许多 domains 中被 observed 的 general phenomena，不只是在 healthcare 中。_ 我们不可能 over-consume 所有东西。如果我们对 medicine 的 over-consume 是相对于其他东西而言的\_relative \_，那么我们就需要一个比同样 applies 于一切的 general bias 更 specific 的 reason。

是的：但这正是我为什么谈到 medicine 的 specifics：medicine 有一些我所描述的 peculiarities（financial advise、day trading、nutrition 等也一样）

> _我的 theory predicts 许多事情：……因此，我 predict，我们看到的所有 waste 都将随着 healthcare 的 knowledge improves 而 disappear……在大多数 developed countries，Healthcare spending 占 GDP 的 % 将 go down。……理想情况下，我们应该就此打赌：\$200 that in 5(or 7? 10?) years time that the evidence will favour my explanation more strongly than a hidden motives based one. (Unless we want to call the biases mentioned above as hidden motives!)_ The claim “evidence will favor biases over hidden motives” seems harder to judge than whether the % of GDP to medicine goes down. I’d bet $10K，按 even odds，赌未来十年，在全球 highest-income 的 1/4 nations 中，用于 medicine 的 GDP 的 % 会 goes up。[这里](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SH.XPD.TOTL.ZS)[有](http://www.oecd.org/els/health-systems/health-data.htm)一些相关的 datasets。

这省略得可真够多的！10 年的 time frame 是针对我提出的 bet，而不是针对 healthcare % 的 going down：我认为 research 的 progresing 比 social structures 更快：在 healthcare 中，RCTs 可能需要长达数十年才能对 practice 产生 significant effect。

## 原文

I wrote a few days ago[this rather lengthy](https://nintil.com/2018/01/16/this-review-is-not-about-reviewing-the-elephant-in-the-brain/) review of The Elephant in the Brain, and I got a reply from Robin Hanson [here](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2018/01/a-long-review-of-elephant-in-the-brain.html). After this reply, I think we are in agreement more than I initially thought, and that is a good thing, but there are still a few things I disagree with in his reply.

I will begin by noting down something that left me surprised to the utmost degree, even though it is a tiny discussion point:

>  (Note that our book never uses “hypocrisy”.)

It is just a comment in passing Hanson makes. Initially I thought that my memory had failed me (my memory is self-admittedly poor) yet again, as when I wrote the bit about hypocrisy in my review I hadn't literally checked the use of the word. But then a commenter on Overcoming Bias pointed out that the book does [use it](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2018/01/a-long-review-of-elephant-in-the-brain.html#comment-3722576756). Bryan Caplan also made a few days later [the same point](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2018/01/elephant_in_the.html) - perhaps they discussed my review over lunch :-) - but he notes that the concept itself pervades the book. Not only it pervades the book, but the word appears in the book (along with hypocrite) a couple of times used in the relevant sense (Discounting references or quotes). It is not a bad thing that the book uses the word, as it is talking precisely about it, but it puzzles me that Bryan and Robin say that the book scrupulously avoids the word. Maybe it was the intention to avoid the word to avoid giving the arguments a moralising tone: hypocrisy sounds like a bad thing, but somehow it slipped into the book. Hanson has been writing about [people being hypocrites](https://www.google.co.uk/search?ei=KpRrWsP8J6KKgAaorqGwAQ&q=robin+hanson+hypocrisy&oq=robin+hanson+hypocrisy&gs_l=psy-ab.3..35i39k1.2360.2746.0.2987.5.5.0.0.0.0.155.272.0j2.2.0....0...1c.1.64.psy-ab..3.2.272....0.sSr74iEXj4U) for a long time after all.

Moving on to more relevant stuff,

> 1.  Other explanations can account for each of puzzling patterns we consider.
> 2.  We shouldn’t call hidden purposes “motives”, nor purposeful ignorance of them “self-deception”.

My comments went along similar but not quite those lines: I said that yes, some other explanations can account for the puzzling patterns, and I also denied that some of the puzzles even existed. I'll discuss the second point first.

> I’ve said this before, but let me repeat: Our focus in this book is on **big puzzling patterns of behavior that don’t fit with the usual purposes people usually cite in the most public of forums. We point to other purposes that people better achieve via these behaviors.** \[...\] We call these purposes “motives” and note that people seem suspiciously unaware that their behaviors achieve them, even though these are familiar purposes with simple connections to behavior. **Such a lack of awareness, created on purpose, we call “self-deception.”** We do not clarify the degree to which people lie or are unconscious of these motives, nor the degree to which behaviors are adaptive in particular circumstances. These things vary greatly by culture, person, and context, and it was hard to fit as much as we did into one book. **We are focused on distal, not proximate, causes.** \[...\] Kel mentions that a well-known author we revere (Trivers) prefers a definition of “self-deception” compatible with our usage. **But Kel still complains that our claims are doubtful given his preferred usage. And even if he accepted our usage there, he says, other claims would remain doubtful given his preferred usage of terms like “aware”, “motive”, and “selfish”.** (Note that our book never uses “hypocrisy”.) His preferred usage of these terms, you see, relies on distinctions regarding lying, awareness, consciousness, and adaptiveness. As we don’t make those distinctions, our claims just can’t use those terms correctly. And thus we are wrong, just wrong, admit it wrong! \[...\] And of course **we haven’t proven the centrality of the hidden motives we postulate**, especially if you require that we first disprove all possible ad hoc explanations for each behavior pattern.

The last paragraph I cite above tells me I was wrong about an assumption I made when I started writing the review: I overestimated how confident the authors were in making the claims made in the book. Seen in this light, from their perspective my 21k words long review must have seemed like an overkill. I had the impression that the claims were meant to be more solid than they were, because even though the authors admitted that there would be mistakes made in the book, the fact that it is a book published by a major academic publisher, and the feedback I had read from people I highly respect made me think that the book really was making super-solid claims with high probability of being true, instead of merely proposing an interesting theory that deserves further investigation.

The previous paragraphs I think misrepresent the points I made regarding "conceptual confusion". I personally think that if one defines their terms clearly, there is little or no issue in accepting them within that particular argumentative framework.

The only trouble with using some words or others is that they can induce confusion about the topics one is discussing if one is not clear enough. I may be too thick or take things too literally, but for me it was not clear enough, though by no means I mean to imply that the authors deliberately seeked to obscure what they wanted to say!

The points I made however, were meant to be discussed within the author's preferred use of words, to facilitate conversation. Ultimately we have some meanings in our heads that we want to convey, and we use words for that, but different people may use the same words meaning or implying different things.

For example, when I said that there is little evidence of "adaptive self-deception", this claim was made within the framework they discuss where they make claims along the lines of "There are different modules in the brain, the press secretary of which is your conscious awareness, there are many things going on you are not aware of, and your brain hides things from you while acting on the true information".

So clearly the authors here are meaning self-deception in the "two inconsistent representations of knowledge" meaning that I and Pinker endorse.

Now, so it happens that this meaning is covered by Trivers' usage, so my concept of self-deception is subsumed by Trivers'. As I said in my previous review, this is all fine. But then one has to be clear when one uses that concept, because that word is addressing different things now. As it stands after my review, there is plenty of evidence of cognitive bias and little of adaptive SD. So claiming that we engage in self-deception collapses into saying that our cognition is biased. That was my point. I thus grant that "self-deception is pervasive" if by that we mean "cognitive biases are pervasive".

At this point one might think that I'm just being a punctilious word nazi of some sort, but it's not that, I am honestly caring only about clarity, and explicitly disregarding anything like an intrinsic meaning for a word.

If the above wasn't clear, imagine that we are considering the effects of drinking on health. It is found that heavy drinking worsens health. So I accept "Heavy drinking causes worse health". Someone comes along and says "For me drinking is a more inclusive concept, we have to include drinking water along with alcohol too". And this person continues: "Drinking (as defined by drinking water and/or alcohol) causes worse health, but there is some heterogeneity in the results in low-alcohol drinking samples". I would accept this claim. But here it seems to me that it is clearer to study the drinking of water and the drinking of alcohol separately. Same for adaptive self-deception and other biases.

The first point of Hanson's reply:

> One can usually construct an ad hoc explanation for most any particular observed pattern. That’s the problem; its too easy. That’s why we held ourselves to the higher standard of trying in each area to suggest **a single main purpose that could explain as many behavior puzzles as possible. Though we mention a few other plausible purposes.** Consider the example of explaining over-consumption of medicine as due to ignorance on the optimal spending level. This is plausible, but if all we knew is that people are ignorant, we would predict under-consumption just as easily as over-consumption. So to explain over-consumption we have to add in an auxiliary assumption about the typical direction of mistake. We also need another auxiliary assumption to explain the strong correlation of this mistake across time and space. Each auxiliary assumption may be plausible, but it isn’t obvious. So the more of them you make, the more your theory loses on prior probability grounds. After all, you must multiply together the prior on each of assumptions to get the prior on a total model that explains the world. In contrast, while a single main explanation for all puzzles also typically need a few auxiliary assumptions, it needs fewer. Which is why a Bayesian analysis tends to favor a simpler explanation when that fits the data as well.

I agree with Robin that the Bayesian approach to arbitering between competing theories is the right one, but the explanations I mentioned are not ad hoc, I see them as relying on general well established principles. For one, one of my assumptions is "People care about their health" or "People care about their family and friends" (Not merely that they want to show it, but that they truly do!) or "The human body is an extremely complex system hard to understand from first principles". These claims may not be true with probability one, but they get close. Furthermore these claims are not new: There were already there and we already believe them, I am just connecting them and drawing out their implications. In contrast, Hanson's theory, while indeed ambitious, is a new one that it would get a much lower initial prior probability because of its novelty.

The core thesis of the book, to restate it once more is that "we are strategically blind to key aspects of our motives" (and that this explains a few puzzles). These key aspects relate to selfishness.

Through my review I gave rejections of some of the puzzles (So if these rejections go trough, then these would be example where the theory predicts something that is not observed, counting against it). For exampe, with body language I argued that we are substantially more aware of its workings than the chapter says, and that the reason it is not taught formally is precisely that we have a good intuitive grasp of it. I'm not claiming one cannot get better at it, but that for most purposes and intents, our intuition suffices. Similarly, for charity the authors argued that people say charity is for maximising good done, but that if that were so, people would behave differently (i.e. they would be EAs). I said, in contrast, that this is not what people aim for when they are donating money. Another one is a causal link between status and health mediated by stress. But as I mentioned in my previous post, that doesn't seem to be the case either.

I also identified at least some cases where the theory would predict something that we do not observe and that was not mentioned in the book: Shouldn't we observe healthcare being used as a gift if we use it to show that we care? It sounds like a vaguely plausible implication of the model, yet it is not an implication that Hanson would accept (I think).

For others I did offer an alternate explanation, I want to mention medicine here as it is argueably one of the topics where getting it right is important given the amount of resources that are at stake.

My explanation for medicine involves the following points:

1.  People care about their health
2.  People care about others's welfare, mostly friends and family
3.  The human body is a highly complex system
4.  The human body is one of the few complex systems that has been around for millenia that humans have had to deal with
5.  People are reluctant to do experiments on human bodies for ethical reasons (Or even do experiments with dead bodies due to religious beliefs in the past)
6.  Good health is most usually a prerequisite for a good life
7.  Trial-and-error learning is extremely hard with complex systems
8.  6\. is even harder without the modern tools of science
9.  Humans have a tendency to see patterns where there are not

I don't see these ad ad-hoc, I see them as general truths, not specific to medicine, and I see them, again, as extremely plausible.

Consider architecture and engineering: Cathedrals or roman engineering were top notch compared to the medicine of their time. Why? Because like most things in the world, classical mechanics or an intuitive understanding thereof makes it easy. Classical mechanics is based on simple principles, and throughout human history most systems we have encountered or built were still explainable in terms of these simple principles.

With the human body, some things seem easy: (Many kinds of) Surgery, dentistry, or ophtamology are easier to understand through classical mechanics. These have advanced the most, but many aspects of medicine are not like that: Cancer, or heart diseases, or infections, or diabetes are hard to understand that way, and thus they took longer to understand and properly cure.

That didn't stop people from trying. But given that we tend to heal naturally, any random treatment (herbs, magic rituals) will seem to cure you. These anecdotes then become folk knowledge, and without science that's the best you can do.

The effect of our expectations on perception (of pain, the placebo effect) also makes it hard to come up with remedies that "really work", beause remedies that "do not really work" actually work! (Meaning that they do relieve pain, but not for the reason you think it does)

My theory predicts a bunch of things: That in the fields that are more like mechanics you will see more progress and better treatments, that over time medicine effectiveness will increase, and supply will match demand. I thus predict that all the waste we see will disappear as knowledge of healthcare improves -especially for healthcare systems dominated by out of pocket payments that make prices visible, but argueably these are not most healthcare systems-. And that this will happen even if the general population or the subpopulation of clinicians do not become aware of the "healthcare is to show that we care" theory. (I also predict that they won't become aware of it). Healthcare spending as a % of GDP will go down in most developed countries.

However, if healthcare is -in part- to show that we care, and people subconsciously want to do that, we shouldn't see this happening. Waste will accompany us forever unless people pay attention to the theory and we engage in some clever Hansonian institutional design to cater to those hidden motives. Or maybe one may still argue that yes, in the long run knowledge will triumph, but that given the current lack of knowledge, healthcare to show that we care explains overconsumption. As Robin said:

> Consider the example of explaining over-consumption of medicine as due to ignorance on the optimal spending level. This is plausible, **but if all we knew is that people are ignorant**, we would predict under-consumption just as easily as over-consumption. So to explain over-consumption we have to add in an auxiliary assumption about the typical direction of mistake. We also need another auxiliary assumption to explain the strong correlation of this mistake across time and space.

I assume you will agree with me that medicine and healthcare are harder and more complex than classical mechanics. This and some others of the claims above explain why we don't know as much as we do in other fields. But this does not explain, as Hanson points out, why over instead of underconsumption.

Well, we should ask the clinicians signing up patients for treatment, and the patients asking for it. Why do they say? One, that they are aware that ~20% of provided care is unnecessary, but that they recommend it anyway to avoid being sued for malpractice, and because patients pressured them into it. ([Lyu et al, 2017](http://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0181970)). This is similar in at least the UK, Italy, Japan, and Australia([Ortashi et al. 2013](https://bmcmedethics.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1472-6939-14-42)). This motive most likely extends beyond economic issues: one of the articles points out that doctors may be willing to overtreat to avoid having to deal with the negative thoughts associated with a patient's worsening of health because of unprovided treatment.

In addition to that, both physicians ([Hoffman & Del Mar, 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28097303)) and patients ([Hoffman & Del Mar., 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25531451)) overestimate on net how good treatment is and underestimate potential harms from treatment. Errors in the opposite direction also were prevalent, but an optimistic bias prevailed.

If we continue through this trail of papers and ask again why both groups overestimate on net we end up at a [set of cognitive biases](https://www.minnpost.com/second-opinion/2016/03/why-we-need-be-aware-therapeutic-illusion): [illusion of control](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4488611/) and confirmation bias, and these two are general phenomena that have been observed across many domains, not just healthcare. Perhaps also complexity bias.

These two can argueably also be leveraged to explain puzzles in finance: why people pay fees so high and get shitty funds from financial advisors. And they may also explain similar other things like dowsing, (believe it or not, some employees of UK water companies still do it), education ("See, this student went far because of me, education works"), or [nutrition science](https://www.vox.com/2016/1/14/10760622/nutrition-science-complicated) (here the parallels to medicine are more striking)

(Maybe I should write a "How cognitive biases rule the world" book instead of reviewing other people's? Or at least write a proper paper so that I can be at the receiving end of a review?)

What is more likely, these biases or hidden motives, all things considered, and thinking bayesianly? We don't have to choose, of course, it can be both, but I see the weight of the evidence as being more heavily on the side of what I say being much bigger than hidden motives (in healthcare).

Ideally we would want to bet on this: $200 that in 5(or 7? 10?) years time that the evidence will favour my explanation more strongly than a hidden motives based one (Unless we want to call the biases mentioned above as hidden motives! But then we reject healthcare as showing care...). As I think the bet is biased in my favor at even odds (Research on hidden motives in health may take its time, and Hanson's paper on the matter was published almost two decades ago and has gotten very little engagement), if I win I get 200, if I lose I pay 400 or something like that. I volunteer Bryan Caplan and [RCAFDM](https://twitter.com/RCAFDM) as arbiters.
