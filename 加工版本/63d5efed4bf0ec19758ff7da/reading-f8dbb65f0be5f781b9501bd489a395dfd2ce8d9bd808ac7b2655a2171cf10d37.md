# How much do our elite know about human progress?

---

## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_最初于 2016 年 12 月 28 日[发布在 Facebook 上](https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=165938533888688&id=100014176268390)。Facebook 只向已 logged in 的人显示这篇文章，所以这里提供的是一份副本，取自 Internet Archive 的 [2022 年 11 月 27 日存档](https://web.archive.org/web/20221127013651/https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=165938533888688&id=100014176268390)。_

过去几周，我一直在反复琢磨这个问题，起因是读到了 Hans Rosling 在 scientific journal Nature 上提供的一份 quiz 的 results（见 [http://www.nature.com/news/three-minutes-with-hans-rosling-will-change-your-mind-about-the-world-1.21143](http://www.nature.com/news/three-minutes-with-hans-rosling-will-change-your-mind-about-the-world-1.21143) ）。

这份 quiz 包含 8 个关于 state of the world 的 fundamental questions：涉及 poverty、life expectancy、wealth、population 等方面。全都是重大而重要的问题。

让我反复琢磨的是，quiz 的 respondents——我猜是 nature.com 的读者——表现远远 worse than chance。也就是说，如果他们只是 guessed their answers at random，整体表现反而会好得多（这些 questions 是 multiple choice）。在 8 个 questions 中，respondents 只有在 2 个上的表现 appreciably better than chance。在大多数 questions 上，他们的表现 worse than chance，有时甚至 much worse than chance。一只 at random 按按钮的黑猩猩，表现都会比 nature.com 的读者更好。

（顺便说一句，我不确定这些 response data 是否来自 nature.com 的读者。它们可能是另外的 data，也许来自 Rosling 的听众。如果是这样，就会 weakens 我下面的 argument。）

这类事情通常不会让我困扰。Media 很喜欢对那些显示 general population 缺乏 basic scientific knowledge 的 surveys 痛心疾首。这种事情不会让我警觉。我们所在的 society 中，大多数人都 specialize，因此，即使我们大多数人在一些 major areas 是 ignorant，也并不奇怪；collectively，我们仍然可以做得相当不错。

但 Rosling 的这份 data——也就是 Nature 的 survey——着实让我耿耿于怀。这份 survey 针对的 group（我想，我也是其中一员）似乎常常认为，自己对重大而重要的问题的 solutions 拥有 special knowledge——比如 climate change、energy、development 等问题。而我从 Rosling 的 data 中得出的认识是，这个 group 并不只是在某些 fundamental 方面对 state of the world 是 ignorant。他们实际上是 anti-informed。

那么，这为什么重要？

在 Twitter 上，我经常看到 Rosling、Max Roser、Steven Pinker 和 Dina Pomeranz 等人发布 graphs，展示 state of the world 的 changes。这些 graphs 往往极其 positive，比如 Roser 那些出色的 graphs，展示了过去 200 年间 poverty、education、literacy 等方面的情况：

（见下面的图片，或：[https://twitter.com/MaxCRoser/status/811587302065602560?lang=en](https://twitter.com/MaxCRoser/status/811587302065602560?lang=en) ）

读到这些 tweets 下的回复，实在令人震惊。很多人一听到世界上的某些事情正在变好，就怒不可遏。许多回复归根结底就是：“不，不，不可能是真的”，或者“我敢打赌，\[无关的事情\]正在变糟，你为什么不关注那个呢，你这个 capitalist conspiracy 的工具。”

当然，尽管这些回复很烦人，也 illustrate 了某种 wilful ignorance，但它们其实无关紧要。更让我困扰的是，一些最常见的回复是下面这些话的变体：“这不重要，climate change 比你所有的 graphs 都重要”；“你的 climate graphs 在哪里？”；“Nukes 会把我们全都杀死”；等等。

这类评论似乎是 wrongheaded，而原因更值得探究。

首先，欣赏 Roser 的 graphs（以及类似的 graphs），并不意味着不 acknowledge climate change、nuclear security 及其他问题。例如，Roser 就曾反复 acknowledged，climate 方面的 challenges 是 huge 而且 critical 的。

但我认为，更重要的是，像 Roser 那样的 graphs 并不会偶然出现。它们是非凡的 human achievements——是卓越的 technical、social 和 organizational invention 的 outcome。如果你不了解这些 facts 的细节，或者你低估它们的重要性，那么你就不可能指望深入 understand 背后的 technical、social 和 organizational invention。而在我看来，这种 understanding 很可能对 solving 像 climate 这样的问题至关重要。

换句话说，那些 anti-Pollyannas，包括我们当中许多自认为掌握了“solutions”的 intellectual elite，实际上已经使自己无法再去 understanding 许多最重要的 human progress 的 basis。

solution 是什么？我不确定。但这条思路让我更加欣赏 Roser、Rosling 等人所做的工作，也让我开始思考，怎样才能使这些工作 scaled up，并更广泛地 incorporated 到我们的 institutions 中。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_Originally [posted to Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=165938533888688&id=100014176268390) on December 28, 2016. Facebook shows it only to people who are logged in, so this is a copy, taken from the Internet Archive's [capture of November 27, 2022](https://web.archive.org/web/20221127013651/https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=165938533888688&id=100014176268390)._

That's a question I've been stewing about for the past few weeks, ever since reading the results from a quiz (at [http://www.nature.com/news/three-minutes-with-hans-rosling-will-change-your-mind-about-the-world-1.21143](http://www.nature.com/news/three-minutes-with-hans-rosling-will-change-your-mind-about-the-world-1.21143) ) in the scientific journal Nature, from Hans Rosling.

The quiz contains 8 fundamental questions about the state of the world: questions about poverty, life expectancy, wealth, population, and so on. All big, important questions.

What has me stewing is that respondents to the quiz - I presume, nature.com's readers - do far worse than chance. That is, they would have done much better overall if they'd simply guessed their answers at random (the questions are multiple choice). Only on 2 of 8 questions do respondents do appreciably better than chance. On most questions they do worse than chance, sometimes much worse than chance. A chimpanzee pushing buttons at random would have done better than nature.com's readers.

(By the way, I'm not certain the response data is from nature.com's readers. It may be separate data, perhaps from Rosling's audiences. If that's the case, it weakens my argument below.)

I'm not usually bothered by this kind of thing. Media love to bemoan surveys showing lack of basic scientific knowledge among the general population. That kind of thing doesn't alarm me. We're a society in which most people specialize, and it's not surprising if most of us are ignorant in major areas; collectively we can still do pretty well.

But this data from Rosling - the Nature survey - really got under my skin. It's a survey of a group (one I'm part of, I guess) that often seems to think it has special knowledge of solutions to big, important problems - things like climate change, energy, development, and so on. And what I take from Rosling's data is that that group isn't just ignorant about the state of the world in some fundamental ways. They're actually anti-informed.

So, why does this matter?

On Twitter, I regularly see people like Rosling, Max Roser, Steven Pinker, and Dina Pomeranz post graphs showing changes in the state of the world. Often, those graphs are extremely positive, like Roser's wonderful graphs on poverty, education, literacy etc over the last 200 years:

(See the images below, or: [https://twitter.com/MaxCRoser/status/811587302065602560?lang=en](https://twitter.com/MaxCRoser/status/811587302065602560?lang=en) )

It is absolutely astonishing to read the responses to such tweets. Many people are furious at the idea that some things in the world are getting better. Many responses boil down to "Nah, nah, can't be true", or "I'll bet \[irrelevant thing\] is getting worse, why don't you focus on that, you tool of the capitalist conspiracy."

Of course, while those responses are irritating, & illustrate a certain kind of wilful ignorance, they don't really much matter. What bothers me more is that some of the most common responses are variants on "It doesn't matter, climate change is more important than all your graphs"; "Where are your climate graphs?"; "Nukes are going to kill us all"; etc.

This type of comment seems wrongheaded for more interesting reasons.

First, appreciating Roser's (and similar) graphs does not mean failing to acknowledge climate change, nuclear security, and other problems. Roser, for instance, has repeatedly acknowledged that the challenges of climate are huge and critical.

But I think the more significant thing is that graphs like Roser's don't happen by accident. They are extraordinary human achievements - the outcome of remarkable technical, social and organizational invention. If you don't know of these facts, in detail, or if you underplay their importance, then you cannot hope to understand the underlying technical, social, and organizational invention in any depth. And it seems to me that that kind of understanding may well be crucial to solving problems like climate, etc.

To put it another way, the anti-Pollyannas, including much of our intellectual elite who think they have "the solutions", have actually cut themselves off from understanding the basis for much of the most important human progress.

What's the solution? I'm not sure. But this line of thinking is deepening my appreciation for the work done by people such as Roser, Rosling et al. And it's making me think about how it can be scaled up & incorporated more broadly into our institutions.
