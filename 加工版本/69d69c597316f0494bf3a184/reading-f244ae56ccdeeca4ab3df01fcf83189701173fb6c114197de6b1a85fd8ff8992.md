# 内部专业知识的价值｜The value of in-house expertise

---

## 正文

这篇文章的另一个标题或许可以是：“Twitter 居然有 kernel team！？”到现在，这种惊讶的感叹我已经听过太多次，早已数不清有多少人对我这样说过（我猜超过十次，但不到一百次）。如果看看那些规模与 Twitter 相差几个二倍因子以内的时髦公司（无论按 market cap 还是 engineers 的数量衡量），它们大多没有类似的 expertise，这往往是 path dependence 的结果——因为它们是在 cloud 中“长大”的，所以不需要像 on prem company 那样依靠 kernel expertise 来维持运转。因此，那些职业生涯一直在更年轻、更时髦的公司度过的人，会惊讶于 Twitter 有 kernel team，从社会背景来看是可以理解的，但我不认为这种惊讶有什么 technical reason。

不管有没有 kernel expertise，Twitter 这种规模的公司都会经常遇到 kernel issues，从 major production incidents 到 papercuts，不一而足。如果没有 kernel team 或同等的 expertise，公司也能勉强应付这些 issues，但既会遇到不必要的问题，也会花费本不必那么长的时间来 mitigate incidents。作为 critical production incident 的一个例子，仅仅因为它已经公开成文，我会引用[这篇文章](https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2020/hunting-a-linux-kernel-bug)，其中平淡地写道：

> 去年早些时候，我们 identified 了一处 firewall misconfiguration，它意外地 dropped 了大部分 network traffic。我们原本以为 resetting 这个 firewall configuration 就能 fix 这个 issue，但 resetting 这个 firewall configuration 却 exposed 了一个 kernel bug

这段话暗示但没有明说的是，这次 firewall misconfiguration 是我在 Twitter 期间发生过的最 severe 的 incident，而且我认为，它实际上也是 Twitter 自 2013 年左右以来最 severe 的 outage。作为一家公司，即使没有 kernel team，或者另一个具备 deep Linux expertise 的 team，我们仍然能够 mitigate 这个 issue，但要弄清最初的 fix 为什么没有奏效，就会花更长时间，而这正是你在 debugging 一次 serious outage 时最不愿遇到的情况。kernel team 的同事已经熟悉了迅速弄清最初的 fix 为什么没有奏效所必需的各种 diagnostic tools 和 debugging techniques，而在某些 peer companies，这些并不是大家普遍掌握的知识（我询问过多家 similar-scale peer companies 的同事，看他们是否认为公司里至少有一个人具备 quickly debug 这个 bug 所必需的知识，许多公司的回答是否定的）。

在各个领域拥有 in-house expertise 的另一个原因是，它们很容易赚回自身成本，这是[一个一般性论点的 special case：large companies 应当比大多数人预想的更大，因为微小的 percentage gains 按 absolute dollars 计算也价值不菲](https://danluu.com/sounds-easy/)。如果在 kernel team 这类 specialist team 的存续期间，仅有一个人找到了某种能 persistently reduced [TCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_cost_of_ownership) 达 0.5% 的办法，就足以永久支付这个 team 的成本，而 Twitter 的 kernel team 已经找到了许多这样的 changes。除了有时能产生这类影响的 [kernel patches](https://patchwork.ozlabs.org/project/netdev/list/?submitter=211&state=*&archive=both&param=4&page=1)，人们还会发现能产生同类影响的 configuration issues 等等。

到目前为止，我只谈了 kernel team，因为它仅仅是存在这一点，就最经常让人感到惊讶；但当人们发现 Twitter 还有一批曾在 Sun 从事 HotSpot 工作的 JVM 人员，比如 Ramki Ramakrishna、Tony Printezis 和 John Coomes 时，我也会得到类似的反应。人们想知道，一家 social media company 为什么需要如此 deep JVM expertise。与 kernel team 的情况一样，我们这种规模、使用 JVM 的公司会遇到古怪的 issues 和 JVM bugs，有具备 deep expertise 的人来 debug 这些 issues 很有帮助。而且，与 kernel team 的情况一样，针对 JVM 的单项 optimizations 就能永久支付这个 team 的成本。一个具体的例子是 [Flavio Brasil 的这个 patch，它 virtualizes compare and swap calls](https://github.com/oracle/graal/pull/636)。

这里的背景是，Twitter 大量使用 Scala。尽管有许多相反的说法，Scala 使用的 memory 更多，而且比 Java significantly slower；如果你 use Scala at scale，这会带来显著的 cost，以至于开展 optimization 工作，reduce idiomatic Scala 与 idiomatic Java 之间的 performance gap，是值得的。

在这个 patch 之前，如果你 profiled 我们的 Scala code，就会看到大量得不合理的时间被花在 Future/Promise 中，其中甚至包括一些你可能会天真地以为 compiler 会 optimize 掉这些工作的情况。原因之一是，Futures 使用一种对 JVM optimization 而言是 opaque 的 [compare-and-swap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compare-and-swap)（CAS）operation。上面链接的 patch 会在 Future 不 escape 这个 method 的 scope 时 avoids CAS operations。[这个配套的 patch](https://github.com/twitter/util/commit/3245a8e1a98bd5eb308f366678528879d7140f5e) 则会在某些不太适合 compiler optimization 的地方 removes CAS operations。这两个 patches 共同将使用 idiomatic Scala 的典型 major Twitter services 的 cost reduced 了 5% 到 15%，足以将 JVM team 的永久成本支付许多遍，而这甚至还不是 Flavio 那一年找到的最大收益。

我不打算逐个 team 细说哪些 teams 能将自身成本赚回许多遍，因为这样的 teams 实在太多了，即使把范围限定为“人们会惊讶于 Twitter 居然拥有的 teams”，也是如此。

一个相关的话题是，人们如何谈论“buy vs. build”的讨论。我见过不少讨论，有人主张“buy”，因为这样就不再需要该领域的 expertise。这可能成立，但我见到这种主张的次数，远多于它实际成立的次数。我认为 distributed tracing 就是这种说法往往不成立的一个例子。[我们之前已经看过 Twitter 从 tracing 中获取 value 的一些方式](https://danluu.com/tracing-analytics/)，这些都源于 Rebecca Isaacs 所确立的愿景。另一方面，当我与规模相近的 peer companies 的人交谈时，他们中的大多数都没有（还没有？）成功从 distributed tracing 中获得显著的 value。这种情况如此普遍，以至于我每年不止一次看到广为传播的 Twitter thread，讲 distributed tracing 如何毫无用处。尽管我们选择了更 expensive 的“build”方案，但仅凭我随手想到的，就有多种 tracing 的用途，已经带来了 building out tracing 的 cost 的 10x 到 100x 的回报；相比之下，许多选择了更便宜的“buy”方案的公司，其人员却经常抱怨 tracing 不值得。

巧的是，我刚刚还与 Pam Wolf 谈过这个话题；她是一位 civil engineering professor，有在多个洲的（civil engineering）industry 工作的经验，她也有一个相关的看法。对于 large scale systems（projects），每一个不由你自己公司处理的领域，都需要一位 in-house expert（owner's-side engineer）。虽然 technically 可以再雇另一家公司来充当 expert，但这比培养或聘用 in-house expertise 更 expensive，而且从长远看，也更 risky。这也与我作为 electrical engineer 工作的经历很相似：那些把 functions outsource 给其他公司、却不保留 in-house expert 的 orgs，会付出非常高的 cost，而且不只是金钱上的。它们不仅 costs 很高，还经常在 long delays 之后 ship sub-par designs。“Buying”可以、也经常确实能 reduce 所需 expertise 的数量，但它往往并不能消除对 expertise 的需要。

这与另一个经常被提出的常见 abstract argument 有关：公司应当专注于“自己的 comparative advantage 领域”、“最重要的 problems”或“core business need”，并将其他一切 outsource。我们已经看过几个这种说法不成立的例子，因为达到足够大的 scale 后，无论某件事是否是业务的 core，拥有 in-house expertise 都比没有更 profitable（你可以主张，所有被 moved in-house 的事情都是业务的 core，但这样一来，coreness 这个概念就毫无用处了）。这条 abstract advice 过于简单化的另一个原因是，企业在一定程度上可以任意选择自己的 comparative advantage 是什么。一个大型的[1](https://danluu.com/in-house/#fn:L)例子，就是 Apple 将 CPU design 转为 in-house。自从以 $278M 收购 PA Semi（此前是 SiByte 的 team，再之前是 DEC 的一个 team）以来，Apple 一直在生产[在 phone 和 laptop power envelope 内以相当大的优势领先的最佳 chips](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1433297089866383364)。但在这次收购之前，Apple 并没有什么特质使这次收购成为必然，也没有什么特质使 CPU design 成为 Apple 固有的 comparative advantage。但如果一家公司可以选择一个领域，并把它变成自己的 comparative advantage 领域，那么说这家公司应当选择专注于自己的 comparative advantage(s)，就算不上多么有帮助的建议。

$278M is a lot of money in absolute terms, but as a fraction of Apple's resources, that was tiny and much smaller companies also have the capability to do cutting edge work by devoting a small fraction of their resources to it, e.g., Twitter, for a cost that any $100M company 所能负担得起的，[created 了 novel cache algorithms 和 data structures](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1381687511362138113)，并且正在开展其他 cutting edge cache 工作。拥有出色的 [cache infra](https://danluu.com/cache-incidents/) 对 Twitter 的业务而言，并不比创造出色的 CPU 对 Apple 的业务而言更为 core，但它确实是 Twitter 可以利用的一根杠杆，能让它赚到原本赚不到的更多的钱。

对于 small companies，为公司涉及的一切都配备 in-house experts 并不合理，但公司不必发展到特别大，在 operating system、language runtime 以及其他人们通常认为相当 specialized 的 components 上拥有 in-house expertise，就已经开始变得合理。回顾 Twitter 的历史，Yao Yue 曾指出，在 Twitter 早期她从事 cache 工作时（当时我们有 ~100 位 engineers），她会经常向 kernel team 求助，以 debugging production incidents；而在某些情况下，如果没有 kernel team 的帮助，debugging 所花的时间很可能轻易就会多出 10x。Social media companies 往往在 per-user 和 per-dollar 的基础上具有相对高的 scale，因此，并不是每家公司在拥有 100 位 engineers 时，都需要同样的 expertise；但即使是只有 100 位 engineers 的 startup，也会在其他一些并非显然属于 core business needs 的领域，从 expertise 中获得回报。

感谢 Ben Kuhn、Yao Yue、Pam Wolf、John Hergenroeder、Julien Kirch、Tom Brearley 和 Kevin Burke 提供的意见、更正和讨论。

## 原文

An alternate title for this post might be, "Twitter has a kernel team!?". At this point, I've heard that surprised exclamation enough that I've lost count of the number times that's been said to me (I'd guess that it's more than ten but less than a hundred). If we look at trendy companies that are within a couple factors of two in size of Twitter (in terms of either market cap or number of engineers), they mostly don't have similar expertise, often as a result of path dependence — because they "grew up" in the cloud, they didn't need kernel expertise to keep the lights on the way an on prem company does. While that makes it socially understandable that people who've spent their career at younger, trendier, companies, are surprised by Twitter having a kernel team, I don't think there's a technical reason for the surprise.

Whether or not it has kernel expertise, a company Twitter's size is going to regularly run into kernel issues, from major production incidents to papercuts. Without a kernel team or the equivalent expertise, the company will muddle through the issues, running into unnecessary problems as well as taking an unnecessarily long time to mitigate incidents. As an example of a critical production incident, just because it's already been written up publicly, I'll cite [this post](https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2020/hunting-a-linux-kernel-bug), which dryly notes:

> Earlier last year, we identified a firewall misconfiguration which accidentally dropped most network traffic. We expected resetting the firewall configuration to fix the issue, but resetting the firewall configuration exposed a kernel bug

What this implies but doesn't explicitly say is that this firewall misconfiguration was the most severe incident that's occured during my time at Twitter and I believe it's actually the most severe outage that Twitter has had since 2013 or so. As a company, we would've still been able to mitigate the issue without a kernel team or another team with deep Linux expertise, but it would've taken longer to understand why the initial fix didn't work, which is the last thing you want when you're debugging a serious outage. Folks on the kernel team were already familiar with the various diagnostic tools and debugging techniques necessary to quickly understand why the initial fix didn't work, which is not common knowledge at some peer companies (I polled folks at a number of similar-scale peer companies to see if they thought they had at least one person with the knowledge necessary to quickly debug the bug and the answer was no at many companies).

Another reason to have in-house expertise in various areas is that they easily pay for themselves, which is a special case of [the generic argument that large companies should be larger than most people expect because tiny percentage gains are worth a large amount in absolute dollars](https://danluu.com/sounds-easy/). If, in the lifetime of the specialist team like the kernel team, a single person found something that persistently reduced [TCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_cost_of_ownership) by 0.5%, that would pay for the team in perpetuity, and Twitter’s kernel team has found many such changes. In addition to [kernel patches](https://patchwork.ozlabs.org/project/netdev/list/?submitter=211&state=*&archive=both&param=4&page=1) that sometimes have that kind of impact, people will also find configuration issues, etc., that have that kind of impact.

So far, I've only talked about the kernel team because that's the one that most frequently elicits surprise from folks for merely existing, but I get similar reactions when people find out that Twitter has a bunch of ex-Sun JVM folks who worked on HotSpot, like Ramki Ramakrishna, Tony Printezis, and John Coomes. People wonder why a social media company would need such deep JVM expertise. As with the kernel team, companies our size that use the JVM run into weird issues and JVM bugs and it's helpful to have people with deep expertise to debug those kinds of issues. And, as with the kernel team, individual optimizations to the JVM can pay for the team in perpetuity. A concrete example is [this patch by Flavio Brasil, which virtualizes compare and swap calls](https://github.com/oracle/graal/pull/636).

The context for this is that Twitter uses a lot of Scala. Despite a lot of claims otherwise, Scala uses more memory and is significantly slower than Java, which has a significant cost if you use Scala at scale, enough that it makes sense to do optimization work to reduce the performance gap between idiomatic Scala and idiomatic Java.

Before the patch, if you profiled our Scala code, you would've seen an unreasonably large amount of time spent in Future/Promise, including in cases where you might naively expect that the compiler would optimize the work away. One reason for this is that Futures use a [compare-and-swap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compare-and-swap) (CAS) operation that's opaque to JVM optimization. The patch linked above avoids CAS operations when the Future doesn't escape the scope of the method. [This companion patch](https://github.com/twitter/util/commit/3245a8e1a98bd5eb308f366678528879d7140f5e) removes CAS operations in some places that are less amenable to compiler optimization. The two patches combined reduced the cost of typical major Twitter services using idiomatic Scala by 5% to 15%, paying for the JVM team in perpetuity many times over and that wasn't even the biggest win Flavio found that year.

I'm not going to do a team-by-team breakdown of teams that pay for themselves many times over because there are so many of them, even if I limit the scope to "teams that people are surprised that Twitter has".

A related topic is how people talk about "buy vs. build" discussions. I've seen a number of discussions where someone has argued for "buy" because that would obviate the need for expertise in the area. This can be true, but I've seen this argued for much more often than it is true. An example where I think this tends to be untrue is with distributed tracing. [We've previously looked at some ways Twitter gets value out of tracing](https://danluu.com/tracing-analytics/), which came out of the vision Rebecca Isaacs put into place. On the flip side, when I talk to people at peer companies with similar scale, most of them have not (yet?) succeeded at getting significant value from distributed tracing. This is so common that I see a viral Twitter thread about how useless distributed tracing is more than once a year. Even though we went with the more expensive "build" option, just off the top of my head, I can think of multiple uses of tracing that have returned between 10x and 100x the cost of building out tracing, whereas people at a number of companies that have chosen the cheaper "buy" option commonly complain that tracing isn't worth it.

Coincidentally, I was just talking about this exact topic to Pam Wolf, a civil engineering professor with experience in (civil engineering) industry on multiple continents, who had a related opinion. For large scale systems (projects), you need an in-house expert (owner's-side engineer) for each area that you don't handle in your own firm. While it's technically possible to hire yet another firm to be the expert, that's more expensive than developing or hiring in-house expertise and, in the long run, also more risky. That's pretty analogous to my experience working as an electrical engineer as well, where orgs that outsource functions to other companies without retaining an in-house expert pay a very high cost, and not just monetarily. They often ship sub-par designs with long delays on top of having high costs. "Buying" can and often does reduce the amount of expertise necessary, but it often doesn't remove the need for expertise.

This related to another common abstract argument that's commonly made, that companies should concentrate on "their area of comparative advantage" or "most important problems" or "core business need" and outsource everything else. We've already seen a couple of examples where this isn't true because, at a large enough scale, it's more profitable to have in-house expertise than not regardless of whether or not something is core to the business (one could argue that all of the things that are moved in-house are core to the business, but that would make the concept of coreness useless). Another reason this abstract advice is too simplistic is that businesses can somewhat arbitrarily choose what their comparative advantage is. A large[1](https://danluu.com/in-house/#fn:L) example of this would be Apple bringing CPU design in-house. Since acquiring PA Semi (formerly the team from SiByte and, before that, a team from DEC) for $278M, Apple has been producing [the best chips in the phone and laptop power envelope by a pretty large margin](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1433297089866383364). But, before the purchase, there was nothing about Apple that made the purchase inevitable, that made CPU design an inherent comparative advantage of Apple. But if a firm can pick an area and make it an area of comparative advantage, saying that the firm should choose to concentrate on its comparative advantage(s) isn't very helpful advice.

$278M is a lot of money in absolute terms, but as a fraction of Apple's resources, that was tiny and much smaller companies also have the capability to do cutting edge work by devoting a small fraction of their resources to it, e.g., Twitter, for a cost that any $100M company could afford, [created novel cache algorithms and data structures](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1381687511362138113) and is doing other cutting edge cache work. Having great [cache infra](https://danluu.com/cache-incidents/) isn't any more core to Twitter's business than creating a great CPU is to Apple's, but it is a lever that Twitter can use to make more money than it could otherwise.

For small companies, it doesn't make sense to have in-house experts for everything the company touches, but companies don't have to get all that large before it starts making sense to have in-house expertise in their operating system, language runtime, and other components that people often think of as being fairly specialized. Looking back at Twitter's history, Yao Yue has noted that when she was working on cache in Twitter's early days (when we had ~100 engineers), she would regularly go to the kernel team for help debugging production incidents and that, in some cases, debugging could've easily taken 10x longer without help from the kernel team. Social media companies tend to have relatively high scale on a per-user and per-dollar basis, so not every company is going to need the same kind of expertise when they have 100 engineers, but there are going to be other areas that aren't obviously core business needs where expertise will pay off even for a startup that has 100 engineers.

Thanks to Ben Kuhn, Yao Yue, Pam Wolf, John Hergenroeder, Julien Kirch, Tom Brearley, and Kevin Burke for comments/corrections/discussion.
