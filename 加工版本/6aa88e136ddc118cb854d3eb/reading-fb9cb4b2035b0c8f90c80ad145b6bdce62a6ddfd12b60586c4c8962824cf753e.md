# 大公司与初创公司｜Big companies v. startups

---

## 正文

有一种说法已经流传了一阵子：你应该加入 startup，因为钱更多，而且工作在 technically 方面更有意思。[Paul Graham 说](http://paulgraham.com/wealth.html)，赚钱的最佳方式是“创办或加入 startup”，这“几百年来一直是可靠的致富方式”，而且你可以“把整个 career 的 earnings 压缩到几年之内”。Michael Arrington 说，你将会[成为历史的一部分](https://www.jwz.org/blog/2011/11/watch-a-vc-use-my-name-to-sell-a-con/)。Joel Spolsky 说，加入 big company 后，你最终会[玩桌上足球，求别人看看你的 code](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/items/2008/05/01.html)。Sam Altman 说，如果你加入 Microsoft，[你不会 build 有意思的东西，也未必能和聪明人共事](http://blog.samaltman.com/advice-for-ambitious-19-year-olds)。他们都声称，如果你去 startup 工作，你会学到更多东西，也会有更好的 options。这些链接有些已经是十年前的了，但相同的观点仍在流传，那几篇文章至今仍被引用。

让我们逐一看看这些观点。

1.  你在 startup 会赚到多得多的钱
2.  你在 big company 不会做有意思的工作
3.  你在 startup 会学到更多东西，之后也会有更好的 options

### 1\. Earnings（收入）

具体 numbers 会因情况而异，但我们可以先做一个 back of the envelope calculation，之后再根据情况 adjust。美国的 Median income 是[大约 $30k/yr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_income_in_the_United_States). The somewhat bogus zeroth order lifetime earnings approximation I'll use is $30k * 40 = $1.2M. A new grad at Google/FB/Amazon with a lowball offer will have a total comp (salary + bonus + equity) of $130k/yr。[根据 glassdoor 目前的 numbers](http://www.glassdoor.com/Salary/Google-Senior-Software-Engineer-Salaries-E9079_D_KO7,31.htm)，在 Google 做到 T5/senior 的人，total comp 应该在 $250k/yr 左右。这些 numbers 相当 conservative[1](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:C)。

一个不算特别成功、但也不算特别不成功的人，大概会在五年内做到 senior[2](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:5)。作为我们的 conservative baseline，先假定我们永远不会超过 senior，进入 compensation 真正大幅飙升的 pay grades。我们预计 earnings（total comp，包括 stock，但不包括 benefits）大致会是这样：

| Year | Total Comp | Cumulative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 130k | 130k |
| 1 | 160k | 290k |
| 2 | 190k | 480k |
| 3 | 220k | 700k |
| 4 | 250k | 950k |
| 5 | 250k | 1.2M |
| … | ... | ... |
| 9 | 250k | 2.2M |
| … | … | … |
| 39 | 250k | 9.7M |

看来，只需六年就能 gross 相当于美国人整个 career 的 income。如果你想针对几年内赚得很多而增加的 tax burden 进行 adjust，那就再加一年。如果你决定住在湾区或 NYC，或许再加一到两年。如果你决定不 retire，那么 40 年 career 的 lifetime earnings 将接近 $10M。

一种常见但错误的反对意见是，你的 earnings 会被湾区的 cost of living 吞噬。这不仅不对，实际情况恰恰相反。你可以在湾区以外为这些公司工作；如果你在一家总部位于 SV 或 Seattle 的热门公司的 satellite office 工作，所在地的 cost of living 接近美国 median，那么这些公司中的大多数可能只会少付你 10%（至少在 US 工作时如此——US 以外的 pay 往往低得多，原因我实在想不明白）。这些地区的较小公司的 Market rate 往往很低。我在 Portland 和 Madison 等地方 interviewed 时，多数 small companies 开出的待遇，与我在同一城市的 big company 能拿到的待遇之间，有 3x-5x 的 difference。在 Austin 这种 market 稍微 thicker 一些的地方，difference 是 2x-3x。在 SF 湾区以外，90%-ile companies 的 pay difference 更大，而不是更小。

另一种反对意见是，[多数公司的多数 programmers 都赚不到这么多钱](http://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/computer-programmers.htm)。如果三四年前你告诉我，有一条 career track，做着与我当时的工作本质上很相似的工作，几年后拿到 $250k/yr 是完全正常的事，我不确定自己会相信。我认识的人里，没有谁赚那么多钱，或许我当时所在公司的 CEO 除外。嗯，除了他，还有那些进入 medicine 或 finance 的人。

那时和现在唯一的区别，就是我去了一家 big company 工作。刚入职时，我在 orientation 听到的常见故事基本都是：“我从没想过自己能在 Google 找到工作，但有个 recruiter 给我发了邮件，我觉得不妨回复一下。”不知为什么，女性尤其容易有这种想法。总之，我把这件事讲给了好几个认为自己进不了某家热门 large company 的人听，结果他们后来都去申请并成功入职了。而如果你最终去了 Google 这样的地方，你会意识到，他们中的大多数也只是和你我一样的普通 programmers。真要说的话，我觉得 Google 的 selective 程度平均而言还不如我待过的那家 startup。当你总共只需要 hire 100 人，其中一半又是你先前在一家 big company 担任 technical fellow、后来在另一家公司担任 SVP 时共事过的人，你完全可以非常缓慢地 hire，并且极其 selective。Big companies 每周会 hire 超过 100 人，这意味着它们的 selective 程度也只能到某个限度。

尽管到处都在渲染进 Google/FB/其他地方有多难，你的 odds 其实没那么差，而且[肯定比你在 startup 发财的 odds 更好](http://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-12-17/big-ipo-tiny-payout-for-many-startup-workers?trk=pulse-det-art_view_ext)。对此，[Patrick McKenzie 有一份方便的 cheatsheet](http://www.kalzumeus.com/2011/10/28/dont-call-yourself-a-programmer/)：

> 进行 Roll d100。（你不是玩这一类的 geek？抱歉，那就 rand(100)。）  
> **0~70**：你的 equity grant 一文不值。  
> **71~94**：你的 equity grant 值一笔一次性的钱，大致相当于你为了给 startup 工作，而没有去 megacorp 拿更高 salary 和更好 benefits 所放弃的钱。  
> **95~99**：你的 equity grant 是一笔足以改变人生的钱。你不会觉得自己富有——你不是自己认识的人中最富的，因为按照 definition，过去几年与你朝夕相处的许多人如今都比你更富——但你的家人再也不会因为你没进入 $FAVORED_FIELD like a proper $YOUR\_INGROUP 而数落你。  
> **100**：你在下一家 Google 工作，富有到连最贪婪的人做梦都不敢想。恭喜。  
> 细心的读者会注意到，100 实际上不会出现在 d100 或 rand(100) 中。

再来看一个更严肃、但 results 大致相同的分析：[80000 hours 发现，YC founder 在 5-9 年后的 average value 是 $18M](https://80000hours.org/2014/05/how-much-do-y-combinator-founders-earn/)。听起来很棒！但这里有几件事需要记住。首先，与 average startup 相比，YC companies 的成功程度非同寻常。其次，80000 hours 在其 analysis 中指出，80% 的钱属于 0.5% 的 companies。另有 22% 的价值足够高，使得 founder equity 胜过去 big company 工作，但剩下 77.5% 的情况并非如此。

如果你是 employee 而不是 founder，numbers 就难看得多了。如果你是非常早期的 employee，能拿到 founder 的 1/10th 那么多 equity，就已经相当幸运了。如果我们猜测，30% 的 YC startups 在 hiring 第一个 employee 之前就 fail，那么 mean equity offering 就是 $1.8M / .7 = $2.6M。这个数足够低，以至于对于 5-9 年的工作，你确实需要跻身那 0.5%，payoff 才会 substantially 好于在 big company 工作，除非 startup 支付非常丰厚的 salary。

从某种意义上说，这些 numbers 过于 optimistic。即使 company 成功了，并有一个不错的 exit，仍有很多事情能让你的 equity grant 变得 worthless。这方面的 statistics 很难获得，但从听到的事例来看，这似乎是 acquisitions 中的常见情况。

此外，那种声称你只需要工作四年的 pitch 通常并不属实。要保留你的 [lottery ticket](https://danluu.com/startup-options/)，直到它 pays out（或 fizzles out），你大概得待得更久。early stage startups 最常见的 equity 形式是 [ISOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive_stock_option)，按照 definition，它们最迟会在你离职后 90 天 expire。如果你很早加入，四年后离开，那么要想有机会让 lottery ticket paying off，你就必须 exercise 你的 options。如果 company 尚未获得很高的 valuation，你或许只需支付 O(median US annual income)，就能 exercise 你的 options。如果 company 看起来像一艘火箭，VCs 纷纷涌入，你还会有一笔巨额 tax bill，而这一切只为了一张 lottery ticket。

例如，假设你很早就加入了 company X，在它 valued at $1M, so the cost exercising all of your options is only $10k 时，拿到了相当于 company 的 1% 的 options。或许你运气不错，四年后，company 的 valued at 是 $1B and your options have only been diluted to .5%. Great! For only $10k，你可以 exercise 你的 options，然后以 $5M. Except that the company hasn't IPO'd yet, so if you exercise your options, you're stuck with a tax bill from making $5M sell 你获得的 equity，而到 company 真正有 IPO 时，你的 stock 可能值从 \$0 to $LOTS 不等。在某些情况下，你可以按照 non-liquid equity 的“value”的某个 fraction 将其 sell，但据我了解，companies 越来越常加入 clauses，limit 你在 company 有 IPO 之前 sell 你的 equity 的能力。而且，即使你的 contract 没有 clause 禁止你在 secondary market 上 selling 你的 options，[companies 有时也会利用 backchannel communications，让你无法 sell 你的 options](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=10705646)。

当然，并非每家 company 都这样——听说 Dropbox 已经连续多年慷慨地提出，按当时的 valuation buy out 人们的 options，而且现在发放的是 RSUs，而不是 options；Pinterest 现在也给人们离职后七年的时间来 exercise 他们的 options——但这种故事少见到足以引人注意。结果就是，在多数 startups，人们都有 incentivized 留下来，即使他们已经不喜欢那份工作了。和那些在颇受好评、highly-valued startups 工作的朋友聊天后，我感觉其中许多 startups 都有相当大一部分 zombie employees，只是在敷衍了事，等待 liquidity event。对 large companies 的一种常见批评是，它们有许多敷衍了事、打算一直待下去的员工，但大多数 large companies 允许你在第一年后的任何时候离开，并带走 equity package 中 pro-rated 的 fraction[3](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:M)。反倒是在 startups，人们即使不在乎那份工作，也有 incentivized 继续待着。

在 big company，只要你迈进了门槛，我们就有 high probability 在六年内获得相当于整个 career 的 income。这比起声称你能在 startup 用三四年做到这一点，还不算那么好，但只要你拿到这份工作，big company 的 risk 就非常 low。在 startup 的世界里，我们拿到的是一张 lottery ticket，对很早加入的 employees 而言，它似乎只有大约 0.5% 的 chance 能 paying off。Paul 在 2004 年写这件事时，Startups 的 expected value 或许曾经 substantially better，但 big company 的 compensation 的 increased 速度，远远超过了 median startup 的 compensation。我们目前正处在世界有史以来对 programmers 最好的 job market。这很可能在某个时候改变。一旦情况改变，走 startup 这条路的 relative returns 大概会显得好得多，但就目前而言，趁 big companies 像发糖一样发钱时 saving up 一些 cash，似乎并不是个坏主意。

还有一点值得注意：你可以通过在 big company 工作并 investing 于 startups，获得在 startup 工作的 upside。截至这次更新时（2020 年年中），companies 以 ~$10M and take checks as small as $5k 的 valuations 来 raise seed rounds 已经很常见。这意味着，用 $100k, you can get as much of the company as you'd get if you joined as a very early employee, perhaps even employee #1 if you're not already very senior or recognized in the industry. But the stock you get by investing has better terms than employee equity not even considering vesting, and since your investment doesn't need to vest and you get it immediately and you typically have to stay for four years for your employee equity to vest, you actually only need to invest $25k/yr 就能获得作为很早加入的 employee 所拥有的 equity benefit。你不仅可以（通过 diversifying）获得更好的 risk adjusted returns，而且，如果你在 big company 工作并每年 invest $25k/yr，你的 income 也会比在 startup 工作多得多。

### 2\. Interesting work：有趣的工作

我们已经确定，big companies 会给你不错的 pay。但人生不只是 making money。毕竟，你每周要花 40 hours 工作（甚至更多）。big companies 的工作究竟有多有趣？Joel 声称，large companies 不会 solve interesting problems，而 Google 则在[向一些 ultimate frisbee 经验比 Python 经验还多的年轻人 paying untenable salaries，他们的主要工作将是在 googleplex 玩桌上足球](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/items/2008/05/01.html)；Sam Altman 对 Microsoft 也说过类似的话（不过措辞克制得多）；Michael O. Church 的评论每三条就有一条在说，Google 如何哄骗大量 overqualified programmers 去接受没人想要的 jobs。基本上，HN 或 reddit 上每个面向 new grads 的建议讨论串，都会有好几个人插话，说你在 startups 获得的 experience 如何比在 big company 苦干获得的 experience 更好。

说 big companies 的工作无聊，这种说法过于宽泛、过于绝对，根本不可能成立。这取决于你想做哪一种工作。我去看那些有很高比例的 papers 让我觉得很有吸引力的 conferences 时，我觉得最有趣的东西，基本上在 big companies 和 academia 之间各占一半，只有（非常）偶尔才有一篇来自 startup 的 paper。比如，看今年的 ISCA，来自 academia 和 industry 的 papers 的 ratio 是 2:1（而且所有 industry papers 都来自 big companies）。但实际看看这些 papers，就会发现相当一部分 academic papers 都在 reproducing big companies 已经做过的 unpublished work，有时甚至是多年前就做过的。如果只看我个人感兴趣的新工作，这个 ratio 大约是 1:1。有些 startup 也在同一个 area 工作而不 publishing，但这种情况相当少见，而且 large companies 做了多得多、并不 publish 的 research。我只是把 papers 当作是否存在我喜欢的那种工作的 proxy。也有许多 areas 并不以 publishing 为常态，但 large companies 完成了其中大部分 cutting edge work。

当然，这里的情况会因你想做什么而因人而异。我不太熟悉 front-end work 的整体状况，但在我看来，big companies 并不像在 large scale systems 领域那样，完成了绝大多数 cutting edge 的 non-academic work。如果我没记错，有一条 HN 评论中，Jonathan Tang 描述了他如何创造出自己的 front-end work：他有了想法，告诉了 manager，并获得了付诸实现的 approval。在 large company 做这种事是可能的，但人们在 small company 追求这类想法时，似乎往往更容易一些。而如果你的兴趣在 product，small companies 看起来是更好的选择（不过，再说一次，我离那个 area 相当远，所以我的了解都是第二手的）。

但如果你对 large systems 感兴趣，在我最近两份工作中，我都见过具有 9 figure pilot budgets 的 speculative research projects 获得 approved。其中一个 products 的 pitch，甚至并不是说这个 project 会为 company 赚钱。而是说，某个特定的 research area 对 company 很重要，而这个 infrastructure project 能让 company 在那个 research area 更快推进。由于这是一家每年有 $X billion dollar 规模的 company，这个 project 只需要带来一个 small percentage 的改善，就值得做。因此，一个以 speed up 另一个 research project 的 progress 为目标的 research project，就这样获得了 approved。在内部，这类事情通常由 politics 决定，有些人会说这使它不值得做。但如果你受得了 big company politics，startups 根本没有 resources 去 fund 那些并非其 business 的 core 的 research problems。而且，许多在 startups 会是 hard problems 的 problems，在 large companies 不过是让人好奇的小问题。

另一面则是，有些 experiments 对 startups 来说很容易做，established companies 却做不了。多年前我参加 [EC](http://www.sigecom.org/) 时，Facebook 当时还比较年轻，Google 做 ad auction 的人对 FB 的人说，FB 正在做的那类 experiments，如果他们的规模也足够小，他们也会做；但如今有那么多钱流经他们的 auctions，他们已经不能随便 change 自己 ad auctions 的 structure 了。和我们讨论的其他一切一样，这里存在 trade-off，真正的问题是如何 weight 这个 trade-off 的各个部分，而不是哪一边在所有方面都更好。

Michael O. Church 的说法稍微弱一些：big companies 有很酷的东西可以做，但你得先做那些无聊的 problems、熬够资历，才会被允许去做。更温和的说法是，能不能做 interesting work，要看你是否走运，能否一开始就落在自己感兴趣的 project 上；但这里的关键是，大多数 companies 都能对你会有多走运给出相当好的 estimate。Google 因其 blind allocation process 而声名狼藉，我认识好几个人最终去了 MS，因为他们面临的选择是 MS 的一个很棒的 project，或者 Google 的 blind allocation；但即使 Google 也已经在一定程度上改变了这一点，如今拿到 offer 时同时获得多个 team options，也并不少见。从这个意义上说，big companies 与 startups 并没有多大不同。确实有些 startups 基本上只会有你感兴趣的 jobs（例如，如果你对 building 一个 distributed database 感兴趣，那么一家 early-stage 的 distributed database startup 就是如此）。但任何规模更大、没那么 specialized 的 startup，都会有你感兴趣的工作，也会有你不感兴趣的工作，而你需要自己弄清楚，你的 offer 是否让你能做自己感兴趣的东西。

值得注意的是，如果如 (1) 所述，你有 leverage 来 negotiate 一份好的 compensation package，那么你也有 leverage 来 negotiate 自己想做的工作。我们正处在可能是有史以来对 programmers 最好的 job market。这可能明天就会改变，但在它改变之前，你有很大的力量去争取自己想做的工作。

### 3\. Learning / Experience（学习 / 经验）

那么，关于 startups 的 experience 更有 valuable 的说法呢？我们没有 data 来进行 rigorous quantitative comparison，但从 qualitatively 来看，startups 到处都在着火，你在四处救火时获得了大量 breadth，却没有时间那么 deeply 地 explore problems。

我 career 的头七年是在一家 startup 度过的，而且我很喜欢那段经历。那里完全是一片混乱，这让我能够处理各种各样不同的事情，并承担比在更大的 company 所能承担的更多 responsibility。我做过的事情，从为一个 in-house distributed system 进行 add fault tolerance，到 owning 一个 project 的四分之一；这个 project 为一个 x86 chip added ARM instructions，既 creating 了当时最快的 ARM chip，也 creating 了唯一能够在 ARM 和 x86 之间 switching on the fly 的 chip[4](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:S)。那是一次很棒的 learning experience。

但我在 big companies 也有过很棒的 learning experiences。在 Google，我的“入门” project 是加入一个此前只有一人的 project，阅读完成了一半的 design doc，提供 feedback，然后开始 implementing。这个 project 的起因是，大家担心，如果某种不太可能、却并非不可能的 scenario 发生，image recognition problems 会要求 Google 将其拥有的 machines 的 number double。这与我的 startup experience 没有太大不同，只不过这里真的有一份 design doc，而且 cutting infra costs 每年可能节省数十亿，而不是数百万。

与一家 startup 中的 equivalent project 相比，那个 project 是更好还是更差的 learning experience？在一家 startup，这个 project 很可能会继续由两个人包办，而我会学到在 time 和 resources 不够、自己完成一半工作的情况下赶出一个 project 时所能学到的一切。但实际情况是，我最终只 owning 了这个 project 的一部分，对其余部分仅仅 provided feedback；而我能在 fleshing out architecture 上拥有相当大的发言权，也仅仅是运气（时机）使然。我肯定没有获得亲自 implemented 其中一半所能获得的同等程度的 understanding。另一方面，更大的 team 意味着我们确实有时间做 design reviews 和 code reviews 之类的事情。

如果你在意 impact，那么由于 big companies operate 的 scale，在一家 large company 产生大的 absolute impact 也更容易。如果我为一家规模与我过去工作过的 startup 相当的 company implemented 我现在正在做的事情，它产生的 impact 可能是 $10k/month。这并非微不足道，但还不足以支付我的 salary。然而，同样的事情在一家 big company 的价值远远超过这个数的 1000x。large companies 之所以有更多产生 high impact 的机会，仅仅是因为它们以更大的 scale operate。由此得到的 corollary 是，startups 足够小，因此更容易对 company 本身产生 impact，即使对世界的 impact 从 absolute terms 来看更小。我做的任何事情都不会决定一家 large company 的成败，但当我在一家 startup 工作时，感觉我们做的事情能够改变 company surviving 的 odds。

至于在 big company 或 startup 工作过之后是否会有更好的 options，如果你想在 startups 工作，那么拥有 startups 的 experience 很可能会让你有更好的 options。如果你想处理那些由 large companies dominated 的 problems，那么在 large companies 的那些领域积累更多 experience 对你更有利。这里没有正确答案。

### 结论

compensation trade-off 随着时间发生了很大变化。Paul Graham 在 2004 年写作时，他使用的是 $80k/yr as a reasonable baseline for what “a good hacker” might make. Adjusting for inflation, [that's about $现在的 100k/yr](http://data.bls.gov/cgi-bin/cpicalc.pl?cost1=80000&year1=2004&year2=2015)。但一个“good hacker”的 total comp 是 $250k+/yr，甚至还没有计算免费食物和非常可靠的 insurance 之类的 perks。这个 trade-off 已经大幅向 large companies 倾斜。

interesting work 的 trade-off 也随着时间发生了很大变化，但这种变化是……bimodal。AWS 和 Azure 的存在意味着，那些原本需要价值数百万美元的 servers 和 operational expertise 的想法，如今几乎不需要 fixed cost，且只需很低的 marginal costs 就能实现。在一家 early-stage startup，你能够做的、此前属于 well funded companies 的 domain 的事情，范围很大，而且仍在扩大。但与此同时，如果你看看 Google 和 MS 在 top systems conferences 上 publishing 的 work，就会发现，startups 距离能够 reproduce 那些 scale-dependent work 比以往任何时候都更远了（而且许多最 interesting 的 work 并不会被 published）。取决于你对哪一类 work 感兴趣，big companies 的情况可能显得 relatively better，也可能显得 relatively worse。

无论如何，现实是，不同 types 的 companies 之间的 difference，小于同一 type 的 companies 之间的 differences。无论我们谈论的是 startups 与 big companies，还是 mobile gaming 与 biotech，都是如此。这是 recursive 的。一家 company 内不同 managers 和 teams 之间的 differences，很容易大于 companies 之间的 differences。如果有人告诉你应该为某一 type 的 company 工作，那么这种建议在很多时候必然是错的，无论对方是主张你应该为 startup 工作的 VC，还是告诉你应该在 research lab 工作的 Turing award winner。

至于我嘛，我不认识你，你最终去 big company、startup，还是介于两者之间的地方，对我都无所谓。无论你如何决定，我都希望你对你的 manager 足够了解，知道对方会支持你；对你的 team 足够了解，知道你喜欢与他们共事；对你的 project 足够了解，知道你觉得它 interesting。Big companies 有一类在 startups 中不常见的 dysfunction[5](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:N)，而 startups 也有[它们自己那些种类的 dysfunction](http://totalgarb.tumblr.com/tagged/startupbullshit)。你应该弄清楚，哪些 tradeoffs 与你相关，以及你愿意接受哪一种 dysfunction。

## 原文

There's a meme that's been going around for a while now: you should join a startup because the money is better and the work is more technically interesting. [Paul Graham says](http://paulgraham.com/wealth.html) that the best way to make money is to "start or join a startup", which has been "a reliable way to get rich for hundreds of years", and that you can "compress a career's worth of earnings into a few years". Michael Arrington says that you'll [become a part of history](https://www.jwz.org/blog/2011/11/watch-a-vc-use-my-name-to-sell-a-con/). Joel Spolsky says that by joining a big company, you'll end up [playing foosball and begging people to look at your code](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/items/2008/05/01.html). Sam Altman says that if you join Microsoft, [you won't build interesting things and may not work with smart people](http://blog.samaltman.com/advice-for-ambitious-19-year-olds). They all claim that you'll learn more and have better options if you go work at a startup. Some of these links are a decade old now, but the same ideas are still circulating and those specific essays are still cited today.

Let's look at these points one one-by-one.

1.  You'll earn much more money at a startup
2.  You won't do interesting work at a big company
3.  You'll learn more at a startup and have better options afterwards

### 1\. Earnings

The numbers will vary depending on circumstances, but we can do a back of the envelope calculation and adjust for circumstances afterwards. Median income in the U.S. is [about $30k/yr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_income_in_the_United_States). The somewhat bogus zeroth order lifetime earnings approximation I'll use is $30k * 40 = $1.2M. A new grad at Google/FB/Amazon with a lowball offer will have a total comp (salary + bonus + equity) of $130k/yr. [According to glassdoor's current numbers](http://www.glassdoor.com/Salary/Google-Senior-Software-Engineer-Salaries-E9079_D_KO7,31.htm), someone who makes it to T5/senior at Google should have a total comp of around $250k/yr. These are fairly conservative numbers[1](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:C).

Someone who's not particularly successful, but not particularly unsuccessful will probably make senior in five years[2](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:5). For our conservative baseline, let's assume that we'll never make it past senior, into the pay grades where compensation really skyrockets. We'd expect earnings (total comp including stock, but not benefits) to looks something like:

| Year | Total Comp | Cumulative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 130k | 130k |
| 1 | 160k | 290k |
| 2 | 190k | 480k |
| 3 | 220k | 700k |
| 4 | 250k | 950k |
| 5 | 250k | 1.2M |
| … | ... | ... |
| 9 | 250k | 2.2M |
| … | … | … |
| 39 | 250k | 9.7M |

Looks like it takes six years to gross a U.S. career's worth of income. If you want to adjust for the increased tax burden from earning a lot in a few years, add an extra year. Maybe add one to two more years if you decide to live in the bay or in NYC. If you decide not to retire, lifetime earnings for a 40 year career comes in at almost $10M.

One common, but false, objection to this is that your earnings will get eaten up by the cost of living in the bay area. Not only is this wrong, it's actually the opposite of correct. You can work at these companies from outside the bay area; most of these companies will pay you maybe 10% less if you work in a location where cost of living is around the U.S. median by working in a satellite office of a trendy company headquartered in SV or Seattle (at least if you work in the US -- pay outside of the US is often much lower for reasons that don't really make sense to me). Market rate at smaller companies in these areas tends to be very low. When I interviewed in places like Portland and Madison, there was a 3x-5x difference between what most small companies were offering and what I could get at a big company in the same city. In places like Austin, where the market is a bit thicker, it was a 2x-3x difference. The difference in pay at 90%-ile companies is greater, not smaller, outside of the SF bay area.

Another objection is that [most programmers at most companies don't make this kind of money](http://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/computer-programmers.htm). If, three or four years ago, you'd told me that there's a career track where it's totally normal to make $250k/yr after a few years, doing work that was fundamentally pretty similar to the work I was doing then, I'm not sure I would have believed it. No one I knew made that kind of money, except maybe the CEO of the company I was working at. Well him, and folks who went into medicine or finance.

The only difference between then and now is that I took a job at a big company. When I took that job, the common story I heard at orientation was basically “I never thought I'd be able to get a job at Google, but a recruiter emailed me and I figured I might as well respond”. For some reason, women were especially likely to have that belief. Anyway, I've told that anecdote to multiple people who didn't think they could get a job at some trendy large company, who then ended up applying and getting in. And what you'll realize if you end up at a place like Google is that most of them are just normal programmers like you and me. If anything, I'd say that Google is, on average, less selective than the startup I worked at. When you only have to hire 100 people total, and half of them are folks you worked with as a technical fellow at one big company and then as an SVP at another one, you can afford to hire very slowly and being extremely selective. Big companies will hire more than 100 people per week, which means they can only be so selective.

Despite the hype about how hard it is to get a job at Google/FB/wherever, your odds aren't that bad, and [they're certainly better than your odds striking it rich at a startup](http://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-12-17/big-ipo-tiny-payout-for-many-startup-workers?trk=pulse-det-art_view_ext), for which [Patrick McKenzie has a handy cheatsheet](http://www.kalzumeus.com/2011/10/28/dont-call-yourself-a-programmer/):

> Roll d100. (Not the right kind of geek? Sorry. rand(100) then.)  
> **0~70**: Your equity grant is worth nothing.  
> **71~94**: Your equity grant is worth a lump sum of money which makes you about as much money as you gave up working for the startup, instead of working for a megacorp at a higher salary with better benefits.  
> **95~99**: Your equity grant is a life changing amount of money. You won't feel rich — you're not the richest person you know, because many of the people you spent the last several years with are now richer than you by definition — but your family will never again give you grief for not having gone into $FAVORED_FIELD like a proper $YOUR\_INGROUP.  
> **100**: You worked at the next Google, and are rich beyond the dreams of avarice. Congratulations.  
> Perceptive readers will note that 100 does not actually show up on a d100 or rand(100).

For a more serious take that gives approximately the same results, [80000 hours finds that the average value of a YC founder after 5-9 years is $18M](https://80000hours.org/2014/05/how-much-do-y-combinator-founders-earn/). That sounds great! But there are a few things to keep in mind here. First, YC companies are unusually successful compared to the average startup. Second, in their analysis, 80000 hours notes that 80% of the money belongs to 0.5% of companies. Another 22% are worth enough that founder equity beats working for a big company, but that leaves 77.5% where that's not true.

If you're an employee and not a founder, the numbers look a lot worse. If you're a very early employee you'd be quite lucky to get 1/10th as much equity as a founder. If we guess that 30% of YC startups fail before hiring their first employee, that puts the mean equity offering at $1.8M / .7 = $2.6M. That's low enough that for 5-9 years of work, you really need to be in the 0.5% for the payoff to be substantially better than working at a big company unless the startup is paying a very generous salary.

There's a sense in which these numbers are too optimistic. Even if the company is successful and has a solid exit, there are plenty of things that can make your equity grant worthless. It's hard to get statistics on this, but anecdotally, this seems to be the common case in acquisitions.

Moreover, the pitch that you'll only need to work for four years is usually untrue. To keep your [lottery ticket](https://danluu.com/startup-options/) until it pays out (or fizzles out), you'll probably have to stay longer. The most common form of equity at early stage startups are [ISOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive_stock_option) that, by definition, expire 90 at most days after you leave. If you get in early, and leave after four years, you'll have to exercise your options if you want a chance at the lottery ticket paying off. If the company hasn't yet landed a large valuation, you might be able to get away with paying O(median US annual income) to exercise your options. If the company looks like a rocket ship and VCs are piling in, you'll have a massive tax bill, too, all for a lottery ticket.

For example, say you joined company X early on and got options for 1% of the company when it was valued at $1M, so the cost exercising all of your options is only $10k. Maybe you got lucky and four years later, the company is valued at $1B and your options have only been diluted to .5%. Great! For only $10k you can exercise your options and then sell the equity you get for $5M. Except that the company hasn't IPO'd yet, so if you exercise your options, you're stuck with a tax bill from making $5M, and by the time the company actually has an IPO, your stock could be worthy anywhere from $0 to $LOTS. In some cases, you can sell your non-liquid equity for some fraction of its “value”, but my understanding is that it's getting more common for companies to add clauses that limit your ability to sell your equity before the company has an IPO. And even when your contract doesn't have a clause that prohibits you from selling your options on a secondary market, [companies sometimes use backchannel communications to keep you from being able to sell your options](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=10705646).

Of course not every company is like this -- I hear that Dropbox has generously offered to buy out people's options at their current valuation for multiple years running and they now hand out RSUs instead of options, and Pinterest now gives people seven years to exercise their options after they leave -- but stories like that are uncommon enough that they're notable. The result is that people are incentivized to stay at most startups, even if they don't like the work anymore. From chatting with my friends at well regarded highly-valued startups, it sounds like many of them have a substantial fraction of zombie employees who are just mailing it in and waiting for a liquidity event. A common criticism of large companies is that they've got a lot of lifers who are mailing it in, but most large companies will let you leave any time after the first year and walk away with a pro-rated fraction of your equity package[3](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:M). It's startups where people are incentivized to stick around even if they don't care about the job.

At a big company, we have a career's worth of income in six years with high probability once you get your foot in the door. This isn't quite as good as the claim that you'll be able to do that in three or four years at a startup, but the risk at a big company is very low once you land the job. In startup land, we have a lottery ticket that appears to have something like a 0.5% chance of paying off for very early employees. Startups might have had a substantially better expected value when Paul wrote about this in 2004, but big company compensation has increased much faster than compensation at the median startup. We're currently in the best job market the world has ever seen for programmers. That's likely to change at some point. The relative returns on going the startup route will probably look a lot better once things change, but for now, saving up some cash while big companies hand it out like candy doesn't seem like a bad idea.

One additional thing to note is that it's possible to get the upside of working at a startup by working at a big company and investing in startups. As of this update (mid-2020), it's common for companies to raise seed rounds at valuations of ~$10M and take checks as small as $5k. This means, for $100k, you can get as much of the company as you'd get if you joined as a very early employee, perhaps even employee #1 if you're not already very senior or recognized in the industry. But the stock you get by investing has better terms than employee equity not even considering vesting, and since your investment doesn't need to vest and you get it immediately and you typically have to stay for four years for your employee equity to vest, you actually only need to invest $25k/yr to get the equity benefit of being a very early employee. Not only can you get better risk adjusted returns (by diversifying), you'll also have much more income if you work at a big company and invest $25k/yr than if you work at a startup.

### 2\. Interesting work

We've established that big companies will pay you decently. But there's more to life than making money. After all, you spend 40 hours a week working (or more). How interesting is the work at big companies? Joel claimed that large companies don't solve interesting problems and that Google is [paying untenable salaries to kids with more ultimate frisbee experience than Python, whose main job will be to play foosball in the googleplex](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/items/2008/05/01.html), Sam Altman said something similar (but much more measured) about Microsoft, every third Michael O. Church comment is about how Google tricks a huge number of overqualified programmers into taking jobs that no one wants. Basically every advice thread on HN or reddit aimed at new grads will have multiple people chime in on how the experience you get at startups is better than the experience you'll get slaving away at a big company.

The claim that big companies have boring work is too broad and absolute to even possibly be true. It depends on what kind of work you want to do. When I look at conferences where I find a high percentage of the papers compelling, the stuff I find to be the most interesting is pretty evenly split between big companies and academia, with the (very) occasional paper by a startup. For example, looking at ISCA this year, there's a 2:1 ratio of papers from academia to industry (and all of the industry papers are from big companies). But looking at the actual papers, a significant fraction of the academic papers are reproducing unpublished work that was done at big companies, sometimes multiple years ago. If I only look at the new work that I'm personally interested in, it's about a 1:1 ratio. There are some cases where a startup is working in the same area and not publishing, but that's quite rare and large companies do much more research that they don't publish. I'm just using papers as a proxy for having the kind of work I like. There are also plenty of areas where publishing isn't the norm, but large companies do the bulk of the cutting edge work.

Of course YMMV here depending on what you want to do. I'm not really familiar with the landscape of front-end work, but it seems to me that big companies don't do the vast majority of the cutting edge non-academic work, the way they do with large scale systems. IIRC, there's an HN comment where Jonathan Tang describes how he created his own front-end work: he had the idea, told his manager about it, and got approval to make it happen. It's possible to do that kind of thing at a large company, but people often seem to have an easier time pursuing that kind of idea at a small company. And if your interest is in product, small companies seem like the better bet (though, once again, I'm pretty far removed from that area, so my knowledge is secondhand).

But if you're interested in large systems, at both of my last two jobs, I've seen speculative research projects with 9 figure pilot budgets approved. In a pitch for one of the products, the pitch wasn't even that the project would make the company money. It was that a specific research area was important to the company, and that this infrastructure project would enable the company to move faster in that research area. Since the company is a $X billion dollar a year company, the project only needed to move the needle by a small percentage to be worth it. And so a research project whose goal was to speed up the progress of another research project was approved. Interally, this kind of thing is usually determined by politics, which some people will say makes it not worth it. But if you have a stomach for big company politics, startups simply don't have the resources to fund research problems that aren't core to their business. And many problems that would be hard problems at startups are curiosities at large companies.

The flip side of this is that there are experiments that startups have a very easy time doing that established companies can't do. When I was at [EC](http://www.sigecom.org/) a number of years ago, back when Facebook was still relatively young, the Google ad auction folks remarked to the FB folks that FB was doing the sort of experiments they'd do if they were small enough to do them, but they couldn't just change the structure of their ad auctions now that there was so much money flowing through their auctions. As with everything else we're discussing, there's a trade-off here and the real question is how to weight the various parts of the trade-off, not which side is better in all ways.

The Michael O. Church claim is somewhat weaker: big companies have cool stuff to work on, but you won't be allowed to work on them until you've paid your dues working on boring problems. A milder phrasing of this is that getting to do interesting work is a matter of getting lucky and landing on an initial project you're interested in, but the key thing here is that most companies can give you a pretty good estimate about how lucky you're going to be. Google is notorious for its blind allocation process, and I know multiple people who ended up at MS because they had the choice between a great project at MS and blind allocation at Google, but even Google has changed this to some extent and it's not uncommon to be given multiple team options with an offer. In that sense, big companies aren't much different from startups. It's true that there are some startups that will basically only have jobs that are interesting to you (e.g., an early-stage distributed database startup if you're interested in building a distributed database). But at any startup that's bigger and less specialized, there's going to be work you're interested in and work you're not interested in, and it's going to be up to you to figure out if your offer lets you work on stuff you're interested in.

Something to note is that if, per (1), you have the leverage to negotiate a good compensation package, you also have the leverage to negotiate for work that you want to do. We're in what is probably the best job market for programmers ever. That might change tomorrow, but until it changes, you have a lot of power to get work that you want.

### 3\. Learning / Experience

What about the claim that experience at startups is more valuable? We don't have the data to do a rigorous quantitative comparison, but qualitatively, everything's on fire at startups, and you get a lot of breadth putting out fires, but you don't have the time to explore problems as deeply.

I spent the first seven years of my career at a startup and I loved it. It was total chaos, which gave me the ability to work on a wide variety of different things and take on more responsibility than I would have gotten at a bigger company. I did everything from add fault tolerance to an in-house distributed system to owning a quarter of a project that added ARM instructions to an x86 chip, creating both the fastest ARM chip at the time, as well as the only chip capable of switching between ARM and x86 on the fly[4](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:S). That was a great learning experience.

But I've had great learning experiences at big companies, too. At Google, my “starter” project was to join a previously one-person project, read the half finished design doc, provide feedback, and then start implementing. The impetus for the project was that people were worried that image recognition problems would require Google to double the number of machines it owns if a somewhat unlikely but not impossible scenario happened. That wasn't too much different from my startup experience, except for that bit about actually having a design doc, and that cutting infra costs could save billions a year instead of millions a year.

Was that project a better or worse learning experience than the equivalent project at a startup? At a startup, the project probably would have continued to be a two-person show, and I would have learned all the things you learn when you bang out a project with not enough time and resources and do half the thing yourself. Instead, I ended up owning a fraction of the project and merely provided feedback on the rest, and it was merely a matter of luck (timing) that I had significant say on fleshing out the architecture. I definitely didn't get the same level of understanding I would have if I implemented half of it myself. On the other hand, the larger team meant that we actually had time to do things like design reviews and code reviews.

If you care about impact, it's also easier to have a large absolute impact at a large company, due to the scale that big companies operate at. If I implemented what I'm doing now for a companies the size of the startup I used to work for, it would have had an impact of maybe $10k/month. That's nothing to sneeze at, but it wouldn't have covered my salary. But the same thing at a big company is worth well over 1000x that. There are simply more opportunities to have high impact at large companies because they operate at a larger scale. The corollary to this is that startups are small enough that it's easier to have an impact on the company itself, even when the impact on the world is smaller in absolute terms. Nothing I do is make or break for a large company, but when I worked at a startup, it felt like what we did could change the odds of the company surviving.

As far as having better options after having worked for a big company or having worked for a startup, if you want to work at startups, you'll probably have better options with experience at startups. If you want to work on the sorts of problems that are dominated by large companies, you're better off with more experience in those areas, at large companies. There's no right answer here.

### Conclusion

The compensation trade-off has changed a lot over time. When Paul Graham was writing in 2004, he used $80k/yr as a reasonable baseline for what “a good hacker” might make. Adjusting for inflation, [that's about $100k/yr now](http://data.bls.gov/cgi-bin/cpicalc.pl?cost1=80000&year1=2004&year2=2015). But the total comp for “a good hacker” is $250k+/yr, not even counting perks like free food and having really solid insurance. The trade-off has heavily tilted in favor of large companies.

The interesting work trade-off has also changed a lot over time, but the change has been… bimodal. The existence of AWS and Azure means that ideas that would have taken millions of dollars in servers and operational expertise can be done with almost no fixed cost and low marginal costs. The scope of things you can do at an early-stage startup that were previously the domain of well funded companies is large and still growing. But at the same time, if you look at the work Google and MS are publishing at top systems conferences, startups are farther from being able to reproduce the scale-dependent work than ever before (and a lot of the most interesting work doesn't get published). Depending on what sort of work you're interested in, things might look relatively better or relatively worse at big companies.

In any case, the reality is that the difference between types of companies is smaller than the differences between companies of the same type. That's true whether we're talking about startups vs. big companies or mobile gaming vs. biotech. This is recursive. The differences between different managers and teams at a company can easily be larger than the differences between companies. If someone tells you that you should work for a certain type of company, that advice is guaranteed to be wrong much of the time, whether that's a VC advocating that you should work for a startup or a Turing award winner telling you that you should work in a research lab.

As for me, well, I don't know you and it doesn't matter to me whether you end up at a big company, a startup, or something in between. Whatever you decide, I hope you get to know your manager well enough to know that they have your back, your team well enough to know that you like working with them, and your project well enough to know that you find it interesting. Big companies have a class of dysfunction that's unusual at startups[5](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/#fn:N) and startups have [their own kinds of dysfunction](http://totalgarb.tumblr.com/tagged/startupbullshit). You should figure out what the relevant tradeoffs are for you and what kind of dysfunction you want to sign up for.
