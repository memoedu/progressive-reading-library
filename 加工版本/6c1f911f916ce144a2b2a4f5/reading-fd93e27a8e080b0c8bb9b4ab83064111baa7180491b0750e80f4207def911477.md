# An Intuitive Explanation of Bayes’s Theorem

## 阅读前置卡

- 价值判断：高。通过筛查、珍珠蛋等例题解释先验、条件概率、后验与似然比，并推导公式、讨论证据和科学方法，兼具入门教学与推理启发价值。
- 信息密度判断：中。概念、计算和推导内容丰富，但采用渐进教学，大量篇幅用于重复说明、练习、过渡和幽默插话，信息密度居中。
- 是否继续阅读：4 精细阅读。值得跟随例题自行计算，精细区分条件概率的方向、先验与后验，以及似然比和独立检验的适用条件；仅看结论难以获得其直觉训练效果。

## 核心洞察

一、公式和定义不等于理解：在设想的搜索情境中，介绍网页只给出贝叶斯定理的公式和定义，却没有解释它是什么、为什么有用，以及为什么令人感兴趣，结果它看起来只是某个莫名其妙的统计学东西。

三、从数字含义建立贝叶斯直觉：现有网上解释按介绍贝叶斯推理的经验来看都太抽象。对于贝叶斯推理，人们常说它非常反直觉，初学者与受过充分训练的专业人士都难学、难以自然运用，指导结束后又会迅速遗忘，仿佛人类内置的心智能力本来就很难理解它。这里尝试另一条路径：通过细致温和的讲解，调动自然频数、空间可视化等理解数字的方式；目的不是传授操纵数字的抽象规则，而是解释数字意味着什么，以及规则为什么如此、而且不可能是别的样子。

四、阳性后的患病概率问题：设参加常规筛查的四十岁女性中，1% 患有乳腺癌；患病女性中，80% 的乳房 X 线检查呈阳性；未患病女性中，9.6% 也呈阳性。问题是：该年龄组的一名女性筛查呈阳性后，实际患有乳腺癌的概率是多少？
[[#^pr-c4dc187ec9b9|正文跳转]]

五、多数医生严重高估概率：对于这道筛查题，通常只有大约 15% 的医生能答对，大多数医生给出同一个错误答案，将患病概率估计为 70% 到 80%，错得离谱。约 15% 的正确率有 Casscells、Schoenberger 和 Graboys 1978 年、Eddy 1982 年、Gigerenzer 和 Hoffrage 1995 年等研究作为依据；这一结果令人惊讶且容易复现，因此已经得到广泛复现。

六、约15%的正确率真实吗？：面对“通常只有大约15%的医生能答对”的说法，设想中的读者质疑：这是真实数字，还是根据某次网络投票编出来的都市传说？回应是：这是真实数字，依据包括 Casscells、Schoenberger 和 Graboys 1978年、Eddy 1982年、Gigerenzer 和 Hoffrage 1995年的研究，以及许多其他研究。
[[#^pr-044e60e93843|正文跳转]]

七、频数版本的表现稍好：把同一问题改写成频数形式后，医生的表现稍好一些：参加常规筛查的四十岁女性中，每 1,000 人有 10 人患有乳腺癌；患病女性中每 1,000 人有 800 人检查呈阳性，未患病女性中每 1,000 人也有 96 人呈阳性。问题改为：若该年龄组的 1,000 名女性接受筛查，阳性者中实际患病的比例大约是多少？
[[#^pr-0f0eb5d40237|正文跳转]]

八、自然频数版本的答对率为46%：在所比较的题目表述中，以10,000名参加常规筛查的四十岁女性为总体、直接给出患癌者和未患癌者中阳性人数的版本，医生表现最好，46%——将近一半——得出了正确答案。
[[#^pr-985e43bd7b2d|正文跳转]]

九、阳性者中的患癌比例约7.8%：题设的10,000名四十岁女性中，100人患乳腺癌，其中80人检查阳性；9,900人没有乳腺癌，其中950人也检查阳性。因此阳性者共950＋80＝1,030人，其中80人患癌，所求比例为80/1,030＝0.07767，约7.8%。
[[#^pr-30712f6919be|正文跳转]]

十、四组计数保留完整人群：筛查前，人群分为100名患癌者和9,900名未患癌者，两组相加为10,000人，确认没有遗漏。检查后分为四组：A组80人患癌且阳性，B组20人患癌且阴性，C组950人未患癌但阳性，D组8,950人未患癌且阴性。A与B之和对应原来的患癌组，C与D之和对应原来的未患癌组。
[[#^pr-de92d631dcb3|正文跳转]]

十一、阳性患者应得到怎样的回答：在题设条件下，阳性患者询问自己患乳腺癌的概率时，医生应该给出的答案是：1,030名阳性者中有80人患癌，约7.8%；如果有13名这样的患者询问，其中大约1人会患癌。
[[#^pr-6f4b33679ee3|正文跳转]]

十二、求后验需要全部三项信息：求出阳性检查者患乳腺癌的概率，始终需要全部三项信息：女性原本患癌的比例、未患癌女性得到假阳性的比例，以及患癌女性得到正确阳性的比例。最常见的错误是忽略前两项，只关注患癌女性得到阳性结果的比例。
[[#^pr-a8a4a58683fc|正文跳转]]

十三、先验、条件概率与后验：患者原本患乳腺癌的比例称为先验概率；患癌患者与未患癌患者各自得到阳性检查结果的概率，是两个条件概率。这些初始信息合称为 the priors。已知患者检查呈阳性时，她患癌的估计概率称为修正概率或后验概率；后验概率部分取决于先验概率。
[[#^pr-459a7e795296|正文跳转]]

十四、极低先验下阳性仍可能极不确定：设想每百万名女性仅一人患乳腺癌的宇宙：即使检查能检出八成癌症，未患癌女性中只有一成出现假阳性，每检出一个真正的癌症病例，仍会有十万个假阳性。在这一假设中，阳性结果确实提高了患癌的估计概率，却不会使其达到确定，甚至谈不上“一个值得注意的机会”；概率只是从 1:1,000,000 变为 1:100,000。
[[#^pr-3e494ddec742|正文跳转]]

十五、检查结果更新而非替换先验：乳房X光检查结果不会取代原有的患癌概率信息，而是使估计概率朝结果的方向移动：阳性使原始概率上升，阴性使其下降。在原题中，女性患癌率为 1%，患癌女性的阳性率为 80%，未患癌女性的阳性率为 9.6%；阳性结果将患癌概率从 1% 提高到 7.8%。
[[#^pr-3a0a987b335e|正文跳转]]

十六、不能把条件概率反过来用：“检查呈阳性的女性患乳腺癌的概率”与“患乳腺癌的女性检查呈阳性的概率”完全不是一回事。大多数人第一次遇到这类问题时，会用患癌女性得到阳性结果的 80% 概率替换原来的 1% 患癌概率；这种操作看起来或许是个好主意，但就是行不通。

十七、为何过马路还需更多信息：要回答“贝叶斯推理者为什么要过马路？”，需要更多信息。

十八、珍珠蛋问题可先尝试心算：珍珠蛋问题的算术足够简单，或许可以在脑中算出，值得先自行尝试。

十九、¬表示否定：符号“¬”是“非”的简写，因此 ¬pearl 表示“不含珍珠”。
[[#^pr-209667a1f859|正文跳转]]

二十、条件概率的方向不能颠倒：P(blue|pearl)表示已知蛋含有珍珠时，它为蓝色的概率：竖线右侧是已知条件或前提，左侧是推论或结论。P(blue|pearl)=30%意味着已知含珍珠的蛋有30%的概率为蓝色；题目要求的却是已知蛋为蓝色时含珍珠的概率，即P(pearl|blue)。
[[#^pr-d006bdc67302|正文跳转]]

二十一、蓝色蛋中含珍珠的比例为三分之二：假设40%的蛋含有珍珠，其中30%为蓝色，则全部蛋中12%既含珍珠又为蓝色；其余60%为空蛋，其中10%为蓝色，则全部蛋中6%既为空又为蓝色。蓝色蛋合计占18%，因此蓝色蛋含珍珠的概率为12/18，即2/3，约67%。
[[#^pr-a2f79650212f|正文跳转]]

二十二、相同蓝色证据仍依赖先验：极端情况说明求解需要全部三项信息。在其余条件不变时，如果每1,000个蛋只有1个含珍珠，得知蛋为蓝色会使含珍珠概率从0.1%升至0.3%，而不是从40%升至67%；如果每1,000个蛋有999个含珍珠，则从99.9%升至99.966%，不含珍珠的概率从1/1,000降至约1/3,000。
[[#^pr-729c41af2405|正文跳转]]

二十三、套用运算不等于回答问题：大多数不熟悉贝叶斯推理的答题者可能回答30%，或用30%的真阳性概率减去10%的假阳性概率而回答20%；这些运算对所问问题毫无道理。用患癌女性检查呈阳性的概率替换原有患癌概率，或用真阳性概率减去假阳性概率来求阳性者患癌的概率，同样完全不相干。这就像面对18人上车、随后又有7人上车而问司机年龄的问题，许多二年级学生回答25岁：他们知道何时被提示执行某种心智程序，却尚未真正把程序与现实联系起来。
[[#^pr-7e01e4a1b116|正文跳转]]

二十四、频数表述更容易唤起贝叶斯推理：Gigerenzer 和 Hoffrage 1995 年的研究表明，同一道应用题使用概率表述时最不容易唤起正确的贝叶斯推理，改用频数表述则稍好。例如，把“1% 的女性患乳腺癌”改成“100 名女性中有 1 名患乳腺癌”，把检出率写成“100 名患乳腺癌的女性中有 80 名乳腺摄影呈阳性”。这种改善可能是因为频数鼓励人们具体想象患癌女性，以及其中患癌且检查呈阳性的女性。
[[#^pr-0b0d03642575|正文跳转]]

25、自然频数把先验包含在表述中：迄今发现最有效的表述是自然频数：100 个蛋中有 40 个含珍珠，其中 12 个是蓝色；60 个空蛋中有 6 个是蓝色。这种表述在呈现条件概率时，也包含了先验概率的信息。如果通过自然实验敲开 100 个蛋来了解这些条件概率，大约就会遇到这些数量，看到含珍珠蓝蛋的频次约为看到蓝色空蛋的两倍。
[[#^pr-15d526e77cec|正文跳转]]

26、自然频数的改善仍不够大：以自然频数呈现问题，会使使用贝叶斯推理的人数比例提高到大约一半。这是朝正确方向迈出的一大步，但恐怕仍不够；涉及真实医生和真实患者时，这样的进步还不够大。

29、先验也能由现实判定对错：先验与最终答案一样有真伪之分：它们反映现实，可以通过与现实比较来判断。例如，认为样本中每 10,000 名女性有 920 人患乳腺癌，而实际只有 100 人患病，这一先验就是错误的。
[[#^pr-b00c3785b889|正文跳转]]

30、本例的先验可能来自三项研究：乳腺癌筛查问题中的先验可能通过三项研究确定：考察患乳腺癌女性的病例史，了解其中乳房 X 光检查阳性的比例；考察未患乳腺癌女性，了解其中检查阳性的比例；通过流行病学研究，了解某一特定人口群体的乳腺癌患病率。
[[#^pr-b00c3785b889|正文跳转]]

31、联合概率与条件概率不可混淆：P(A,B) 与 P(B,A) 相同，但 P(A|B) 与 P(B|A) 不是一回事，P(A,B) 与 P(A|B) 则完全不同。混淆这些概率量中的一部分或全部，是一种常见错误。
[[#^pr-4b3953aaa960|正文跳转]]

32、互补概率只有一个自由度：P(cancer) 与 P(¬cancer) 受 P(A) + P(¬A) = 1 约束，因此两个量之间只有一个自由度。已知 P(¬cancer) = 0.99，就能推出 P(cancer) = 1 − P(¬cancer) = 0.01。
[[#^pr-29976cc0f127|正文跳转]]

33、互补结果只有一个自由度：P(positive|cancer) 与 P(¬positive|cancer) 只有一个自由度：患有乳腺癌的女性，要么得到乳腺摄影阳性结果，要么没有得到。
[[#^pr-4347e81aa4ba|正文跳转]]

34、两类人群的阳性率可独立变化：P(positive|cancer) 与 P(positive|¬cancer) 有两个自由度，不能从其中一个推得另一个。检测可以对 80% 的癌症患者和 9.6% 的健康患者给出阳性，也可以分别对 70% 和 2% 给出阳性，甚至可以分别对 30% 和 92% 给出“阳性”。
[[#^pr-4347e81aa4ba|正文跳转]]

35、乘法关系约束三个概率量：P(positive,cancer) = P(positive|cancer) × P(cancer)。知道患者中患癌者的比例，以及癌症患者得到阳性结果的概率，就能相乘求得患者中既患癌又得到阳性结果的比例。这个等式减少了一个自由度，使这三个量共有两个自由度；知道其中任意两个，就能推得第三个。
[[#^pr-3d93d2e0043c|正文跳转]]

36、条件概率用对应人群作分母：将既患乳腺癌又得到阳性结果的人数，除以乳腺癌患者总人数，可以估计 P(positive|cancer)。这类诊断检测正是如此校准的：例如研究 8,520 名乳腺癌患者，发现其中约 6,816 人得到阳性结果，6,816/8,520 = 80%。若颠倒除法方向，则会得到 125% 这样的荒谬结果。
[[#^pr-67aa32197727|正文跳转]]

37、阳性总量由两类人群相加：P(positive) = P(positive,cancer) + P(positive,¬cancer)，因此这三个量只有两个自由度。阳性女性要么患乳腺癌，要么没有：将两类人数相加可得阳性总人数；知道阳性总人数及其中患癌的人数，也就能推得阳性却未患癌的人数。一般而言，P(A,B) + P(A,¬B) = P(A)；对称地，P(A,B) + P(¬A,B) = P(B)。
[[#^pr-c1b0a80d897c|正文跳转]]

38、边缘概率不能任意相乘：仅给定阳性概率和患癌概率，不能通过相乘确定四个联合概率。只有统计独立时，P(positive,¬cancer)=P(positive)×P(¬cancer) 才成立；这意味着患癌与否不影响乳腺摄影呈阳性的机会，相当于要求两个条件概率相等，从而消去一个自由度。
[[#^pr-e02edbabad3f|正文跳转]]

39、四个联合概率有三个自由度：患癌且阳性、患癌且阴性、未患癌且阳性、未患癌且阴性这四组人数，在理论上可以任意指定，作为人数有四个自由度。但归一化为整个群体中的比例后，四个联合概率之和必须为1，因此只剩三个自由度；指定 A、B、D 三组的比例，就能推出 C 组的比例。
[[#^pr-e02edbabad3f|正文跳转]]

40、从分组人数计算概率：以 A、B、C、D 分别表示患癌且阳性、患癌且阴性、未患癌且阳性、未患癌且阴性的人数，患癌概率为 (A+B)/(A+B+C+D)，患癌者检测呈阴性的条件概率为 B/(A+B)。
[[#^pr-858bf26e1a85|正文跳转]]

41、三项信息确定整个问题：关联患癌率与检测结果的整套概率只有三个自由度；本题的先验概率和两个条件概率恰好提供这三个自由度。对于贝叶斯问题，任何彼此之间具有三个自由度的三个量，都应当能在逻辑上确定整个问题。
[[#^pr-62fbcedce5ab|正文跳转]]

42、相同似然比不等于同样有用：真阳性概率除以假阳性概率，称为检测的似然比，它概括了阳性结果会使先验概率移动多少。但阳性结果的似然比并未确定阴性结果的意义，也未确定检测发挥作用的频率。命中率80%、假阳性率9.6%的乳腺摄影，与命中率8%、假阳性率0.96%的检测具有相同似然比，但前者在各方面都更有用：它更经常检出疾病，其阴性结果也是健康的更强证据。
[[#^pr-1f8fd41e1631|正文跳转]]

43、后验可作为下一次更新的先验：假设乳腺癌先验患病率为1%，先进行标准乳腺摄影，再进行与之独立的、为例题虚构的 Tams-Braylor 细胞分裂检测；后者检查是否有细胞比其他细胞分裂得更快，假定真阳性率为90%、假阳性率为5%。若两项检测均为阳性，可以把乳腺摄影阳性后的7.8%后验概率作为新的先验，再用第二项检测更新，所得患癌概率为60%。
[[#^pr-d38740bab11b|正文跳转]]

44、相同似然比对应相同阳性更新：假设三项乳腺癌检测相互独立，其阳性结果的似然比分别为：A 的 80%/9.6% = 8.33，B 的 18.0（例如 90%/5%），C 的 3.5。C 的比值可以来自 70%/20%，也可以来自 35%/10%；对于这里的阳性结果计算，两者没有区别。
[[#^pr-9e47f6aa4710|正文跳转]]

45、把先验概率换成赔率：将概率改写为赔率可以简化计算：假设某个人群的乳腺癌先验患病率为 1%，则每 100 名女性中有 1 名患病、99 名未患病，患病与未患病的先验赔率就是 1:99。
[[#^pr-36442fd1924a|正文跳转]]

46、独立检测用似然比连乘更新赔率：在先验患病率为 1%、三项检测相互独立且全部呈阳性的假设下，将先验赔率 1:99 与三项阳性似然比 25:3、18:1、7:2 相乘，得到阳性者中患病与未患病的赔率：1 × 25 × 18 × 7 : 99 × 3 × 1 × 2 = 3,150:594。
[[#^pr-5175a58f88e7|正文跳转]]

47、三项阳性后的患病概率为84%：在上述先验和独立检测假设下，将三项检测均阳性后的患病赔率 3,150:594 换回概率，患病项除以两项之和，得到 3,150/(3,150 + 594) = 84%。
[[#^pr-d5be1edec3f3|正文跳转]]

48、独立检验的更新顺序无关：用先验赔率乘以各项独立检验的似然比进行更新时，赔率比的写法不影响计算：8.33:1、25:3 和 75:9 完全相同。实施这些检验的顺序或计算结果的顺序，也不影响最终结果。
[[#^pr-a20446aa3188|正文跳转]]

49、用分贝衡量可信度与证据：E. T. Jaynes 在《Probability Theory With Applications in Science and Engineering》中建议，用分贝衡量可信度和证据。
[[#^pr-4063770eb9d5|正文跳转]]

50、分贝表达强度的指数级差异：分贝用于衡量强度的指数级差异，计算方式是分贝数 = 10 × log₁₀(强度)，反过来，强度 = 10^(分贝数/10)。如果汽车喇叭声音每平方米每秒携带的能量是闹钟的 10,000 倍，它就比闹钟响 40 分贝；鸟鸣携带的能量可能比闹钟少 1,000 倍，因此比闹钟轻 30 分贝。
[[#^pr-98b4753987fc|正文跳转]]

51、用分贝相加累积证据：将赔率与似然比分别取以10为底的对数，再乘以10，就能把乘法更新写成分贝相加。在三个独立检验均呈阳性的例子中，先验赔率1:99约为−20分贝；似然比25:3、18:1和7:2分别约为9、13和5分贝。三个结果合计提供27分贝的证据，使后验可信度变为7分贝，对应赔率约5:1，患乳腺癌的概率由1%升至约83%。
[[#^pr-f0d156580087|正文跳转]]

52、火花诊断须计入两种来源：假设故障装置中30%的软管堵塞；拨弄装置时，堵塞者产生火花的概率为45%，未堵塞者为5%。观察到火花后，软管堵塞的概率按(45%×30%)/(45%×30%＋5%×70%)计算：先分别计算堵塞且产生火花、未堵塞却产生火花的比例，再用前者除以两者之和。
[[#^pr-edcf9bcb8426|正文跳转]]

53、一般形式即贝叶斯定理：上述计算的完全一般形式称为贝叶斯定理（Bayes’s Theorem），也称贝叶斯法则（Bayes’s Rule）。
[[#^pr-f981772c1d7e|正文跳转]]

54、新证据用于更新概率：对于待研究的现象 A 和作为其证据的观察 X，贝叶斯定理给出更新规则：P(A|X) = [P(X|A) × P(A)] / [P(X|A) × P(A) + P(X|¬A) × P(¬A)]。在乳腺摄影检查的例子中，A 是患乳腺癌，X 是检查呈阳性；所求是在知道阳性结果之后，患乳腺癌的新概率。
[[#^pr-3c1206af114d|正文跳转]]

55、贝叶斯方法界定证据收益：贝叶斯定理描述什么使某个东西成为证据，以及它包含多少证据。贝叶斯方法在统计学中已经是最好的：它界定从给定证据中能够获得的最大收益，正如热力学界定从温差中能够获得的最大做功量，因此统计模型通过与它比较来评判。在认知科学中，“贝叶斯推理者”是表示“理性心智”的技术上精确的代称。
[[#^pr-488d0f1ba1a1|正文跳转]]

56、不能只看证据与假设的匹配：面对表明 A 可能成立的证据 X，人们往往只凭 X 看起来与 A 有多匹配来判断 A 的可能性，而没有充分考虑 A 的先验频率。在乳腺摄影检查的例子中，把阳性女性患癌的概率判断为 70%–80%，就没有注意最初患癌的女性究竟占 1% 还是 10%。多注意先验频率，是部分弥补人类与生俱来不足所需牢记的事情之一。
[[#^pr-c1905fb4b67f|正文跳转]]

57、强证据取决于非假设情形：判断 X 对 A 构成多少证据，不能过分关注 P(X|A) 而忽视 P(X|¬A)：不仅要看 A 为真时多么预期出现 X，也要看 A 不为真时多么不预期出现 X。下雨几乎必然使草地湿，但洒水器或晨露也可能使草地湿，因此湿草地并不一定意味着下雨。反过来，假如不下雨时草地从不湿，那么草地湿就始终表明正在下雨，即使只有 50% 的下雨次数会使草地变湿。证据始终来自两个条件概率之间的差异；强证据并非来自 A 导致 X 的概率非常高，而是来自非 A 导致 X 的概率非常低。
[[#^pr-5680ecbc0be5|正文跳转]]

58、实验检验也须比较其他解释：科学中的贝叶斯革命，不仅来自认知科学家注意到心理现象中的贝叶斯结构，以及各领域科学家用贝叶斯方法评判统计方法，还来自“科学本身是贝叶斯定理的特例，实验证据就是贝叶斯证据”这一观念。在贝叶斯革命倡导者的理解中，实验对理论的证实或否证受贝叶斯规则支配：不仅必须考虑自己的理论是否预测该现象，还必须考虑其他可能的解释是否也预测该现象。
[[#^pr-ed1fcde2c7e1|正文跳转]]

59、预测成功不能确定证实理论：即使 P(X|A)≈1，观察到 X 也不能确定地证实 A：另一条件 B 也可能满足 P(X|B)≈1，此时 X 并不支持 A 胜过 B。要确定地证实 A，必须知道的不是 P(X|A)≈1，而是 P(X|¬A)≈0；但我们无法遍历所有可能的替代解释，因此无法知道这一点。广义相对论推翻得到极充分证实的牛顿引力理论时，人们发现牛顿的所有预测都只是爱因斯坦预测的一个特例。
[[#^pr-164bc5be377e|正文跳转]]

60、证伪主义的贝叶斯解释与修正：此前最流行的科学哲学大概是波普尔的证伪主义，而贝叶斯革命目前正在推翻这一旧哲学。波普尔认为，理论可以被确定地证伪，却永远不能被确定地证实。这一证伪思想也是贝叶斯规则的一个特例：若 P(X|A)≈1，即理论作出了确定预测，观察到 ¬X 就会非常强烈地证伪 A。不过，这并不意味着波普尔所说的确定证伪成立：证伪本质上仍是概率性的，与证实并不受根本不同的规则支配。
[[#^pr-164bc5be377e|正文跳转]]

61、似然比限定成功预测的收益：似然比 P(X|A)/P(X|¬A) 决定观察到 X 会在多大程度上改变 A 的概率，也就是 X 的证据强度。理论 A 可以用概率 1 预测 X，却无法控制分母 P(X|¬A)，因为总有一些替代理论也预测 X。即使采用符合当前证据的最简单理论，仍可能遇到替代理论预测、当前理论却不预测的证据。因此，成功预测带来的收益存在上限，证实性证据的似然比能达到多高也存在上限。
[[#^pr-b3340a74d3a3|正文跳转]]

62、证伪远强于证实：如果证据 Y 确定不被理论 A 预测，它就是反对 A 的极强证据；P(Y|A) 极小时，似然比也极小，倒转的似然比则极大，观察到 Y 会极大地支持 ¬A 胜过 A。例如，P(Y|A)=0.0001%、P(Y|¬A)=1% 时，似然比为 1:10,000，相当于 −40 分贝的证据。证伪远强于证实，源于极强证据并非来自 A 导致 X 的极高概率，而是来自非 A 导致 X 的极低概率；这构成了波普尔证伪主义启发价值的贝叶斯基础。
[[#^pr-0931f1152b2d|正文跳转]]

63、提高概率必须承担检验风险：可证伪性可以被理解为贝叶斯概率守恒规则的表现：如果 X 是支持理论的正面证据，¬X 就会在某种程度上反驳该理论，不能把两种结果都解释为证实。要提高一个理论的概率，就必须让它接受可能降低其概率的检验；这不只是科学社会过程中识别潜在作弊者的规则，也是贝叶斯概率论的推论。
[[#^pr-783b075564ef|正文跳转]]

64、证伪仍然是概率性的：波普尔关于只有证伪、不存在证实的观点是错误的。证伪虽然比证实构成非常强的证据，本质上仍然是概率性的，并不像波普尔所主张的那样，受与证实根本不同的规则支配。
[[#^pr-783b075564ef|正文跳转]]

65、科学方法也是贝叶斯特例：认知科学中的许多现象、科学家使用的统计方法，以及科学方法本身，最终都成为贝叶斯定理的特例；贝叶斯革命由此而来。
[[#^pr-9842c9292988|正文跳转]]

66、从左侧辨认对象与证据：如果分不清贝叶斯定理中的 A 和 X，可以从最容易解释的等式左侧 P(A|X) 开始：A 是想了解的对象，X 是观察它的方式，也是用于对 A 作出推断的证据。一般而言，P(Q|P) 表示给定 P 时 Q 的概率，即 P 蕴含 Q 的程度；P(Q←P) 会是更合理的记法，但现在已来不及采用。
[[#^pr-a83c056c3600|正文跳转]]

67、将绝对人数归一化为联合概率：假设共有10,000名女性，其中80人患有乳腺癌且乳腺摄影检查呈阳性，则联合概率 P(Q,P)＝80/10,000＝0.8%。这就是把绝对人数80相对于全体女性构成的群体归一化为概率。
[[#^pr-8dc6126909ae|正文跳转]]

68、联合概率与条件概率的分母不同：P(Q,P) 是整个总体中同时具有 Q 和 P 属性的比例，P(Q|P) 则是所有具有 P 属性的对象中，同时具有 Q 和 P 属性的比例。假设共有 89,031 名女性，其中 7,915 人乳腺摄影检查呈阳性，641 人患有乳腺癌且检查呈阳性：前者是从全体 89,031 人中随机选中这 641 人之一的概率，后者则是从检查呈阳性的 7,915 人中随机选中这 641 人之一的概率。
[[#^pr-71345a2a79a6|正文跳转]]

69、已知条件就是收窄考察群体：从某种意义上说，P(Q|P) 就是 P(Q,P|P)，额外写出 P 是冗余的：对象已知具有 P 属性，所调查的是 Q。考察的是 P 群体内同时具有 Q 和 P 的对象，而不是把整个 Q 群体放进 P 群体内考察。将 P 视为已知后，原先全样本中 Q 的频率会变为 P 子样本中 Q 的频率；仅相对于这个 P 群体而言，P(Q,P) 与 P(Q) 相等，因为寻找同时具有 Q 和 P 的对象，就是寻找具有 Q 的对象。
[[#^pr-21c839404c90|正文跳转]]

70、条件确定不等于结论确定：P(Q|P) 中，竖线右侧的 P 是收窄注意力的已知条件：只考察 P 为真的事物或事件，在这个限定的世界中，P 的概率为 1；但 Q 并不确定，其概率可能是 10%、90% 或其他数值。贝叶斯定理左侧的 P(A|X) 因而表示观察到 X 后更新的 A 的概率，其中 X 是观察或证据，A 是想要了解的属性。
[[#^pr-345d0fb34e71|正文跳转]]

71、用证据群体归一化联合概率：P(A|X)＝P(X,A)/P(X)，也就是 P(X,A)/[P(X,A)＋P(X,¬A)]。分子是 X 与 A 同时发生的概率，分母是 X 与 A 同时发生及 X 与非 A 同时发生的概率之和：在观察到 X 的群体中，求其中同时具有 A 的部分所占的比例。
[[#^pr-b6d9b6089b0d|正文跳转]]

72、因果与推断方向相反：基本因果关系通常从事实指向观察，例如从乳腺癌到乳腺摄影阳性；基本推理步骤通常从观察指向事实。贝叶斯定理左侧的 P(cancer|positive) 表示从阳性观察推断乳腺癌概率上升，条件概率的蕴涵方向按从右到左书写；右侧的 P(positive|cancer) 和 P(positive|¬cancer) 则描述基本因果步骤。
[[#^pr-589d5ce13d20|正文跳转]]

73、连接理性推断与物理因果：贝叶斯定理左端是理性推断，右端是物理因果，将心智与现实放在同一个等式两侧；科学方法也是它的一个特例。用诗意的说法，它将推理绑定进了物理宇宙。
[[#^pr-3c751d518151|正文跳转]]

你的朋友和同事正在谈论一种叫作“Bayes’s Theorem”或“Bayes’s Rule”的东西，或者一种叫作 Bayesian reasoning 的东西。他们听起来还对此热情高涨，于是你上网搜索，找到一个介绍 Bayes’s Theorem 的网页，然后……

就是这个 equation。没别的了。只有一个 equation。你找到的网页给出了它的 definition，却没有说它是什么，为什么有用，也没有说你的朋友为什么会对它感兴趣。它看起来就像某个莫名其妙的 statistics 玩意儿。

^pr-d75c41049cd6

为什么一个 mathematical concept 会让学习它的人产生这种奇怪的热情？如今席卷 sciences 的所谓 Bayesian Revolution 究竟是什么，竟声称连 experimental method 本身都可以被它 subsume 为一个 special case？Bayes 的追随者知道什么秘密？他们看见了怎样的光芒？

^pr-4f6399a70511

很快你就会知道。很快你就会成为我们中的一员。

虽然网上已经有一些关于 Bayes’s Theorem 的解释，但根据我尝试向人们介绍 Bayesian reasoning 的经验，现有的网上解释都太 abstract。Bayesian reasoning 非常 _counterintuitive_。人们不会 intuitively employ Bayesian reasoning，在接受指导时觉得 Bayesian reasoning 很难学，而指导一结束，又会迅速忘记 Bayesian methods。这一点对于初学者和某个领域里受过充分训练的专业人士同样成立。Bayesian reasoning 显然属于这样一类东西：就像 quantum mechanics 或 Wason Selection Test 一样，人类凭借自身内置的 mental faculties，本来就很难理解它。

至少他们是这么说的。这里，我将尝试对 Bayesian reasoning 作出一种 _intuitive_ 的解释——这篇入门介绍会细致温和到令人难以忍受，调动人类理解 numbers 的各种方式，从 natural frequencies 到 spatial visualization。目的不是传达 manipulating numbers 的 abstract rules，而是传达这些 numbers 意味着什么，以及这些 rules 为什么是这样（而且不可能是别的样子）。读完之后，你会在梦里看见 Bayesian problems。

^pr-12cb74951307

那么，让我们开始吧。

这里有一道 story problem，描述的是 doctors 经常遇到的情况：

> 参加 routine screening 的四十岁 women 中，1% 患有 breast cancer。患有 breast cancer 的 women 中，80% 会得到 positive mammographies。没有 breast cancer 的 women 中，9.6% 也会得到 positive mammographies。这个 age group 中的一名 woman 在一次 routine screening 中得到了 positive mammography。她实际患有 breast cancer 的 probability 是多少？

^pr-c4dc187ec9b9

你认为答案是什么？如果你以前没有遇到过这类 problem，请先花一点时间想出自己的答案，再继续读下去。

接下来，假设我告诉你，大多数 doctors 对这道 problem 都会给出同一个错误答案——通常只有大约 15% 的 doctors 能答对。（“真的吗？15%？这是一个真实的 number，还是根据某次网络投票编出来的都市传说？”这是一个真实的 number。参见 Casscells、Schoenberger 和 Graboys 1978；[1](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote1) Eddy 1982；[2](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote2) Gigerenzer 和 Hoffrage 1995；[3](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote3) 以及许多其他 studies。这是一个令人惊讶且容易 replicate 的 result，因此已经被广泛 replicated。）

^pr-044e60e93843

对于上面这道 story problem，大多数 doctors 将 probability estimate 为 70% 到 80% 之间，这错得离谱。

下面是这道 problem 的另一个版本，doctors 在这个版本上的表现稍好一些：

> 参加 routine screening 的四十岁 women 中，每 1,000 人有 10 人患有 breast cancer。患有 breast cancer 的 women 中，每 1,000 人有 800 人会得到 positive mammographies。没有 breast cancer 的 women 中，每 1,000 人也有 96 人会得到 positive mammographies。如果这个 age group 中的 1,000 名 women 接受一次 routine screening，那么得到 positive mammographies 的 women 中，实际患有 breast cancer 的 fraction 大约是多少？

^pr-0f0eb5d40237

假设某个 demographic 中 breast cancer 的 prior prevalence 是 1%。假设我们作为 doctors，有三个针对 breast cancer 的 independent tests 可用。我们的第一个 test，即 test _A_，是一个 mammography，其 likelihood ratio 为 80%/9.6% = 8.33。第二个 test，即 test _B_，其 likelihood ratio 为 18.0（例如，来自 90% versus 5%）；第三个 test，即 test _C_，其 likelihood ratio 为 3.5（可能来自 70% versus 20%，也可能来自 35% versus 10%；这没有区别）。假设一名 patient 在全部三个 tests 上都得到 positive result。这名 patient 患有 breast cancer 的 probability 是多少？

这里有一个有趣的窍门，可以 simplifying bookkeeping。如果某个 demographic 中 breast cancer 的 prior prevalence 是 1%，那么每 100 名 women 中有 1 名患有 breast cancer，而每 100 名 women 中有 99 名没有 breast cancer。因此，如果我们把 1% 的 _probability_ rewrite 为一个 _odds ratio_，那么 odds 就是 1:99。

^pr-36442fd1924a

而三个 tests _A_、_B_ 和 _C_ 的 likelihood ratios 是：

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td>8.33 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>25 : 3</td></tr><tr><td>18.0 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>18 : 1</td></tr><tr><td>3.5 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>7 : 2 .</td></tr></tbody></table>

患有 breast cancer 且在全部三个 tests 上 score positive 的 women，versus 没有 breast cancer 且在全部三个 tests 上 score positive 的 women，其 _odds_ 将 equal：

1 × 25 × 18 × 7 : 99 × 3 × 1 × 2 = 3,150 : 594 .

^pr-5175a58f88e7

要从 odds 中 recover probability，我们只需写出：

3,150/(3,150 + 594) = 84% .

^pr-d5be1edec3f3

无论 odds ratios 如何书写，这都始终有效；也就是说，8.33:1 与 25:3 或 75:9 完全相同。tests 按什么顺序 administered，或 results 按什么顺序 computed，都没有影响。proof 留给读者作为 exercise。

E. T. Jaynes, in _Probability Theory With Applications in Science and Engi­neer­ing_, suggests that credibility and evidence should be measured in decibels.[5](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote5)

^pr-4063770eb9d5

Decibels（分贝）？

Decibels 用于 measuring intensity 的 exponential differences。例如，如果汽车喇叭的声音携带的 energy（per square meter per second）是闹钟声音的 10,000 倍，那么汽车喇叭就会响 40 decibels。鸟鸣携带的 energy 可能比闹钟少 1,000 倍，因此会轻 30 decibels。要得到 decibels 的 number，你要取 logarithm base 10，再 multiply by 10：

^pr-98b4753987fc

decibels = 10 log10 (intensity)

或

intensity = 10decibels/10 .

假设我们一开始认为，一名 woman 患有 breast cancer 的 prior probability 是 1%，对应一个 1:99 的 odds ratio。然后我们 administer 三个 tests，其 likelihood ratios 分别为 25:3、18:1 和 7:2。你可以 multiply 这些 numbers……或者你也 _可以_ 只 add 它们的 logarithms：

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">1/99</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>−20</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">25/3</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>9</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">18/1</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>13</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">7/2</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>5 .</td></tr></tbody></table>

起初，一名 woman 患有 breast cancer 是相当 unlikely 的——我们的 credibility level 是 −20 decibels。随后，三个 test results 出来了，分别对应 9、13 和 5 decibels 的 evidence。这使 credibility level 总共 raises 了 27 decibels，也就是说，−20 decibels 的 prior credibility 变为 7 decibels 的 posterior credibility。因此，odds 从 1:99 变为 5:1，probability 从 1% 变为约 83%。

^pr-f0d156580087

> 你是一名维修 gizmos 的 mechanic。当一个 gizmo stops working 时，有 30% 的情况是由于 blocked hose。如果一个 gizmo 的 hose 是 blocked，那么 prodding 这个 gizmo 会 produce sparks 的 probability 是 45%。如果一个 gizmo 的 hose 是 unblocked，那么 prodding 这个 gizmo 会 produce sparks 的 chance 只有 5%。一位顾客给你带来一个 malfunctioning gizmo。你 prod 这个 gizmo，发现它 produces sparks。一个 spark-producing gizmo 有 blocked hose 的 probability 是多少？

你为 solve 这个 problem 而 performed 的 arithmetical operations，其 sequence 是什么？

(45% × 30%)/(45% × 30% + 5% × 70%)

^pr-edcf9bcb8426

或

类似地，为了求出一名有 positive mammography 的 woman 患有 breast cancer 的 chance，我们 computed：

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td colspan="4" class="numerator" style="border-bottom: 1px solid currentColor; text-align: center;"><em>P</em>(positive|cancer) × <em>P</em>(cancer)</td></tr><tr><td rowspan="2"><img src="https://www.readthesequences.com/wiki/uploads/big_left_parens.svg" alt=""></td><td>&nbsp;</td><td><em>P</em>(positive|cancer)×<em>P</em>(cancer)</td><td rowspan="2"><img src="https://www.readthesequences.com/wiki/uploads/big_right_parens.svg" alt=""></td></tr><tr><td>+</td><td><em>P</em>(positive|¬cancer) × <em>P</em>(¬cancer)</td></tr></tbody></table>

_也就是_

$$
\frac{P(\mathrm{positive}, \mathrm{cancer})}{P(\mathrm{positive}, \mathrm{cancer}) + P(\mathrm{positive},  \neg \mathrm{cancer})}
$$

_也就是_

$$
\frac{P(\mathrm{positive}, \mathrm{cancer})}{P(\mathrm{positive})}
$$

_也就是_

_P_(cancer|positive) .

这个 calculation 的 fully general form 称为 _Bayes’s Theorem_ 或 _Bayes’s Rule_。

^pr-f981772c1d7e

Bayes’s Theorem（贝叶斯定理）：

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

当有某个我们想要 investigate 的 phenomenon _A_，以及一个作为关于 _A_ 的 evidence 的 observation _X_ 时——例如，在前面的例子中，_A_ 是 breast cancer，_X_ 是一个 positive mammography——Bayes’s Theorem 告诉我们，在 given _new evidence_ _X_ 的情况下，应当如何 _update_ 我们对 _A_ 的 probability。

^pr-3c1206af114d

到了这里，Bayes’s Theorem 可能显得极其 obvious，甚至是 tautological，而不是令人兴奋的新东西。如果如此，这篇 introduction 就已经 _完全实现_ 了它的目的。

Bayes’s Theorem describes 什么使某个东西成为“evidence”，以及它有多少 evidence。Statistical models 通过与 _Bayesian method_ comparison 来 judged，因为在 statistics 中，Bayesian method 已经是最好的了——Bayesian method defines 你能从一项给定的 evidence 中获得的最大收益，正如 thermodynamics defines 你能从一个 temperature differential 中获得的 maximum amount of work。这就是你会听到 cognitive scientists 谈论 _Bayesian reasoners_ 的原因。在 cognitive science 中，_Bayesian reasoner_ 是我们用来表示 _rational mind_ 的 technically precise code word。

^pr-488d0f1ba1a1

你还可以通过研究 Bayes’s Theorem，学到一些关于 human reasoning 的 general heuristics。

例如，在许多关于 Bayes’s Theorem 的讨论中，你可能会听到 cognitive psychologists 说，人们 _没有充分地将 prior frequencies 考虑在内_，意思是，当人们面对一个 problem，其中有某个 evidence _X_ indicating condition _A_ 可能 hold true 时，他们往往只根据 evidence _X_ 看起来与 _A_ 有多么 match 来 judge _A_ 的 likelihood，而没有考虑 _A_ 的 prior frequency。例如，如果你认为在 mammography 的例子中，那名 woman 患有 breast cancer 的 chance 在 70%–80% 的 range 内，那么这种 reasoning 就是对 problem 中 given 的 prior frequency insensitive；它没有注意到，一开始患有 breast cancer 的究竟是 1% 的 women，还是 10% 的 women。“多注意 prior frequency！”是人类需要牢记的许多事情之一，以部分弥补我们与生俱来的不足。

^pr-c1905fb4b67f

一个相关的 error 是，在 determining _X_ 对于 _A_ 而言有多少 evidence 时，过多关注 _P_(_X_|_A_)，却没有充分关注 _P_(_X_|¬_A_)。一个 result _X_ 在多大 degree 上是 _A_ 的 _evidence for_，不仅 depends on 这条 statement 的 strength：_如果 A 是 true，我们会 expect to see result X_；也 depends on 这条 statement 的 strength：_如果 A 不是 true，我们 **不会** expect to see result X_。例如，如果正在下雨，这就 very strongly implies 草地是湿的——_P_(wetgrass|rain) ≈ 1——但看到草地是湿的，并不一定意味着刚刚下过雨；可能是洒水器被打开了，或者你看到的是清晨的露水。由于 _P_(wetgrass|¬rain) 是 substantially greater than zero，_P_(rain|wetgrass) 就是 substantially less than one。另一方面，如果不下雨时草地 _从不_ 湿，那么知道草地是湿的就会 _始终_ 表明正在下雨，_P_(rain|wetgrass) ≈ 1，即使 _P_(wetgrass|rain) = 50%；也就是说，即使只有 50% 的下雨次数会让草地变湿。Evidence 始终是两个 conditional probabilities 之间的 _differential_ 所产生的结果。_Strong_ evidence 不是 A leads to _X_ 的 very high probability 所产生的 product，而是 _not-A_ 可能 led to _X_ 的 very _low_ probability 所产生的 product。

^pr-5680ecbc0be5

_Bayesian revolution in the sciences_ 的动力，不仅来自越来越多 cognitive scientists 突然注意到 mental phenomena 中存在 Bayesian structure；不仅来自各个领域的 scientists 学会通过与 Bayesian method 的比较来 judge 他们的 statistical methods；还来自这样一种 idea：_science 本身是 Bayes’s Theorem 的一个 special case；experimental evidence 是 Bayesian evidence_。Bayesian revolutionaries 认为，当你 perform 一个 experiment，并获得“confirms”或“disconfirms”你的 theory 的 evidence 时，这种 confirmation 和 disconfirmation 受 Bayesian rules 支配。例如，你不仅必须考虑你的 theory 是否 predicts 这个 phenomenon，还必须考虑其他 possible explanations 是否也 predict 这个 phenomenon。

^pr-ed1fcde2c7e1

此前，最流行的 philosophy of science 大概是 Karl Popper 的 _falsificationism_——这就是 Bayesian revolution 目前正在推翻的旧 philosophy。Karl Popper 认为 theories 可以被 definitely falsified，却永远不能被 definitely confirmed，这个 idea 又是 Bayesian rules 的一个 special case；如果 _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1——如果 theory 作出了 definite prediction——那么 observing ¬_X_ 就会 very strongly falsifies _A_。另一方面，如果 _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1，而我们 observe _X_，这并不能 definitely confirm 这个 theory；可能存在另一个 condition _B_，使得 _P_(_X_|_B_) ≈ 1，在这种情况下，observing _X_ 并不 favor _A_ over _B_。要让 observing _X_ 能够 definitely confirm A，我们必须知道的不是 _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1，而是 _P_(_X_|¬_A_) ≈ 0；但这是我们无法知道的，因为我们无法遍历所有 possible alternative explanations。例如，当 Einstein 的 theory of General Relativity 推翻 Newton 那个得到 incredibly well-confirmed 的 theory of gravity 时，人们发现，Newton 的所有 predictions 都只是 Einstein 的 predictions 的一个 special case。

^pr-164bc5be377e

你甚至可以 mathematically formalize Popper 的 philosophy。_X_ 的 likelihood ratio，即 quantity _P_(_X_|_A_)/_P_(_X_|¬_A_)，determines observing _X_ 会在多大程度上 slides _A_ 的 probability；likelihood ratio 所说明的，就是 _X_ 作为 evidence 有多 strong。好吧，在你的 theory _A_ 中，只要你愿意，你可以用 probability 1 来 predict _X_；但你无法控制 likelihood ratio 的 denominator，即 _P_(_X_|¬_A_)——总会有一些 alternative theories 也 predict _X_，而尽管我们采用 fits 当前 evidence 的 simplest theory，你也可能有一天遇到某种 evidence，它是某个 alternative theory 所 predicts 的，却不是你的 theory 所 predicts 的。这就是推翻 Newton 的 theory of gravity 的那个隐蔽陷阱。因此，你能从 successful predictions 中获得的收益存在 limit；对于 _confirmatory_ evidence，likelihood ratio 能达到多高也存在 limit。

^pr-b3340a74d3a3

另一方面，如果你遇到某个 evidence _Y_，它确定 _不_ 被你的 theory 所 predicted，那么这就是反对你的 theory 的 _enormously_ strong evidence。如果 _P_(_Y_|_A_) 是 infinitesimal，那么 likelihood ratio 也将是 infinitesimal。例如，如果 _P_(_Y_|_A_) 是 0.0001%，而 _P_(_Y_|¬_A_) 是 1%，那么 likelihood ratio _P_(_Y_|_A_)/_P_(_Y_|¬_A_) 就是 1:10,000。这可是 −40 decibels 的 evidence！或者，把 likelihood ratio flipping 过来，如果 _P_(_Y_|_A_) 是 _very small_，那么 _P_(_Y_|¬_A_)/_P_(_Y_|_A_) 就会是 very large，这意味着 observing _Y_ 会 greatly favors ¬_A_ over _A_。Falsification 比 confirmation 要 stronger 得多。这是前面那个观点的 consequence：_very strong_ evidence 并非来自 _A_ leads to _X_ 的 very high probability，而是来自 _not-A_ 可能 led to _X_ 的 very _low_ probability。这就是构成 Popper 的 falsificationism 的 heuristic value 之基础的 precise Bayesian rule。

^pr-0931f1152b2d

同样，Popper 关于 idea 必须是 falsifiable 的论断，可以被 interpreted 为 Bayesian conservation-of-probability rule 的一种 manifestation；如果 result _X_ 是支持 theory 的 positive evidence，那么 result ¬_X_ 就会在某种程度上 disconfirmed 这个 theory。如果你试图把 _X_ 和 ¬_X_ 都 interpret 为“confirming”这个 theory，Bayesian rules 会说这是不可能的！要 increase 一个 theory 的 probability，你就 _必须_ 让它接受可能 decrease 其 probability 的 tests；这不仅是在 science 的 social process 中识别潜在作弊者的一条 rule，而且是 Bayesian probability theory 的 consequence。另一方面，Popper 认为 _只有_ falsification 而 _不存在_ confirmation 的 idea，结果是 incorrect 的。Bayes’s Theorem 表明，与 confirmation 相比，falsification 是 _非常_ strong evidence，但 falsification 在性质上仍然是 probabilistic；它并不像 Popper 所主张的那样，受与 confirmation fundamentally different 的 rules 支配。

^pr-783b075564ef

因此，我们发现，cognitive sciences 中的许多 phenomena，加上 scientists 使用的 statistical methods，再加上 scientific method 本身，最终都成了 Bayes’s Theorem 的 special cases。Bayesian revolution 由此而来。

^pr-9842c9292988

既然已经明确介绍了 Bayes’s Theorem，我们就可以明确讨论它的 components。

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

我们先从 _P_(_A_|_X_) 开始。如果你有时弄不清 Bayes’s Theorem 中什么是 _A_、什么是 _X_，就从 equation 的 left side 上的 _P_(_A_|_X_) 开始；那是最容易 interpret 的部分。在 _P_(_A_|_X_) 中，_A_ 是我们想要了解的对象。_X_ 是我们 observing 它的方式；_X_ 是我们用来对 _A_ 作出 inferences 的 evidence。请记住，对于每个 expression _P_(_Q_|_P_)，我们想了解的是 given _P_ 时 _Q_ 的 probability，也就是 _P_ implies _Q_ 的 degree——一种更合理、但现在已经来不及采用的 notation，会是 _P_(_Q_ ← _P_)。

^pr-a83c056c3600

_P_(_Q_|_P_) 与 _P_(_Q_,_P_) closely related，但它们并不 identical。当 Expressed 为 probability 或 fraction 时，_P_(_Q_,_P_) 是所有事物中具有 property _Q_ 和 property _P_ 的事物的 proportion；例如，在所有女性构成的 group 中，“患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammography 的女性”的 proportion。如果女性的 total number 是 10,000，而 80 名女性患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammography，那么 _P_(_Q_,_P_) 就是 80/10,000 = 0.8%。你可以说，absolute quantity 80 正在相对于所有女性构成的 group 被 normalized 为 probability。或者，为了说得更清楚，假设在共有 89,031 名女性的 total sample group 中，有一个由 641 名患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammography 的女性组成的 group。六百四十一就是 absolute quantity。如果你从整个 sample 中选出一名 random 女性，那么你选中一名患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammography 的女性的 probability 就是 _P_(_Q_,_P_)，也就是 0.72%（在这个例子中）。

^pr-8dc6126909ae

另一方面，_P_(_Q_|_P_) 是在 _所有具有 P 的事物_ 中，具有 property _Q_ 和 property _P_ 的事物的 proportion；例如，在 _所有有 positive mammographies 的女性_ 构成的 group 中，患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammography 的女性的 proportion。如果有 641 名女性患有 breast cancer 且有 positive mammographies，7,915 名女性有 positive mammographies，而女性共有 89,031 名，那么 _P_(_Q_,_P_) 就是当你从整个 89,031 人的 group 中 picking at random 时，选中那 641 名女性之一的 probability；而 _P_(_Q_|_P_) 则是当你从较小的 7,915 人的 group 中 picking at random 时，选中那 641 名女性之一的 probability。

^pr-71345a2a79a6

从某种意义上说，_P_(_Q_|_P_) 实际上意味着 _P_(_Q_,_P_|_P_)，但每次都 specifying 那个额外的 _P_ 会是 redundant。你已经 _知道_ 它具有 property _P_，所以你正在 _investigating_ 的 property 是 _Q_——尽管你考察的是 group _P_ 内 group (_Q_,_P_) 的 size，而不是 group _P_ 内 group _Q_ 的 size（后者毫无意义）。这就是把 right-hand side 的 property 视为 _given_ 的含义；它意味着你知道自己只在具有 property _P_ 的事物所构成的 group 内开展工作。当你把注意力收窄到只看这个较小的 group 时，许多其他 probabilities 都会 change。如果你把 _P_ 视为 _given_，那么 _P_(_Q_,_P_) 就 equals _P_(_Q_)——至少，_relative to the group P_ 是如此。_原来的_ _P_(_Q_)，即“整个 sample 内具有 property _Q_ 的事物”的 frequency，会被 revised 为“具有 property _P_ 的事物构成的 subsample 内具有 property _Q_ 的事物”的新 frequency。如果 _P_ 是 _given_，如果 _P_ 就是我们的整个世界，那么寻找 (_Q_,_P_) 就与只寻找 _Q_ 是同一回事。

^pr-21c839404c90

如果你把注意力收窄到只看涂成蓝色的蛋的 population，那么突然间，“一个蛋含有珍珠的 probability”就变成了一个不同的 number；这个 proportion 对蓝色蛋的 population 和所有蛋的 population 而言是 different 的。_given_，也就是收窄我们注意力的 property，始终位于 _P_(_Q_|_P_) 的 _right_ side；_P_ 成为我们的世界，成为我们所看到的全部，而在“given”的另一边，_P_ 始终具有 probability 1——这就是把 _P_ 视为 given 的含义。因此，_P_(_Q_|_P_) 的意思是：“如果 _P_ 具有 probability 1，那么 _Q_ 的 probability 是多少？”或者：“如果我们把注意力收窄到只看 _P_ 为 true 的事物或 events，那么 _Q_ 的 probability 是多少？”位于 given 另一边的 statement _Q_，则 _并不_ certain——它的 probability 可能是 10%、90%，或任何其他 number。因此，当你使用 Bayes’s Theorem，并把 left side 的部分写作 _P_(_A_|_X_)——看到 _X_ 后如何 _update_ _A_ 的 probability，_given_ 我们知道 _X_ 时 _A_ 的新 probability，_X implies A_ 的 degree——你就能看出，_X_ 始终是 _observation_ 或 _evidence_，而 _A_ 则是正在被 investigated 的 property，是你想要了解的对象。

^pr-345d0fb34e71

Bayes’s Theorem 的 right side 通过以下 steps 从 left side derived 而来：

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td><em>P</em>(<em>A</em>|<em>X</em>)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td><em>P</em>(<em>A</em>|<em>X</em>)</td></tr></tbody></table>

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X,A)}{P(X)}
$$

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X,A)}{P(X,A) + P(X, \neg A)}
$$

^pr-b6d9b6089b0d

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

derivation 完成后，equation 的 right side 上的所有 implications 都具有 _P_(_X_|_A_) 或 _P_(_X_|¬_A_) 的 form，而 left side 上的 implication 则是 _P_(_A_|_X_)。这种 symmetry 的出现，是因为 elementary _causal relations_ 通常是从 facts 到 observations 的 implications，例如从 breast cancer 到 positive mammography。elementary _steps in reasoning_ 通常是从 observations 到 facts 的 implications，例如从 positive mammography 到 breast cancer。Bayes’s Theorem 的 left side 是一个 elementary _inferential_ step，从 positive mammography 的 observation，走向 breast cancer 的 probability increased 这一 conclusion。Implication 按 right-to-left 书写，所以我们在 equation 的 left side 写下 _P_(cancer|positive)。Bayes’s Theorem 的 right side 描述 elementary _causal_ steps——例如，从 breast cancer 到 positive mammography——所以 Bayes’s Theorem 的 right side 上的 implications 采取 _P_(positive|cancer) 或 _P_(positive|¬cancer) 的 form。

^pr-589d5ce13d20

这就是 Bayes’s Theorem。左端是 Rational inference，右端是 physical causality；一个一边是 mind、另一边是 reality 的 equation。还记得 scientific method 最终如何成为 Bayes’s Theorem 的一个 special case 吗？如果想说得诗意一些，你可以说，Bayes’s Theorem 将 reasoning 绑定进了 physical universe。

\[**Editor’s Note:** This is an abridgement of the [original](http://yudkowsky.net/rational/bayes) version of this essay, which contained many interactive elements.\]

Your friends and colleagues are talking about something called “Bayes’s Theorem” or “Bayes’s Rule,” or something called Bayesian reasoning. They sound really enthusiastic about it, too, so you google and find a web page about Bayes’s Theorem and…

It’s this equation. That’s all. Just one equation. The page you found gives a definition of it, but it doesn’t say what it is, or why it’s useful, or why your friends would be interested in it. It looks like this random statistics thing.

Why does a mathematical concept generate this strange enthusiasm in its students? What is the so-called Bayesian Revolution now sweeping through the sciences, which claims to subsume even the experimental method itself as a special case? What is the secret that the adherents of Bayes know? What is the light that they have seen?

Soon you will know. Soon you will be one of us.

While there are a few existing online explanations of Bayes’s Theorem, my experience with trying to introduce people to Bayesian reasoning is that the existing online explanations are too abstract. Bayesian reasoning is very _counterintuitive_. People do not employ Bayesian reasoning intuitively, find it very difficult to learn Bayesian reasoning when tutored, and rapidly forget Bayesian methods once the tutoring is over. This holds equally true for novice students and highly trained professionals in a field. Bayesian reasoning is apparently one of those things which, like quantum mechanics or the Wason Selection Test, is inherently difficult for humans to grasp with our built-in mental faculties.

Or so they claim. Here you will find an attempt to offer an _intuitive_ explanation of Bayesian reasoning—an excruciatingly gentle introduction that invokes all the human ways of grasping numbers, from natural frequencies to spatial visualization. The intent is to convey, not abstract rules for manipulating numbers, but what the numbers mean, and why the rules are what they are (and cannot possibly be anything else). When you are finished reading this, you will see Bayesian problems in your dreams.

And let’s begin.

Here’s a story problem about a situation that doctors often encounter:

> 1% of women at age forty who participate in routine screening have breast cancer. 80% of women with breast cancer will get positive mammographies. 9.6% of women without breast cancer will also get positive mammographies. A woman in this age group had a positive mammography in a routine screening. What is the probability that she actually has breast cancer?

What do you think the answer is? If you haven’t encountered this kind of problem before, please take a moment to come up with your own answer before continuing.

Next, suppose I told you that most doctors get the same wrong answer on this problem—usually, only around 15% of doctors get it right. (“Really? 15%? Is that a real number, or an urban legend based on an Internet poll?” It’s a real number. See Casscells, Schoenberger, and Graboys 1978;[1](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote1) Eddy 1982;[2](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote2) Gigerenzer and Hoffrage 1995;[3](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote3) and many other studies. It’s a surprising result which is easy to replicate, so it’s been extensively replicated.)

On the story problem above, most doctors estimate the probability to be between 70% and 80%, which is wildly incorrect.

Here’s an alternate version of the problem on which doctors fare somewhat better:

> 10 out of 1,000 women at age forty who participate in routine screening have breast cancer. 800 out of 1,000 women with breast cancer will get positive mammographies. 96 out of 1,000 women without breast cancer will also get positive mammographies. If 1,000 women in this age group undergo a routine screening, about what fraction of women with positive mammographies will actually have breast cancer?

And finally, here’s the problem on which doctors fare best of all, with 46%— nearly half—arriving at the correct answer:

> 100 out of 10,000 women at age forty who participate in routine screening have breast cancer. 80 of every 100 women with breast cancer will get a positive mammography. 950 out of 9,900 women without breast cancer will also get a positive mammography. If 10,000 women in this age group undergo a routine screening, about what fraction of women with positive mammographies will actually have breast cancer?

The correct answer is 7.8%, obtained as follows: Out of 10,000 women, 100 have breast cancer; 80 of those 100 have positive mammographies. From the same 10,000 women, 9,900 will not have breast cancer and of those 9,900 women, 950 will also get positive mammographies. This makes the total number of women with positive mammographies 950 + 80 or 1,030. Of those 1,030 women with positive mammographies, 80 will have cancer. Expressed as a proportion, this is 80/1,030 or 0.07767 or 7.8%.

To put it another way, before the mammography screening, the 10,000 women can be divided into two groups:

-   Group 1: 100 women _with_ breast cancer.
-   Group 2: 9,900 women _without_ breast cancer.

Summing these two groups gives a total of 10,000 patients, confirming that none have been lost in the math. After the mammography, the women can be divided into four groups:

-   Group _A_: 80 women _with_ breast cancer and a _positive_ mammo­graphy.
-   Group _B_: 20 women _with_ breast cancer and a _negative_ mammo­graphy.
-   Group _C_: 950 women _without_ breast cancer and a _positive_ mammo­graphy.
-   Group _D_: 8,950 women _without_ breast cancer and a _negative_ mammo­graphy.

The sum of groups _A_ and _B_, the groups with breast cancer, corresponds to group 1; and the sum of groups _C_ and _D_, the groups without breast cancer, corresponds to group 2. If you administer a mammography to 10,000 patients, then out of the 1,030 with positive mammographies, eighty of those positive-mammography patients will have cancer. This is the correct answer, the answer a doctor should give a positive-mammography patient if she asks about the chance she has breast cancer; if thirteen patients ask this question, roughly one out of those thirteen will have cancer.

The most common mistake is to ignore the original fraction of women with breast cancer, and the fraction of women without breast cancer who receive false positives, and focus only on the fraction of women with breast cancer who get positive results. For example, the vast majority of doctors in these studies seem to have thought that if around 80% of women with breast cancer have positive mammographies, then the probability of a woman with a positive mammography having breast cancer must be around 80%.

Figuring out the final answer always requires _all three_ pieces of information—the percentage of women with breast cancer, the percentage of women without breast cancer who receive false positives, and the percentage of women with breast cancer who receive (correct) positives.

The original proportion of patients with breast cancer is known as the _prior probability_. The chance that a patient with breast cancer gets a positive mammography, and the chance that a patient without breast cancer gets a positive mammography, are known as the two _conditional probabilities_. Collectively, this initial information is known as _the priors_. The final answer—the estimated probability that a patient has breast cancer, given that we know she has a positive result on her mammography—is known as the _revised probability_ or the _posterior probability_. What we’ve just seen is that the posterior probability depends in part on the prior probability.

To see that the final answer always depends on the original fraction of women with breast cancer, consider an alternate universe in which only one woman out of a million has breast cancer. Even if mammography in this world detects breast cancer in 8 out of 10 cases, while returning a false positive on a woman without breast cancer in only 1 out of 10 cases, there will still be a hundred thousand false positives for every real case of cancer detected. The original probability that a woman has cancer is so extremely low that, although a positive result on the mammography does _increase_ the estimated probability, the probability isn’t increased to certainty or even “a noticeable chance”; the probability goes from 1:1,000,000 to 1:100,000.

What this demonstrates is that the mammography result doesn’t _replace_ your old information about the patient’s chance of having cancer; the mammography _slides_ the estimated probability in the direction of the result. A positive result slides the original probability upward; a negative result slides the probability downward. For example, in the original problem where 1% of the women have cancer, 80% of women with cancer get positive mammographies, and 9.6% of women without cancer get positive mammographies, a positive result on the mammography _slides_ the 1% chance upward to 7.8%.

Most people encountering problems of this type for the first time carry out the mental operation of _replacing_ the original 1% probability with the 80% probability that a woman with cancer gets a positive mammography. It may seem like a good idea, but it just doesn’t work. “The probability that a woman with a positive mammography has breast cancer” is not at all the same thing as “the probability that a woman with breast cancer has a positive mammography”; they are as unlike as apples and cheese.

Why did the Bayesian reasoner cross the road?

You need more information to answer this question.

Suppose that a barrel contains many small plastic eggs. Some eggs are painted red and some are painted blue. 40% of the eggs in the bin contain pearls, and 60% contain nothing. 30% of eggs containing pearls are painted blue, and 10% of eggs containing nothing are painted blue. What is the probability that a blue egg contains a pearl? For this example the arithmetic is simple enough that you may be able to do it in your head, and I would suggest trying to do so.

A more compact way of specifying the problem:

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td><em>P</em>(pearl)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>40%</td></tr><tr><td><em>P</em>(blue|pearl)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>30%</td></tr><tr><td><em>P</em>(blue|¬pearl)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>10%</td></tr><tr><td><em>P</em>(pearl|blue)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>?</td></tr></tbody></table>

The symbol “¬” is shorthand for “not,” so ¬pearl reads “not pearl.”

The notation _P_(blue|pearl) is shorthand for “the probability of blue given pearl” or “the probability that an egg is painted blue, given that the egg contains a pearl.” The item on the right side is what you _already know_ or the _premise_, and the item on the left side is the _implication_ or _conclusion_. If we have _P_(blue|pearl) = 30%, and we _already know_ that some egg contains a pearl, then we can _conclude_ there is a 30% chance that the egg is painted blue. Thus, the final fact we’re looking for—“the chance that a blue egg contains a pearl” or “the probability that an egg contains a pearl, if we know the egg is painted blue”—reads _P_(pearl|blue).

40% of the eggs contain pearls, and 60% of the eggs contain nothing. 30% of the eggs containing pearls are painted blue, so 12% of the eggs altogether contain pearls and are painted blue. 10% of the eggs containing nothing are painted blue, so altogether 6% of the eggs contain nothing and are painted blue. A total of 18% of the eggs are painted blue, and a total of 12% of the eggs are painted blue and contain pearls, so the chance a blue egg contains a pearl is 12/18 or 2/3 or around 67%.

As before, we can see the necessity of all three pieces of information by considering extreme cases. In a (large) barrel in which only one egg out of a thousand contains a pearl, knowing that an egg is painted blue slides the probability from 0.1% to 0.3% (instead of sliding the probability from 40% to 67%). Similarly, if 999 out of 1,000 eggs contain pearls, knowing that an egg is blue slides the probability from 99.9% to 99.966%; the probability that the egg does _not_ contain a pearl goes from 1/1,000 to around 1/3,000.

On the pearl-egg problem, most respondents unfamiliar with Bayesian reasoning would probably respond that the probability a blue egg contains a pearl is 30%, or perhaps 20% (the 30% chance of a true positive minus the 10% chance of a false positive). Even if this mental operation seems like a good idea at the time, it makes no sense in terms of the question asked. It’s like the experiment in which you ask a second-grader: “If eighteen people get on a bus, and then seven more people get on the bus, how old is the bus driver?” Many second-graders will respond: “Twenty-five.” They understand when they’re being prompted to carry out a particular mental procedure, but they haven’t quite connected the procedure to reality. Similarly, to find the probability that a woman with a positive mammography has breast cancer, it makes no sense whatsoever to _replace_ the original probability that the woman has cancer with the probability that a woman with breast cancer gets a positive mammography. Neither can you subtract the probability of a false positive from the probability of the true positive. These operations are as wildly irrelevant as adding the number of people on the bus to find the age of the bus driver.

A study by Gigerenzer and Hoffrage in 1995 showed that some ways of phrasing story problems are much more evocative of correct Bayesian reasoning.[4](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote4) The least evocative phrasing used probabilities. A slightly more evocative phrasing used frequencies instead of probabilities; the problem remained the same, but instead of saying that 1% of women had breast cancer, one would say that 1 out of 100 women had breast cancer, that 80 out of 100 women with breast cancer would get a positive mammography, and so on. Why did a higher proportion of subjects display Bayesian reasoning on this problem? Probably because saying “1 out of 100 women” encourages you to concretely visualize _X_ women with cancer, leading you to visualize _X_ women with cancer and a positive mammography, etc.

The most effective presentation found so far is what’s known as _natural frequencies_—saying that 40 out of 100 eggs contain pearls, 12 out of 40 eggs containing pearls are painted blue, and 6 out of 60 eggs containing nothing are painted blue. A _natural frequencies_ presentation is one in which the information about the prior probability is included in presenting the conditional probabilities. If you were just learning about the eggs’ conditional probabilities through natural experimentation, you would—in the course of cracking open a hundred eggs—crack open around 40 eggs containing pearls, of which 12 eggs would be painted blue, while cracking open 60 eggs containing nothing, of which about 6 would be painted blue. In the course of learning the conditional probabilities, you’d see examples of blue eggs containing pearls about twice as often as you saw examples of blue eggs containing nothing.

Unfortunately, while natural frequencies are a step in the right direction, it probably won’t be enough. When problems are presented in natural frequencies, the proportion of people using Bayesian reasoning rises to around half. A big improvement, but not big enough when you’re talking about real doctors and real patients.

What if the priors I want aren’t available on Kazaa?

There’s a small, cluttered antique shop in a back alley of San Francisco’s Chinatown. _Don’t ask about the bronze rat._

Actually, priors are true or false just like the final answer—they reflect reality and can be judged by comparing them against reality. For example, if you think that 920 out of 10,000 women in a sample have breast cancer, and the actual number is 100 out of 10,000, then your priors are wrong. For our particular problem, the priors might have been established by three studies—a study on the case histories of women with breast cancer to see how many of them tested positive on a mammography, a study on women without breast cancer to see how many of them test positive on a mammography, and an epidemiological study on the prevalence of breast cancer in some specific demographic.

The probability _P_(_A_,_B_) is the same as _P_(_B_,_A_), but _P_(_A_|_B_) is not the same thing as _P_(_B_|_A_), and _P_(_A_,_B_) is completely different from _P_(_A_|_B_). It’s a common confusion to mix up some or all of these quantities.

To get acquainted with all the relationships between them, we’ll play “follow the degrees of freedom.” For example, the two quantities _P_(cancer) and _P_(¬cancer) have one degree of freedom between them, because of the general law _P_(_A_) + _P_(¬_A_) = 1. If you know that _P_(¬cancer) = 0.99, you can obtain _P_(cancer) = 1 − _P_(¬cancer) = 0.01.

The quantities _P_(positive|cancer) and _P_(¬positive|cancer) also have only one degree of freedom between them; either a woman with breast cancer gets a positive mammography or she doesn’t. On the other hand, _P_(positive|cancer) and _P_(positive|¬cancer) have two degrees of freedom. You can have a mammography test that returns positive for 80% of cancer patients and 9.6% of healthy patients, or that returns positive for 70% of cancer patients and 2% of healthy patients, or even a health test that returns “positive” for 30% of cancer patients and 92% of healthy patients. The two quantities, the output of the mammography test for cancer patients and the output of the mammography test for healthy patients, are in mathematical terms independent; one cannot be obtained from the other in any way, and so they have two degrees of freedom between them.

What about _P_(positive, cancer), _P_(positive|cancer), and _P_(cancer)? Here we have three quantities; how many degrees of freedom are there? In this case the equation that must hold is

_P_(positive, cancer) = _P_(positive|cancer) × _P_(cancer).

This equality reduces the degrees of freedom by one. If we know the fraction of patients with cancer, and the chance that a cancer patient has a positive mammography, we can deduce the fraction of patients who have breast cancer _and_ a positive mammography by multiplying.

Similarly, if we know the number of patients with breast cancer and positive mammographies, and also the number of patients with breast cancer, we can estimate the chance that a woman with breast cancer gets a positive mammography by dividing: _P_(positive|cancer) = _P_(positive, cancer)/_P_(cancer). In fact, this is exactly how such medical diagnostic tests are calibrated; you do a study on 8,520 women with breast cancer and see that there are 6,816 (or thereabouts) women with breast cancer and positive mammographies, then divide 6,816 by 8,520 to find that 80% of women with breast cancer had positive mammographies. (Incidentally, if you accidentally divide 8,520 by 6,816 instead of the other way around, your calculations will start doing strange things, such as insisting that 125% of women with breast cancer and positive mammographies have breast cancer. This is a common mistake in carrying out Bayesian arithmetic, in my experience.) And finally, if you know _P_(positive, cancer) and _P_(positive|cancer), you can deduce how many cancer patients there must have been originally. There are two degrees of freedom shared out among the three quantities; if we know any two, we can deduce the third.

How about _P_(positive), _P_(positive, cancer), and _P_(positive, ¬cancer)? Again there are only two degrees of freedom among these three variables. The equation occupying the extra degree of freedom is

_P_(positive) = _P_(positive, cancer) + _P_(positive, ¬cancer) .

This is how _P_(positive) is computed to begin with; we figure out the number of women with breast cancer who have positive mammographies, and the number of women without breast cancer who have positive mammographies, then add them together to get the total number of women with positive mammographies. It would be very strange to go out and conduct a study to determine the number of women with positive mammographies— just that one number and nothing else—but in theory you could do so. And if you then conducted another study and found the number of those women who had positive mammographies _and_ breast cancer, you would also know the number of women with positive mammographies and no breast cancer—either a woman with a positive mammography has breast cancer or she doesn’t. In general, _P_(_A_,_B_) + _P_(_A_,¬_B_) = _P_(_A_). Symmetrically, _P_(_A_,_B_) + _P_(¬_A_,_B_) = _P_(_B_).

What about _P_(positive, cancer), _P_(positive, ¬cancer), _P_(¬positive, cancer), and _P_(¬positive, ¬cancer)? You might at first be tempted to think that there are only two degrees of freedom for these four quantities—that you can, for example, get _P_(positive, ¬cancer) by multiplying _P_(positive) × _P_(¬cancer), and thus that all four quantities can be found given only the two quantities _P_(positive) and _P_(cancer). This is not the case! _P_(positive, ¬cancer) = _P_(positive) × _P_(¬cancer) only if the two probabilities are _statistically independent_—if the chance that a woman has breast cancer has no bearing on whether she has a positive mammography. This amounts to requiring that the two conditional probabilities be equal to each other—a requirement which would eliminate one degree of freedom. If you remember that these four quantities are the groups _A_, _B_, _C_, and _D_, you can look over those four groups and realize that, in theory, you can put any number of people into the four groups. If you start with a group of 80 women with breast cancer and positive mammographies, there’s no reason why you can’t add another group of 500 women with breast cancer and negative mammographies, followed by a group of 3 women without breast cancer and negative mammographies, and so on. So now it seems like the four quantities have four degrees of freedom. And they would, except that in expressing them as _probabilities_, we need to normalize them to _fractions_ of the complete group, which adds the constraint that _P_(positive, cancer) + _P_(positive, ¬cancer) + _P_(¬positive, cancer) + _P_(¬positive, ¬cancer) = 1. This equation takes up one degree of freedom, leaving three degrees of freedom among the four quantities. If you specify the _fractions_ of women in groups _A_, _B_, and _D_, you can deduce the fraction of women in group _C_.

Given the four groups _A_, _B_, _C_, and _D_, it is very straightforward to compute everything else:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\mathrm{cancer}) &= \frac{A + B}{A + B + C + D} \\
P( \neg \mathrm{positive} \mid \mathrm{cancer}) &= \frac{B}{A + B ,}
\end{aligned}
$$

and so on. Since { _A_, _B_, _C_, _D_} contains three degrees of freedom, it follows that the entire set of probabilities relating cancer rates to test results contains only three degrees of freedom. Remember that in our problems we always needed _three_ pieces of information—the prior probability and the two conditional probabilities—which, indeed, have three degrees of freedom among them. Actually, for Bayesian problems, any three quantities with three degrees of freedom between them should logically specify the entire problem.

_The probability that a test gives a true positive_ divided by _the probability that a test gives a false positive_ is known as the _likelihood ratio_ of that test. The likelihood ratio for a positive result summarizes how much a positive result will slide the prior probability. Does the likelihood ratio of a medical test then sum up everything there is to know about the usefulness of the test?

No, it does not! The likelihood ratio sums up everything there is to know about the _meaning_ of a _positive_ result on the medical test, but the meaning of a _negative_ result on the test is not specified, nor is the frequency with which the test is useful. For example, a mammography with a hit rate of 80% for patients with breast cancer and a false positive rate of 9.6% for healthy patients has the same likelihood ratio as a test with an 8% hit rate and a false positive rate of 0.96%. Although these two tests have the same likelihood ratio, the first test is more useful in every way—it detects disease more often, and a negative result is stronger evidence of health.

Suppose that you apply _two_ tests for breast cancer in succession—say, a standard mammography and also some other test which is _independent_ of mammography. Since I don’t know of any such test that is independent of mammography, I’ll invent one for the purpose of this problem, and call it the Tams-Braylor Division Test, which checks to see if any cells are dividing more rapidly than other cells. We’ll suppose that the Tams-Braylor gives a true positive for 90% of patients with breast cancer, and gives a false positive for 5% of patients without cancer. Let’s say the prior prevalence of breast cancer is 1%. If a patient gets a positive result on her mammography _and_ her Tams-Braylor, what is the revised probability she has breast cancer?

One way to solve this problem would be to take the revised probability for a positive mammography, which we already calculated as 7.8%, and plug that into the Tams-Braylor test as the new prior probability. If we do this, we find that the result comes out to 60%.

Suppose that the prior prevalence of breast cancer in a demographic is 1%. Suppose that we, as doctors, have a repertoire of three independent tests for breast cancer. Our first test, test _A_, a mammography, has a likelihood ratio of 80%/9.6% = 8.33. The second test, test _B_, has a likelihood ratio of 18.0 (for example, from 90% versus 5%); and the third test, test _C_, has a likelihood ratio of 3.5 (which could be from 70% versus 20%, or from 35% versus 10%; it makes no difference). Suppose a patient gets a positive result on all three tests. What is the probability the patient has breast cancer?

Here’s a fun trick for simplifying the bookkeeping. If the prior prevalence of breast cancer in a demographic is 1%, then 1 out of 100 women have breast cancer, and 99 out of 100 women do not have breast cancer. So if we rewrite the _probability_ of 1% as an _odds ratio_, the odds are 1:99.

And the likelihood ratios of the three tests _A_, _B_, and _C_ are:

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td>8.33 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>25 : 3</td></tr><tr><td>18.0 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>18 : 1</td></tr><tr><td>3.5 : 1</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td>7 : 2 .</td></tr></tbody></table>

The _odds_ for women with breast cancer who score positive on all three tests, versus women without breast cancer who score positive on all three tests, will equal:

1 × 25 × 18 × 7 : 99 × 3 × 1 × 2 = 3,150 : 594 .

To recover the probability from the odds, we just write:

3,150/(3,150 + 594) = 84% .

This always works regardless of how the odds ratios are written; i.e., 8.33:1 is just the same as 25:3 or 75:9. It doesn’t matter in what order the tests are administered, or in what order the results are computed. The proof is left as an exercise for the reader.

E. T. Jaynes, in _Probability Theory With Applications in Science and Engi­neer­ing_, suggests that credibility and evidence should be measured in decibels.[5](https://www.readthesequences.com/An-Intuitive-Explanation-Of-Bayess-Theorem#footnote5)

Decibels?

Decibels are used for measuring exponential differences of intensity. For example, if the sound from an automobile horn carries 10,000 times as much energy (per square meter per second) as the sound from an alarm clock, the automobile horn would be 40 decibels louder. The sound of a bird singing might carry 1,000 times less energy than an alarm clock, and hence would be 30 decibels softer. To get the number of decibels, you take the logarithm base 10 and multiply by 10:

decibels = 10 log10 (intensity)

or

intensity = 10decibels/10 .

Suppose we start with a prior probability of 1% that a woman has breast cancer, corresponding to an odds ratio of 1:99. And then we administer three tests of likelihood ratios 25:3, 18:1, and 7:2. You could multiply those numbers… or you _could_ just add their logarithms:

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">1/99</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>−20</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">25/3</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>9</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">18/1</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>13</td></tr><tr><td>10 log<sub>10</sub>(<span class="fraction">7/2</span>)</td><td class="equal_sign">≈</td><td>5 .</td></tr></tbody></table>

It starts out as fairly unlikely that a woman has breast cancer—our credibility level is at −20 decibels. Then three test results come in, corresponding to 9, 13, and 5 decibels of evidence. This raises the credibility level by a total of 27 decibels, meaning that the prior credibility of −20 decibels goes to a posterior credibility of 7 decibels. So the odds go from 1:99 to 5:1, and the probability goes from 1% to around 83%.

$$

_which is_

_P_(cancer|positive) .

The fully general form of this calculation is known as _Bayes’s Theorem_ or _Bayes’s Rule_.

Bayes’s Theorem:

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

When there is some phenomenon _A_ that we want to investigate, and an observation _X_ that is evidence about _A_—for example, in the previous example, _A_ is breast cancer and _X_ is a positive mammography—Bayes’s Theorem tells us how we should _update_ our probability of _A_, given the _new evidence_ _X_.

By this point, Bayes’s Theorem may seem blatantly obvious or even tautological, rather than exciting and new. If so, this introduction has _entirely succeeded_ in its purpose.

Bayes’s Theorem describes what makes something “evidence” and how much evidence it is. Statistical models are judged by comparison to the _Bayesian method_ because, in statistics, the Bayesian method is as good as it gets—the Bayesian method defines the maximum amount of mileage you can get out of a given piece of evidence, in the same way that thermodynamics defines the maximum amount of work you can get out of a temperature differential. This is why you hear cognitive scientists talking about _Bayesian reasoners_. In cognitive science, _Bayesian reasoner_ is the technically precise code word that we use to mean _rational mind_.

There are also a number of general heuristics about human reasoning that you can learn from looking at Bayes’s Theorem.

For example, in many discussions of Bayes’s Theorem, you may hear cognitive psychologists saying that people _do not take prior frequencies sufficiently into account_, meaning that when people approach a problem where there’s some evidence _X_ indicating that condition _A_ might hold true, they tend to judge _A_’s likelihood solely by how well the evidence _X_ seems to match _A_, without taking into account the prior frequency of _A_. If you think, for example, that under the mammography example, the woman’s chance of having breast cancer is in the range of 70%–80%, then this kind of reasoning is insensitive to the prior frequency given in the problem; it doesn’t notice whether 1% of women or 10% of women start out having breast cancer. “Pay more attention to the prior frequency!” is one of the many things that humans need to bear in mind to partially compensate for our built-in inadequacies.

A related error is to pay too much attention to _P_(_X_|_A_) and not enough to _P_(_X_|¬_A_) when determining how much evidence _X_ is for _A_. The degree to which a result _X_ is _evidence for_ _A_ depends not only on the strength of the statement _we’d expect to see result X if A were true_, but also on the strength of the statement _we **wouldn’t** expect to see result X if A weren’t true_. For example, if it is raining, this very strongly implies the grass is wet—_P_(wetgrass|rain) ≈ 1— but seeing that the grass is wet doesn’t necessarily mean that it has just rained; perhaps the sprinkler was turned on, or you’re looking at the early morning dew. Since _P_(wetgrass|¬rain) is substantially greater than zero, _P_(rain|wetgrass) is substantially less than one. On the other hand, if the grass was _never_ wet when it wasn’t raining, then knowing that the grass was wet would _always_ show that it was raining, _P_(rain|wetgrass) ≈ 1, even if _P_(wetgrass|rain) = 50%; that is, even if the grass only got wet 50% of the times it rained. Evidence is always the result of the _differential_ between the two conditional probabilities. _Strong_ evidence is not the product of a very high probability that A leads to _X_, but the product of a very _low_ probability that _not-A_ could have led to _X_.

The _Bayesian revolution in the sciences_ is fueled, not only by more and more cognitive scientists suddenly noticing that mental phenomena have Bayesian structure in them; not only by scientists in every field learning to judge their statistical methods by comparison with the Bayesian method; but also by the idea that _science itself is a special case of Bayes’s Theorem; experimental evidence is Bayesian evidence_. The Bayesian revolutionaries hold that when you perform an experiment and get evidence that “confirms” or “disconfirms” your theory, this confirmation and disconfirmation is governed by the Bayesian rules. For example, you have to take into account not only whether your theory predicts the phenomenon, but whether other possible explanations also predict the phenomenon.

Previously, the most popular philosophy of science was probably Karl Popper’s _falsificationism_—this is the old philosophy that the Bayesian revolution is currently dethroning. Karl Popper’s idea that theories can be definitely falsified, but never definitely confirmed, is yet another special case of the Bayesian rules; if _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1—if the theory makes a definite prediction—then observing ¬_X_ very strongly falsifies _A_. On the other hand, if _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1, and we observe _X_, this doesn’t definitely confirm the theory; there might be some other condition _B_ such that _P_(_X_|_B_) ≈ 1, in which case observing _X_ doesn’t favor _A_ over _B_. For observing _X_ to definitely confirm A, we would have to know, not that _P_(_X_|_A_) ≈ 1, but that _P_(_X_|¬_A_) ≈ 0, which is something that we can’t know because we can’t range over all possible alternative explanations. For example, when Einstein’s theory of General Relativity toppled Newton’s incredibly well-confirmed theory of gravity, it turned out that all of Newton’s predictions were just a special case of Einstein’s predictions.

You can even formalize Popper’s philosophy mathematically. The likelihood ratio for _X_, the quantity _P_(_X_|_A_)/_P_(_X_|¬_A_), determines how much observing _X_ slides the probability for _A_; the likelihood ratio is what says how strong _X_ is as evidence. Well, in your theory _A_, you can predict _X_ with probability 1, if you like; but you can’t control the denominator of the likelihood ratio, _P_(_X_|¬_A_)—there will always be some alternative theories that also predict _X_, and while we go with the simplest theory that fits the current evidence, you may someday encounter some evidence that an alternative theory predicts but your theory does not. That’s the hidden gotcha that toppled Newton’s theory of gravity. So there’s a limit on how much mileage you can get from successful predictions; there’s a limit on how high the likelihood ratio goes for _confirmatory_ evidence.

On the other hand, if you encounter some piece of evidence _Y_ that is definitely _not_ predicted by your theory, this is _enormously_ strong evidence against your theory. If _P_(_Y_|_A_) is infinitesimal, then the likelihood ratio will also be infinitesimal. For example, if _P_(_Y_|_A_) is 0.0001%, and _P_(_Y_|¬_A_) is 1%, then the likelihood ratio _P_(_Y_|_A_)/_P_(_Y_|¬_A_) will be 1:10,000. That’s −40 decibels of evidence! Or, flipping the likelihood ratio, if _P_(_Y_|_A_) is _very small_, then _P_(_Y_|¬_A_)/_P_(_Y_|_A_) will be very large, meaning that observing _Y_ greatly favors ¬_A_ over _A_. Falsification is much stronger than confirmation. This is a consequence of the earlier point that _very strong_ evidence is not the product of a very high probability that _A_ leads to _X_, but the product of a very _low_ probability that _not-A_ could have led to _X_. This is the precise Bayesian rule that underlies the heuristic value of Popper’s falsificationism.

Similarly, Popper’s dictum that an idea must be falsifiable can be interpreted as a manifestation of the Bayesian conservation-of-probability rule; if a result _X_ is positive evidence for the theory, then the result ¬_X_ would have disconfirmed the theory to some extent. If you try to interpret both _X_ and ¬_X_ as “confirming” the theory, the Bayesian rules say this is impossible! To increase the probability of a theory you _must_ expose it to tests that can potentially decrease its probability; this is not just a rule for detecting would-be cheaters in the social process of science, but a consequence of Bayesian probability theory. On the other hand, Popper’s idea that there is _only_ falsification and _no such thing_ as confirmation turns out to be incorrect. Bayes’s Theorem shows that falsification is _very_ strong evidence compared to confirmation, but falsification is still probabilistic in nature; it is not governed by fundamentally different rules from confirmation, as Popper argued.

So we find that many phenomena in the cognitive sciences, plus the statistical methods used by scientists, plus the scientific method itself, are all turning out to be special cases of Bayes’s Theorem. Hence the Bayesian revolution.

Having introduced Bayes’s Theorem explicitly, we can explicitly discuss its components.

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

We’ll start with _P_(_A_|_X_). If you ever find yourself getting confused about what’s _A_ and what’s _X_ in Bayes’s Theorem, start with _P_(_A_|_X_) on the left side of the equation; that’s the simplest part to interpret. In _P_(_A_|_X_), _A_ is the thing we want to know about. _X_ is how we’re observing it; _X_ is the evidence we’re using to make inferences about _A_. Remember that for every expression _P_(_Q_|_P_), we want to know about the probability for _Q_ given _P_, the degree to which _P_ implies _Q_—a more sensible notation, which it is now too late to adopt, would be _P_(_Q_ ← _P_).

_P_(_Q_|_P_) is closely related to _P_(_Q_,_P_), but they are not identical. Expressed as a probability or a fraction, _P_(_Q_,_P_) is the proportion of things that have property _Q_ and property _P_ among all things; e.g., the proportion of “women with breast cancer and a positive mammography” within the group of all women. If the total number of women is 10,000, and 80 women have breast cancer and a positive mammography, then _P_(_Q_,_P_) is 80/10,000 = 0.8%. You might say that the absolute quantity, 80, is being normalized to a probability relative to the group of all women. Or to make it clearer, suppose that there’s a group of 641 women with breast cancer and a positive mammography within a total sample group of 89,031 women. Six hundred and forty-one is the absolute quantity. If you pick out a random woman from the entire sample, then the probability you’ll pick a woman with breast cancer and a positive mammography is _P_(_Q_,_P_), or 0.72% (in this example).

On the other hand, _P_(_Q_|_P_) is the proportion of things that have property _Q_ and property _P_ among _all things that have P_; e.g., the proportion of women with breast cancer and a positive mammography within the group of _all women with positive mammographies_. If there are 641 women with breast cancer and positive mammographies, 7,915 women with positive mammographies, and 89,031 women, then _P_(_Q_,_P_) is the probability of getting one of those 641 women if you’re picking at random from the entire group of 89,031, while _P_(_Q_|_P_) is the probability of getting one of those 641 women if you’re picking at random from the smaller group of 7,915.

In a sense, _P_(_Q_|_P_) really means _P_(_Q_,_P_|_P_), but specifying the extra _P_ all the time would be redundant. You already _know_ it has property _P_, so the property you’re _investigating_ is _Q_—even though you’re looking at the size of group (_Q_,_P_) within group _P_, not the size of group _Q_ within group _P_(which would be nonsense). This is what it means to take the property on the right-hand side as _given_; it means you know you’re working only within the group of things that have property _P_. When you constrict your focus of attention to see only this smaller group, many other probabilities change. If you’re taking _P_ as _given_, then _P_(_Q_,_P_) equals just _P_(_Q_)—at least, _relative to the group P_ . The _old_ _P_(_Q_), the frequency of “things that have property _Q_ within the entire sample,” is revised to the new frequency of “things that have property _Q_ within the subsample of things that have property _P_.” If _P_ is _given_, if _P_ is our entire world, then looking for (_Q_,_P_) is the same as looking for just _Q_.

If you constrict your focus of attention to only the population of eggs that are painted blue, then suddenly “the probability that an egg contains a pearl” becomes a different number; this proportion is different for the population of blue eggs than the population of all eggs. The _given_, the property that constricts our focus of attention, is always on the _right_ side of _P_(_Q_|_P_); the _P_ becomes our world, the entire thing we see, and on the other side of the “given” _P_ always has probability 1—that is what it means to take _P_ as given. So _P_(_Q_|_P_) means “If _P_ has probability 1, what is the probability of _Q_?” or “If we constrict our attention to only things or events where _P_ is true, what is the probability of _Q_?” The statement _Q_, on the other side of the given, is _not_ certain—its probability may be 10% or 90% or any other number. So when you use Bayes’s Theorem, and you write the part on the left side as _P_(_A_|_X_)—how to _update_ the probability of _A_ after seeing _X_, the new probability of _A_ _given_ that we know _X_, the degree to which _X implies A_—you can tell that _X_ is always the _observation_ or the _evidence_, and _A_ is the property being investigated, the thing you want to know about.

The right side of Bayes’s Theorem is derived from the left side through these steps:

<table class="equation"><tbody><tr><td><em>P</em>(<em>A</em>|<em>X</em>)</td><td class="equal_sign">=</td><td><em>P</em>(<em>A</em>|<em>X</em>)</td></tr></tbody></table>

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X,A)}{P(X)}
$$

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X,A)}{P(X,A) + P(X, \neg A)}
$$

$$
P(A \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A)}{P(X \mid A)  \times  P(A) + P(X \mid  \neg A)  \times  P( \neg A)}
$$

Once the derivation is finished, all the implications on the right side of the equation are of the form _P_(_X_|_A_) or _P_(_X_|¬_A_), while the implication on the left side is _P_(_A_|_X_). The symmetry arises because the elementary _causal relations_ are generally implications from facts to observations, e.g., from breast cancer to positive mammography. The elementary _steps in reasoning_ are generally implications from observations to facts, e.g., from a positive mammography to breast cancer. The left side of Bayes’s Theorem is an elementary _inferential_ step from the observation of positive mammography to the conclusion of an increased probability of breast cancer. Implication is written right-to-left, so we write _P_(cancer|positive) on the left side of the equation. The right side of Bayes’s Theorem describes the elementary _causal_ steps—for example, from breast cancer to a positive mammography—and so the implications on the right side of Bayes’s Theorem take the form _P_(positive|cancer) or _P_(positive|¬cancer).

And that’s Bayes’s Theorem. Rational inference on the left end, physical causality on the right end; an equation with mind on one side and reality on the other. Remember how the scientific method turned out to be a special case of Bayes’s Theorem? If you wanted to put it poetically, you could say that Bayes’s Theorem binds reasoning into the physical universe.

Okay, we’re done.
