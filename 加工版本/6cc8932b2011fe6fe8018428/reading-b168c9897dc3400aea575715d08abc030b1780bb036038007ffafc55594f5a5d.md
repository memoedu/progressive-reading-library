# Some stories about comparative advantage

---

## 正文

_这篇文章是我为 [Figuring Good Out](http://a-nice-place-to-live.blogspot.com/2014/12/figuring-good-out-december.html) 这个 effective altruism blogging carnival 的十二月刊所写的文章。本月的主题是“blind spots”。_

_感谢 [Ruthie](http://www.ruthiebyers.com/) 引发了促成这篇文章的讨论，并通读了一份草稿。_

* * *

我最喜欢的一个关于“effective altruist blind spot”的故事是这样的。

去年八月，Ruthie 和我参加了 Effective Altruism Summit 的招待会。那时 Ruthie 对这个 community 还不太了解，所以她向其他与会者打听情况。

“你们的 EA group 里都是些什么人？”她问他。

“噢，各种各样的人都有！”他回答道。“Mathematicians，还有 economists，还有 philosophers，还有 computer scientists……”

那位朋友似乎没有想到，这些人基本上都是同一类人。

* * *

不过，我其实不打算把 diversity 当作 effective altruism 的一个 blind spot 来谈。我不认为它_本身_就是：虽然我们目前并不十分 diverse，但我不认为这是因为大家不在乎。我交谈过的许多人都同意，如果这个 movement 少一些 monoculture 的色彩，就会更好。这个 blind spot 比这更微妙。

问题首先在于，这些人中很少有人觉得自己有 levers 能对此做些什么。他们都忙着取得 good grades，或 earning to give，或攀登 academic totem pole，或做其他耗尽所有时间的事情，而这些事情并不能让他们处于很适合 reach out to 与自己不同的人的位置。

对每个人而言，这听起来都颇为合理。但从整体来看，似乎几乎没有人在采取积极行动，让传统 EA demographics 之外的人产生兴趣。而 on the margin，多做一些这样的事情，似乎远比取得 better grades 或 higher expected income 更令人振奋。

我认为，人们在问自己这样一个问题（而且问得对）：“我会比其他 EA-folk 更擅长 expanding EA’s diversity 吗？”但我怀疑，他们随后拿来与自己 comparing 的，是按照自己那些最 extroverted、拥有最广 social circles 的朋友来 modeled 的 hypothetical EA-folk。于是他们认定，应该由一些更 extroverted 的 EA-folk 来承担这件事。

但恰恰因为 effective altruism 目前并不十分 diverse，这个 movement 实际上并不_拥有_那些极其 extroverted 的 socialite 人物！只有许多非常勤奋好学、忙于大学学业或工作的 philosophers 和 mathematicians。我们的 coordination problems 就由此而来。人们当然应该考虑自己的 comparative advantage——但他们需要确保，自己是在与正确的那一群人 comparing。

* * *

Centre for Effective Altruism 目前正在为_五个 positions_招聘。

我知道，他们以前曾[提到](https://80000hours.org/2014/11/mid-year-review-september-2014/)，无法按自己希望的速度 hire。我不知道目前这一轮会不会也如此——最近情况似乎在[好转](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/permalink/791112014278491/)。

我确实知道，CEA 正在提供 \$1,000 的 referral bonus，约为其 typical starting salary 的 4-5%。对于一家已经拥有广泛 media reach 的 nonprofit 来说，这笔 referral bonus 相当丰厚。

这令人惊讶：它表明，也许_想为 Centre for Effective Altruism 工作的 aspiring effective altruists 还不够多。_我能想到几个可能的原因：

1.  每个人都认为，[replaceability](https://80000hours.org/2012/03/the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1/) 意味着他们对 CEA 的 marginal contribution 是 zero。

2.  每个人都认为，effective altruism movement 中最 central 的 organization 所做的工作，出乎意料地并不十分 effective。

3.  每个人都认为，虽然他们会在 CEA 做的工作是 valuable 的，但那不会是他们的 comparative advantage，所以应该让别人来做。

我不认为 (1) 很有可能，因为 CEA 已经有过找不到足够多所需 talent 的记录；到现在，人们应该意识到，为他们工作并不是 replaceable 的。原因 (2) 似乎也值得怀疑；人们通常似乎会以其他方式 support CEA 的 mission，例如 donating money，或把其 employees 视为 high-status。

我认为最后一个原因最有可能。但如果有这么多人都在遵循自己的 comparative advantage，以至于 CEA 无法 fill 所需的 positions，那么其中至少有一些人一定弄错了！

* * *

我记得，Nir Eyal 最初请我帮助 run [Harvard College Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/)（当时名为 Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy）时，我有些困惑。难道不应该有其他更 qualified 的人来做这件事吗？因为我很确定自己会做得很糟。

嗯，我对了一半。“HHIP”差不多整个第一年都在一片混乱中摸索挣扎。我们 organized 了几场 talks 和 dinners，但这只能算是在最宽泛的意义上使用“organize”（以及“我们”）这个词。没有 continuity，也没有什么能让人们有理由真正来参加我们的 events。唯一挽救了 attendance 的，大概是我总也忍不住要说 effective altruism，这至少让一些人去听了一场 talk，看看我究竟在唠叨些什么。

不过，事情最终还是顺畅了起来，这在很大程度上要归功于 John Sturm 开始帮忙 organizing。如今 HCEA 已经有一个由六到八人组成的 board——其中一些人甚至可能拥有_真正的 organizational skills。_他们的 events 越来越多，以至于一度难以确保这些 events 在时间上都不 overlapped。

我当然不是 running HCEA 的 best fit。其他许多由更 proficient 的 leaders 创办的 student groups，在起步时都轻松得多。但我仍然很高兴，自己当时没有理会 comparative advantage，而是投入了 HCEA 的工作。我从一开始就在，让它能够步履蹒跚地运转起来，这一点很重要；这样 John，以及后来我们其他出色的 organizers，才能找到这个 group 并加入它。

* * *

我并不是想反对 comparative advantage 的整套理论。首先，那样我就太虚伪了，因为我自己选择了在 software industry 中 earning-to-give，而不是到 GiveWell 做一段试用，或抓住向 CEA 申请的机会。更广泛地说，我认为由 comparative advantage 驱动的 decisions 往往确实很好——当然比由 uninformed gut feeling、status-seeking、random guessing 或其他常见 decision-making _modi operandi_ 驱动的 decisions 更好。

但我确实认为，我们需要更细致地理解 comparative advantage heuristic，理解它在哪些情况下 succeeds，又在哪些情况下 fails 或 causes coordination problems。我自己还没有完全理清该如何看待 comparitive advantage。但希望这些 case studies 至少能帮助我们迈出第一步。

## 原文

_This post is my contribution to the December edition of [Figuring Good Out](http://a-nice-place-to-live.blogspot.com/2014/12/figuring-good-out-december.html), the effective altruism blogging carnival. This month’s topic is “blind spots.”_

_Thanks to [Ruthie](http://www.ruthiebyers.com/) for sparking the discussion that led to this post and for reading over a draft._

* * *

My favorite “effective altruist blind spot” story goes like this.

Last August, Ruthie and I attended the Effective Altruism Summit reception. Ruthie still didn’t have a good sense of the community at that point, so she was asking other attendees about it.

“What kind of people are in your EA group?” she asked him.

“Oh, all different kinds!” he replied. “Mathematicians, and economists, and philosophers, and computer scientists…”

It didn’t seem to occur to the fellow that these were all basically the same kind of person.

* * *

I’m actually not going to talk about diversity as a blind spot of effective altruism, though. I don’t think it is, _per se_: while we’re not very diverse right now, I don’t think it’s for want of caring. Plenty of people I’ve talked to agree that the movement would be better off as less of a monoculture. The blind spot is subtler than that.

It starts with the fact that few of these people feel like they have levers to do anything about it. They’re all busy getting good grades, or earning to give, or climbing the academic totem pole, or something else that sucks up all their time and doesn’t put them in a very good position to reach out to people different from themselves.

This sounds pretty reasonable for each individual person. But looking at the big picture, it seems like almost nobody is taking active steps to interest people outside the traditional EA demographics. And on the margin, more of that seems a lot more exciting than better grades or higher expected income.

I think that people are (correctly) asking themselves the question, “would I be better at expanding EA’s diversity than other EA-folk?” But then I suspect that they’re comparing themselves to hypothetical EA-folk modeled on their most extroverted friends, with the widest social circles. And they decide that some more extroverted EA-folk should be the ones to take this on.

But precisely because effective altruism isn’t very diverse right now, the movement doesn’t actually _have_ those incredibly extroverted socialite people! Just a lot of very studious philosophers and mathematicians who are busy with college or work. And that’s where our coordination problems come from. People should definitely be thinking about their comparative advantage—but they need to make sure they’re comparing to the right set of people.

* * *

The Centre for Effective Altruism is hiring for _five positions_ right now.

I know that they’ve previously [mentioned](https://80000hours.org/2014/11/mid-year-review-september-2014/) not being able to hire as fast as they want to. I don’t know if this current round will go similarly or not—things seemed to be [looking up](https://www.facebook.com/groups/effective.altruists/permalink/791112014278491/) recently.

I do know that CEA is offering a $1,000 referral bonus, which is about 4-5% of their typical starting salary. That’s pretty beefy for a referral bonus from a nonprofit with an already-wide media reach.

This is surprising: it suggests that perhaps _not enough aspiring effective altruists want to work for the Centre for Effective Altruism._ I can think of a couple possible reasons for this:

1.  Everyone thinks that [replaceability](https://80000hours.org/2012/03/the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1/) means their marginal contribution to CEA is zero.

2.  Everyone thinks that the work being done by the effective altruism movement’s most central organization is, against all odds, not very effective.

3.  Everyone thinks that although the work they would do at CEA is valuable, it wouldn’t be their comparative advantage, so they should let someone else do it.

I don’t think (1) is very likely, because CEA already has a track record of being unable to find as much talent as they want; people should realize by now that working for them is not replaceable. Reason (2) seems doubtful as well; people generally seem to support CEA’s mission in other ways, e.g. by donating money or treating its employees as high-status.

I think the last reason is the most likely. But if so many people are following their comparative advantage that CEA can’t fill the positions they need, at least some of those people must be wrong!

* * *

When Nir Eyal first asked me to help run [Harvard College Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/) (then Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy), I remember being a little puzzled. Shouldn’t there be someone else more qualified to do this? Because I was pretty sure I’d be terrible at it.

Well, I was half right. “HHIP” spent roughly its first year flailing around in utter disarray. We organized a couple talks and dinners, but only in the loosest sense of the word “organize” (and “we”). There was no continuity and nothing to give people a reason to actually go to our events. Probably the only thing that saved our attendance was my inability ever to shut up about effective altruism, which at least got some people to go to one talk to see what I was blabbing about.

Eventually, though, things smoothed out, largely thanks to John Sturm starting to help with the organizing. Now HCEA is up to a board of six or eight people—some of whom might even have _actual organizational skills._ Their events are getting so numerous that at one point they had trouble making sure none of them overlapped.

I certainly wasn’t the best fit for running HCEA. Many other student groups, started by more proficient leaders, have had a much easier time getting started. But I’m still glad that I ignored my comparative advantage and worked on HCEA. It was important that I was there at the beginning to get it limping along so that John, and later our other champion organizers, could find the group and join it.

* * *

I’m not trying to argue against the whole edifice of comparative advantage. For one thing, that would be pretty hypocritical of me, as someone who chose earning-to-give in the software industry over a trial period with GiveWell or the chance to apply to CEA. More broadly, I think decisions driven by comparative advantage are often really good—certainly better than decisions driven by uninformed gut feeling, status-seeking, random guessing, or other common decision-making _modi operandi_.

But I do think we need a more nuanced understanding of the comparative advantage heuristic, where it succeeds and where it fails or causes coordination problems. I haven’t totally clarified for myself how to think about comparitive advantage. But hopefully these case studies are at least helpful in getting started.
