## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_作者：Michael Nielsen（2023年1月3日）_

为了帮助自己理解 scientific result，我常常觉得，写一种我称为 _discovery fiction_ 的东西很有帮助。我的意思是：一个关于我本可以如何 discovered 那个 result 的 plausible story——由小的 questions 和 ideas、false starts 和 backtracking，以及最终通向那个 result 的 incremental steps 构成的一条脉络。这些东西都不应凭空出现：它们都应当是 simple、几乎 obvious 的 steps。

我写过很多 discovery fiction。例如，我有一条关于[“discovering” Hindu-Arabic numerals](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1174420006907473920) 的 Twitter thread。还有另一条关于[“discovering” quantum teleportation](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1132063254849527808) 的 Twitter thread。我写过 discovery fiction essays，讲述如何 [“discover” Bitcoin](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/)，以及如何 [“discover” Bloom filter data structure](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/why-bloom-filters-work-the-way-they-do/)。我偶尔也会在较长的书和 essays 中加入 discovery fiction。例如，[我的第一本 quantum computing 书](https://smile.amazon.com/Quantum-Computation-Information-10th-Anniversary/dp/1107002176/ref=sr_1_2?crid=OVOOVDVNZ9JQ&keywords=nielsen+chuang&qid=1672781606&sprefix=nielsen+chuang%252Caps%252C155&sr=8-2&ufe=app_do%253Aamzn1.fos.006c50ae-5d4c-4777-9bc0-4513d670b6bc)（与 Ike Chuang 合著）的 section 6.2 解释了读者本可以如何 plausibly “discovered” quantum search algorithm。

虽然它看起来或许是一种 expository form，但我写 discovery fiction 有一个更自私的理由：把它作为加深自身理解的一种方式。例如，在 1990s，当我了解到 cypherpunks，以及 [David Chaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chaum) 对 digital cash 的 invention 时，我开始对 cryptocurrency 感兴趣。到了 2013，在听说 Bitcoin 好几年之后，我想详细理解它。我读了 Bitcoin white paper，觉得从大体上“理解”它很容易。但即使仔细读过几遍，我的理解仍然很浅。我能跟上那些 steps，但如果你把 white paper 从我手里拿走，我就无法从头 reinvented 那个 protocol，尽管由于先前的兴趣，其中许多 ideas 我已经很熟悉。我只是还没有充分理解那篇 white paper。

为了加深理解，我写了一篇 discovery fiction essay，以这样一个 question 开头：我们可以如何 design 一种 digital currency？接着，它描述了关于这种 currency 的一个极其 simple、obvious 的 idea——一个显然 flawed 的 back-of-the-napkin idea，任何熟悉 computers 的人，只要认真对待这个 question，都会想到类似的东西。然后，这篇 essay 指出这个 idea 的一个 obvious problem，并提出一个 simple、obvious 的 fix。接着又指出另一个 obvious problem，再提出另一个 simple fix。如此反复，直到最后我们“discover”了 Bitcoin！这种 approach 非常具体地展示了 Bitcoin 的每个 element 是如何以及为什么被 introduced 的，以及它起什么作用；它也清楚地说明，Bitcoin 的某些 features 有一点 arbitrary，以及它们本可以如何有所不同。相比之下，Bitcoin white paper（大体上）是把这些 ideas 以一种更加 complex、杂乱的方式呈现出来。

Discovery fiction 往往比 conventional presentations 长得多。这并不奇怪：通常，verify 一个 problem 的 solution，要比 find 这个 solution 容易得多[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn1)。我的经验是，如果以相近的详细程度写作，discovery fiction 通常比 conventional explanation 长 2-5 倍。这或许解释了它为什么不更常见：它表面上显得 inefficient，甚至冗长。但无论作为作者还是读者，discovery fiction 的好处都是，它能让你更清楚地理解 solution 从何而来。而且我发现，写得好的 discovery fiction 即使篇幅更长，读起来也更轻松、更快。

当然，discovery fiction 这种 form 肯定不是我首创的。Galileo 在 1632 年写的 [Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_Concerning_the_Two_Chief_World_Systems) 严格来说并不是 discovery fiction，但与它有很多重合之处。我第一次有意识地接触如今被我称为 discovery fiction 的东西，是读到 Imre Lakatos 于 1976 年出版的精彩著作 [Proofs and Refutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_and_Refutations)。在这本书里，Lakatos 逐步建立起对一个 object 的 Euler characteristic，以及 Euler formula 的理解。它并不是纯粹的 discovery fiction——Lakatos 是一位 philosopher，他的作品有特定的意图——但已经足够接近，可以算作这种 form 的一个 example。此后，许多 scientists 告诉我，他们在 teaching 和 research work 中都会使用 discovery fiction。我后来也了解到少量已经发表的 discovery fiction examples。我所知道的最好的汇集，来自 Alessandro Cosentino 的[这篇 StackExchange 帖子](https://cstheory.stackexchange.com/questions/8869/casual-tours-around-proofs)。尤其是 Tim Gowers，他写过许多与 discovery fiction 相近的文章，其中有些可以被视为这种 form 的 examples——例如，可以看看这篇关于 normal subgroup 的 idea 从何而来的 [discussion](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2011/11/20/normal-subgroups-and-quotient-groups/)[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn2)。收集更多 discovery fiction examples，甚至写一部 discovery fiction 的历史，应该会很有意思。

虽然这种 form 由来已久，但“discovery fiction”这个 term 似乎是我创造的[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn3)。我写这篇短文，是因为我发现这个 term 极其有用[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn4)。它指的是一种用于 scientific results 的 idealized _literary form_[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn5)。它的目标不同于 science 中最常用的 literary forms。例如，一篇 scientific paper 的目标（通常）是解释一个新的 scientific result 的 claim（或 claims），以及 evidence 和 supporting reasoning。paper 不需要解释一个人本可以如何自行得到这一 discovery，除非这种解释对于 verification of correctness 必不可少。如果某件事不属于 evidence 和 argument 的 chain，它对这篇 paper 就不是至关重要的！其结果往往是，许多（有时是大多数）原本能让读者理解这个 result 究竟如何 obtained 的路标，都被删去了。这些话都不是在批评 scientific papers 这种 literary form，也无意暗示 papers “应该”是 discovery fiction。我只是想强调，它们与 discovery fiction 的目标确实不同[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn6)。

或许更令人意外的是，discovery fiction 与真实的 history of science 也有很大不同。discovery 的真实历史往往极其 complex，涉及大量的偶然性、highly contingent local knowledge 和幸运的巧合。确实，有时读过 history of science 之后，对于一个 discovery 是如何做出的，人们仍几乎像之前一样困惑。借用 Toynbee 的说法，history of science 有时似乎只是一个 random fact 接着另一个[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn7)。相比之下，好的 discovery fiction 虽然完全可以自由地从历史中_借用_素材——作为灵感，或作为让叙述生动起来的 illustration——却不必忠于历史。它唯一的义务，就是 construct 一个令人满意的 idealized story，说明那个 discovery 本可以如何 plausibly 做出。

并不是所有 discoveries 都需要 discovery fiction。有时，人们以一种 easy-to-understand 的方式做一个 well-motivated experiment，得到一个 striking result。这就不需要 discovery fiction！但当其中有一条棘手的 conceptual path，而究竟是什么引向了 discovery 又并不 obvious 时，discovery fiction 就更有用。我的许多工作都在 computer science、design 和 theoretical physics 领域。这些领域都面向新 concepts 和 hard-to-find arguments 的 discovery。因此，它们很适合 discovery fiction。我预计，science 的其他部分对它的需求会有相当大的差异。

归纳并重申一下，discovery fiction 的 characteristics 包括：

-   discovery fiction 的目标是 construct 一个令人满意的 idealized story，说明那个 discovery 本可以如何 plausibly 做出；
-   Discovery fiction 通常从一个 simple question 开始，这个 question 对 intended audience 而言是 natural 且 well-motivated 的。像“Bitcoin 究竟如何 work？”或“我们可以如何 invent quantum teleportation？”这样的 Questions，自然会有感兴趣的读者群体；
-   discovery fiction 中所采取的 argument steps 是 simple 且 natural 的，并且 intended audience 中的读者本来也可能 plausibly 采取这些 steps；以及
-   discovery fiction 的 intended audience 可能只有一个人：作者自己。

我写这篇短文，是想鼓励其他人创作更多 discovery fiction。这可以通过 expository papers、书和 essays 来完成；也可以通过 Twitter、TikTok 和 YouTube 等较新的 forms，以及尚待发现的 forms 来完成。而且，我希望人们能找到办法，在 research papers 中更多地使用这种 form。我个人很喜欢阅读 discovery fiction，而且与 conventional presentations 相比，我常常能从中学到更多（也更快！）。

我写这篇短文，也是为了强调 discovery fiction 在 research 和 creative work 中有多么有用。它是一种 forcing function，或者一种可以用来加深自身理解的 point of view[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn8)。我在 2019 年写了那条关于 quantum teleportation 的简短 Twitter discovery fiction。在那之前，我已经写过三篇关于 quantum teleportation 的 papers，在 laboratory 做过最早的一批 quantum teleportation experiments 中的一个，并就此做过无数次 talks。我当时简直可以发誓，自己对 quantum teleportation 了解得极为透彻。然而，写那条 thread 仍大大增进了我的理解。这才是我对 discovery fiction 感兴趣的主要原因：它是一种迫使我加深自身理解的 point of view。exposition 只是一个 side effect。

你可能会想，如果我主要把 discovery fiction 看成帮助个人理解的工具，那是否意味着我的大多数 discovery fiction 都是私下写的？我的确有一些私下写的 discovery fiction，但我发现公开写作最有用：公开写作——无论采用什么形式——也是改善思考的一种可靠方式。公开写作能改善思考，有很多原因，包括：(a) 你可能错得令人尴尬；以及 (b) 你会用想象中的读者的标准来要求自己。只要你恰当地选择了想象中的读者，这两件事都会大大提高写作质量，进而提高思考质量[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn9)。当我只为自己写作时，它太常会变成对 result 的 low fidelity rederivation。这也有用，但我可能会自欺欺人，以为自己理解得比实际更透彻。想象中不那么宽容的读者，能帮助我避免自欺。

关于把写作 discovery fiction 作为改善自身理解的方式，还有很多可以说。我只谈两点。第一，有一种 variant form，叫作 _multiple discovery fiction_，你在其中描述 _multiple_ 个可能的 discovery stories，通常（但并非总是）从相当不同的 initial ideas 出发。这需要做很多工作，但也可能极其令人愉快——我会感到自己真正熟悉了这个 subject，尤其是当我有三个或更多 discovery stories 时。第二：我发现，结合 memory systems 的使用来写 discovery fiction，会出乎意料地令人愉悦，正如[这里所描述的](https://cognitivemedium.com/srs-mathematics)。

与其他任何 literary form 一样，discovery fiction 有好的，也有差的。science fiction 作家 Theodore Sturgeon 曾被问到，为什么 90% 的 science fiction 都是垃圾。据说他回答，90% 的 science fiction 当然是垃圾：任何东西都有 90% 是垃圾。多年前，Bret Victor 写过一篇精彩的 essay，介绍了 ["explorable explanations"](http://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/) 这一 idea。那篇 essay 发表后，许多人都尝试写这样的 explorable explanations。正如 Sturgeon's Law 所说，大多数都不太好。但问题或许比 Sturgeon's Law 所暗示的还要严重。许多尝试 explorable explanations 的人，似乎对 form 的兴趣远远超过了对自己所写内容的兴趣。不出所料，这些情况下的成果并不太好[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn10)。我必须承认，这也让我对 discovery fiction 有些忐忑：我很希望这个 term 流行起来，也希望这种 form 得到广泛得多的使用（即使这个 term 没有流行起来）。但我希望，写 discovery fiction 的人真正关心的是把它写好，而不只是试验这种 form！

我将 discovery fiction 描述为一种满足某些 criteria 的 idealized literary form。当然，我并不是想暗示它“必须”是 pure。它不是 formal specification；这既不是 haiku，也不是 iambic pentameter。相反，我是在为一种 literary form 命名并加以描述：人们早已经常少量使用它，偶尔也会大量使用。确实，人们常说，scientific papers 的“好作者”会加入富有洞见的“motivating remarks”。这往往意味着，他们掺入了一些 discovery fiction。但我希望看到多得多的 discovery fiction，也希望更多人意识到，它对增进自己的理解有多大帮助。

_By: Michael Nielsen (January 3, 2023)_

To help me understand a scientific result, I often find it helpful to write what I call _discovery fiction_. By this I mean: a plausible story of how I could have discovered that result – an arc of small questions and ideas, false starts and backtracking, incremental steps eventually leading to the result. None of these things should come out of thin air: they should all be simple, almost-obvious steps.

I've written a lot of discovery fiction. For instance, I have a Twitter thread on ["discovering" the Hindu-Arabic numerals](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1174420006907473920). And another Twitter thread on ["discovering" quantum teleportation](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1132063254849527808). I have discovery fiction essays about how to ["discover" Bitcoin](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/) and how to ["discover" the Bloom filter data structure](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/why-bloom-filters-work-the-way-they-do/). I've occasionally included discovery fiction inside longer books and essays. For example, section 6.2 of [my first quantum computing book](https://smile.amazon.com/Quantum-Computation-Information-10th-Anniversary/dp/1107002176/ref=sr_1_2?crid=OVOOVDVNZ9JQ&keywords=nielsen+chuang&qid=1672781606&sprefix=nielsen+chuang%252Caps%252C155&sr=8-2&ufe=app_do%253Aamzn1.fos.006c50ae-5d4c-4777-9bc0-4513d670b6bc) (jointly written with Ike Chuang) explains how the reader could plausibly have "discovered" the quantum search algorithm.

Although it perhaps seems an expository form, I write discovery fiction for a more selfish reason: as a way of deepening my own understanding. For instance, in the 1990s I got interested in cryptocurrency, when I became aware of the cypherpunks and [David Chaum's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chaum) invention of digital cash. In 2013, after several years of hearing about Bitcoin, I wanted to understand it in detail. I read the Bitcoin white paper, and found it easy to "understand" in broad strokes. But even after several close reads my understanding was shallow. I could follow the steps, but if you'd taken the white paper away from me I couldn't have reinvented the protocol from scratch, even though many of the ideas were familiar because of my prior interest. I just didn't understand the white paper well enough.

To deepen my understanding I wrote a discovery fiction essay that begins with the question: how could we design a digital currency? And then describes an extremely simple, obvious idea for such a currency – an obviously flawed back-of-the-napkin idea, the kind of thing anyone familiar with computers would come up with if they took the question seriously. Then the essay points out an obvious problem with the idea. And proposes a simple, obvious fix. Then points out another obvious problem. And another simple fix. And so on, over and over, until eventually we "discover" Bitcoin! This approach shows very concretely how and why each element of Bitcoin is introduced, and what purpose it serves; it also makes clear that some features of Bitcoin are a little arbitrary, and how they could have been different. By contrast, the Bitcoin white paper (mostly) presents the ideas in a more complex jumble.

Discovery fiction is often much longer than conventional presentations. This is not surprising: it's typically much easier to verify the solution to a problem than it is to find the solution[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn1). My experience is that discovery fiction is usually 2-5 times longer than a conventional explanation, if written at similar levels of detail. This may explain why it is not more common: it superficially appears inefficient, even long-winded. But the benefit of discovery fiction, as both writer and reader, is that it leaves you understanding much better where the solution came from. And I find well-written discovery fiction easier and faster to read, even given the greater length.

Of course, I certainly didn't originate discovery fiction as a form. Galileo's 1632 [Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_Concerning_the_Two_Chief_World_Systems) isn't strictly discovery fiction, but it has much overlap. My first conscious exposure to what I now call discovery fiction was Imre Lakatos's wonderful 1976 book [Proofs and Refutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_and_Refutations). In the book, Lakatos develops an understanding of the Euler characteristic for an object, and of the Euler formula. It's not pure discovery fiction – Lakatos is a philosopher and has a particular agenda for his work – but close enough that it counts as an example of the form. Many scientists have since told me they use discovery fiction in both their teaching and their research work. And I've since learned of a handful of published examples of discovery fiction. The best collection I know comes from [this StackExchange post](https://cstheory.stackexchange.com/questions/8869/casual-tours-around-proofs) by Alessandro Cosentino. Tim Gowers, in particular, has written many pieces which are adjacent to discovery fiction, and in some cases may be considered examples of the form – see, for instance, this [discussion](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2011/11/20/normal-subgroups-and-quotient-groups/) of where the idea of a normal subgroup comes from[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn2). It would be interesting to collect together many more examples of discovery fiction, or even to write a history of discovery fiction.

Although the form is old, the term "discovery fiction" seems to be my coinage[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn3). I wrote this brief note because I've found the term extremely useful[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn4). It refers to an idealized _literary form_ for scientific results[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn5). It has a different goal from the most commonly used literary forms for science. For instance, the goal of a scientific paper is (typically) to explain the claim (or claims) of a new scientific result, and the evidence and supporting reasoning. The paper does not need to explain how one could have arrived at the discovery oneself, except insofar as that is essential for verification of correctness. If something is not part of the chain of evidence and argument, it's not crucial for the paper! The effect is often to remove many (and sometimes most) signposts that would allow a reader to understand how the result was ever obtained. None of this is a criticism of scientific papers as a literary form, nor is it intended to suggest that papers "should" be discovery fiction. I just wish to stress that they have genuinely different goals from discovery fiction[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn6).

Perhaps more surprisingly, discovery fiction is also very different from actual history of science. The real history of discovery is often extraordinarily complex, involves large amounts of chance and highly contingent local knowledge and fortunate happenstance. Indeed, sometimes reading the history of science leaves one almost as bewildered as before about how a discovery is made. To paraphrase Toynbee, the history of science sometimes seems to be just one random fact after another[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn7). By contrast, while good discovery fiction is entirely free to _borrow_ from history – as inspiration or illustration to enliven – it has no allegiance to history. Its only obligation is to construct a satisfying idealized story about how the discovery could plausibly have been made.

Not all discoveries need discovery fiction. Sometimes, people do a well-motivated experiment in an easy-to-understand way, and get a striking result. No discovery fiction needed! But when there's a tricky conceptual path, and it's not obvious what led to a discovery, then discovery fiction is more useful. Much of my work has been in computer science, design, and theoretical physics. These are all oriented toward discovery of new concepts and hard-to-find arguments. As such, they're well suited to discovery fiction. I expect the need in other parts of science to vary quite a bit.

To sum up and restate, the characteristics of discovery fiction include:

-   The goal of discovery fiction is to construct a satisfying idealized story about how the discovery could plausibly have been made;
-   Discovery fiction typically begins with a simple question, one that is natural and well-motivated for the intended audience. Questions like "how does Bitcoin work, in detail?" or "how could we invent quantum teleportation?" have natural constituencies;
-   The steps in argument taken in discovery fiction are simple and natural and could plausibly have been taken by a reader in the intended audience; and
-   The intended audience for discovery fiction may be an audience of one: the writer.

I wrote this note to encourage other people to create more discovery fiction. This may be done in expository papers and books and essays; in newer forms like Twitter and TikTok and YouTube, and in forms yet to be discovered. And, I hope, people will find ways of using the form more in research papers. I personally enjoy reading discovery fiction, and often learn more (and more rapidly!) from it than from conventional presentations.

I also wrote this note to stress how useful discovery fiction is in research and creative work. It's a forcing function or point of view that can be used to deepen one's own understanding[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn8). I wrote my short Twitter discovery fiction about quantum teleportation in 2019. Prior to that I'd written three papers about quantum teleportation, did one of the first quantum teleportation experiments in the laboratory, and gave countless talks about it. I would have sworn I knew quantum teleportation extremely well. And yet writing that thread greatly improved my understanding. That's really the main reason I'm interested in discovery fiction: as a point of view that forces me to deepen my own understanding. The exposition is merely a side effect.

You may wonder, if I think of discovery fiction primarily as a tool for personal understanding, then does that mean most of my discovery fiction is private? I do have some private discovery fiction, but I've found it most useful to write in public: writing in public – in any form – is also a reliable way to improve my thinking. There are many reasons writing in public can improve one's thinking, including: (a) you can be embarrassingly wrong; and (b) you hold yourself to the standards of your imagined audience. Provided you've chosen your imagined audience well, both these things greatly improve the quality of your writing and so of your thinking[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn9). When I write just for myself, too often it becomes a low fidelity rederivation of the result. That's useful, but I can fool myself into believing I've understood it better than I have. A less forgiving imagined audience helps me avoid fooling myself.

There is far more that could be said about writing discovery fiction as a way of improving one's own understanding. I'll make just two remarks. First, there is a variant form, _multiple discovery fiction_, where you describe _multiple_ possible stories of discovery, usually (though not always) by starting with quite different initial ideas. It's a lot of work, but can be immensely enjoyable – I get a sense of really knowing my way around the subject, especially when I have three or more stories of discovery. And second: I've found discovery fiction surprisingly pleasurable to write when combined with the use of memory systems, as [described here](https://cognitivemedium.com/srs-mathematics).

As with any other literary form, there is good discovery fiction, and bad. The science fiction writer Theodore Sturgeon was once asked why 90% of science fiction is crap. He supposedly replied that of course 90% of science fiction is crap: 90% of everything is crap. Many years ago, Bret Victor wrote a marvelous essay introducing the idea of ["explorable explanations"](http://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/). After the essay, many people wrote attempts at such explorable explanations. In keeping with Sturgeon's Law, most weren't very good. But the problem was perhaps worse even than Sturgeon's Law would suggest. Many of the people attempting explorable explanations seemed much more interested in the form than in what they were writing. Not surprisingly, the results in such cases were not very good[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html#fn10). I must admit to some trepidation about discovery fiction on this account: I'd love for the term to take off, and (even if the term doesn't take off) for the form to be much more widely used. But I hope people writing discovery fiction are genuinely interested in just doing a great job, independent of experimenting with the form!

I've described discovery fiction as an idealized literary form meeting certain criteria. Of course, I don't mean to imply that it "must" be pure. It's not a formal specification; this isn't haiku or iambic pentameter. Rather, I'm giving a name to and describing a literary form that is already routinely used in small doses, and occasionally used in large doses. Indeed, people will often say a "good writer" of scientific papers is someone who includes insightful "motivating remarks". Often this means they're mixing in some discovery fiction. But what I'd like to see is much more discovery fiction, and more awareness of how useful it is for improving one's own understanding.
