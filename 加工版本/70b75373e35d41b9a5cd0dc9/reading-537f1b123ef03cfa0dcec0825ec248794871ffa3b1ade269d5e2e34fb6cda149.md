## 正文

_2025年7月13日_

时间是1963年6月11日早晨8点。在 South Vietnam 的 Saigon 城内，超过300位 Buddhist monks 和 nuns 已聚集在 Saigon 最大的 Pagoda——Xa Loi Pagoda 内。他们走了出来，排成 procession，一边 chanting，一边敲着小鼓。他们跟随一辆淡蓝色轿车，车内坐着一位备受尊敬的66岁 monk，名叫 Thich Quang Duc[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn1)，还有两位随侍的 monks。

他们走向一个繁忙的路口，那里离 presidential palace 有几个街区。他们在9am刚过时抵达。procession 吸引了大批人群，其中包括两位 American reporters；他们前一天得到消息，得知将有值得报道的事情发生，但并未被告知具体是什么。

Thich Quang Duc 和他的随侍者走下汽车，在地上放了一个小枕头。他以 lotus posture 端坐下来。他是 alert 的，却也 serene、assured，完全掌控着自己的 body。他的随侍者用 five gallons 的 gasoline 从头到脚浇遍他的全身。他拿起一根木制火柴。划燃它。然后将它丢进自己的膝间，点燃了自己。

火焰咆哮着，将他完全吞没。

他没有喊叫，而是大声 recites：“向 Buddha of Infinite Light 致敬”，如此重复了约30 seconds，直到升腾的火焰淹没他的声音。从那时起，人们再也听不到他的声音，但他仍继续端坐了大约六分钟。正如一位 reporter 所写：“他的 body 正缓慢地 withering 和 shriveling up，他的头部 blackening 和 charring。空气里弥漫着 human flesh 燃烧的气味……他没有动过一块 muscle，也没有发出一声声响；他外在的 composure 与周围人们的哭号形成了鲜明对照。”

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/assets/selfimmolation.jpg)

Quang Duc 并不是 depressed，也不是 suicidal。他在自己的 community 中十分活跃，也备受尊重。另一位 monk，Thich Nhat Hanh，在此前一年里一直与他同住；他写道，Thich Quang Duc 是“一个非常善良而 lucid 的人……他焚烧自己时很 calm，完全拥有自己的 mental faculties。”他也并非 isolated，并非独自或 impulsively 行动。正如我们将会看到的，这是他 carefully 作出的 decision，得到了他的 community 的支持，也是作为这个 community 的一员作出的。

## 这场 sacrifice 的背景

当时，South Vietnam 由 President Ngo Dinh Diem 领导。他是一位 devout Catholic，于1955年掌权，随后对 South Vietnam 占 majority 的 Buddhist population 发起 oppressive actions。最初的措施包括让 Catholics 担任 civil service 和 army posts，并在 loans、land distribution 和 taxes 方面给予他们 preferential treatment。随着时间推移，Diem 不断升级这些措施，并在1963年禁止在 Vesak 期间悬挂 Buddhist flag；Vesak 是纪念 Buddha 诞辰的 festival。5月8日，在 Vesak 庆祝活动期间，government forces 对正在 protesting 这项 ban 的 unarmed Buddhists opened fire，killing 了九人，其中包括两名儿童，并 injured 了更多人。

shooting 发生后，protests 在 South Vietnam 各地爆发。主要 Buddhist groups 的 Leaders 在 Saigon 最大的 Pagoda——Xa Loi Pagoda 会面，决定成立“Inter-Sect Committee for the Protection of Buddhism”。这个 Committee 的目的是帮助 co-ordinate protests，并与 government 进行 negotiate。

遗憾的是，用于 negotiation 的 standard measures——petitions、street fasting、protests，以及对 concessions 的 demands——都被 Diem government 无视，或遭到 force 回应，正如 Vesak shooting 中发生的那样。Inter-Sect Committee 很快意识到，还需要采取更多行动。在 Buddhist teaching 中，suicide 是一种 unwholesome action，是对第一条 moral precept——refrain from taking any life（包括你自己的）——的 violation。但为了他人的利益而 sacrificing 你的 body，被认为并非对这条 precept 的 violation，而是 generosity 和 compassion 的最高 form。公元二世纪的 Lotus Sutra 明确表达了这一点，其中讲述了一位 Bodhisattva 的故事：他以 self-immolated 作为服务所有 sentient beings 的 act，他的 body 所燃起的火 illuminating 了世界长达1,200年。

由于 conventional measures 正在 failing，Inter-Sect Committee 决定考虑更 extreme 的 measures，包括 voluntary self-immolation 这一想法。虽然 extreme，他们仍希望这会引发一场 international media incident，让人们关注 South Vietnam 的 Buddhists 所遭受的 suffering。他们在 meeting minutes 中指出了 photographs 聚焦 international attention 的力量：“一具 body 能抵达一万张 leaflets 无法抵达的地方。”这将是一项帮助 awaken 世界的 Bodhisattva deed。

至少三位较年轻的 monks 自愿提出要 self-immolate。但 Committee 的 leaders 担心，这一提议虽然 generous，却过于 impulsive。他们坚持，这个 act 应由一位备受尊敬、具有无可动摇的 calm 的 elder 来完成。Thich Quang Duc 是一位66岁的 monk，曾帮助建造许多 Pagodas，并 instruct 许多较年轻的 monks；6月9日，他向 Committee 提出由自己来做。

6月10日，Inter-Sect Committee 联系了至少四位驻 Saigon 的 international media 成员，告诉他们次日上午会发生一件“大事”，请他们到场。其中一位是 Associated Press 的 photographer Malcolm Browne。他说，除了预期会有某种 protest 之外，自己对将会看到什么“毫无头绪”。Thich Quang Duc 和他的随侍者走下汽车时，Browne 在15 meters 之外，就站在围成一圈、正在 chanting 的 monks 外侧。Browne 拍摄了超过100张 photos，强忍着 gasoline 和 human flesh 燃烧的气味所引发的 nausea，也抵抗着内心的恐惧，为 Thich Quang Duc 的 sacrifice 留下了永久的 visual record。

这场 sacrifice 并没有白费。第二天，Browne 的 photos 登上了世界各地 newspapers 的 front page。它们震惊了各地的人们，并激起 South Vietnam 的 mass protests。据报道，US President John F. Kennedy 初次看到这张 photo 时惊呼：“天哪！”US government 曾在扶植和支持 anti-communist 的 Diem 方面发挥关键作用，此时撤回了支持；仅仅几个月后，它又支持了一场 coup，导致 Diem 死亡、government 更迭，以及 anti-Buddhist policy 的终结[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn2)。

## 我们该如何理解 self-immolation？

我第一次看到 Thich Quang Duc 的 self-immolation 的照片时，还是个大约十岁的男孩。

我既震惊，又惊愕。

什么会让一个人选择这样做？

这似乎涉及超出我以往一切想象的 pain。

然而，那是他 voluntarily 承担的 pain，为的是满足他的 community 的需要。他的 pain，以及周围 chanting monks 的 witnessing，向世界诉说着：“这就是我们受到的伤害。”

他对自身 intention 的 belief 究竟有多深？不仅要点燃并丢下火柴，而且要 deliberately 这样做，还要坐在那里，不叫出声来？

这世上有什么值得我为之 endure 这一切吗？在我的一生中，_究竟_会不会有这样的事物？我认识拥有这种 belief 的人吗？他是如何拥有如此强烈的 belief、如此惊人的 resolve 的？

他走出汽车、坐到垫子上时，是什么感受？gasoline 被浇到身上时呢？他 nervous 吗？他与自己即将做的事情有着 Connected 的感受吗？还是 numb，只是在凭惯性行事？

他作出最终决定、点火焚身时，带着多少 conscious intention？他主要是在 anticipating pain 吗？在想着所爱的人吗？也许还在考虑放下火柴，站起身，走开？那完全可以理解，是一个再有人情味不过的选择。

而最重要的是，当他燃烧时：他正在经历什么？他怎么能 bear 那样的燃烧，却不叫出声来？

也许最令我惊异的是：他经历了这一切，却并不知道自己 actions 的 outcome 会是什么。他一定想过，自己的 sacrifice 会不会徒劳无功，被世界忽视。他始终不知道，自己帮助促成了 South Vietnam 对 Buddhists 的 oppression 的终结。

这件事非同寻常，几乎令人无法理解。但理解它的最佳方式，不是将其视为 anomaly，而是将其视为我们所有人一生中选择作出的种种大小 sacrifices 的一个 extreme example。这篇 essay 以 Thich Quang Duc 的 sacrifice 为透镜，帮助我们更好地理解自己如何作出这些 choices。

## 那是什么样的感受？他怎么能 endured 那样的 pain？

最无法回避的问题是：Thich Quang Duc 当时正在 experiencing 什么？我们能看见他的 outward composure，但他内在发生着什么？他又怎么能 endure 如此的 pain？

让我们先从他怎么能 endured 那种 pain 这个问题开始。在探究这个问题时，我们会发现，自己逐渐被引向一个更深的问题：他当时究竟正在 experiencing 什么。

对于他如何 endured 那种 pain，最显而易见的 explanations 似乎都不充分。也许你会想，他使用了 anesthetic。但这不太可能：Vietnamese monks 会立誓不使用 mind-clouding drugs，而 independent witnesses 注意到，他的 speech 是 clear 的，movement 是 decisive 的。又或者，他进入了某种 shock：burned-away nerve endings 与 stress-induced analgesia 共同作用，削弱了 pain 的 impact。这些因素很可能最终确实 ameliorate 了 pain。但起初并没有。再作大胆些的推测，也许他患有 congenital analgesia，一种人们不会 feel pain 的 rare condition。这也不太可能——这类 conditions 极为 rare，而且没有 evidence 表明他有这样的 condition。

确实，inhaling 火焰中的 carbon monoxide 会 reduced 那种 pain，大概是在约 30 到 60 seconds 之后。但 full carbon monoxide stupor 会让人瘫软下去。Thich Quang Duc 却保持挺直坐姿达六分钟。而且，无论如何，在 carbon monoxide narcosis 来得及 take effect 之前，仍有最初的那段时间。

最有可能的是，deep meditation 帮助 reduce 了他的 pain[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn3)。他以 full lotus position 坐着，双腿交叉，每只脚都放在对侧 thigh 上。他左手持着一串 wooden prayer beads，这种物件常在 meditation 中用作 sensory focus。而且，如前所述，他反复念诵 Vietnamese mantra “Nam mô A Di Đà Phật”——意思是“向 Buddha of Infinite Light 致敬”——至少持续了三十秒，直到升腾的火焰淹没他的声音。

但说“meditation 让他 feel less pain”，并没有解释多少。它如何 change 了他的 experience of pain？除非你是一位 experienced meditator，并且用过 meditation 来 modulate 自己的 pain，否则这句话并没有多少意义。它不过是 experience 的一个 label，并未传达实际的 felt sense。那种 experience 是什么感觉？它与 non-meditator 的 experience 有什么不同？

探究这些问题的一种 approach，是借助 science，详细 description 随着 meditative mastery 的发展，人们的 brains 及其 experience of pain 如何产生 corresponding changes。这样的 studies 已经开展了数百项。大多数提供的是关于具体机制的详细 findings：例如，一项 study 教 novices 如何 focus 于自己的 breath，在 attention 游离时将它重新带回；随后将一个热得令人不适的 probe 接触 novice 的 calf；再利用 MRI 发现，meditation 会 changes brain 中 respond 于该 probe 的 parts。通常，这种 response 出现在 anterior insula，这个 brain 的 part 一般会像 internal alarm bell 一样，对 bodily threats 发出警报。但 scientists 所 observed 到的是，activity 转移到了 fronto-polar 和 dorsolateral prefrontal cortex，这些 regions 通常参与 deliberate planning 和 cognitive re-appraisal。

这种 description 令人失望。如果你不理解 meditators 正在做什么，而 anterior insula 这样的 brain regions 对你也没有什么意义，那么这些 studies 并不能让你对正在发生的事多理解多少。这感觉有点像听人说：“进行 wibble wobble 导致 brain 的 gyrating goonbas area 出现 flibble flobble，于是 meditators 就 felt less pain。”不过，也许读过数百项这样的 studies 之后，你会开始逐步了解不同 brain regions 之间的 connections、它们如何彼此 interrelate，以及如何与 subjective experiences of pain interrelate，从而更深入地理解 brain 如何 feels 和 modulates pain。

有些 studies 确实更直接地联系到了我们大多数人已经理解的事物。在一项 study 中，scientists 用在冰水中浸透的毛巾裹住几乎赤裸的 monks，再让他们待在寒冷的房间里。对于 ordinary subjects，这很快就会 produces hypothermia。这些长期沉浸于 meditative practice 的 monks 却能 raise 自己的 body temperature，有时甚至能将 finger and toe temperatures 提高多达 8 degrees celsius，并在约 15 minutes 内烘干浸透的毛巾。后续的 MRI studies 显示，这些 monks 的 brains 出现了丰富多样的 changes，从 frontal gamma waves 的 massive surge，到 hypothalamus 和 periaqueductal gray 中 heightened activity——这些 regions 会 increase body heat 并 damp pain。与此同时，上文提到的 anterior insula alarm centre 也安静了下来。这些 results 要容易理解得多，因为 hypothermia、raised temperature、冰冷的水带来的 shock 等事物，对我们大多数人来说都有熟悉的意义。你会在心里想：“即使我不知道 ‘frontal gamma waves’ 和 ‘anterior insula’ 是什么，我无疑也能了解它们在 brain 中发挥的 role。而且听起来，人类正在开始详细理解 meditative practice 与 brain 中 changes 之间的 relationship，以及这如何 modulates pain，尽管仍有许多东西需要学习。'

可惜，无论这样的 descriptions 变得多么出色，单凭它们自身，都无法跨越 description 与 experience 之间的鸿沟。这就像 playing the violin：你可以得到一份极其 detailed、accurate 而且 insightful 的 description，告诉你如何 play the violin well，却仍然完全不知道 play the violin well 是什么感受。我们可以理解将 meditative technique 与 pain 联系起来的 mechanisms。然而，这并不能告诉我们 meditate 是什么感受，它如何 affects 我们的 experience of pain，或者它如何能让 mind over matter 取得如此惊人的 victory，战胜我们 body 最迫切的 demands。

不过，假设有人不是向我们 describing meditation，而是指导我们如何 meditate。我们尝试，experience 到一些东西；再多尝试一些，再多 experience 一些；change 并 develop 我们的做法，以及我们的 experience。这一切都不需要了解 anterior insula、gamma waves 或 dorsolateral prefrontal cortex。然而，我们人类显然又足够 similar，以至于学习 similar techniques 往往（并非总是！）能带给我们 similar experiences。我们（目前）无法让彼此拥有 particular experiences。但我们可以给予提示、diagnose differences、提出建议。与此同时：我们又有足够多的 differences，以至于对一个人 works 的方法，对另一个人可能并不 work，或者需要 modification。而且，我们永远无法确定自己的 experience 与另一个人的究竟有多 similar。我们甚至并不真正知道，拥有 similar experience 意味着什么。因此：我们可以学习 meditative technique。我们可以学习 modulate 自己的 pain response。我们可以通过亲身 experience，至少稍稍理解被浇上 gasoline 并点燃是什么感受。但仍有一个 fundamental mystery：我们永远无法进入[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn4) Thich Quang Duc 的 experience。

## 我们如何决定要为什么而 sacrifice？

我们无法知道，在那个时刻成为 Thich Quang Duc 是怎样的感受。但一个更 tractable、且至少同样有趣的问题是：逐渐成为他，是怎样的感受？他是如何最终作出 drop the match 的 choice 的？这些问题之所以重要，是因为我们都面对类似的问题。“我们如何决定要为什么而 sacrifice？”是一个很好的问题，其 depth 几乎是 open-ended 的，与许多其他重要问题和关键的 life decisions 相连。我们如何决定自己想要什么？success 意味着什么？我们对 success 的理解是如何在 individually 和 collectively 两个层面被 constructed 的？我们 value 什么？

我把这些问题 framing 为 intellectual questions。但这只是其中很小的一部分。我们的答案 grounded in 我们的 friendships、我们的 community、我们的 culture。它们不只是 intellectual 的问题，也是 emotional、social 和 cultural 的问题。我们的答案源自我们对 universe 最基本的 stances。Thich Quang Duc 的 sacrifice 不是一次 impulsive 的孤立举动，也不是从某种 purely philosophical argument 中产生的。它是一生 becoming 的 outcome。在这一生中，他成为了 Unified Buddhist Church of Vietnam 内备受尊崇的人物；也正是这一生，让他在那个地点、那个时刻，拥有那样的 serenity、那样的 conviction，尤其是那种为他人 take on pain 的 capacity。它只可能源自一种 grounded in 他的整个 being 的 powerful belief。他是如何做到如此深刻地 believe 的？与其说我们决定要为什么而 sacrifice，不如说我们逐渐 embody 了某些 sacrificial values。这个 embodiment 的 process 是如何发生的？也许还有一个更好的问题：什么样的 community 能够 produce 这样的 individual？一种 culture 中的哪些 values、beliefs 和 practices，会 lead to 哪些 qualities 的 sacrifice[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn5)？

这些问题太大，无法在这里回答！但我们可以围绕它们探索、自由发挥，让理解稍稍有所增进。我会在后续文章中再来讨论它们。

## 作为 moral invention 的 Self-immolation

在 1990s，一位名叫 Zell Kravinsky 的富有商人开始 donating 数百万美元，以 support public health。随着他的 philanthropic giving 增多，他开始思考自己还能做些什么。Kravinsky 得知，kidney donor 因 transplant procedure 而 dying 的 odds 约为 1 in 4,000。在 transplant 之后，大多数 donors 仅靠一个 kidney 仍能生活得很好，看起来几乎 unaffected，同时却 transforming 了 recipient 的 life。得知这些事实后，我们大多数人也许会觉得有趣，然后就继续自己的生活。Kravinsky 的 empathy 和 generosity 则更进一步。通过一次 moral imagination 的飞跃，他决定向一个自己并不认识的陌生人 donate a kidney altruistically。他认定：“对一个否则就会 die 的人 withhold a kidney，就意味着将自己的 life valuing 为一个陌生人的 4,000 倍”。

许多人反对 Kravinsky 的 reasoning，有些人甚至觉得它 abhorrent，是一种与自己亲人的 disconnection，或是 alienation from self。我不打算在这里梳理这些问题。相反，我想指出，Kravinsky 当时正在 inventing 一种新的 moral act：purely altruistic kidney donation。即使你认为那个 act 具有 negative moral value，认为这样做是坏事，它仍然 rooted in 一种 expanded 我们的 moral universe 的 moral imagination。最初那点 moral imagination 的火花，resulted in 一种更广泛的 moral invention，一种新的、repeatable、scalable 的 altruistic giving 形式。

自 Kravinsky 的 donation 以来，已经有许多文章讨论这件事，也围绕它展开了大量辩论。已有十多个 organizations 成立，以 encourage 并 enable 人们进行 altruistic kidney donations。在我写下这些文字时，全世界已有数千个 kidneys 被 altruistically donated。虽然这些 donations 对相关的人意义重大，但人们很容易得出这样的结论：这个数量小到不足以 meaningfully change 我们整个 society。然而，moral invention 的 acts 往往需要时间，才会逐渐显出其重要性。类似的 moral invention 的 acts 曾多次 changed 并 expanded human civilization，既带来过 good，也带来过 ill。它们是 human inventions 中最非凡的一类。

想想 slavery。在 human history 的很长一段时间里，slavery 都很常见，包括 Ancient Greece、Rome、China，以及许多其他 ancient civilizations。Bible 经常提及 slavery，有时还对它作出 positive 的 framing。即使 Jesus 也没有 explicitly condemn slavery。直到 4th Century，Nyssa 镇（位于今天的 Turkey）一位名叫 Gregory 的 Christian Bishop，才首次有力地这样做。Gregory 将一个 human owning 另一个 human 的观念称为“opposition to God”，并问道：“谁能为了钱而 sell 一个 made in the image of God 的人？”Gregory 在当时是一位重要人物，在其他事务上也很有影响力，但他关于 slavery 的 ideas 尽管得到了有力的 argued，当时仍然被忽视。然而，一千多年后，Gregory 的 argument 再次出现，最终被 18th- 和 19th-century 的重要 Christian abolitionists 引用，作为 emancipation 的古老 precedent。事情往往就是这样：moral invention changes 我们与 universe 的 relationship；起初，其 effect 几乎难以察觉，但它逐渐 embodied 在 movements 和 organizations 中，由它们 scale 那种 change。

如今，我们把 abolitionism 这样的 ideas 视为理所当然。它们是我们能够选择的 moral acts 的 repertoire 的一部分。但要 conceive 它们，需要极大的 imagination，几乎需要某种 moral genius。它们 change 我们与 universe 的 relationship，宣告“这种 act 是 possible 的”，同时也对这个 act 中的 value 作出某种 assertion，无论是 good 还是 evil。而通过 expanding 我们的 moral acts 的 repertoire，并 changing 我们的 values，它们也 change 了我们可能决定为之 sacrifice 的种种方式的 range。当然，abolitionism 和 altruistic kidney donation 只是更大的一组 moral inventions 中的两个 examples：street protest；hunger strike；gender equality；racial equality；human rights；将 kindness elevation 为一种 virtue（“turn the other cheek”、“love thy neighbour as thyself”）；animal rights；以及许许多多其他例子。

作为 protest 的 Self-immolation 是一种 moral invention。这个 idea 并非 originate 于 Thich Quang Duc 和 Inter-Sect Committee。在一个类似、但远没有这么广为人知的案例中，1948 年，一位名叫 Kuo-shun 的 Vietnamese Monk 以 self-immolated 来 protest Maoist 对 Buddhist temples 的 destruction。此事是否帮助 inspire 了 Thich Quang Duc 的 protest，尚不确定，但确有可能。此外还有其他 antecedents——从前面提到的 Lotus Sutra，到 Russia 在 17th 至 20th centuries 间历史上的 gari mass burnings；在那里，整群人以 self-immolated 来 protest 强加于其 Christian faith 的 changes。因此，Thich Quang Duc 的 self-immolation 很可能 inspired by 更早的 acts。但它也与那些 acts 不同：例如，在 ceremonial、community 和 media 方面。就这个意义而言，它是一个持续进行的 moral invention 的 process 的一部分。尤其是，它极大地 contributed to 了 self-immolation 作为一种 protest 形式的 spread。sociologist Michael Biggs[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn6) 已 identified 出在 Thich Quang Duc 之后的四十年里，超过 500 起作为 protest 的 self-immolations，其中大多数或全部似乎都部分地 inspired by Thich Quang Duc。

我一直以 tacitly positive 的措辞讨论 Thich Quang Duc 的 sacrifice。但我并不想 uncritically venerate 这种 sacrifice。与 Kravinsky 的 kidney donation 一样，虽然它具有 admirable qualities，但也有许多 downsides，其 value 也可能受到争议。在 Biggs identified 的 500 起 self-immolations 中，许多似乎毫无意义，甚至是 evil。例如：India 有超过 200 人以 self-immolated，来 protest government 为 lower castes reserve university places 的 plans。这似乎并不是为 greater good 服务的 self-sacrifice。相反，这些人中的许多人很可能在自己的 lives 中缺乏 meaning，并把 sacrifice 的宏大姿态误当成了真正的 meaning。Moral invention 往往很难 judge，部分原因在于，它取决于 redefining 我们与其余 universe 的 relationship。

Moral invention 很重要。正如我上面所说，它 expands 我们能够 conceive 自己采取的 actions 的 range。它也 changes 我们思考自己的 actions 的 meaning 的方式。在这一切之中，它 changes 了我们可能为之 sacrifice 的东西。我不会在这里解答这些问题，但许多伴随而来的问题令我着迷：moral invention 是如何运作的？moral imagination 的 source 是什么？它是否是某种 platonic realm，就像 mathematics 和部分 design 中似乎存在的那样？我并不确定，不过我怀疑，在某些方面确实如此。但在我知道的几乎所有案例中，它都不是凭空产生的。从 Jesus[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn7) 到 Mary Wollstonecraft，再到 Gandhi 和 Thich Quang Duc，moral imagination 和 moral invention 的 acts 往往 sourced in 并 provoked by suffering 或 injustice。

## 关于 sacrifice 的选择

写这篇 essay 时，我颇为挣扎。Sacrifice 往往是个令人不自在的话题，而在讨论 Thich Quang Duc 时，我大体上一直与那种不自在保持着距离。他的故事有一种幽暗的吸引力，却也离我们很远：我们大多数人永远不会认真考虑作出如此非同寻常的 sacrifice。但讨论之下潜藏着一个直击要害的个人问题：我是不是太 selfish 了？为了他人，我愿意作出什么 sacrifices，哪怕只是很小的 sacrifices？为了 greater good 呢？该如何 balance sacrifice 与 self-interest？

我希望你在阅读时，也一直受到这些问题的牵动。就我自己而言：我的生活往往以自己的 creative pursuits 为中心，我保护着这些 pursuits，不让来自 the other 的许多要求侵扰它们。这样的保护对于 individual creative contribution 是必要的：individual creators 会发现，世上有许多力量试图 co-opt 他们的 creative energy。只有受到坚决的捍卫，那种 energy 才能存续。不过，我仍然怀疑自己是不是做得太过了。在自己的生活中，我是不是太 self-centered 了？有时是不是为了自己的 self-aggrandizement？在一种过得好的生活中，sacrifice 扮演什么 role？我欠自己什么？我欠 the unself 什么？

modesty 和 shame 都使我不愿在这里过于深入地讨论 sacrifice。说是 Modesty，是因为这样的讨论太容易滑向 internet confessional 中的 self-indulgence，甚至 exhibitionism。说是 shame，是因为我害怕得出这样的结论：我一直过着 self-centered 的生活，需要作出重大改变。我还没有充分克服这些感受，无法完全坦率地写出来；也还没有想得足够深入，无法写好。但我还是会提出几点 observations。

首先：sacrifice 有两种很不相同的 meanings。一种是 sacrifice for self，另一种是 sacrifice for unself。Sacrifice for self 是为了我们自己的 ends，去做困难、痛苦或危险的事。这些事可能很普通：不让自己吃渴望的甜食；早起写书；尽管不舒服，仍然努力锻炼[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn8)。它们也可能很 extreme：2003 年，rock climber Aron Ralston 在 Utah 进行 solo canyoneering 时，手臂被一块落下的巨石压住。在巨石下困了五天后，他用一把 multi-tool 对自己的手臂进行 amputate，随后 rappelled 出 canyon，并得到了救助。所有这些都涉及为了我们自己的 longer-term ends 而放弃某些东西——comfort、safety，甚至 body parts。

我们常常赞颂那些广为人知的 extreme sacrifice for self 事例。伟大的 tennis player Rafael Nadal 以非同寻常的 dedication，以及愿意为自己的 tennis 倾尽一切而闻名。他遭受过多次足以终结大多数 players 的 careers 的 injuries，却总能归来，并 dominate tennis。他以 practicing 时的 intensity 而著称，那甚至超过许多 professionals 在 game day 所能达到的程度。我们大多数人无法经常鼓起那样 extreme 的 will。但同样，Nadal 以及其他像他一样的人，也得到了极其丰厚的回报。尽管其他人当然也从他的工作中受益——事实上，他给数百万人带来了快乐，他们喜欢看他把一块 rubber 击过 nylon net——但这在核心上似乎仍是一种 sacrifice for self。

相比之下，Thich Quang Duc 作出的是一种 sacrifice for unself。这是 soldier 为救朋友而扑向 live grenade 的那种 sacrifice。是 Jesus 在 Cross 上为 humanity 而 sacrificing 自己的故事中的那种 sacrifice。也可以是更不张扬的方式：向 homeless shelter 或 food bank 作出的 anonymous donation。照料一位 debilitated 的家人。放弃 individual ambitions 来抚养子女。通常，很难 assess 这些 actions 在多大程度上是为了 self，又在多大程度上是为了 unself。我听过有人出于根本上 selfish 的理由，主张“人们应该多生孩子”（“这是我做过的最有意义的事”），也听过有人以不生孩子是 selfish 为由提出同样的主张。我怀疑这两类理由可能都成立：对一些人来说，生孩子是一种重大的 sacrifice，而对另一些人来说则不是。不管怎样，所有这些 examples 都在不同程度上是 sacrifice for unself 的 forms，也是 love 的 forms。

从内在感受而言，Thich Quang Duc 觉得自己是 selfless 的吗？我毫不怀疑他感受到了 loss 和 pain。但我也毫不怀疑，他承受这些，一部分是为了换取一种让自己的生活更有意义的方式。许多 sacrifice for unself 似乎都在于，把这一 act 转化为一种在你看来符合自己 best interest 的 act。在某种意义上，这是在将 the other 作为 self 来 internalizing。这样的 speculation 让我不自在，因为它似乎 reduce 了他的 sacrifice 中的 meaning。但我认为，更好的做法是珍视 self 的 expansion。我们都会 selfishly 行事。但我们之中，有些人的 senses of self 更值得敬佩，有些则不那么值得。因此，一件事有多 selfish，可能取决于 vantage point。从内在感受而言，sacrifice 并不完全像是 sacrifice。

我最喜欢的 quotes 之一出自作家 Lois Bujold：

> 所有伟大的 human deeds 都既 consume 又 transform 它们的 doers。想想 athlete、scientist、artist，或 independent business creator。为了自己的 goals，他们舍弃 time 和 energy，以及许多其他 choices 和 pleasures；作为回报，他们最真切地成为了自己。false destiny 或许可以由这一点识别出来：它只 consumes，却不 transforming，也不归还 enlarged self。

也就是说，精心选择的 sacrifice 所带来的一个 consequence，是 growth 和 transformation。或许，你为了什么而 sacrifice，部分取决于你是否正在以值得的方式 growing，是否在被 consumed 的同时也被 transformed，是否正在 developing 那个 enlarged self。的确，我们对自己的 deeds 所持的 standards 越高，sacrifice 就越大，transformation 也就越大。按 Bujold 字面所描述的 sense，Thich Quang Duc 并没有得到 positively transformed。但或许，他觉得自己正在为 humanity 中更大的 transformation 作出贡献。他的 act of sacrifice 所归还的不是 enlarged self，而是 enlarged humanity。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_July 13, 2025_

It is 8 o'clock in the morning of 11 June, 1963. In the city of Saigon in South Vietnam more than 300 Buddhist monks and nuns have gathered inside the largest Pagoda in Saigon, the Xa Loi Pagoda. They emerge, walking in procession, chanting and beating small drums. They follow a pale blue sedan, containing a revered 66 year old monk named Thich Quang Duc[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn1), as well as two attendant monks.

They walk toward a busy intersection, a few blocks away from the presidential palace. They arrive shortly after 9am. A large crowd has gathered, attracted by the procession, including two American reporters, tipped off the day before that something newsworthy will happen, although they have not been told what.

Thich Quang Duc and his attendants exit the car, and place a small pillow on the ground. He seats himself upright in the lotus posture. He is alert but serene, assured and in full command of his body. His attendants douse him from head to toe with five gallons of gasoline. He takes a single wooden match. Strikes it. And drops it into his lap, setting himself on fire.

The flames roar, engulfing him entirely.

He does not cry out, but instead recites aloud "homage to the Buddha of Infinite Light", repeating this for about 30 seconds until the rising flames drown his voice. From that point on his voice can no longer be heard, but he continues to sit upright for roughly six minutes, while, as one reporter wrote, "his body was slowly withering and shriveling up, his head blackening and charring. In the air was the smell of burning human flesh… he never moved a muscle, never uttered a sound, his outward composure in sharp contrast to the wailing people around him."

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/assets/selfimmolation.jpg)

Quang Duc was not depressed nor suicidal. He was active in his community, and well respected. Another monk, Thich Nhat Hanh, who had lived with him for the prior year, wrote that Thich Quang Duc was "a very kind and lucid person… calm and in full possession of his mental faculties when he burned himself." Nor was he isolated and acting alone or impulsively. As we'll see, the decision was one he made carefully, with the blessing of and as part of his community.

## Background to the sacrifice

At the time, South Vietnam was led by President Ngo Dinh Diem, a devout Catholic who had taken power in 1955, and then instigated oppressive actions against the Buddhist majority population of South Vietnam. This began with measures like filling civil service and army posts with Catholics, and giving them preferential treatment on loans, land distribution, and taxes. Over time, Diem escalated his measures, and in 1963 he banned flying the Buddhist flag during Vesak, the festival in honour of the Buddha's birthday. On May 8, during Vesak celebrations, government forces opened fire on unarmed Buddhists who were protesting the ban, killing nine people, including two children, and injured many more.

After the shooting, protests erupted throughout South Vietnam. Leaders of the main Buddhist groups met in the Xa Loi Pagoda, the largest Pagoda in Saigon, and decided to form the "Inter-Sect Committee for the Protection of Buddhism". The Committee's purpose was to help co-ordinate protests and negotiate with the government.

Unfortunately, standard measures for negotiation – petitions, street fasting, protests, and demands for concessions – were ignored by the Diem government, or met with force, as in the Vesak shooting. The Inter-Sect Committee quickly realized something more was needed. In Buddhist teaching, suicide is an unwholesome action, a violation of the first moral precept to refrain from taking any life (including your own). But sacrificing your body for the benefit of others was considered not to be a violation of the precept, but rather the highest form of generosity and compassion. This was made explicit in the Lotus Sutra, from the second century CE, which told the story of a Bodhisattva who self-immolated as an act of service to all sentient beings, with the fire of his body illuminating the world for 1,200 years.

Since conventional measures were failing, the Inter-Sect Committee decided to consider more extreme measures, including the idea of a voluntary self-immolation. While extreme, they hoped it would create an international media incident, to draw attention to the suffering of Buddhists in South Vietnam. They noted in their meeting minutes the power of photographs to focus international attention: "one body can reach where ten thousand leaflets cannot." It was to be a Bodhisattva deed to help awaken the world.

At least three younger monks volunteered to self-immolate. But the leaders of the Committee worried that this offer, while generous, was overly impulsive. They insisted the act be done by a revered elder of unassailable calm. Thich Quang Duc, a 66 year old monk who had helped build many Pagodas and instruct many younger monks, offered himself to the Committee on June 9.

On June 10, the Inter-Sect Committee contacted at least four Saigon-based members of the international media, telling them to be present for a "major event" that would occur the next morning. One of them was a photographer from the Associated Press, Malcolm Browne, who said he had "no idea" what he'd see, beyond expecting some kind of protest. When Thich Quang Duc and his attendants exited the car, Browne was 15 meters away, just outside the ring of chanting monks. Browne took more than 100 photos, fighting off nausea from the smell of burning gasoline and human flesh, and struggling with the horror, as he created a permanent visual record of Thich Quang Duc's sacrifice.

The sacrifice was not in vain. The next day, Browne's photos made the front page of newspapers around the world. They shocked people everywhere, and galvanized mass protests in South Vietnam. US President John F. Kennedy reportedly exclaimed "Jesus Christ!" upon first seeing the photo. The US government, which had been instrumental in installing and supporting the anti-communist Diem, withdrew its support, and just a few months later supported a coup that led to Diem's death, a change in government, and the end of anti-Buddhist policy[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn2).

## What are we to make of self-immolation?

I first saw the image of Thich Quang Duc's self-immolation as a boy of about ten years old.

I was shocked and astonished.

What could make someone choose to do this?

It appeared to involve pain beyond what I'd ever previously conceived.

And yet it was pain that he took upon himself voluntarily, to serve his community's need. His pain, and the witnessing of the chanting monks around him, said to the world: "this is how we are hurt."

How deep must his belief in his own intention have been? To not only light and drop the match, but to do so deliberately, and to sit there and not cry out?

Was there anything in this world I would be willing to endure this for? Would there _ever_ be any such thing, in all my life? Did I know people with such belief? How had he come to have such intensity of belief, such incredible resolve?

What was he feeling as he exited the car and sat down on the cushion? As the gasoline was poured over him? Was he nervous? Connected to what he was about to do? Or numb, proceeding on auto-pilot?

With how much conscious intention did he make the final decision to light himself on fire? Was he mostly anticipating pain? Thinking of loved ones? Perhaps considering putting the match down, standing up, and walking away? It would have been understandable, a very human choice.

And, most of all, as he burned: what was he going through? How could he bear to burn so, and not cry out?

Perhaps most astonishing of all to me: he went through all this despite not knowing what the outcome of his actions would be. He must have wondered if his sacrifice would be in vain, ignored by the world. He never knew that he helped precipitate the end of the oppression of Buddhists in South Vietnam.

It was an event so extraordinary as to almost defy comprehension. But it's best understood not as an anomaly, but rather as an extreme example of the many sacrifices, small and large, that we all choose to make through our lives. This essay uses Thich Quang Duc's sacrifice as a lens to better understand how we make these choices.

## What was it like? How could he have endured that pain?

The most unavoidable question is: what was Thich Quang Duc experiencing? We can see his outward composure, but what was happening inside? And how could he endure such pain?

Let us begin with the question of how he could have endured the pain. As we explore this question, we'll find ourselves drawn deeper into the question of what he was actually experiencing.

The most obvious explanations for how he endured the pain seem inadequate. Perhaps, you might think, he used an anesthetic. But it's unlikely: Vietnamese monks take vows against mind-clouding drugs, and independent witnesses noted his clear speech and decisive movement. Or perhaps he went into a kind of shock, a combination of burned-away nerve endings, and stress-induced analgesia, dulling the impact of the pain. Those things likely did eventually ameliorate the pain. But not initially. More speculatively, perhaps he had congenital analgesia, a rare condition where people don't feel pain. That's also unlikely – such conditions are extremely rare, and there's no evidence he had such a condition.

It is true that inhaling carbon monoxide from the flames would have reduced the pain, probably after about 30 to 60 seconds. But full carbon monoxide stupor makes people slump. Thich Quang Duc remained upright for six minutes. And, in any case, there was still the initial period, before carbon monoxide narcosis had time to take effect.

What seems most likely is that deep meditation helped reduce his pain[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn3). He sat in full lotus position, legs crossed, each foot resting on the opposite thigh. He held a string of wooden prayer beads in his left hand, often used as a sensory focus in meditation. And, as mentioned earlier, he repeated the Vietnamese mantra "Nam mô A Di Đà Phật" – meaning "homage to the Buddha of Infinite Light" – for at least thirty seconds, when the rising flames drowned his voice.

But saying "meditation made him feel less pain" explains little. How did it change his experience of pain? Unless you are an experienced meditator who has used meditation to modulate your own pain, the statement has little meaning. It's a mere label for experience, and doesn't convey the actual felt sense. What does that experience feel like? How is it different from the experience of a non-meditator?

One approach to these questions is to use science to develop a detailed description of how developing meditative mastery causes corresponding changes in people's brains and their experience of pain. Hundreds of such studies have been done. Most offer detailed nuts-and-bolts findings: one study, for example, taught novices how to focus on their breath, returning attention when it wanders; then applied an uncomfortably hot probe to the novice's calf; and used MRI to find that meditation changes which parts of the brain respond to the probe. Ordinarily, the response is seen in the anterior insula, a part of the brain which typically acts as an internal alarm bell for bodily threats. But what the scientists observed was that the activity moved to the fronto-polar and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, regions usually involved in deliberate planning and cognitive re-appraisal.

This kind of description is disappointing. If you don't understand what the meditators are doing, and brain regions like the anterior insula aren't meaningful to you, then these studies don't add much to your understanding of what's going on. It feels a bit like hearing that "doing the wibble wobble caused the gyrating goonbas area of the brain to flibble flobble, and so the meditators felt less pain." Still, perhaps with hundreds of such studies, you'd begin to build up a picture of the connections between different brain regions, how they interrelate to one another, and to subjective experiences of pain, and so arrive at a deeper understanding of how the brain feels and modulates pain.

Some studies do connect more directly to things most of us already understand. In one study, scientists wrapped almost-naked monks in towels that had been soaked in icy water, and placed the monks in a cold room. In ordinary subjects this quickly produces hypothermia. The monks, steeped in meditative practice, were able to raise their body temperature, in some cases boosting their finger and toe temperatures by up to 8 degrees celsius, and drying the soaking-wet towel within about 15 minutes. Followup MRI studies showed a rich variety of changes in the monks' brains, from a massive surge in frontal gamma waves, to heightened activity in the hypothalamus and periaqueductal gray – regions that increase body heat and damp pain. At the same time, the anterior insula alarm centre mentioned above also fell quiet. These results are much more intelligible, since things like hypothermia, raised temperature, the shock of ice-cold water, and so on, have familiar meanings to most of us. And you think to yourself: "even if I've no idea what 'frontal gamma waves' and the 'anterior insula' are, I could no doubt learn about the role they play in the brain. And it sounds like humanity is starting to develop a detailed understanding of the relationship between meditative practice and changes in the brain, and how this modulates pain, even if there's still much to learn.'

Unfortunately, however good such descriptions get, on their own they don't bridge the gap between description and experience. It's like playing the violin: you can have an incredibly detailed and accurate and insightful description of how to play the violin well, and yet have no idea at all of what it feels like to play the violin well. We can understand the mechanisms relating meditative technique and pain. And yet that doesn't tell us what it's like to meditate, how it affects our experience of pain, or how it can enable such an incredible victory of mind over matter, triumph over our body's most urgent demands.

Suppose, however, that instead of describing meditation, someone instructs us in how to meditate. We try it, experience something, try some more, experience some more, change and develop what we do, what we experience. All without needing to know about the anterior insula or gamma waves or the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. And yet, we humans are apparently similar enough to one another that learning similar techniques often (not always!) gives us similar experiences. We can't (yet) make one another have particular experiences. But we can give hints, diagnose differences, make suggestions. At the same time: we are different enough that what works for one person may not work for another, or need modification. And we can never be sure how similar our experience is to that of another. We don't even really know what it means to have a similar experience. And so: we can learn meditative technique. We can learn to modulate our own pain response. We can come to understand experientially, at least a little, what it would be like to be doused in gasoline and set alight. But there is still a fundamental mystery: we are forever barred[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn4) from the experience of Thich Quang Duc.

## How do we decide what to sacrifice for?

We cannot know what it was to be Thich Quang Duc in that moment. But a more tractable and at least equally interesting question is: what was it like to become him? How did he come to make that final choice to drop the match? These questions matter because we all face similar questions. "How do we decide what to sacrifice for?" is a great question, a question of almost open-ended depth, connected to many other important questions and crucial life decisions. How do we decide what we want? What does success mean? How is our understanding of success constructed, both individually and collectively? What do we value?

I'm framing these as intellectual questions. But that is only a small part of the story. Our answers are grounded in our friendships, in our community, in our culture. They are not just intellectual, but also emotional, social, and cultural questions. Our answers emerge out of our most basic stances toward the universe. Thich Quang Duc's sacrifice wasn't an impulsive one-off, nor did it arise from some purely philosophical argument. It was the outcome of a lifetime of becoming. One that saw him become a venerated figure within the Unified Buddhist Church of Vietnam. One that saw him in that place and that time, with that serenity and that conviction, and most of all with that capacity to take on pain for others. It can only have arisen out of a powerful belief grounded in his entire being. How did he come to believe so deeply? We do not so much decide what to sacrifice for, rather we come to embody certain sacrificial values. How does that process of embodiment come about? And perhaps an even better question: what kinds of community can produce such an individual? What values and beliefs and practices in a culture lead to what qualities of sacrifice[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn5)?

These questions are too large to answer here! But we can explore and riff on them, and make some small increment in understanding. I will return to them in later writing.

## Self-immolation as moral invention

In the 1990s, a wealthy businessman named Zell Kravinsky began donating millions of dollars to support public health. As he increased his philanthropic giving, he wondered what more he could do. Kravinsky learned that the odds of a kidney donor dying from the transplant procedure was about 1 in 4,000. After the transplant, most donors continue to live well with just one kidney, apparently near unaffected, while transforming the recipient's life. Upon learning these facts, most of us would perhaps find them interesting, and then move on. Kravinsky's empathy and generosity went deeper. In a leap of moral imagination, he decided to donate a kidney altruistically to a stranger, someone he didn't know. He decided: "to withhold a kidney from someone who would otherwise die means valuing one's own life at 4,000 times that of a stranger".

Many people object to Kravinsky's reasoning, some even finding it abhorrent, a form of disconnection from his own loved ones, or alienation from self. I won't attempt to sort through those issues. Rather, I want to point out that Kravinsky was inventing a new kind of moral act: purely altruistic kidney donation. Even if you think that act had negative moral value, that it was a bad thing to do, it was still rooted in moral imagination that expanded our moral universe. That initial spark of moral imagination resulted in a broader moral invention, a new, repeatable, scalable form of altruistic giving.

Since Kravinsky's donation, many articles have been written about it, and it's been debated at great length. More than a dozen organizations have been founded to encourage and enable people to make altruistic kidney donations. As I write, several thousand kidneys have been altruistically donated worldwide. While those donations are of great importance for the people involved, it's tempting to conclude that the number is small enough that this doesn't meaningfully change our society as a whole. But acts of moral invention often take time to grow in importance. Similar acts of moral invention have changed and expanded human civilization many times, for both good and for ill. They are one of the most remarkable of human inventions.

Consider slavery. For much of human history, slavery was commonplace, including in Ancient Greece, Rome, China, and many other ancient civilizations. The Bible mentions slavery often, sometimes framing it positively. Even Jesus does not explicitly condemn slavery. That was first done forcefully in the 4th Century, by a Christian Bishop named Gregory, in the town of Nyssa (in modern Turkey). Gregory calls the idea of one human owning another "opposition to God", and asks: "Who can sell a man, made in the image of God, for money?" Gregory was an important person in his time, and influential on other matters, but his ideas on slavery were ignored at the time, despite being forcefully argued. And yet more than a thousand years later, Gregory's argument resurfaced, and was eventually quoted by important 18th- and 19th-century Christian abolitionists, as an ancient precedent for emancipation. This is often the way: moral invention changes our relationship to the universe; at first the effect is barely noticeable, but it is gradually embodied in movements and organizations that scale that change.

Today, we take ideas like abolitionism for granted. They are part of the repertoire of moral acts available to us. But they required great imagination, almost a kind of moral genius, to conceive. They change our relationship to the universe, saying "this type of act is possible", and also making some assertion about the value in the act, for good or for evil. And by expanding our repertoire of moral acts and changing our values, they also change the range of ways we may decide to sacrifice for. Of course, abolitionism and altruistic kidney donation are just two examples of a larger constellation of moral inventions: the street protest; the hunger strike; gender equality; racial equality; human rights; the elevation of kindness to a virtue ("turn the other cheek", "love thy neighbour as thyself"); animal rights; and many, many more.

Self-immolation as protest is a kind of moral invention. The idea did not originate with Thich Quang Duc and the Inter-Sect Committee. In a similar though much less well-known case, in 1948 a Vietnamese Monk named Kuo-shun self-immolated to protest Maoist destruction of Buddhist temples. It's uncertain, but plausible that this helped inspire Thich Quang Duc's protest. And there were other antecedents, too – from the Lotus Sutra mentioned earlier, to the historical gari mass burnings in Russia in the 17th through 20th centuries, where entire groups of people self-immolated to protest changes imposed on their Christian faith. So Thich Quang Duc's self-immolation was likely inspired by earlier acts. It was also different to them: for instance, in the ceremonial and community and media aspects. In that sense, it was part of an ongoing process of moral invention. In particular, it contributed greatly to the spread of self-immolation as a form of protest. The sociologist Michael Biggs[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn6) has identified more than 500 self-immolations as protest in the four decades after Thich Quang Duc, most or all of which appear to have been inspired in part by Thich Quang Duc.

I've discussed Thich Quang Duc's sacrifice in tacitly positive terms. But I don't want to uncritically venerate this kind of sacrifice. As with Kravinsky's kidney donation, while it had admirable qualities, it also had many downsides, and the value may be contested. Among the 500 self-immolations identified by Biggs, many seem pointless, even evil. For example: more than 200 people in India self-immolated in protest over government plans to reserve university places for lower castes. This doesn't seem like self-sacrifice in service of a greater good. Rather, it seems likely many of these people lacked meaning in their own lives, and confused the grand gesture of the sacrifice for true meaning. Moral invention is often difficult to judge, in part because it hinges on redefining our relationship to the rest of the universe.

Moral invention matters. As I said above, it expands the range of actions we can conceive of taking. And it changes the way we think about the meaning of our actions. In all this it changes what we may sacrifice for. I won't address the questions here, but I am fascinated by many concomitant questions: how does moral invention work? What is the source of moral imagination? Is it some platonic realm, as seems to be true in mathematics and parts of design? I am not sure, though I suspect that in some regards it is. But in almost all cases I know of, it did not come out of thin air. From Jesus[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn7) to Mary Wollstonecraft to Gandhi to Thich Quang Duc, acts of moral imagination and moral invention are often sourced in and provoked by suffering or injustice.

## The choice of sacrifice

I struggled writing this essay. Sacrifice is often an uncomfortable topic, and in discussing Thich Quang Duc, I've kept that discomfort mostly at arm's length. His story is darkly compelling but also distant: most of us will never seriously consider making such an extraordinary sacrifice. But lurking beneath the discussion is a jugular personal question: am I too selfish? What sacrifices am I willing to make, even small ones, in service of others? In service of the greater good? How to balance sacrifice with self-interest?

I hope these questions have pulled at you as you've read, too. For myself: I've lived a life often centered on my creative pursuits, protecting those pursuits from many of the demands of the other. Such protection is necessary for individual creative contribution: individual creators find the world contains many forces attempting to co-opt their creative energy. That energy only survives if fiercely defended. Still, I wonder if I've gone too far. Have I been too self-centered in my own life? And was it sometimes for my own self-aggrandizement? What is the role of sacrifice in a life well lived? What do I owe myself? What do I owe the unself?

Both modesty and shame contribute to my aversion to discussing sacrifice too deeply here. Modesty, because such discussion too easily veers into the self-indulgence and even exhibitionism of the internet confessional. And shame, because I fear concluding that I have lived a self-centered life, and need to make a major change. I haven't overcome these feelings enough to write entirely frankly; nor have I yet thought deeply enough to write well. But I will make a few observations.

To begin: there are two quite different meanings of sacrifice. There is sacrifice for self, and sacrifice for unself. Sacrifice for self is doing difficult, painful, or dangerous things in service of our own ends. They may be quite ordinary: denying ourselves the sweet food we crave; getting up early to write a book; exercising hard, despite discomfort[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/sacrifice/index.html#fn8). They may be extreme: in 2003 the rock climber Aron Ralston's arm was pinned by a falling boulder while solo canyoneering in Utah. After five days stuck under the boulder, he used a multi-tool to amputate his own arm, rappelled out of the canyon, and got help. All these involve giving up something – comfort, safety, even body parts – for our own longer-term ends.

Often, we celebrate extreme public cases of sacrifice for self. The great tennis player Rafael Nadal was known for his extraordinary dedication, and willingness to put everything into his tennis. He suffered multiple injuries that would have ended most players' careers, only to come back and dominate tennis. He was renowned for practicing with an intensity beyond what many professionals can achieve even on game day. Most of us can't routinely muster that kind of extreme will. But by the same token, Nadal and others like him, are extremely well-rewarded. While other people certainly benefit from his work – indeed he gave joy to millions of people, who enjoyed seeing him hit a piece of rubber over a nylon net – it seems at core a sacrifice for self.

By contrast, Thich Quang Duc made a sacrifice for unself. It's the sacrifice of a soldier throwing themself on a live grenade to save their friends. Of the story of Jesus on the Cross, sacrificing himself for humanity. Or in quieter ways: the anonymous donation to the homeless shelter or food bank. Caring for a debilitated family member. Giving up individual ambitions to support one's children. Often, it's difficult to assess how much such actions are for self or for unself. I've heard it argued that "people should have more children" both for fundamentally selfish reasons ("it's the most meaningful thing I've ever done") and on the grounds that not having children is selfish. I suspect both types of reason may be true: for some people having children is a great sacrifice, while for others it is not. Regardless, all these examples are, in different measures, forms of sacrifice for unself, forms of love.

Did Thich Quang Duc feel selfless, from the inside? I have no doubt he felt loss and pain. But also that he suffered those in part in trade for a way of making his own life more meaningful. Much of sacrifice for unself seems to consist of transmuting the act into one that you view as in your own best interest. In some sense, it's about internalizing the other as self. I'm uncomfortable with this speculation, since it seems to reduce the meaning in his sacrifice. But I think it's better to value the expansion in self. We all act selfishly. But some of us have more and less admirable senses of self. And so how selfish something is may depend upon the vantage point. From the inside, sacrifice doesn't entirely feel like sacrifice.

One of my favourite quotes is from the author Lois Bujold:

> All great human deeds both consume and transform their doers. Consider an athlete, or a scientist, or an artist, or an independent business creator. In service of their goals they lay down time and energy and many other choices and pleasures; in return, they become most truly themselves. A false destiny may be spotted by the fact that it consumes without transforming, without giving back the enlarged self.

That is, a consequence of a well-chosen sacrifice is growth and transformation. Perhaps what you sacrifice for is determined in part by whether you are growing in worthwhile ways, consumed but also transformed, developing the enlarged self. Indeed, the higher our standards for our deeds, the greater the sacrifice, and the greater the transformation. Thich Quang Duc was not positively transformed in the sense Bujold literally describes. But perhaps he felt himself as contributing to a larger transformation in humanity. His act of sacrifice wasn't giving back the enlarged self; it was giving back an enlarged humanity.
