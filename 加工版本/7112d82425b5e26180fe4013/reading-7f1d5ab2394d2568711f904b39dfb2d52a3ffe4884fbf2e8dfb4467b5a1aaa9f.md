最近我一直在想，那些做出了最出色工作的同事，究竟有什么与众不同。

你可能认为，让人在 research 或 engineering 中真正 effective 的主要因素是 technical ability；在一般人群中，确实如此。不过，在我的 Anthropic 同事中，我们通过筛选 technical ability 处于极高 percentile 的人，已经 [restricted the range](https://www.statology.org/restriction-of-range/)，所以剩下的 differences 虽然仍然重要，却没有那么关键了。相反，人们最大的 bottleneck 最终会变成获取 _leverage_ 的能力——也就是找到并 execute 具有很大 impact-per-hour multiplier 的工作。

例如，下面这些 Anthropic 的工作类型，往往具有很高的 impact-per-hour，或者在做得好时具有很高的 impact-per-hour ceiling（当然，这份列表远非 exhaustive！）：

-   对 tooling、documentation 或 dev loops 进行 Improving。花很少的时间，以恰当的方式 fixing 一个 papercut，就能为数百名 users 节省数小时的 debugging，或在 inefficient workflows 中艰难操作的时间。
-   对 promising research directions 进行 Identifying。像 [character](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-character) 和 [computer use](https://www.anthropic.com/news/3-5-models-and-computer-use) 这样的工作，最初只占一个人一部分的时间，赌的是将某种 technique applied 到某个 problem 上会取得很好的效果，最终却对 Anthropic 的 research direction 产生了重大影响。
-   System design（系统设计）。这通常只占 execute 一个 project 所需总时间的一小部分，但如果做得好，就能让最终的 system 好得多，并省去大量工作。
-   对 data 进行 Collecting 和 digging into。几乎总是这样：如果我们开始查看某个此前没有的新 data source，就会发现巨大的 ~problems~ _opportunities for improvement_，并大幅改变我们的 prioritization。
    -   有意思的是，即使这类工作的一些形式看起来非常机械重复，也往往具有极高的 high leverage。例如，你可能认为，像“查看 100-1000 个 individual data points，寻找 anomalies”这样的 task 可以 delegated 给一名新入职的初级员工，但事实上，对这件事最执着的，恰恰是我们最资深的 researchers 和 managers——而他们常常会借此发现 severe issues，否则我们根本不会注意到。

我认为，寻找 high-leverage 工作包含两个 interrelated components：

-   **Agency：**也就是将努力促成事情发生的 initiative/proactiveness，与确保自己能够成功的 [relentlessness 和 resourcefulness](https://paulgraham.com/relres.html) 结合起来。
-   **Taste：**你需要有良好的 intuition，知道尝试哪些事情会奏效，哪些不会。Taste 无论在“in the large”（挑选 important problems）还是“in the small”（挑选能够有效 solving 这些 problems 的 approaches）上都很重要；我通常看到，人们先在后者上变得出色，然后才是前者。

没有 taste，你很可能会朝错误的方向努力。没有 agency，即使方向正确，你也很可能毫无进展。

怎样才能在这些方面有所提高？我认为，主要靠练习：与现实之间良好的 feedback loops，是任何东西都无法替代的。所以，这里最重要的启示也许是：_你有权_尝试发挥良好的 taste 和高度的 agency！不过，下面还有一些更具体的建议，它们曾帮助我在这些 dimensions 上有所提高。

## I. Agency（能动性）

### 理解 root goal，并从它出发 work backwards

获取更多 leverage 最简单的方法之一，就是针对一个你已经在努力实现的 goal，想出一种更好的方法来实现同一件事。（例如：改变你的 experiment design，让它与 high-level question 更直接相关；找到一种 building 一个 tool 的 80/20 方法；干脆决定完全不做某件事；等等。）为此，你需要仔细跟进具体 project 所针对的 high-level context 和 priorities，才能理解并判断其中的 trade-offs。

显然，无论谁在监督你的 project，都会试着向你指出他们所能想到的、实现 goal 最 effective 的方法。但寻找做一件事最 effective 的方法，需要大量时间和注意力；他们可能还有许多别的事情要做，而且掌握的详细 context 也远少于你，所以很容易有所遗漏！

例如，2022 年底我在 Anthropic 试岗时，被分配了一项 task：为我们的 compute cluster building 一个 custom scheduler，以 fix 那个 default scheduler 中一些给 researchers 添了不少麻烦的 behaviors。但在观察团队工作一周，并思考 system design 之后，我开始担心，这个 project 最终会给负责 cluster 的团队带来很高的 maintenance burden，而那个团队已经 overloaded；最后，我建议推迟这个 project。事实证明，这种 second-order effect 会让这个 project 对“让 clusters 保持良好运转！”这一 root goal 产生 net-negative 的影响！

### 不要太依赖许可或鼓励

关于 highest-leverage projects，有一个反直觉的事实：事先在多数人看来，它们往往并不 _显然_ 具有 high-leverage（因为如果显然如此，它们早就已经完成了）。这意味着，人们常常会怀疑推进这些 projects 的价值。

这种怀疑可能有几个原因（在现实世界中，大多数 projects 都混合存在这两种情况）：

-   人们可能低估 project 将产生的 impact。例如，我发现，人们常常低估与 metrics、instrumentation，或一般意义上的查看 data 有关的工作的价值，因为你无法 predict 自己具体会学到什么，或因此产生什么具体的 impact。正因如此，许多团队持续在查看 data 方面 under-invest。
-   这个 project 可能看起来太难，难以用 high-impact 的方式 execute。例如，我认识的一个人 built 了一个 system，通过对 _每一条 kernel log message_ 进行 parsing 和 categorizing，来 identify 一个 data center 中的 unhealthy hosts，最后每年节省了 $X00m/yr（如果我没记错的话）。他最初提出这个 project 时，其他人认为这需要一整个团队花上多个季度，可能是因为这项工作极其枯燥，而且 detail-oriented；但这个人即便面对枯燥的工作，也擅长保持 focused、做到 detail-oriented，所以只用了几个月就完成了。

我自己的一个例子是：加入 Anthropic 的 inference 团队不久，团队中的几个人提议彻底 rewriting 我们的 inference service codebase，因为他们觉得当时的 version 是 overcomplicated。我对此有些怀疑，依据的是“多数 rewrites 会 fail，而且多数人对它们需要多久都过于乐观”这一 [standard software engineering heuristic](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/)；不过，我没有强烈反对，因为我知道自己掌握的 context 不多。结果，这次 rewrite 只花了大约一周，就立刻带来了 integer-multiple efficiency improvement，而且让进一步实现 efficiency improvements 容易得多。我很庆幸大家没有听从我的疑虑！

应对这种 dynamic 的一种 tactic 是：不要请求别人批准你去做某件事，而是直接 [告诉他们你打算做什么](https://trainingmag.com/change-top-down-leadership-with-i-intend-to/)（这也隐含地给他们留下了空间：如果他们有强烈意见，可以提出反对或 course-correct）。

当然，同样是“high leverage projects 并不显而易见”这一现象，也意味着，你自主发起的一些 high-impact project 尝试很可能会 fail，因为你误判了它们的 impact 或 difficulty。因此，你应该把它们视为 _可能有回报、也可能没有回报的 bets_，并且——至少在你已经证明自己能在相当长的 time horizons 内做出好的 bets 之前——最好把它们与 low-risk projects 一起纳入 portfolio，避免出现这样的局面：你一头扎进单个 high-risk project，结果没能成功，人们因而不再信任你自行挑选 projects 的能力。

### 让 success 成为 inevitable

high-agency 的人有一个共同 trait：他们对 _achieving a goal_ 承担 accountability，而不只是做一些 work。

下面两种 operating modes 之间存在巨大 difference：

1.  我的 goal 是在月底前 ship 这个 project，所以我要让大家尽快开始 working on 它。
2.  我的 goal 是在月底前 ship 这个 project，所以我要 list out 届时需要完成的所有事情，从 ship date 开始 working backwards 来制定 schedule，确保 critical path 足够 short，确保我们有足够的 staffing 来做任何事情，弄清楚如果 schedule slips，我们要 cut 什么，如实判断我们需要多少 slop，按照 schedule 来 track progress，一发现任何 slippage 就立即 surface，如果需要人手，就从其他地方调人过来……

Mode 1 会让你成为一个 [leaky abstraction](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)——如果你的 project 是 critical，就需要其他人不断 monitoring 它，并弄清楚如何 resolve blockers。我用来指代处于 mode 2 的 [concept handle](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z3b7sidNrEkNaY9qfGwZjwz) 是“让 success 成为 inevitable”，因为“inevitable”这个标准意味着，其他人可以不再花费相当大一部分 mental energy 来担心这个 project。

能够让人放心地把某件事变成 inevitable 的人非常少见，而且他们通常是一个 team 或 company 能同时做多少不同事情的 bottleneck。所以，如果是别人在负责让你的 project 成为 inevitable，你就在 consuming 这种 scarce resource 的一部分；如果是你自己让自己的 projects 成为 inevitable，你就是这种 resource 的 _producer_，而且你在帮助自己的 team unblock 一个 key constraint。

（当然，在实践中，你永远无法实现 full inevitability，但更接近它仍然会带来巨大 difference！）

## II. Taste（品味）

### 找到你的 angle

我在上文写道，你应该预期 high-impact projects 是 non-obvious 的，因为如果它们是 obvious，就早已有人完成了。这指向另一个有意思的 dynamic：不同人的“最佳 taste 所在的 zone”很少会有很大程度的 overlap。相反，大多数人的 taste 的 quality 是高度 idiosyncratic 和 area-specific 的，这些 areas 可以 localized 到例如“language model personality design”或“什么 blog post titles 会在 Hacker News 上 get upvoted”（这是我自己 good taste 所在的 areas 中没那么有用的一个……）。

因此，一个值得持续关注的重要 signal 是：你的 taste 在哪里最好？

我注意到，很多人 underestimate 自己的 taste，因为他们以为拥有 good taste，感觉应该像是自己非常聪明、能干，或者很擅长做事情。遗憾的是，我得告诉你，至少如果你和我类似，你永远不会觉得自己聪明、能干或擅长做事情；相反，你只会越来越觉得，其他所有人都莫名其妙地不擅长这些事情。

因此，我在这里建议的 prompt 是：**哪些事情让你觉得，其他所有人都莫名其妙地做不好？** 这很可能就是一个 sign，说明你在这方面拥有 good taste。

如果这个 prompt 没有立即带来什么收获，也没关系；你可能只是身处一个有很多非常出色的人的 team，而你还没有在哪件事上拥有真正 unique 的 angle。即便如此，这个问题的一个 weakened version——在哪方面，似乎最多的人表现得最不称职？——也是一个有用的 gradient signal！

### 认真地 Think

理解 taste 的一种方式是，它关乎_你的 predictive models 和 search heuristics 的 quality_。如果我这样 design 这个 experiment，我会 find 什么？如果我这样 design 这个 tool，它 use 起来会有多容易？如果我这样 write 这份 doc，它会在多大程度上引起人们的共鸣？

进行足够的 search 和 prediction，以想出出色的 ideas，需要时间。我最先拥有一定程度的 taste 的 domain 是 software design，我记得有过[一次相当明显的 phase transition](https://www.benkuhn.net/conviction/#:~:text=Since%20I%20had%20plenty%20of%20free%20time%2C%20I%20spent%20it%20thinking%20through%20my%20plans%20from%20first%20principles.)，那时我获得了通过更深入地思考自己的 designs 来 improve 它们的能力。在那之后，我花了很多时间对许多不同的 design improvements 进行 iterating——其中大多数我从未 implemented，因为我无法想出足够让我满意的方案，但有少数最终带来了重大成果。

在这方面 improve 的最简单方法，就是试试看！每当你在斟酌要做什么时，explicitly 问自己：“如果我 choose option A，我 predict 会发生什么？”并尝试 unroll 这条 trajectory。即使你觉得自己已经在 intuitively predicting 自己的 choices 的 results，我也发现，explicit 地做这件事会带来出乎意料的巨大帮助——我的一位 manager role models 每次在我向他请教时，都会问我这个问题（“你觉得会发生什么？”），而它竟然经常帮助我意识到新的事情，频繁得有些不可思议。（如果想做得更好，之后再 revisit 你的 predictions。）

我发现，以下事情值得花很多时间思考：

-   要 work on 什么。改变你的 prioritization，往往是 improving 你的 leverage 的最大单一 lever！
-   广义上的“Design”——无论是 experiment design、system design、org design、process design，还是 outlining blog posts 等等。
-   projects 本来可以怎样进展得更好，以及我应该从中学到什么——见下文。

（另见：[认真地 Think](https://www.benkuhn.net/thinkrealhard/)。不过，如果你的自然 failure mode 是 overthink 事情，你或许应该[反过来运用这条建议](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/24/should-you-reverse-any-advice-you-hear/)。）

### 对你的 thinking 进行 Reflect

如果 taste 关乎你的 predictive models 和 search heuristics 的 quality，那么，从你获得的 data 中充分提取对它们的每一个可能的 update，就很重要。

因此，在我共事过的人当中，许多最 effective 的人也进行了最多的 _metacognition_，也就是 reflecting 自己（以及自己 team）的 work 和 thought processes，并弄清楚如何 improve 它们。他们往往也是最有可能 identify 我们的 processes 或 mental models 的 improvements 的人——例如：

-   哇，我们几个月前就应该 added 这个 instrumentation，因为我们从中学到了那么多有价值的东西。我大概应该 up-weight 我的这条 heuristic：“只要你没有在 looking at 关于某件重要事情的 data，你就很可能没有察觉到某处已经起火了。”
-   我觉得我们正在 working inefficiently，因为我们正在 overestimating 我们距离完成有多近；我们需要退后一步，improve 我们的 tooling。另外，我注意到我们还挺经常陷入这种 pattern；下次我要试着更早注意到它！
-   回过头来看，如果我们更早想到 X，本可以 avoided 这个 problem——下次我们处于类似情况时，要记住这一点。
-   我觉得，我们这里的 key constraint 是没有足够多具备 Y skillset 的人。我们能否以某种方式调动人员，让更多合适的人 working on 这个 problem？

他们往往不只是进行自己的 metacognition，还会通过与 team 分享这些 reflections、安排 retrospectives 等方式，帮助推动“group metacognition”。即使其中每一条 lessons 单独看来都很小，它们也会随着时间 compound，帮助个人和 teams 变得更加 effective。

我用来鼓励自己更多地 metacogitate 的最佳习惯，是做一次 [weekly review](https://www.benkuhn.net/weekly/)。自从我写下那篇文章以来，我的 reviews 的 format 和 typical outcomes 已经 evolved 了很多，但我仍然在做，而且发现它们对于帮助我 improve 自己当前着重 improving 的任何方面，都极其有价值！

（另见／延伸阅读：Chris Olah 的 [Research Taste Exercises（研究品味练习）](https://colah.github.io/notes/taste/)）

I’ve been thinking recently about what sets apart my coworkers who’ve done the best work.

You might think that the main thing that makes people really effective at research or engineering is technical ability, and among the general population that’s true. Among my Anthropic coworkers, though, we’ve [restricted the range](https://www.statology.org/restriction-of-range/) by screening for extremely high-percentile technical ability, so the remaining differences, while they still matter, aren’t quite as critical. Instead, people’s biggest bottleneck eventually becomes their ability to get _leverage_—i.e., to find and execute work that has a big impact-per-hour multiplier.

For example, here are some types of work at Anthropic that tend to have high impact-per-hour, or a high impact-per-hour ceiling when done well (of course this list is extremely non-exhaustive!):

-   Improving tooling, documentation, or dev loops. A tiny amount of time fixing a papercut in the right way can save hundreds of users hours of debugging or schlepping through inefficient workflows.
-   Identifying promising research directions. Things like [character](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-character) and [computer use](https://www.anthropic.com/news/3-5-models-and-computer-use) started off as a fraction of one person’s time, taking a bet that some technique applied to some problem was going to work really well, and ended up being a major influence on Anthropic’s research direction.
-   System design. This tends to be a small part of the overall time to execute a project, but when done well, it can make the final system way better and save lots of work.
-   Collecting and digging into data. It’s almost always the case that, if we start looking at some new source of data that we didn’t previously have, we discover huge ~problems~ _opportunities for improvement_, and it changes our prioritization a lot.
    -   Interestingly, even versions of this work that seem very rote are often extremely high leverage. For example, you might think that a task like “look at 100-1000 individual data points for anomalies” could be delegated to a junior hire, but in fact, the people who are most obsessive about this are our most senior researchers and managers—and they often find severe issues by doing it that we wouldn’t have noticed otherwise.

I think of finding high-leverage work as having two interrelated components:

-   **Agency:** i.e. some combination of the initiative/proactiveness to try to make things happen, and [relentlessness and resourcefulness](https://paulgraham.com/relres.html) to make sure you’ll succeed.
-   **Taste:** you need a good intuition for what things will and won’t work well to try. Taste is important both “in the large” (picking important problems) and “in the small” (picking approaches to solving those problems that will work well); I usually see people first become great at the latter, then the former.

Without taste, you’re likely to work toward the wrong thing. Without agency, even if you work toward the right thing, you’re likely to get nowhere.

How can someone improve at these? Mostly, I think, by practicing: there’s no substitute for good feedback loops with reality. So maybe the most important takeaway here is that _you have permission_ to try to exercise good taste and high agency! But below are some more specific pieces of advice that have helped me improve on these dimensions.

## I. Agency

### Understand and work backwards from the root goal

One of the easiest ways to get more leverage is to take a goal you’re already trying to accomplish, and figure out a better way to accomplish the same thing. (For example: changing your experiment design to be more directly relevant to the high-level question; finding an 80/20 way of building a tool; just deciding not to do something entirely; etc.) In order to do this, you need to keep careful track of the high-level context and priorities that your specific project is aimed at, so that you can understand and judge the trade-offs.

Obviously, whoever’s supervising your project will make an attempt to point you at the most effective way of achieving the goal that they can think of. But finding the most effective way of doing something takes a lot of time and attention, they probably have a lot of other things to do, and they also have much less detailed context than you, so they’re liable to miss things!

For example, when I did a work trial at Anthropic in late 2022, I was assigned the task of building a custom scheduler for our compute cluster to fix some unfortunate behaviors in the default scheduler that were making researchers’ lives hard. But after seeing the team work for a week and thinking about the system design, I started to worry that this project would end up imposing a high maintenance burden on the team that owned the cluster, which was already overloaded, and I ended up suggesting that we postpone it. It turned out that second-order effect would have made this project net-negative for the root goal of “keep the clusters working well!”

### Don’t rely too much on permission or encouragement

A counterintuitive fact about the highest-leverage projects is that they’re often not _obviously_ high-leverage to most people in advance (because if they were, they would already have been done a long time ago). That means people are often skeptical of the value of pursuing them.

That skepticism can be for a couple reasons (in the real world, most projects have some mix of both going on):

-   People might underrate the impact that the project will have. For example, I find that people often underestimate the value of work related to metrics, instrumentation, or generally looking at data, because you can’t predict what specific things you would learn or what specific impact you’d have as a result. Because of that, many teams consistently under-invest in looking at data.
-   The project might seem too hard to execute in a high-impact way. For example, someone I knew built a system to identify unhealthy hosts in a data center by parsing and categorizing _every kernel log message_, which ended up saving $X00m/yr (iirc). When he proposed the project initially, others thought it would take a whole team multiple quarters, probably because it’s extremely boring and detail-oriented; but because this person was good at focused, detail-oriented work even when it was boring, he finished it in a couple months.

A personal example: shortly after I joined Anthropic’s inference team, a couple folks on the team proposed completely rewriting our inference service codebase, because they felt the current version was overcomplicated. I was somewhat skeptical of this, based on [the standard software engineering heuristic](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/) of “most rewrites fail and most people are over-optimistic about how long they will take,” although I didn’t push back very hard because I knew I didn’t have much context. It turned out that the rewrite took about a week, was an immediate integer-multiple efficiency improvement, and made it much easier to make further efficiency improvements. I’m glad that people didn’t listen to my skepticism!

One tactic for addressing this dynamic is that instead of asking people for approval to go do something, you can just [tell them what you intend to do](https://trainingmag.com/change-top-down-leadership-with-i-intend-to/) (implicitly giving them space to object or course-correct if they feel strongly).

Of course, the same “high leverage projects are non-obvious” phenomenon also means that some of your self-driven high-impact project attempts will probably fail, because you misjudged their impact or difficulty. Because of this, you should think of them as _bets that might or might not pay off_, and—at least until you’ve proven your ability to make good bets over fairly long time horizons—it’s best to keep these as part of a portfolio with low-risk projects as well, to avoid the situation where you bite off a single high-risk project, it doesn’t pan out, and people lose trust in your ability to pick your own projects.

### Make success inevitable

A common trait of high-agency people is that they take accountability for _achieving a goal_, not just doing some work.

There’s a huge difference between the following two operating modes:

1.  My goal is to ship this project by the end of the month, so I’m going to get people started working on it ASAP.
2.  My goal is to ship this project by the end of the month, so I’m going to list out everything that needs to get done by then, draw up a schedule working backwards from the ship date, make sure the critical path is short enough, make sure we have enough staffing to do anything, figure out what we’ll cut if the schedule slips, be honest about how much slop we need, track progress against the schedule and surface any slippage as soon as I see it, pull in people from elsewhere if I need them…

Mode 1 makes you a [leaky abstraction](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)—if your project is critical, someone else needs to be constantly monitoring it and figuring out how to resolve blockers. My [concept handle](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z3b7sidNrEkNaY9qfGwZjwz) for being in mode 2 is “making success inevitable,” because “inevitable” is the bar where other people can stop spending substantial fractions of mental energy on worrying about the project.

People who can be trusted to make something inevitable are really rare, and are typically the bottleneck for how many different things a team or company can do at once. So if someone else is responsible for making your project inevitable, you’re consuming some of that scarce resource; if you’re the one making your own projects inevitable, you’re a _producer_ of that resource, and you’re helping unblock a key constraint for your team.

(Of course in practice you will never achieve full inevitability, but getting closer to it still makes a huge difference!)

## II. Taste

### Find your angle

I wrote above that you should expect high-impact projects to be non-obvious because if they were obvious, they’d have been done already. This points to another interesting dynamic, which is that it’s quite rare for different people’s “zone of best taste” to overlap very much. Instead, the quality of most people’s taste is highly idiosyncratic and area-specific, where areas can be as localized as e.g. “language model personality design” or “what blog post titles will get upvoted on Hacker News” (one of my own less-useful areas of good taste…)

For this reason, an important signal to keep track of is: where is your taste the best?

I’ve noticed a lot of people underestimate their own taste, because they expect having good taste to feel like being very smart or competent or good at things. Unfortunately, I am here to tell you that, at least if you are similar to me, you will never feel smart, competent, or good at things; instead, you will just start feeling more and more like everyone else mysteriously sucks at them.

For this reason, the prompt I suggest here is: **what does it seem like everyone else is mysteriously bad at?** That’s probably a sign that you have good taste there.

It’s okay if this prompt doesn’t immediately yield anything; you might just be on a team with a lot of really good people, where you don’t have a really unique angle on anything yet. Even so, a weakened version of this question—where does it seem like the most people are being the least competent?—is a useful gradient signal!

### Think real hard

One way of thinking about taste is that it’s about _the quality of your predictive models and search heuristics_. If I design the experiment this way, what will I find? If I design the tool this way, how easy will it be to use? If I write the doc this way, how much will it resonate with people?

Doing enough search and prediction to come up with great ideas takes time. The first domain that I got some degree of taste in was software design, and I remember [a pretty clear phase transition](https://www.benkuhn.net/conviction/#:~:text=Since%20I%20had%20plenty%20of%20free%20time%2C%20I%20spent%20it%20thinking%20through%20my%20plans%20from%20first%20principles.) where I gained the ability to improve my designs by thinking harder about them. After that point, I spent a lot of time iterating on many different design improvements—most of which I never implemented because I couldn’t come up with something I was happy enough with, but a few of which turned into major wins.

The easiest way to improve at this is just to try it! Whenever you’re debating what to do, explicitly ask yourself “what do I predict will happen if I choose option A?” and try to unroll the trajectory. Even if you think you’re already intuitively predicting the results of your choices, I’ve found it helps surprisingly much to be explicit—one of my manager role models asks me this (“what do you think will happen?”) every time I ask him for advice and it’s kind of silly how often it helps me realize something new. (For bonus points, revisit your predictions afterwards.)

Things that I’ve found benefit from a lot of thinking time:

-   What to work on. Changing your prioritization is often the single biggest lever for improving your leverage!
-   “Design” broadly construed—whether that’s experiment design, system design, org design, process design, outlining blog posts, etc.
-   How projects could have gone better and what I should learn from them—see below.

(See also: [Think real hard](https://www.benkuhn.net/thinkrealhard/). Although if you’re someone whose natural failure mode is to overthink things, it’s possible you should [reverse this advice](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/24/should-you-reverse-any-advice-you-hear/).)

### Reflect on your thinking

If taste is about the quality of your predictive models and search heuristics, it’s important to wring out every possible update to these from the data that you get.

For that reason, many of the most effective people I’ve worked with also do the most _metacognition_, i.e., reflecting on their own (and their team’s) work and thought processes, and figuring out how to improve them. They’re often the people who are most likely to identify improvements to our processes or mental models—things like:

-   Wow, we should have added this instrumentation months ago because we learned so much valuable stuff from it. I should probably up-weight my heuristic that “any time you’re not looking at data about something important, you’re probably unaware of some fire.”
-   I think we’re working inefficiently because we’re overestimating how close we are to being done; we need to step back and improve our tooling. Also, I’ve noticed we get into this pattern kinda often; I’ll try to notice it earlier next time!
-   In retrospect, we could have avoided this problem if we’d thought about X earlier—let’s keep that in mind next time we’re in a similar situation.
-   I think our key constraint here is we don’t have enough people with Y skillset. Can we move people around somehow to get more of the right people working on the problem?

Often, they don’t just do their own metacognition but also help drive “group metacognition” by sharing these reflections with the team, scheduling retrospectives, etc. Even if each of the lessons here is small individually, they compound over time to help people and teams become much more effective.

My best habit for encouraging myself to metacogitate more is a [weekly review](https://www.benkuhn.net/weekly/). The format and typical outcomes of my reviews have evolved a lot since I wrote that, but I still do them and find them extremely valuable for helping me improve at whatever I’m currently focused on improving at!
