# 零零碎碎

---

## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_一位同事请我简短列出一些做好 creative research work 的 heuristics 或 principles。以下是我为回应这个问题而进行的三个小时的意识流思考，略作修订——我对自己感受的强烈程度感到惊讶！理想情况下，它应该经过大幅修订和改进，但与其等待一个假想中的改进版本，似乎不如先发布这份不完美的笔记。背景：这些想法来自我在 theoretical physics、computer science、tools for thought 和 metascience 中的工作；后两者在很大程度上还是 proto-fields。读者请自行斟酌：把这些想法写在屏幕上，感觉像是在自吹自擂，尤其是我经常因为自己工作的 progress（或者毫无 progress）而沮丧！但我也相信，creative research 是我们 researchers 共同参与的事业；如果每个人都分享自己对于如何开展它的想法、我们发现什么对自己有效、什么无效、我们在哪里感到沮丧，等等，我们所有人都会受益。因此，我很希望看到其他类似的文档！写于 January 2023，发布于 July 2023。_

**警惕 general principles：**工作的 Principles 并不像 laws of nature。对一个人有效的做法，对另一个人往往很糟糕。在你人生某个时期有效的做法，在另一个时期可能效果很差。因此，你需要由各种（有时 contradictory 的）heuristics 拼接而成的一套办法。事情进展顺利时，就继续做你正在做的事。陷入困境时，就尝试其他 heuristics。这样做可能很难：如果你固执而坚定，你很可能会加倍投入，试图硬撑着走出困境。而如果你不固执、不坚定，又很难成为一名好的 researcher。的确：好的 researchers 往往把固执、坚定，_以及_惊人的灵活性和改变方向的意愿，非同寻常地结合在一起。

与此相关：你必须弄清楚，**你自己**需要怎样工作。你与其他人很可能有一些共同之处——毕竟我们都与 East African plains apes 相差不远（！）——但也会有许多个人特性。我认识的杰出 researchers 大多是极其不同寻常的人，他们的工作方式也往往极其不同寻常。

**警惕建议：**建议的问题往往在于，最需要认真对待它的人没有认真对待，而最需要忽略它的人又没有忽略。你属于哪一类？你怎么知道？如果你尊敬的人给你建议，而它让你产生共鸣、受到鼓舞，那就照着做！如果它拖慢了你的脚步，有时是因为你需要改变正在做的事。但有时则是因为这条建议不适合你。即使最优秀的人，也会给出与你不相配的建议。试着摆弄它。考虑同样有力却方向相反的建议。还有没有其他方式可以消化它？如果没有，不理会它也没关系。但不要让它使你消沉。

（我早年的 physics 老师中，有相当一部分人告诉我，我不适合 physics，还详细解释了原因，这个比例高得令人惊讶。回头看，我很清楚，那些人根本不知道自己在说什么。但这段经历让我在很长一段时间里举步维艰。）

**Momentum：势头。**总的来说：momentum 最重要，momentum 最重要，momentum 最重要。而 corollary 是：尽你所能去 cultivate motivation[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn1)。

**作为 mutual mentorship 的 Collaboration：**最好的 collaborations 往往包含 mutual mentorship[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn2)。也就是说，你的 collaborator 帮助你成长，你也帮助他们成长。这在一定程度上是你们各自 capacities 的 function。但它也部分反映了你们学习的意愿。一件奇怪的事是：对于那些让我学到最多东西的人，我当时往往并没有注意到。我所学的东西太陌生，以至于直到很久以后，我才意识到自己当时_确实在_学习。一个例子是我与 Ben Schumacher 在 quantum information 上的 collaboration：Ben 看待世界和提出问题的方式，与我认识的任何人都不同。这甚至远比我从他那里学到的那些（非常有价值的）technical 东西更有价值。

寻找比你年轻得多、也比你年长得多的 mentors。显然，这在职业生涯的起点或终点可能并不适用！困扰年长 researchers 的最糟糕的 patterns 之一，是他们相信自己比年轻人懂得更多。他们较年轻的 collaborators 最后往往沦为苦力——干活的工具。要在你 60 多岁、70 多岁和 80 多岁时仍然保持创造活力，办法似乎是找到那些二三十岁、能让你 learn deeply 的人[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn3)。这可能意味着帮助他们推进_他们的_ projects，而不是你的。

无论你多大年纪，学会与许多不同年龄的人交谈，都是一种 competitive advantage。这可能令人畏怯，尤其是在刚开始的时候。

T. E. Lawrence 的传记作者之一 John Mack 曾说，Lawrence 的根本特质是“一种 enablement 的力量”。他发现了许多这样的事例：Lawrence 的人生与另一个人短暂交会，有时只有几分钟，却会改变那个人的人生轨迹。具体情形是这样的：Lawrence 会深入询问这位新相识的人，引出他们想要的东西——有时甚至是他们自己都不曾真正承认的渴望——然后把那份渴望清晰透彻地呈现在他们面前，如同一面镜子，并向他们说明一种非常具体的方式，让他们看到自己有力量、有能力立即朝它行动。不是每个人都是 Lawrence。但你可以有意识地以 enablement 的态度与人交往，并培养这项 skill。它确实是一项 skill。

**frontier 的移动可以比任何人都快：**这一刻，令人兴奋的 research frontier 还在这里，就在你所在的位置，下一刻，它便到了某个遥远的 area。这与 wavefront 能够比 wave 中任何 particle 移动得（快得多！）的情形非常相似。这种情况的发生有几个原因。当然，它之所以成为可能，是因为有数量庞大的人群，具备各种不同的 expertise 组合；当你已不再身处那个令人兴奋的 frontier 时，另一个人却在某天醒来，发现自己的 expertise 如今炙手可热，而就在一个月前，它还无人问津。这也部分是因为 capital 的移动，比 individuals acquire expertise 快得多[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn4)。

这是一种在情绪上很难应对的处境。不同的人有不同的回应方式。有些人总是在追逐浪潮。这通常会带来乏味、跟风的 research。另一种做法是问：我能否找到某个 secret thesis，让我拥有 unique edge？答案往往是否定的。但有时，人们能找到走到浪潮前面的办法。不管怎样，这都比单纯追逐浪潮更好。第二种做法则是尝试 cultivate 你自己的 vision，有意绕开时髦的东西。

就我个人而言，当我能作出一份重要的 unique contribution 时，我便觉得工作有意义。我大部分工作所处的 areas，都是我希望在未来几十年后能成为 full-fledged fields 的地方，做着我希望属于 foundational work 的工作。另一些人则喜欢 hot fields 中 competition 带来的刺激，享受以微弱优势赢得竞赛。我曾与一位 CRISPR scientist 交谈，他告诉我，他喜欢比 competitors 提前一两天 submitting a paper。他说：“我们大家做 science，不就是为了这个 \[competition\] 吗？”我宁愿把自己的眼睛挖出来。这也不完全意味着他错了：我猜，对他而言，这就是唯一能激励他做 science 的方式。但这确实意味着：理解你自己（真实的！）motivations 非常重要。然后，再据此弄清楚最适合自己的工作方式。

## 零零碎碎

1.  最重要的 highest order bit 是你所从事的 _整体_ area 的 fertility，而不是你自己。在 1920s 从事 quantum mechanics，或在 2010s 从事 CRISPR，或在 1970s 从事 interface design，你很可能都会做得不错。假设：“我在一个极其 fertile 的 subject area 中 worked，那时它还没有变得极其热门，身处相当不错的 environment，而且我 worked hard，并怀有 imagination、courage 和 determination。”你会做得极其出色。相比之下：你可以才华惊人，也非常勤奋，但如果身处错误的 area，就只会 make a minor contribution。即使是平庸之辈，也能在极其 fertile、却 under-subscribed 的 areas 中 make significant contributions。与此相关：年轻时做出过杰出工作的年长 researchers，经常接受采访，谈论他们是如何做到的。他们往往给出各种 process explanations（“你看，我们的 seminar culture 是……”）。这些内容很有意思，也可能有所帮助，但与“我在一个极其 fertile 的 subject area 中 worked very hard \[等等\]”相比，都只是 low-order bits。
2.  其次重要的 highest order bit——两者密切相关！——是 developing good taste。这并不像有些人以为的那样，意味着弄清什么正流行，然后跟着人群走。你的工作充其量也只是别人无论如何都会做出的工作。相反，它意味着 developing 你自己的一套套 heuristics，用来判断什么具有 fundamental importance，哪些是重大的 overall drivers 和 opportunities，以及你能够 develop 哪些 special skills，使你得以 contribute。Einstein 的职业生涯中，有一点让我很喜欢：他 published 了（如果我没记错）六种用于 determining Avogadro's number 的 methods。在我们今天看来，这很奇怪。但我相信，这很好地 reflects 了 Einstein 内在的 taste，即对某种 understanding the world 的方式的执着。正是同一种 taste 引领他走向 special and general relativity，以及他的其他 major discoveries。
3.  好的 environment != 有名气的 environment。在其他条件相同的情况下，名气有所帮助。但最好的 environments 往往在默默无闻的地方。
4.  Courage、imagination 和 determination 通常比聪明更重要。在令人惊讶的程度上，research 关乎 emotional self-management。
5.  有什么是你知道、而其他人都不知道的？有什么是你能做到、而其他人都做不到的？如果你刚开始职业生涯，而答案是“没多少”，那么你能否找到一些 routine projects，既能帮助别人，又能帮助你 develop unique capabilities？哪些 capabilities 能使我们在未来 3-30 年里 solve 最深刻的 problems？你能否找到办法，开始 develop 它们，同时仍做些有用的事？
6.  鉴于以上种种：你所 work on 的 problems 极其重要。我说的是“work on”，但这并不等同于“pick”。Einstein 对 Maxwell's equations 的一些奇怪的 transformation properties 产生了兴趣；在试图 resolve 它们的过程中，他 discovered，space 和 time、mass 和 energy 并非看上去那样，而是 fundamentally different。Darwin 参加了一次 _geology_ expedition，却因为如此好奇、善于观察，最终 understanding 了 origin of species。伟大的 problems 必须经过 discovered；problem 的 solution 往往只占整个故事中很小的一部分，绝大部分其实都关乎 discovering the problem。
7.  你从最优秀的人那里学到的东西中，有惊人之多属于 tacit knowledge。他们如何 respond emotionally；何时 work hard，何时放松；他们如何 play with ideas；他们 ignore 什么；他们如何应对潮流；他们如何回应消息；他们如何 approach problems；他们如何安排一天；如何安排一年；如何安排十年。以及无数其他难以言说的事情。Gabriel García Márquez 谈到阅读 Kafka 时说：“我心想，我以前不知道原来还可以这样写。如果早知道，我很久以前就开始写了。”更一般地说，仔细观察那些格外 creative 的人，会拓展你对自己可以做什么、做什么可能有益的认识。
8.  对自己工作的 Cynicism 极具 destructive 性。如果你发现自己正变得 cynical，就要准备好对自己的 environment 作出 drastic changes。从小处开始，但要：换 supervisor、换城市、换 field，做点什么。burnout 也是如此，尽管理由不同。
9.  不要、不要、不要在 grant applications 中撒谎。这部分是出于公益之心，但还有一个非常重大的自利理由：如果你撒谎，它_一定会_让你对自己正在做的事情感到困惑，而这会 damage 你的工作。无论你多么认为不是这样，事实都是如此。顺便说一下，这并不意味着你就必须做自己声称要做的事情（除非那是 grant 的一个 condition）：如果你正在学到有趣的东西，你的 project 就会 change，有时甚至会 radically change。但 clarity 和 sincerity 是宝贵的 intellectual fuel。
10.  Research 涉及大量 routine janitorial work。即使是最聪明的人，通常也会做海量的 routine work。做这些工作，是在为不那么 routine 的工作 lays the groundwork。我经常见到 grad students 不愿亲自动手做 routine calculations，盼望凭借灵光一现就能 solve the problem；随后看到一位 Fields Medallist 或 Nobelist 挽起袖子，一页接一页地做非常 elementary calculations，只为追查每一条线索，实在让人清醒。据说高尔夫球手 Jack Nicklaus 曾声称，自己练习得越努力，就越幸运。在 research 中似乎也是如此，而且体现在两个不同方面：你越努力，就会越幸运；你在 routine things 上越努力，就会拥有越多 insight[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn5)。
11.  正如一位朋友爱说的：“Motivation 随 action 而来。”如果你一直等着有了 motivated 的感觉才行动，就永远什么也做不成。另一方面，如果你反复 act，却仍然没有变得 motivated，你大概就需要找一个不同的 project、不同的 environment，或不同的 subject area。
12.  诚实的 post mortems 出乎意料地有帮助。在 project 开始前和进行过程中，坦诚面对自己的恐惧与不足，也同样有帮助。
13.  Courage 很重要。举个例子，这是无数例子中的一个：人们通常认为，大型 research collaborations 是由 senior researchers 安排的。但 discovered accelerating universe 的两个 collaborations 中，有一个主要由两位 postdocs（Adam Riess 和 Brian Schmidt）organized 和 driven。他们看到了用 type 1A supernovae 来 determine expansion of the universe 是否正在 accelerating 的 opportunity，尽管不具备通常所需的资历，还是直接去召集了为此需要的所有人和 resources。结果，他们 transformed 了我们对 universe 的 understanding，showing Einstein 在一个重要方面是错的。还赢得了 Nobel Prize。
14.  把一门 subject 教好，是学习它的绝佳方式。这之所以成为老生常谈，是因为它是真的。不过，它有一个有趣的 corollary：通过挑战自己教得更好，你可以学到更多。我确信，Feynman 从 _The Feynman Lectures on Physics_ 中 learned 的，比他的任何一个学生都多。而你可以投入比 Feynman 更多的 creativity 和努力。看看 Brian Moriarty、Vi Hart 或 Grant Sanderson 这样的人，就知道一个人能在 explanation 中倾注多少 creativity。Andrei Kolmogorov 决定开设一门 lecture class，讲授 Hilbert's problems 中的一个；那门课带来了该 problem 的 solution。这是一个 extreme example，但这种 pattern 并不罕见。
15.  Difficult 完全不等同于 important，也就是说，困难并不等于重要。
16.  学会理解 work hard 意味着什么：这出乎意料地困难。它意味着知道什么时候该加把劲，什么时候不该。它意味着知道如何好好度假，如何享受生活。你_绝不能成为苦役般的劳作者_——这在有抱负的人当中是一个出乎意料地常见的问题。你必须敏锐地觉察那些蕴含 leverage、creative opportunity 和 insight 的时刻。当然，它也意味着知道如何极其努力地工作，并怀有极大的 courage 和 determination。
17.  与此相关：照顾好自己的身体。照顾好自己的 energy levels。
18.  做些傻乎乎的工作：graphene 的 co-discoverer Andre Geim 专门留出周五下午，做那些在脑子里挥之不去的 foolish experiments。这既为他带来了 Nobel Prize，也带来了 igNobel Prize，还带来了另外几条重要的 lines of work。
19.  Groucho's law of creative work：“你永远不该 work on 任何你能为之获得 funding 的 project”：这显然带点玩笑意味。但其中也有某种深层道理。如果很容易获得 funding，你的工作很可能就是那种别人无论如何也会去做的工作。你能做些什么，是否则就不会有人去做的？research 中存在着一种类似 efficient market hypothesis 的东西，即认为存在一个 research projects 的 space，我们的 institutions 基本上注定会把其中的 projects 做完。你要摆脱它，在做重要事情的同时 bend the market。你能否想到什么，是现有 institutions 没有 courage 或 imagination 去 support 的？你能否想出某种办法，无论如何也把它做出来？
20.  度假——真正的度假，那种纯粹为了享受而做事的度假——往往有助于走出困境：对我来说，好好度假的模式，是先给自己 2-3 天，几乎什么也不做。然后去探索世界。不要、不要、不要半休假，一边还在回复 email，做些零零碎碎的工作，_等等_。构思、计划，或 work on 某个**别的** project，都没关系——在 Hawaii 度假期间（那次度假非常放松！），我意外地在一阵密集的工作中写出了自己的书“Reinventing Discovery”的 first draft。但不要做_任何_你认为属于 routine work 的事情。
21.  刚起步时，很容易无所适从。你不知道的东西太多了！实际上，你几乎不需要知道什么。找一件重要、而你又能 make a contribution 的事，然后 work on 它。这是与出色的 researchers 共事的一大好处。他们会帮助你找到重要、而你又能够 contribute 的事情。PhD students 常常以为，他们的 advisors 会帮助他们弄清**如何** solve a problem，但 advisors 所做的更重要的事情，是帮助他们找到这样的 problems：(a) 是 tractable 的；(b) 具有一定 importance；(c) 能帮助他们 develop；以及 (d) 帮助他们知道该 ignore 什么。
22.  Courage 是一个关键要素[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn6)。而且必须拿出它来：只要你仅仅沿着某个已有 community 默许的 paths 前进，你的工作就会属于那种无论如何都可能出现的工作。要超越这一点，你必须 construct 自己的 belief。我在这件事上挣扎得很厉害。
23.  话虽如此，在力所能及的范围内：尽可能把你的 understanding 和 creative work 拿出来与人交流。与支持你的人交谈——即使他们并不理解——也极有帮助。我想，我从未开过一门没有带来十倍回报的课；尽管如此，我还是经常犹豫（“工作量太大了！”）
24.  process advice 的危险：我在这里给出了大量 process advice。它可能让人无所适从。归根结底，你就是得动手去做。继续阅读来自各种不同来源的大量建议，对任何看起来能立刻有所帮助的建议付诸行动，反思这些建议，然后试着在大多数时候忘掉它们！

1.  每一位 top performer 都是 intensely motivated 的。关于 Michael Jordan 的故事中，有一件事令人印象深刻：为了 motivate 自己，他会做到令人不安的地步。他会凭空编造或近乎凭空编造对方球员对他的些微怠慢，然后对这些幻想念念不忘，有时持续数年，以此 fueling 他自己的 drive。这不是一种好的生活方式。但作为一个说明如何通过 action 来 cultivated drive 的例子，它很有意思。我不推荐 Jordan 的做法，但研究其他人如何 cultivate drive 是有用的。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref1)

2.  我经常听到有人抱怨：“我找不到任何 mentors。”他们通常对 mentorship 抱有一个 broken model：他们希望有人拿出数百小时的时间，手把手地带着自己。他们不如设法与朋友 collaborate，并设法做些真正对他人有帮助的事。我的一位朋友会自愿在 conferences 和 lab meetings 上做详尽的笔记，并分发给大家。我认识的几个人编过多份非常有用的 annotated bibliographies。那些设法真正帮助他人的人很受欢迎，也很快就能找到可以 collaborate 的人。（这绝_不_意味着你应该期待任何回报。）那些只是盼着有人可怜自己的人，就没那么受欢迎了。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref2)

3.  我在 Jill Tarter、Lenny Susskind 和 Stirling Colgate 这样的人身上注意到了这一点。这里只举众多例子中的一个：几年前，我邀请 Tarter 参加一场活动——她是 SETI 的伟大先驱之一，当时已是 75 岁上下。活动安排在周五晚上和周六。她回复说，她很愿意周五晚上来，但如果周六不能到场，我是否介意，因为她那天要主持一整天的 SETI hackathon，而且非常期待参与其中。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref3)

4.  这有时会造成一种错觉，仿佛作出 capital allocation decisions 的人是 progress 的推动者。这在多大程度上属实，值得思考。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref4)

5.  关于 hard work：请看下文关于 drudgery 的评论。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref5)

6.  十几岁时，我对 research 的印象是，它是围绕 insight 和 brilliance 展开的。如今，我更倾向于认为它需要 courage、imagination 和 determination。当然需要 [Relentless resourcefulness](http://www.paulgraham.com/relres.html)，但还需要更多东西。[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fnref6)

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_A colleague asked me for a short list of heuristics or principles for doing good creative research work. Here's three hours of stream-of-consciousness thinking in response, lightly revised -- I was surprised by the strength of my feeling! It would ideally be heavily revised and improved, but it seemed better to release an imperfect set of notes than to await a hypothetical improved version. Context: informed by my work in theoretical physics, computer science, tools for thought, and metascience; the latter two are very much proto-fields. Caveat emptor: it feels like bragging to be putting such thoughts to screen, especially since I'm so often frustrated by the progress (or lack thereof) in my work! But I also believe creative research is something we researchers are all in together, and that we'd all be better off if everyone shared their thoughts on how to do it, what we find works for us, what doesn't, where we get frustrated, and so on. So I'd love to see other documents like this! Written January 2023, released July 2023._

**Beware general principles:** Principles of work aren't like the laws of nature. What works for one person is often terrible for another. What works at one time in your life may work terribly at another. So you need a patchwork of (sometimes contradictory) heuristics. When things are going well, keep doing what you're doing. When you're in a rut, try other heuristics. It can be hard to do that: if you're stubborn and determined, you'll likely double down, and try to tough out the rut. And it's hard to be a good researcher if you're not stubborn and determined. Indeed: good researchers are often a remarkable combination of stubborn, determined, _and_ astonishingly flexible and willing to re-orient.

Related: you must figure out how **you** need to work. There will likely be some overlap with other people – we're all near enough to East African plains apes(!) – but also many idiosyncrasies. The great researchers I've known were mostly extremely unusual individuals, and often had extremely unusual approaches to work.

**Beware advice:** The trouble with advice is often that the people who most need to take it seriously, don't, and the ones who most need to ignore it, don't. Which category are you in? How do you know? If someone you respect gives you advice, and it resonates and inspires you, run with it! If it slows you down, sometimes it's because you need to change what you're doing. But sometimes it's because it's the wrong advice for you. Even the very best people will dispense advice that's mismatched for you. Play with it. Consider the equal and opposite advice. Is there some other way to metabolize it? And if not, it's fine to disregard it. But don't let it get you down.

(A surprising fraction of my early physics teachers told me I wasn't suited to physics, explaining in detail why. In retrospect it's clear to me that those people didn't know what they were talking about. But this experience made things very difficult for a long time.)

**Momentum:** In general: momentum rules, momentum rules, momentum rules. And the corollary is: do everything you can to cultivate motivation[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn1).

**Collaboration as mutual mentorship:** The best collaborations often involve mutual mentorship[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn2). That is, your collaborator helps you grow, and you help them grow. This is partially a function of your respective capacities. But it's also in part a reflection of your willingness to learn. A strange thing: I often didn't notice the people from whom I learned the most, at the time. What I was learning was so unfamiliar that I didn't even realize I _was_ learning, until much later. An example was my collaboration on quantum information with Ben Schumacher: Ben approached the world and asked questions in a way unlike anyone I knew. This was far more valuable than even the (very valuable) technical things I learned from him.

Find mentors much younger and much older than you. Obviously, this may not apply at the beginning or end of a career! One of the worst patterns afflicting older researchers is that they believe they know better than younger people. Their younger collaborators often end up as peons – vessels for work. The way to remain creatively alive into your 60s and 70s and 80s seems to be to find people in their 20s and 30s from whom you can learn deeply[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn3). That may mean helping them with _their_ projects, not yours.

No matter what your age, learning to talk to people of many different ages is a competitive advantage. This can be intimidating, especially at first.

John Mack, one of the biographers of T. E. Lawrence, remarked that Lawrence's essential quality was "a power of enablement". He found many instances where Lawrence's life would briefly intersect some other person, sometimes for just a few minutes, and yet it would change the course of that person's life. The pattern was this: Lawrence would question his new acquaintance deeply, elicit what they wanted – sometimes desires they had barely acknowledged to themselves – and then hold that desire up in crystalline form, a kind of mirror, and explain to them a very concrete way in which they had the power and capability to immediately act toward it. Not everyone is Lawrence. But you can consciously approach people with an attitude of enablement, and cultivate the skill. It is a skill.

**The frontier can move faster than any human:** One moment the exciting research frontier is over here, where you are, the next it's in some distant area. This is very similar to the way a wavefront can move (much!) faster than any particle in a wave. There are several reasons this happens. It is, of course, enabled by the enormous ocean of people with many different combinations of expertise; while you are no longer at the exciting frontier, someone else woke up one day to find that their expertise is now hot, when just a month earlier it was sleepy. It's also partly because capital can move much faster than individuals can acquire expertise[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn4).

This is an emotionally challenging situation. Different people respond in different ways. Some are always chasing after the wave. This usually leads to boring, me-too research. One alternate approach is to ask: is there any secret thesis I can find, something that gives me a unique edge? Often, the answer is no. But sometimes people find a way to get ahead of the wave. In any case, it's better than pure wave chasing. And a second approach is to try to cultivate your own vision, deliberately routing around what is fashionable.

Personally, I find work meaningful when I can make a unique contribution of importance. Most of my work has been in areas that I hope will be full-fledged fields decades in the future, doing what I hope is foundational work. Some people instead love the thrill of competition in hot fields, and enjoy narrowly winning races. I had a conversation with a CRISPR scientist who told me that he loved submitting a paper a day or two ahead of his competitors. He said: "Isn't that \[competition\] why we all do science?" I'd rather gouge my eyes out. Doesn't mean he was wrong, exactly: for him, I suspect, it's the only way he'd be motivated to do science. But it does mean: understanding your own (honest!) motivations matters a lot. And then figuring out the best way to work in response.

## Bits and pieces

1.  The highest order bit is the fertility of the _overall_ area you are working in, not you yourself. Work on quantum mechanics in the 1920s, or CRISPR in the 2010s, or interface design in the 1970s, and you would likely do well. Suppose: "I worked in a subject area that was incredibly fertile, before it became super-fashionable, in a pretty good environment, and I worked hard and with imagination and courage and determination". You would do extremely well. By contrast: you can be astoundingly talented and hard working, but if you're in the wrong area you will only make a minor contribution. Even duffers can make significant contributions in areas that are incredibly fertile, but under-subscribed. Related: old researchers who did great work in their youth are often interviewed about how they did it. They often give all kinds of process explanations ("see, our seminar culture was…"). It's interesting stuff, and can be helpful, but low-order bits compared to: "I worked very hard in a subject area that was incredibly fertile \[etc\]".
2.  The next highest order bit – closely related! – is developing good taste. This does not mean, as some people think, figuring out what's fashionable, and going where the crowd is going. Your work will, at very best, be work that someone else would have done anyway. Rather, it means developing your own sets of heuristics for what is of fundamental importance, what are the big overall drivers and opportunities, and what special skills you can develop that will enable you to contribute. Something I love about Einstein's career is that he published (IIRC) six methods for determining Avogadro's number. To our modern eye, this looks strange. But I believe it reflects Einstein's underlying taste well, an obsession with a certain way of understanding the world. It's the same kind of taste that led him to special and general relativity and his other major discoveries.
3.  Good environment != branded environment. All other things equal, brand helps. But often the best environments are in no-name places.
4.  Courage, imagination, and determination are usually more important than being smart. To an astounding extent, research is about emotional self-management.
5.  What do you know that no-one else does? What can you do that no-one else can? If it's early in your career, and the answer is "not much", then can you find routine projects that will help other people, and help you develop unique capabilities? What capabilities will enable us to solve the deepest problems over the next 3-30 years? Can you find ways of beginning to develop those, while still doing something useful?
6.  Given all that: the problems you work on matter enormously. I say "work on" but that's not the same as "pick". Einstein got interested in some strange transformation properties of Maxwell's equations; in trying to resolve that, he discovered that space and time, mass and energy, are not as they seemed, but fundamentally different. Darwin went on a _geology_ expedition, but was so curious and observant that he ended up understanding the origin of species. Great problems have to be discovered; often the solution of the problem is only a tiny part of the story, most of it is really about discovering the problem.
7.  A shocking fraction of what you learn from the best people is tacit knowledge. How they respond emotionally; when they work hard, when they relax; the way they play with ideas; what they ignore; how they respond to fashion; how they respond to news; how they approach problems; how they structure their day; how they structure their year; how they structure their decade. And a million other hard-to-articulate things. Gabriel García Márquez said of reading Kafka: "I thought to myself that I didn't know anyone was allowed to write things like that. If I had known, I would have started writing a long time ago". More generally, close examination of unusually creative people will expand your sense of what you're allowed to do, and what may be beneficial to do.
8.  Cynicism about your work is incredibly destructive. If you find yourself getting cynical, be prepared to make drastic changes to your environment. Start small, but: change supervisor, change city, change field, do something. Ditto burnout, though for different reasons.
9.  Do not, do not, do not lie in your grant applications. This is partially for public-spirited reasons, but there's a really big selfish reason: if you lie it _will_ confuse you about what you're doing, and that will damage your work. This is true no matter how much you think it isn't. This does not, by the way, mean you are then tied to do what you said you were going to do (unless that's a condition of the grant): if you're learning interesting things, then your project will change, sometimes radically. But clarity and sincerity are valuable intellectual fuel.
10.  Research involves tremendous amounts of routine janitorial work. Even the smartest people usually do enormous amounts of routine work. Doing it lays the groundwork for the less routine work. I've often witnessed grad students who are reluctant to get their hands dirty doing routine calculations, hoping they can solve the problem with a stroke of insight; it's sobering to then see a Fields Medallist or Nobelist roll up their sleeves and do page after page of very elementary calculations, just chasing down every single lead. The golfer Jack Nicklaus supposedly claimed that the harder he practiced the luckier he got. The same seems to be true in research, in two different ways: the harder you work, the luckier you will get; and the harder you work at routine things, the more insight you will have[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn5).
11.  "Motivation follows action", as a friend likes to say. If you keep waiting to be motivated, you'll never get anything done. On the other hand, if you repeatedly act and aren't getting motivated, you probably need to find a different project, a different environment, or a different subject area.
12.  Honest post mortems are surprisingly helpful. So is being honest about your fears and shortcomings, in advance and during the course of the project.
13.  Courage matters. An example, one of infinitely many: large research collaborations are usually thought of as being arranged by senior researchers. But one of the two collaborations that discovered the accelerating universe was largely organized and driven by two postdocs (Adam Riess and Brian Schmidt). They saw the opportunity to use type 1A supernovae to determine whether the expansion of the universe was accelerating, and just went and rounded up all the people and resources they needed to do it, despite not having the usual seniority. And, as a result, transformed our understanding of the universe, showing that Einstein was wrong in an important way. And won a Nobel Prize.
14.  Teaching a subject well is a superb way to learn it. This is a cliche because it's true. It has a curious corollary, though, which is that by challenging yourself to teach better you can learn more. I'm sure Feynman learned more from _The Feynman Lectures on Physics_ than any of his students. And you can pour more creativity and effort in than even Feynman did. Look at people like Brian Moriarty or Vi Hart or Grant Sanderson to see just how much creativity someone can pour into explanation. Andrei Kolmogorov decided to teach a lecture class about one of Hilbert's problems; that class led to the solution of the problem. This is an extreme example, but not an unusual pattern.
15.  Difficult is not at all the same as important.
16.  Learn what it means to work hard: this is surprisingly difficult. It means knowing when to push, when not. It means knowing how to vacation well and how to enjoy your life. You _must not become a drudge_ – a surprisingly common problem among the ambitious. You must become alive to moments of leverage and creative opportunity and insight. And, of course, it also means knowing how to work incredibly hard and with great courage and determination.
17.  Related: Take care of your body. Take care of your energy levels.
18.  Do some damn-fool work: the co-discoverer of graphene, Andre Geim, set aside Friday afternoons to do foolish experiments that he couldn't quite get out of his head. It led to both his Nobel Prize and an igNobel Prize, as well as several other important lines of work.
19.  Groucho's law of creative work: "You should never work on any project for which you can get funding": Obviously somewhat tongue-in-check. But there's some underlying truth. If it's easy to get funding, yours is probably the kind of work someone else will do anyway. What can you do that won't otherwise be done? There's a kind of analog of the efficient market hypothesis in research, the notion that there's a space of research projects our institutions are pretty much just going to get done. You want to escape out of that, and bend the market, while doing something important. Is there anything you can think of that existing institutions don't have the courage or imagination to support? Is there some way you can figure out how to do it anyway?
20.  Vacation – real vacation, the kind where you do things purely for enjoyment – often helps get out of a rut: For me, the pattern of good vacation is giving myself 2-3 days of doing almost nothing. And then going to explore the world. Do not, do not, do not, go on a half vacation, where you still answer your email, do bits and pieces of work, _etc_. It's fine to plot and plan and to work on some **other** project – I accidentally wrote the first draft of my book "Reinventing Discovery" in an enormous flurry of work while on (a very relaxing!) vacation in Hawaii. But don't do _anything_ that you think of as routine work.
21.  When you're getting started it's easy to be paralyzed. There's so much you don't know! In fact, you barely need to know anything. Find something which matters, and where you can make a contribution, and work on it. This is a major benefit of working with terrific researchers. They will help you find things that matter, and where you can contribute. PhD students often think their advisors will help them with **how** to solve a problem, but the much more important thing they do is help them find problems which: (a) are tractable; (b) of some importance; (c) will help them develop; and (d) help them know what to ignore.
22.  Courage is a key ingredient[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/qtr/index.html#fn6). And it must be taken: insofar as you only follow paths tacitly approved by some pre-existing community, your work will be of a kind that could have happened anyway. To go beyond this, you must construct your own belief. I struggle greatly with this.
23.  That said, insofar as you can: socialize as much of your understanding and creative work as you can. Talking to supportive others – even if they don't understand – is immensely helpful. I don't think I've ever run a class that didn't give back tenfold; despite that, I often hesitate ("it's too much work!")
24.  The perils of process advice: I've given tonnes of process advice here. It can be paralyzing. In the end, you've just got to get on with it. Keep reading lots of advice, from many different sources, act on anything that seems immediately helpful, reflect on the advice, and then try to forget it much of the time!
