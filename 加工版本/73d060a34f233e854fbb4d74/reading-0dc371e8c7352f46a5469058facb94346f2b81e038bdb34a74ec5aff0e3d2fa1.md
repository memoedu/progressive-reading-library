# I. 序曲

## 阅读前置卡

- 价值判断：高。以游戏中的注意力分配解释信息获取，并延伸到报告、论文阅读和研究会面，提供了具体、可操作的观察与行动方法。
- 信息密度判断：高。多个案例分别支撑精确信息、提前观察、依据信息行动、筛选信息及借助外部资源等不同原则，后续研究场景又给出了具体应用。
- 是否继续阅读：3 全文。值得阅读全文：游戏案例说明各项原则的适用理由，研究场景进一步展示如何调整注意力，仅看结论容易漏掉这些条件与做法。

## 核心洞察

一、岩浆让冲刺落点成为难题：《Hades》的大多数关卡要求一边躲避箭矢、投掷炸弹、近战怪物和尖刺陷阱，一边尽可能快地砍杀；Asphodel 还要求避免踏入岩浆。这里的大多数岛屿比角色的冲刺距离更窄，因此很容易直接冲出陆地，落入致命的岩浆。
[[#^pr-90f72e3a28dc|正文跳转]]

二、盯住角色，余光追踪敌人：《Hades》几乎就是一款弹幕游戏，避免伤害才是关键。百分之八十的时间里，目光都需要紧盯 Zagreus，确保角色精确闪避，远离、脱离或直接穿过敌人的攻击。角色自身的大多数攻击能覆盖大片区域，因此用周边视野追踪敌人，就足以把攻击瞄准大致正确的方向。妻子原先把大部分时间用于盯着敌人；升级选择和敌人攻击模式的建议大多充其量只帮了一点忙，她大概又在岩浆里死了五六次。学会把目光固定在角色身上之后，她只尝试了几次就通过了 Asphodel。
[[#^pr-426758c5f3a2|正文跳转]]

三、先问应该看哪里：把目光和注意力集中到正确的地方，是一种普遍的技能。这里关注的不是如何根据所见作出好决策，也不是如何更好地执行决策，而是更上游的问题：最初应该把目光放在哪里，才能接收到正确的信息？
[[#^pr-4badaf245728|正文跳转]]

四、五款游戏带来五条注意力启示：五款典型电子游戏分别带来了对“目光该放在哪里？”的不同回答，并由此得出五条关于分配注意力的普遍启示。
[[#^pr-85dd93d61952|正文跳转]]

五、相似玩法下的表面差异：Hades 与 Dark Souls 在很多方面相似：都属于动作角色扮演游戏，都以“击杀、死亡、学习、重复”为核心玩法循环，也都被广泛认为是有史以来最好的游戏之一。它们表面上的差异主要在审美层面，例如 Hades 的叙事更轻松，而 Dark Souls 的叙事则更接近不存在。
[[#^pr-6ccda0960fad|正文跳转]]

六、为何一款看自己，一款看敌人？：我玩这两款游戏时有一个鲜明的经历差异：在 Hades 中，我盯着自己的角色；在 Dark Souls 中，我盯着敌人。为什么两款游戏中的注视对象会如此不同？
[[#^pr-a082beb81921|正文跳转]]

七、镜头提示不等于注视理由：Dark Souls 的镜头从角色肩膀上方跟随移动，使玩家不得不盯着敌人；Hades 的等距视角则以玩家角色为中心。镜头轻轻提示目光该聚焦在哪里，这是好的游戏设计，但并没有真正解释为什么那里才是正确的注视位置。
[[#^pr-79208c5d8cec|正文跳转]]

八、看向最需要精确信息的地方：目光应该放在最需要精确信息的地方。Hades 与 Dark Souls 都要求玩家根据信息作出反应，以躲避敌人攻击，但需要反应的信息完全不同：Hades 需要空间精度，Dark Souls 需要时间精度。
[[#^pr-0c8be816e2f0|正文跳转]]

九、Hades 要看清脚下的安全空间：Hades 用大红圈标出炸弹三秒后将落下的位置。躲避时不需要确切知道炸弹何时被投出或由谁投出，提前一秒离开红圈完全没问题；但必须看清准确落点，才能正确冲刺出爆炸区域。当几十枚炸弹和弹射物在屏幕上飞来飞去时，可能只剩一小片安全地面，向任何方向偏差一英寸都意味着灾难。因此，应把视线集中在自己的角色及周围地面上，以对来袭攻击获得最高程度的空间精度。
[[#^pr-f928a02a9809|正文跳转]]

十、需要精确时机，就盯住敌人：在 Dark Souls 的这个例子中，boss 先蓄力，再使出左扫、右扫、停顿、突刺的三连击。特大剑攻击范围巨大，无论如何都来不及冲出范围；只要确切掌握攻击时机，站位就没那么重要，避伤要靠在三个正确的 0.2 秒窗口内翻滚，利用无敌帧。这个 boss 有五种攻击模式，蓄力动画中膝盖移动的方向决定它是否使用这套连招，因此最好盯着敌人，才能在恰好正确的时刻反应。
[[#^pr-116b3b2241ef|正文跳转]]

十一、把中央视觉用在刀刃上：人的高精度中央视觉有限，应把它用在最需要的地方，不要耗费在周边视觉同样能够胜任的地方。
[[#^pr-a971920bfeea|正文跳转]]

十二、Osu! 的节奏与难度变化：Osu! 的圆圈随歌曲节拍出现在屏幕上；要获得最多分数，需要准确地按正确节奏点击它们。更难的谱面中，圆圈更小、间距更远、数量更多：一星谱面可能让你沿平缓弧线每隔一秒点击一次，而同一首歌的五星谱面则会迫使光标每秒在屏幕上来回飞行八次。
[[#^pr-4d44fc9d1bfe|正文跳转]]

十三、熟练时，目光领先于动作：我逐渐掌握节奏游戏中某首曲目的一个关键指标，是目光看得更靠前。初学钢琴曲时，我只盯着紧接着的下一个音符并尝试弹出；越来越熟练后，就能提前看两三个音符，甚至为这一页中段即将出现的难点做好准备，手指远远落在思考进度后面。
[[#^pr-21bce2f01860|正文跳转]]

十四、用打字观察提前阅读：去 TypeRacer 玩几场比赛，留意相对于正在输入的内容，自己能够提前读到多远。我预测，随着练习增多，阅读进度会越来越领先于手指，打字也会因此更加流畅。看着自己提前两秒为身体排队安排指令，是一种近似解离的体验。
[[#^pr-1a8d0ad071ba|正文跳转]]

十五、侦察不能止于获取信息：我过去和现在都是很弱的 Starcraft 玩家，每场都先发展经济，再生产军事单位，达到人口上限后进攻敌方基地。听说“侦察是好事”后，我开始每场派工人侦察，却无论看到什么都继续执行同一套建造顺序。即使发现特别危险的敌军袭来，也只是更加急迫地执行原计划，玩法没有实质变化；这样的侦察浪费了宝贵资源和心力。
[[#^pr-8b722fb62e2e|正文跳转]]

十六、依据信息行动，才能学会收集：把目光放在正确的地方，是为了获取正确的信息，而获取信息的意义在于改善决策。反过来，改善信息获取的最好办法就是依据信息采取行动；否则，不仅无法从收集信息中获益，也学不会如何收集得更好。如果重新学习 Starcraft 的侦察，我会先建立一张流程图，列明自己会根据收到的信息改变哪些选择。
[[#^pr-467d92b156fd|正文跳转]]

十七、庞大信息与系统令人应接不暇：Dota 令人应接不暇，是因为它呈现了数量庞大的信息与系统：数百种英雄和技能、数百种物品及物品交互，以及大量反直觉却引人入胜的机制。这些机制想必都经过了“这不是 bug，这是 feature”的过程。
[[#^pr-8e3f66827cf4|正文跳转]]

十八、大量可用信息仍可能无关紧要：玩 Dota 时，需要查看敌我小兵血条来补刀和反补，追踪金钱以购买物品，留意小地图以防偷袭，观察双方血量、法力和敌方装备来寻找应对机会。然而，这些信息可能全都无关紧要：屏幕上唯一重要的信息，也许只是显示游戏已开始1分50秒的计时器。
[[#^pr-f085542e59bf|正文跳转]]

十九、堆野利用的是区域为空：野怪在按住 ALT 即可显示的黄色矩形区域内生成；每过一分钟，只要刷新区域为空就会刷新，原有野怪不必死亡，只需离开区域。在整分钟前约7秒攻击野怪，让它们追赶并离开区域，就能生成另一份野怪，这种“堆野”是辅助的一项关键工作。优秀的辅助可以在开局不久堆出两份、三份甚至四份野怪，即使全队尚无人能直接打过其中单独一只；十五分钟后，升级后的队友可以回来击杀整堆，获得大量金钱与经验。
[[#^pr-c0fabe3f8f8b|正文跳转]]

二十、刷新条件也让堆野容易被干扰：刷新机制只检查黄色区域是否为空，不管里面是什么，因此敌人可以轻易干扰堆野。对手只需在1:00时走进对应区域并站上一秒，就能破坏整个堆野计划；还可能在开局购买一件隐形物品，赶在你抵达之前将其放进区域。
[[#^pr-fbfc8f33d2ca|正文跳转]]

二十一、辅助离线可能打开进攻窗口：有些辅助每分钟都要堆野或拉野，离开时会使所辅助的英雄容易陷入一打二。敌方辅助离线，可能正是采取进攻并拿下前期击杀的最佳机会，因此需要频繁查看计时器。有些前期策略的关键，是恰好在1:50正确发动全面进攻，而不是例如1:30。
[[#^pr-97f0c535731e|正文跳转]]

二十二、积极筛选不显眼的关键信息：不妨把世界视为一心让你眼花缭乱的家伙，而自己的工作就是拨开眼前的亮片：积极筛选，找出与决策相关的信息，这些信息可能一点也不显眼。
[[#^pr-5d1aa9a285a8|正文跳转]]

二十三、探索游戏不等于掌握深层奥秘：在《Kerbal Space Program》中，完成教程可以学会基础，探索系统本身也很有趣；但除非你真是火箭科学家，否则会错过许多能通过游戏学到的深层奥秘。你不可能独自想出最优重力转弯轨迹；若不稍作网络搜索或进行大量试错，空气制动很可能意外变成撞地制动。不了解质心、升力中心与推力中心的正确关系，建造太空飞机会如同噩梦；你也极不可能自行弄清火箭赋予的是恒定量的动量而非恒定量的能量，以及如何通过在近拱点加速利用这一事实。更不要试图仅凭目测，找出接下来十个游戏年中的完美转移窗口，像旅行者二号那样完成连续四次引力助推。
[[#^pr-c045c7f7bc9e|正文跳转]]

二十四、正确的观察位置可能在游戏之外：有一类游戏，不在第二块显示器上打开 wiki 或电子表格，即使并非完全无法游玩，也会玩得很困难。这可能源于糟糕的设计，但同样常见的是，游戏本身就有充分理由让人这样玩；必要时仍拒绝看向游戏之外，恰恰说明思维僵化。查阅攻略或 wiki、用电子表格计算最优装备组合、用在线工具寻找行星间最佳转移窗口，都可能比游戏窗口本身更值得关注；有些游戏本来就该在多块显示器上玩。
[[#^pr-eda27da89f89|正文跳转]]

25、用五个问题检查注意力方向：在学术数学的报告、论文和会面中，可以借助五个问题判断目光最好放在哪里：应该关注自己，还是另一个人？应该向未来看多远？怎样根据收到的信息采取行动？扑面而来的信息中，哪些与决策相关？要在这项活动中进步，最好的办法会不会在活动本身之外？
[[#^pr-ef6a94f0467a|正文跳转]]

26、报告时把目光移向未来和听众：作报告时，过度在意自己就像一直盯着自己；告诉自己不要如此，几乎与努力不去想北极熊一样无用，消极地排除某种念头很少有帮助。应把目光移向未来和他人：排练报告，预判最难解释的部分；真正看着听众，辨认谁在投入、谁在走神。挑选一两位表情生动的听众仔细观察，并根据这些信息采取行动——他们紧皱的眉头会告诉你是否讲得太快。
[[#^pr-e8a3f0e8f48d|正文跳转]]

27、听报告要筛选面向自己的部分：报告的信息通常多到任何一位听众都无法全部消化。有时这是报告人的过错，但同样常见的是有意设计的信息过载，其功能类似于价格细分：不同抽象层次的信息，让背景差异很大的听众都能有所收获。本科生也许只能理解开头一张幻灯片，研究生也许只能理解前十分钟，而好奇心旺盛的教师可能是唯一理解最后那些晦涩、随口带过的类域论联系的人。因此，要积极筛选，专注于报告中面向自己的部分。
[[#^pr-6c9f61d628d8|正文跳转]]

28、本科生还期待听懂全部内容：Noga Alon 开玩笑说，听报告对谁都不容易，因为没有人能理解全部内容；但对本科生尤其困难，因为他们还指望自己能做到。
[[#^pr-6c9f61d628d8|正文跳转]]

29、前十分钟的经典背景更有趣：报告本身的主题很少能像前十分钟顺带提到的背景材料那样有趣。如果还不了解那些反复出现的核心定理、例子和最简单的证明技巧，这些经典内容才是真正的瑰宝。有时候，旁听某个领域的若干场报告，每场只听前十分钟，就能学会一整个新的子领域；可以带些不引人注目的东西，让自己在其余五十分钟里有事可做。
[[#^pr-75c66892fb26|正文跳转]]

30、未解问题可能是报告的关键：从不据以采取行动的信息，就是无用的信息。报告中最重要的信息可能是一个未解决的问题，因为它无疑是最容易据以采取行动的信息。一场介绍某个重要旧定理变体的报告中，导师看似十分投入，实际并没有继续听报告，而是把这一小时的大部分时间用于寻找前五分钟介绍的那个旧定理的更好证明。
[[#^pr-68779cff2407|正文跳转]]

31、听到有趣问题就立即尝试解决：时不时专为寻找新问题而去听报告，一听到有趣的问题就停止听讲，马上尝试解决它，是这里实践过的听报告方式；就这段个人经历而言，这样做成功过几次。
[[#^pr-0443dbee5ec6|正文跳转]]

32、证明宜分层组织，而非线性阅读：数学证明很少适合线性撰写或阅读。理想的组织方式是一组细节逐渐增加的提纲：五个词的标题、一段长的摘要、两页引言、四页技术提纲，最后才是完整的20页证明。这些层次让读者选择适合自身需要的理解程度。
[[#^pr-620ebc9b290d|正文跳转]]

33、先辨方向，再选择深入的细节：不知道证明要走向何处，就很难跟上它。不先读证明提纲，就无法分辨十个引理中哪些是常规内容、哪些是关键创新；不先从高层次过一遍计算，就无从知道哪些变量值得追踪、哪些只是用过即弃的误差项。盲目逐行阅读容易陷入先前论文的黑箱、开放问题，以及解释草略且可能有笔误的计算；这些细节或许值得探索，但最好先全部标出，再选择深入哪一个。
[[#^pr-b83735dd0cb5|正文跳转]]

34、先看全貌与陈述，再核查计算：阅读论文时，只要可能就应向前看：先从高层次浏览，理解全貌；再读所有定理和引理的陈述，看清它们如何衔接；最后决定深入哪些细节。只有已经知道某项困难计算会带来什么收益时，才去核查它。
[[#^pr-5bce8107f265|正文跳转]]

35、独自研究与合作基本可互换：研究中的绝大多数时间，是在独自撞墙或与别人一起撞墙；这两种活动基本上可以自由互换，以适应个人的内向程度。就我自己的体验而言，比起独自工作，我通常更喜欢与别人见面，一起做研究。
[[#^pr-899dbe0bf9c0|正文跳转]]

36、合作中的陷阱是只会附和：与别人合作时，尤其在年轻、缺乏自信时，很容易不知不觉成为Richard Hamming所称的“声音吸收器”。Hamming在《你与你的Research》中把有效交流比作达到“临界质量”：他觉得交谈是好事，但头脑风暴很少值得，必须挑选有能力的交谈对象；应摆脱那些人虽好却只会说“哦，对”的对象，寻找能联想到相关事物、提出其他思考方向并反过来激发自己的人。
[[#^pr-1f1076de7bcc|正文跳转]]

37、执着于亲自解题可能阻碍贡献：除了缺乏自信，“声音吸收器”的主要错误可能是误解了研究会面中注意力该放在哪里：完全执着于亲自解决问题，因为没有产生有趣的解题想法，就完全不作贡献。这就像过于在意自己，即使自身已没有可获取的有用信息，仍把目光停留在自己身上。
[[#^pr-984068768b6b|正文跳转]]

38、没有原创想法，也能推动解题：亲自解决问题是研究会面的一个很好结果，却绝不是唯一目标；亲自解决问题与让问题得到解决之间有天壤之别。如果合作者确实有能力，他们与你一样有可能想出下一个关键想法，因此真正以让问题得到解决为目标，就意味着要花相当一部分时间支持别人的思考：复述他们的想法，记下并核查他们的计算，为黑板上的内容画好图或构造类比，表达对其洞见的热情。即使没有产生任何原创想法，这些事情仍能帮助问题得到解决。
[[#^pr-1e1fb1de77fc|正文跳转]]

39、提高解题能力比解决当前问题更有价值：让问题得到解决比亲自解决问题更有价值，而总体上提高自己的问题解决能力又比前者更有价值；如果你仍在进行自己的 Gravity Turn，这一点就更是如此。
[[#^pr-8ff4240663b1|正文跳转]]

40、用相当一部分注意力观察导师思维：尤其在与博士生导师或另一位资深导师会面时，要将不到一半但相当可观的一部分注意力用于建立对导师思维过程的模型。弄清并记下他们反复使用的例子和引理、本能进行的计算与简化、被问题卡住时的反应，以及具体如何检索文献、针对什么事情向谁求助、如何决定是否放弃及何时放弃。这些决定没有一个是任意的，它们构成了对所在领域地形的具身模型；观察对方，往往比茫然地盯着问题更值得花时间。
[[#^pr-8ff4240663b1|正文跳转]]

41、先看见决策所需信息，才能恰当行动：研究就像一个更简单的 Dota：人们受到四面八方的信息轰炸，其中大多数甚至未被注意到，同时还必须作出复杂、重尾的决策。在任何这样的游戏中，一项基本技能都是将字面或比喻意义上的目光投向最有价值、与决策最相关的信息。对信息作出反应和执行是之后的事；如果连自己需要做什么都没有看到，就永远不可能恰当地行动。

## 正文

## I. 序曲

我妻子刚开始玩精彩的 action roguelike [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_\(video_game\)) 时，卡在了 Asphodel。Hades 的大多数 levels 都需要一边 dodging arrows、lobbed bombs、melee monsters 和 spike traps，一边尽可能快地 hacking and slashing，但 Asphodel 又增加了一重难度：在 Greek underworld 这片别具魅力的郊区里，你既要应付上述所有情况，还要尽量不 step in lava。Asphodel 的大多数 islands 都比你的 dash 距离更窄，所以很难不直接 dash 出 solid ground，落入滚烫的死亡陷阱。

^pr-90f72e3a28dc

我给妻子提了一些关于 upgrade choices（\*咳咳\* Athena dash \*咳咳\*）和 enemy attack patterns 的建议，但这些建议大多充其量也只帮了一点忙。她大概又在 lava 里 died 了五六次。不过，有一个小窍门立刻产生了肉眼可见的效果。

“盯着你自己看。”

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/hades.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/hades.png)

_留神脚下。_

看着妻子玩，我逐渐意识到她犯了一个根本性的错误：_她的目光放错了地方。_她没有看着自己的 character Zagreus，而是把大部分时间花在盯着 enemies 上，试图对它们的 movements 和 attacks 作出 react。

Hades 几乎就是一款 bullet hell game：avoiding damage 才是关键。百分之八十的时间里，你的目光都需要紧盯 Zagreus 那作为 protagonist 的紧实屁股，确保他能 dodges precisely，远离、脱离或直接穿过 enemy attacks。与此同时，Zagreus 自己的大多数 attacks 都能 hit 大片 areas，因此用 peripheral vision 来 tracking enemies，就足以把你的 attacks aim 到大致正确的 direction。妻子学会把目光固定在 Zagreus 身上之后，只尝试了几次就通过了 Asphodel。

^pr-426758c5f3a2

这篇文章讨论的是一种普遍的 skill：把目光和 attention 集中到正确的地方。本文关注的不是“如何根据你看到的东西 make good decisions？”以及“如何更好地 executing 那些 decisions？”这些常见问题，而是一个更上游的问题：“最初应该把目光放在哪里，才能 receive 正确的 information？”

^pr-4badaf245728

在第 II 部分，我会描述五款 archetypal video games。在我的记忆中，它们各有不同，是因为我从每一款中学到了对“目光该放在哪里？”的不同回答。我从中得出了五条关于 attention-paying 的普遍启示。对 video games 不感兴趣的读者，可以放心跳过第 II 部分。

^pr-85dd93d61952

在第 III 部分，我会把这些启示 apply 到人们在 graduate school 中苦苦应付的三种具体 minigames：research meetings、seminar talks 和 paper-reading。这三种情境都可能有令人应接不暇的 information 需要关注，而其中的关键，就是恰当地集中目光，以 perceive 最有价值的 subset。

## II. 从 Video Games 中得到的启示

### 看我，还是看你？

[Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_\(video_game\)) 和 [Dark Souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Souls) 在很多方面都是相似的 games。两者都属于 action RPGs 这一大类 genre，都具有“kill, die, learn, repeat”这一 core gameplay loop，也都被广泛认为是有史以来最好的 games 之一。它们表面上的 differences 主要在于 aesthetic：例如，Hades 的 storytelling 更 lighthearted，Dark Souls 的则更接近不存在。

^pr-6ccda0960fad

但我玩这两款 games 的经历有一个鲜明的 difference：在 Hades 中，我盯着自己；在 Dark Souls 中，我盯着 enemy。为什么？

^pr-a082beb81921

一个显而易见的回答是：在 Dark Souls 中，camera 从你的肩膀上方跟着你移动，所以你不得不盯着 enemies；而在 Hades 中，isometric camera 则 centered on 你自己的 character。这是好的 game design，因为 camera 本身就会轻轻提示你的目光该 focus 在哪里，但这并没有真正解释为什么那个地方是正确的。

^pr-79208c5d8cec

更有意思的回答是：_你的目光应该放在你最需要 precise information 的地方。_

^pr-0c8be816e2f0

在这两款 games 中，gameplay 都围绕着对 information 作出 reacting 来 avoid enemy attacks 展开，但你究竟需要 react 的是什么，却完全不同。简而言之，你在 Hades 中需要的是 _spatial precision_，在 Dark Souls 中需要的则是 _temporal precision_。

在 Hades 中，一个 enemy 会 winds up，然后 lobs 一枚又大又闪亮的 bomb。game 会用一个大大的 red circle，marks 它在三秒后将要 land 的位置。你不需要确切知道 bomb 是什么时候被 lobbed 的，也不需要知道是谁 lobbed 的——提前一秒离开 red circle 完全没问题。但你确实需要看清它究竟会 land 在哪里，才能正确地 dash 出 blast zone。当几十枚 bombs 和 projectiles 在 screen 上飞来飞去时，可能只剩下一小片 safe ground 可供你 dash 过去，而在任何 direction 上偏差一英寸都意味着灾难。因此，你要把 vision 集中在自己和周围的 ground 上，以便对 incoming attacks 获得最高程度的 _spatial precision_。

^pr-f928a02a9809

在 Dark Souls 中，一个 boss 会 winds up，然后 launches 一个 three-hit combo：left swipe、right swipe、pause、lunge。只要你确切知道它_什么时候_到来，你站在哪里其实就没那么重要——boss 的 ultra greatsword 所 hits 的 area 如此巨大，无论如何你都来不及 dash away。相反，avoid damage 的办法是在正确的三个 0.2-second intervals 内 press 那个 roll button，享受那些美妙的 invincibility frames。不过，真正有趣的部分是什么呢？这个 boss 实际上有五种不同的 attack patterns，而他是不是正在使用这一种，取决于 wind-up animation 中他膝盖 move 的 direction。所以，在 Dark Souls 中，你最好盯着 enemy，才能在恰好正确的时刻 react。

^pr-116b3b2241ef

人的眼睛所拥有的 high-precision central vision 是有限的，所以要把它用在刀刃上。不要把它花在 peripheral vision 就能同样胜任的地方。

^pr-a971920bfeea

### 看现在，还是看未来？

Rhythm games 已经流行很久了，因此你大概玩过其中某个经典：Guitar Hero、Beat Saber、Osu!、piano。我们就以 [Osu!](https://osu.ppy.sh/home) 作为 prototypical example。它的 core gameplay 很简单：circles 随着歌曲的 beat 出现在 screen 上，为了获得最多的 points，你需要 accurately 地、按照正确的 rhythm 来 click 它们。更难的 beatmaps 有更小的 circles，它们之间相距更远，数量也更多；一张 one-star beatmap 可能让你沿着平缓的 arcs，每隔一秒 clicking 一次，而同一首歌的 five-star map 则会迫使你的 cursor 每秒在 screen 上来回飞行八次。

^pr-4d44fc9d1bfe

有一个关键 indicator，表明我正在 mastering 一款 rhythm game 中的某首 piece：我的目光看得更靠前了。第一次 learning 一首 piano piece 时，我起初只会盯着紧接着的下一个 note，并试着 hit 它。但随着我对这首 piece 越来越熟练，我就不再看着马上要 play 的那个 note，而是可以提前看两三个 notes，甚至为这一页中段即将出现的难点做好准备。我的手指远远落在我正在思考的部分后面。

^pr-21bce2f01860

Exercise：去 [https://play.typeracer.com/](https://play.typeracer.com/) 玩几场 races，留意相对于你正在 typing 的内容，你能提前读到多远。我预测，随着 practice 增多，你会读到比手指更靠前、越来越靠前的地方，而你的 typing 也会因此更加 smoother。看着自己提前两秒为自己的身体 queue up commands，是一种 quasi-dissociative 的体验。

^pr-1a8d0ad071ba

### 依据 Information 采取行动（Act on Information）

作为一个很弱的 Starcraft player（而且我至今仍是一个很弱的 Starcraft player），我每次进入 game 时都带着同一个简单计划。每场 game，我都会把自己的 economy 发展到一定 size，然后转而 producing military units。等我的 army 达到 supply cap，我就会让它蜂拥着冲向 enemy base。

后来某个时候，我听说“Scouting 是 Good”，于是每场 match 开始时，我都会浪费宝贵的 resources 和 mental energy，派出 workers 去 scout out 我的 enemy 正在做什么。不幸的是，我对 scouting 的理解也就止于 acquiring information。无论我在 enemy base 看到了什么，我都会继续遵循自己那套千篇一律的 build order。即使在最好的情况下，看到一支特别 dangerous 的 army 向我袭来，我的 react 也不过是 _extra urgently_ 地 executing 那套 build order。这无非让我的额头多暴起几根青筋，而我的 gameplay 并没有任何实质变化。

^pr-8b722fb62e2e

把眼睛放在正确的地方，就是为了 gather 正确的 information，而 information-gathering 的意义在于 improve decision-making。反过来，想要 improve information-gathering，最好的办法就是依据 information 采取行动。如果你不依据 information 采取行动，那么不仅无法从 gathering 它中获益，也学不会如何把它 gather 得更好。如果我回头重新学习 Starcraft 中的 scouting，我会先 building 一个 flowchart，列出我会根据收到的 information 而改变哪些 choices。

^pr-467d92b156fd

### 积极筛选（Filter Aggressively）

有人向我介绍了 [Dota 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dota_2)，2020 年夏天，我在它上面花了大约四百个小时（当然，这仍然让我只是一个彻头彻尾的 beginner，所以这一部分请谨慎看待）。Dota 之所以令人应接不暇，是因为它向你呈现了数量庞大的 information 和 systems——数百种 heroes 和 abilities、数百种 items 和 item interactions，还有大量 counterintuitive 却引人入胜的 mechanics，想必都经过了“这不是 bug，这是 feature”这条 pipeline。

^pr-8e3f66827cf4

玩 Dota 就意味着不断受到 information 的冲击。我盯着 enemy minions 的 health bars，以便恰当地对它们 last hit，否则就赚不到 money。我盯着自己的 minions，以便对它们 deny，不让 opponent 得手。我 track 自己的 gold，以便一旦买得起，就立刻 buy 所需的 items。我留意 minimap，确保没有 enemy team 的人绕过来 gank。我观察我方 team 和 enemy team 的 health 和 mana bars，寻找 weak link 或 heal 的机会。我可以 click on enemy heroes，查看它们的 inventories，弄清谁是 strong、谁是 weak，再相应地 react。然而，这一切可能都是 extraneous information：也许 screen 上唯一重要的 information，是 screen 顶端那个显示 game 已经开始 1 minute 和 50 seconds 的 timer。

^pr-f085542e59bf

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/dota.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/dota.png)

_这个 clock 可能是这块 screen 上最重要的 information。_

要理解为什么在上述所有 information 中，game timer 可能是最 decision-relevant 的，你就得理解 Dota 中一个特别曲折的 game mechanic，也就是 [jungle monster respawn system](https://dota2.fandom.com/wiki/Neutral_creeps#Spawning)。你看，Dota 中的 jungle monsters 各自在一个 rectangular yellow box 内 spawn，按住 ALT 就能让它 visible。这个 game 的 coded 方式是：每过一分钟，只要某个 monster 的 spawn box 是 empty，它就会 respawns。你没看错——monsters 不必 die 就能 respawn，它们只需要离开那个 box。Exploiting 这个 respawn mechanic，为同一个 monster 制造许多 copies，就叫作“stacking”，这是 support players 的一项关键工作：如果你在整分钟到来前约 7 seconds attack jungle monsters，它们就会 chase 你，距离恰好足以让这个 monster 的一个 duplicate copy 得以 spawns。这意味着，在 game 开始后不久，一个优秀的 support player 就可以为队友“stack”出一个 jungle monster 的两份、三份，甚至四份 copies，留待他们以后 kill，_即使整个 team 都没有人 strong 到足以 directly fight 其中单独一个_。十五分钟后，leveled up 的队友便可以回来 kill 整个 stack，获取大量 gold 和 experience。

^pr-c0fabe3f8f8b

无穷无尽的 factors 让 Stacking 变得更加 complicated，但最有意思的大概是这一点：enemy 可以轻易 disrupt 你的 stacks。game code 只会 checks 那个 yellow spawn box 是否为 _empty_，而不管 box 里面是什么。一个有眼力的 opponent 只需要在 1:00 时走进对应的 box，站上一秒，就能破坏你的整个 stacking game-plan。更阴险的是，他可能在 game 开始时就 buy _一个 invisible item_，赶在你抵达该区域之前，把它 drop 到 box 里。

^pr-fbfc8f33d2ca

总之，有些 support players 每分钟都有一项工作要做，不是 stacking，就是一种与之密切相关、叫作“pulling”的事情。他们离开去做这项工作时，所 supporting 的 hero 就容易陷入 two-on-one。这正是 game timer 派上用场的地方：enemy support 离开 lane，可能就是我采取 aggressive 行动并拿下 early kill 的最佳机会。因此，在 screen 上所有花哨的 information 中，我需要频繁 checking game timer。有些 early game strategies 的关键，在于恰好在 1:50 正确地 launching all-out attacks，而不是比如说 1:30。

^pr-97f0c535731e

不妨把这个世界当成一心想让你[眼花缭乱](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YW3MIixEps4)的家伙，而你的工作就是把那些亮片从眼前拨开。要积极 Filter，找出 decision-relevant information，而这些 information 可能一点也不显眼。

^pr-5d1aa9a285a8

### 把目光投向 Game 之外

有一类 video games，如果不在 second monitor 上打开 wiki 或 spreadsheet，就算并非完全没法玩，也会玩得很困难。这可能是因为糟糕的 game design，但同样常见的情况是，game 本来就有充分理由让人这样玩；在必要时仍拒绝看向 game 之外，恰恰说明思维僵化。

想想 [Kerbal Space Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerbal_Space_Program)。你可以通过玩完 tutorials 学会 basics，光是探索 systems 也能获得许多乐趣。但除非你真的是 rocket scientist，否则你会错过许多可以通过这个 game 学到的深层奥秘。你不可能独自想出 optimal gravity turn trajectory。如果你不稍微 Googling 一下，或者进行大量 trial-and-error，你对 [aerobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobraking) 的尝试很可能会沦为意外的 [lithobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobraking)。如果不了解 center of mass、center of lift 和 center of thrust 之间正确的 relationship，building 一个 spaceplane 会让你如同身处噩梦；你也极不可能自己弄清，rocket 是一种 imparts _constant amount 的 momentum_ 而非 constant amount 的 energy 的 machine，或者弄清你可以通过在 periapsis 处 accelerating 来 exploit 这个事实。至于尝试靠目测，在接下来十个 in-game 年里找出 perfect transfer window，像 [Voyager II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_assist) 那样完成 four sequential gravity assists——那可千万别。

^pr-c045c7f7bc9e

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/ksp.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/ksp.png)

_有些 games 本来就该在 multiple monitors 上玩。_

正确的观察位置，完全可能在 game 之外。无论是查阅 guides 或 wikis，把 numbers 填入 spreadsheet 来 calculate optimal item builds，还是使用 online tools 寻找 planets 之间最佳的 transfer windows，这些地方都可能比 game window 本身更值得你投去目光。

^pr-eda27da89f89

## III. Research 的应用（Applications to Research）

在这篇文章的最后一部分，我把上述 principles 应用于 academic mathematics 中的三个 core minigames：talks、papers 和 meetings。对于这些 minigames 中的每一个，我们都会借助以下问题，试着弄清楚眼睛最好该看向哪里。

1.  我应该 focus on 自己，还是另一个人？ 
2.  我应该向未来看多远？
3.  我怎样才能对收到的 information 进行 act on？
4.  在所有扑面而来的 information 中，什么是 decision-relevant 的？
5.  要在这个 game 中 get better，最好的办法会不会在 game 本身之外？

^pr-ef6a94f0467a

### Talks（报告）

在 giving a talk 时，self-consciousness 就像一直盯着自己。告诉自己不要 self-conscious，差不多就像努力不去想那只 [polar bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironic_process_theory) 一样没用；negative effort 很少能帮上忙。把目光移向别处：移向未来，也移向其他人。对你的 presentation 进行 Rehearse，并 anticipate 那些最难 explain 的部分。对你的 audience 给予 Pay attention，而且要真正看着他们。看看自己能不能辨认出谁是 engaged 的，谁在走神。找出一两位面部表情生动的 audience members，仔细观察他们，并对这些 information 进行 act on——他们紧皱的眉头会告诉你，你是不是讲得太快了。

^pr-e8a3f0e8f48d

在 listening to a talk 时，要意识到，通常 information 会多到任何一位 audience member 都无法全部 digest。有时候这是 speaker 的过错，但同样常见的是，这种 information overload 是有意设计的，其 functions 类似于 [price segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_discrimination)。Noga Alon 最近对我开玩笑说：“去听 talk 对谁来说都不容易，因为没有人 understands 全部内容；但对 undergraduates 来说尤其困难，因为他们还指望自己能做到。”同一场 talk 中会呈现各种 levels of abstraction 的 Information，让 backgrounds 差异很大的 audience members 都能有所收获。一名 undergraduate student 也许只能 understand 开头的 slide，一名 graduate student 也许只能 understand 前十分钟，而那位好奇心旺盛的 faculty member，则会是唯一一个 understands 最后那些晦涩、随口带过的、关于与 class field theory 的 connections 的话的人。要针对 talk 中专门面向你的那些部分进行 Filter aggressively。

^pr-6c9f61d628d8

记住，talk 本身的 topic 很少能像前十分钟里顺带提到的 background material 那样有趣。如果你还不知道这些 classics——那些一再提到的 core theorems 和 examples，以及反复出现的最简单的 proof techniques——它们才是真正的瑰宝。有时候，只要去旁听某个 area 的若干场 talks，并且每场只听前十分钟，你就能学会一整个新的 subfield。带点不引人注目的东西，让自己在其余五十分钟里有事可做。

^pr-75c66892fb26

还要记住：你从不 act on 的 information，就是 useless information。几年前，我在一堂 computer science lecture 上打瞌睡，讲的是某个 Important Old Theorem 的一种 variation。就在我昏昏欲睡时，我惊讶地发现，坐在附近的 adviser 却对这场 talk 相当 engaged。我很好奇是什么吸引了他的注意，在走回 math department 的路上，他为我解了惑：他并没有在听 talk，而是把这一小时的大部分时间都用来寻找前五分钟介绍的那个 Important Old Theorem 的一个 better proof。从这件事中，我明白了，talk 中最重要的 information 可能是一个 unsolved problem，因为它无疑是最容易 act on 的 information。

^pr-68779cff2407

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/jordan_meme.jpg?w=800)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/jordan_meme.jpg)

_一场 seminar talk 之后，我的 PhD adviser。_

与 adviser 的这番交谈对我影响很大，所以我时不时会去听一场 talk，唯一目的就是听到新的 problems，以此实践这种视角。一听到有趣的 problem，我就不再听下去，马上试着 solve 它。就个人经历而言，这样做成功过几次。

^pr-0443dbee5ec6

### Papers（论文）

本节的大部分内容已经在 Of Math and Memory（[第一部分](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2019/11/13/of-math-and-memory-part-1/)、[第二部分](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2020/01/27/of-math-and-memory-part-2/)、[第三部分](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2020/05/16/of-math-and-memory-part-3-final/)）中讲过，不过我会在这里重述相关部分。Mathematical proofs 很少是要让人 linearly 地 written 或 read 的。相反，它们理想的 arranged 方式，是一组 detail 逐渐增加的 outlines：一个五个词的 title、一段长的 abstract、两页 introduction、四页 technical outline，最后才是完整的 20-page proof。每个 outline 都比下一个更 higher-resolution，让 readers 有机会挑选适合自己需要的 level of understanding。

^pr-620ebc9b290d

这种 organization 是为了解决一个 basic difficulty：不知道 proof 要走向何处，就很难 follow 它。不先 reading 那个 proof outline，你就无法分辨十个 lemmas 中哪些是 boilerplate，哪些是 critical innovations。不先在 high level 上 running through 一遍 calculation，你就无从知道 alpha、n、epsilon、x 和 y 中哪些值得 track，哪些只是 throwaway error terms。在不知道自己要去哪里的情况下，line-by-line 地 Reading 一篇 paper，很容易迷失在细枝末节里，被拖进无穷无尽的兔子洞——来自先前 papers、需要 unpack 的 black boxes，需要反复琢磨的 open problems，以及解释草略、可能含有 typos 的 computations——这些兔子洞或许值得探索，但在挑一个钻进去之前，最好先把它们全部标出来。

^pr-b83735dd0cb5

在 reading 一篇 paper 时，只要有可能，就把目光朝向未来，就像玩 TypeRacer 时提前读后面的几个词一样。先在 high level 上 Scan 这篇 paper，以 understand 它的 big picture，然后 read 所有 theorem 和 lemma statements，看看它们如何 fit together，最后才决定钻进哪些细枝末节。只有已经知道某个困难的 computation 会带来什么 payoff 时，才去 check 它。

^pr-5bce8107f265

### Meetings（会面）

在 research 中，你绝大多数时间都以两种方式之一度过：独自撞墙，或与别人一起撞墙。这两种活动基本上可以自由互换，以适应你的 introversion 程度，而我发现，比起独自工作，我通常更喜欢与别人见面，一起做 research。

^pr-899dbe0bf9c0

与别人一起工作的陷阱之一，尤其是在你年轻、缺乏自信时，就是你很容易不知不觉地滑入 Richard Hamming 所称的“sound absorber”角色：

> 就我自己而言，我觉得与别人交谈是件好事；但一场 brainstorming 很少值得。我确实会专门去找人交谈，说：“你看，我觉得这里一定有什么东西。这是我觉得自己看到的……”然后开始来回交流。但你得挑选有能力的人。换个 analogy，你知道那个叫作 \`critical mass.’ If you have enough stuff you have critical mass. There is also the idea I used to call \`sound absorbers’ 的概念。当你身边有太多 sound absorbers 时，你提出一个 idea，他们只是说：“对，对，对。”你想要的是让那种 critical mass 运作起来；“对，这让我想起了某某东西”，或者，“你有没有想过那个或这个？”与别人交谈时，你要摆脱那些人虽好、却只会说“哦，对”的 sound absorbers，找到那些也会反过来激发你的人。 
> 
> ~ Richard Hamming，《你与你的 Research》

^pr-1f1076de7bcc

除了缺乏自信之外，我怀疑“sound absorbers”犯的主要错误，是他们对 research meeting 中自己的目光应该放在哪里有错误的认识。我觉得，一个“sound absorber”完全执着于_亲自 solving 这个 problem_。因为没有 generated 用于 solving 这个 problem 的有趣 ideas，他们就完全不作任何 contribute。同样，这个错误就像过于在意自己，在自己身上没有任何有用 information 可获取时，仍把目光停留在自己身上。

^pr-984068768b6b

亲自 solving 这个 problem 当然是 research meeting 的一个很好的 outcome，但这绝不是唯一的 goal。首先，_亲自 solving 这个 problem_与_让这个 problem 得到 solved_之间有天壤之别。如果你的 collaborators 确实有能力，他们和你一样有可能想出下一个关键 idea，因此，真正为_让这个 problem 得到 solved_而 optimizing，就意味着要花相当一部分时间 supporting 别人的 thought processes。把他们的想法复述给他们，记下并 check 他们的 calculations，为他们在黑板上做的事情画一幅好图或构造一个 analogy，表达你对他们 insights 的热情。你完全可以在没有 generating 任何一个 original thought 的情况下做到这一切，并且仍然有助于_让这个 problem 得到 solved。_

^pr-1e1fb1de77fc

_让这个 problem 得到 solved_比_亲自 solving 这个 problem_更有价值，但比这更有价值的是_在总体上 improving 自己的 problem-solving 能力_；如果你仍在进行自己的 [Gravity Turn](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2021/08/16/gravity-turn/)，这一点就更是如此。尤其是在与你的 PhD adviser 或另一位资深 mentor 会面时，要将不到一半但相当可观的一部分 attention 放在 modelling 你的 mentor 的 thought processes 上。弄清并记下：他们一次又一次从自己的工具箱中拿出哪些 examples 和 lemmas，会本能地做哪些 calculations 和 simplifications，以及在被一个 problem 卡住时如何 react。了解他们 perform literature searches 的具体方式，关于什么事情会向谁求助，以及他们如何决定是否放弃、何时放弃。这些 decisions 没有一个是 arbitrary 的；它们构成了一个关于你所在 field 的地形的 embodied model。观察对方，往往比茫然地盯着 problem 更值得你花时间。

^pr-8ff4240663b1

匆匆作个 conclusion，research 就像一个更简单的 Dota：我们受到来自四面八方的 information 轰炸，其中大多数我们甚至没有注意到，同时还得作出 complicated、heavy-tailed 的 decisions。在任何这样的 game 中，一项 fundamental skill 就是将你的目光——无论字面意义上还是比喻意义上——投向最有价值、最 decision-relevant 的 information。对这些 information 的 Reacting 和 executing 是之后的事，但如果你连自己需要做什么都没有看到，就永远不可能恰当地行动。

^pr-69d1bfd6c79a

## 原文

## I. Prelude

When my wife first started playing the wonderful action roguelike [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_\(video_game\)), she got stuck in Asphodel. Most Hades levels involve dodging arrows, lobbed bombs, melee monsters, and spike traps all whilst hacking and slashing as quickly as possible, but Asphodel adds an extra twist: in this particular charming suburb of the Greek underworld, you need to handle all of the above whilst also trying not to step in lava. Most of the islands in Asphodel are narrower than your dash is far, so it’s hard not to dash straight off solid ground into piping-hot doom.

I gave my wife some pointers about upgrade choices (\*cough\* Athena dash \*cough\*) and enemy attack patterns, but most of my advice was marginally helpful at best. She probably died in lava another half-dozen times. One quick trick, however, had an instant and visible effect.

“Stare at yourself.”

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/hades.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/hades.png)

_Watch your step._

By watching my wife play, I came to realize that she was making one fundamental mistake: _her eyes were in the wrong place._ Instead of watching her own character Zagreus, she spent most of her time staring at the enemies and trying to react to their movements and attacks.

Hades is almost a bullet hell game: avoiding damage is the name of the game. Eighty percent of the time your eyes need to be honed on Zagreus’s toned protagonist butt to make sure he dodges precisely away from, out of, or straight through enemy attacks. In the meantime, most of Zagreus’s own attacks hit large areas, so tracking enemies with peripheral vision is enough to aim your attacks in the right general direction. Once my wife learned to fix her eyes on Zagreus, she made it through Asphodel in only a few attempts.

This is a post about the general skill of focusing your eyes, and your attention, to the right place. Instead of the standard questions “How do you make good decisions based on what you see?” and “How do you get better at executing those decisions?”, this post focuses on a question further upstream: “Where should your eyes be placed to receive the right information in the first place?”

In Part II, I describe five archetypal video games, which are distinguished in my memory by the different answers to “Where do your eyes go?” I learned from each of them. I derive five general lessons about attention-paying. Part II can be safely skipped by those allergic to video games.

In Part III, I apply these lessons to three specific minigames that folks struggle with in graduate school: research meetings, seminar talks, and paper-reading. In all three cases, there can be an overwhelming amount of information to attend to, and the name of the game is to focus your eyes properly to perceive the most valuable subset.

## II. Lessons from Video Games

### Me or You?

[Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_\(video_game\)) and [Dark Souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Souls) are similar games in many respects. Both live in the same general genre of action RPGs, both share the core gameplay loop “kill, die, learn, repeat,” and both are widely acknowledged to be among the best games of all time. Their visible differences are mostly aesthetic: for example, Hades’ storytelling is more lighthearted, Dark Souls’ more nonexistent.

But there is one striking difference between my experiences of these two games: in Hades I stared at myself, and in Dark Souls I stared at the enemy. Why?

One answer is obvious: in Dark Souls, the camera follows you around over your shoulder, so you’re forced to stare at the enemies, while in Hades the isometric camera is centered on your own character. This is good game design because the camera itself gently suggests the right place for your eyes to focus, but it doesn’t really explain why that place is right.

The more interesting answer is that _your eyes go where you need the most precise information._

In both games, gameplay centers around reacting to information to avoid enemy attacks, but what precisely you need to react to is completely different. Briefly, you need _spatial precision_ in Hades, and _temporal precision_ in Dark Souls. 

In Hades, an enemy winds up and lobs a big sparkly bomb. The game marks where it’ll land three seconds later as a big red circle. You don’t need to know precisely when the bomb was lobbed and by whom – getting out of the red circle one second early is fine. But you do need to see precisely where it’ll land so you can dash out of the blast zone correctly. When there’s dozens of bombs and projectiles flying across the screen, there might be only a tiny patch of safe ground for you to dash to, and being off by an inch in any direction spells disaster. So you center your vision on yourself and the ground around you, to get the highest level of _spatial precision_ about incoming attacks.

In Dark Souls, a boss winds up and launches a three-hit combo: left swipe, right swipe, pause, lunge. As long as you know precisely _when_ it’s coming, where you’re standing doesn’t matter all that much – the boss’s ultra greatsword hits such a huge area that you won’t be able to dash away in time regardless. Instead, the way to avoid damage is to press the roll button in the right three 0.2-second intervals and enjoy those sweet invincibility frames. The really fun part, though? The boss actually has five different attack patterns, and whether he’s doing this particular one depends on the direction his knees move in the wind-up animation. So you better be staring at the enemy in Dark Souls to react at precisely the right time.

Human eyes have a limited amount of high-precision central vision, so make it count. Don’t spend it where peripheral vision would do just as well.

### Present or Future?

Rhythm games have been popular for a long time, so you’ve probably played one of the greats: Guitar Hero, Beat Saber, Osu!, piano. Let’s take [Osu!](https://osu.ppy.sh/home) as a prototypical example. The core gameplay is simple: circles appear on the screen to the beat of a song, and to earn the most points, you click them accurately and at the right rhythm. Harder beatmaps have smaller circles that are further apart and more numerous; a one-star beatmap might have you clicking every other second along gentle arcs, while a five-star map for the same song forces your cursor to fly back and forth across the screen eight times a second.

There’s one key indicator that I’m mastering a piece in a rhythm game: my eyes are looking farther ahead. When learning a piano piece for the first time, I start off just staring at and trying to hit the immediate next note. But as I get better at the piece, instead of looking at the very next note I have to play, I can look two or three notes ahead, or even prepare for an upcoming difficulty halfway down the page. My fingers lag way behind the part I’m thinking about.

Exercise: head on over to [https://play.typeracer.com/](https://play.typeracer.com/) and play a few races, paying attention to how far ahead you can read compared to what you’re currently typing. I predict that with more practice, you’ll read further and further ahead of your fingers, and your typing will be smoother for it. It’s a quasi-dissociative experience to watch yourself queue up commands for your own body two seconds in advance.

### Act on Information

As a weak Starcraft player (and I remain a weak Starcraft player), I went into every game with the same simple plan. Every game, I’d build up my economy to a certain size, and then switch over to producing military units. When my army hit the supply cap, I’d send it flooding towards the enemy base.

At some point, I heard that “Scouting is Good,” so at the beginning of each match I’d waste precious resources and mental energy sending workers to scout out what my enemy was doing. Unfortunately, acquiring information was as far as my understanding of scouting extended. Regardless of what I saw at the enemy base, I’d continue following my one cookie-cutter build order. At very best, if I saw a particularly dangerous army coming my way, I’d react by executing that build order _extra urgently_. This amounted to the veins in my forehead popping a bit more while nothing tangible changed about my gameplay.

To place your eyes in the right place is to gather the right information, and the point of information-gathering is to improve decision-making. Conversely, the best way to improve at information-gathering is to act on information. If you don’t act on information, not only do you not benefit from gathering it, you do not learn to gather it better. If I went back to learn scouting in Starcraft again, I’d start by building a flowchart of what choices I’d change depending on the information I received.

### Filter Aggressively

I was introduced to [Dota 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dota_2) and spent about four hundred hours on it in the summer of 2020 (of course, this makes me an absolute beginner, so take this part with a healthy pinch of salt). Dota is overwhelming because of the colossal amount of information and systems presented to you – hundreds of heroes and abilities, hundreds of items and item interactions, and multitudes of counterintuitive but fascinating mechanics that must have gone through the “it’s not a bug, it’s a feature” pipeline.

To play Dota is to be constantly buffeted by information. I watch the health bars of the enemy minions to last hit them properly, or I won’t make money. I watch my own minions to deny them from my opponent. I track my gold to buy the items I need as soon as I can afford them. I pay attention to the minimap to make sure nobody from the enemy team is coming around to gank. I watch my team’s health and mana bars, and the enemy team’s, to look for a weak link or opportunity to heal. I can click on the enemy heroes to look at their inventories, figure out who is strong and who is weak, and react accordingly. And this might all be extraneous information: maybe the only important information on the screen is the timer at the top of the screen which says the game started 1 minute and 50 seconds ago.

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/dota.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/dota.png)

_The clock might be the most important information on this screen._

To understand why the game timer might be the most decision-relevant information out of all of the above, you have to understand a particularly twisted game mechanic in Dota, the [jungle monster respawn system](https://dota2.fandom.com/wiki/Neutral_creeps#Spawning). You see, the jungle monsters in Dota each spawn in a rectangular yellow box that you can make visible by holding ALT. The game is coded such that, every minute, a monster respawns if its spawn box is empty. You read that right – the monsters don’t have to die to respawn, they just have to leave the box. Exploiting this respawn mechanic to make many copies of the same monster is called “stacking,” and is a key job for support players: if you attack the jungle monsters about 7 seconds before the minute, they’ll chase you just far enough that a duplicate copy of the monster spawns. This means that near the beginning of the game, a good support player can “stack” two, three, or even four copies of a jungle monster for his teammates to kill later, _even if nobody on the entire team is strong enough to fight a single one directly_. Fifteen minutes later, leveled up teammates can come back and kill the entire stack for massive amounts of gold and experience.

Stacking is further complicated by an endless litany of factors, but the most interesting is probably this: the enemy can easily disrupt your stacks. The game code only checks if the yellow spawn box is _empty_, not what’s inside the box. A discerning opponent can foil your whole stacking game-plan just by walking into the appropriate box at the 1:00 mark and standing there for a second. More deviously yet, he might buy _an invisible item_ at the beginning of the game to drop in the box before you even reach the area.

Anyhow, some support players have a job to do every minute, which is either stacking or a closely related thing called “pulling.” When they’re gone doing this job, this leaves the hero they’re supporting vulnerable to a two-on-one. This is where the game timer comes in: the enemy support leaving the lane might be my best opportunity to be aggressive and land an early kill. And so, out of all the fancy information on the screen, I need to be checking the game timer frequently. Some early game strategies hinge on correctly launching all-out attacks at 1:50, and not, say, 1:30.

Treat the world as if it’s out to [razzle dazzle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YW3MIixEps4) you, and your job is to get the sequins out of your eyes. Filter aggressively for the decision-relevant information, which may not be obvious at all. 

### Looking Outside the Game

There is a certain class of video games that are difficult, if not impossible, to play without a wiki or spreadsheet open on a second monitor. This can be due to poor game design, but just as often it’s the way the game is meant to be played for good reason, and it’s the mark of an inflexible mind to refuse to look outside the game when this is necessary.

Consider [Kerbal Space Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerbal_Space_Program). You can learn the basics by playing through the tutorials, and can have plenty of fun just exploring the systems. But unless you’re a literal rocket scientist you’ll miss many of the deep secrets to be learned through this game. There’s no way you’ll come up with the optimal gravity turn trajectory yourself. If you don’t do a little Googling or a lot of trial-and-error, your attempts at [aerobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobraking) will probably devolve into unintentional [lithobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobraking). You’ll have a nightmarish time building a spaceplane without knowing the correct relationship between center of mass, center of lift, and center of thrust, and it’s highly unlikely you’ll figure out that a rocket is a machine that imparts _a constant amount of momentum_ instead of a constant amount of energy, or that you can exploit this fact by accelerating at periapsis. And god forbid you try to eyeball the perfect transfer window in the next in-game decade to make four sequential gravity assists like [Voyager II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_assist).

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/ksp.png?w=1024)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/ksp.png)

_Some games are meant to be played on multiple monitors._

The right place to look can be outside the game entirely. Whether it’s looking up guides or wikis, plugging in numbers to a spreadsheet to calculate optimal item builds, or using online tools to find the best transfer windows between planets, these can be the right place to put your eyes instead of on the game window itself.

## III. Applications to Research

In this last part of the post, I apply the principles above to three core minigames in academic mathematics: talks, papers, and meetings. For each of these minigames, we’ll try to figure out the best places for our eyes to go, informed by the following questions.

1.  Should I focus on myself or the other person? 
2.  How far into the future should I be looking?
3.  How can I act on the information I receive?
4.  Out of all the information being thrown at me, what is decision-relevant?
5.  Might the best way to get better at the game be outside the game itself?

### Talks

When giving a talk, self-consciousness is akin to keeping your eyes on yourself. Telling yourself not to be self-conscious is about as useful as trying not to think about the [polar bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironic_process_theory); negative effort rarely helps. Move your eyes elsewhere: to the future and to other people. Rehearse your presentation and anticipate the most difficult parts to explain. Pay attention to your audience and actually look at them. See if you can figure out who is engaged and who is daydreaming. Find one or two audience members with lively facial expressions, study them, and act on that information – their furrowed brows will tell you whether you’re going too fast.

When listening to a talk, realize that there will typically be more information than any one audience member can digest. Sometimes this is the fault of the speaker, but just as often, this information overload is by design and functions similarly to [price segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_discrimination). Noga Alon recently joked to me, “Going to a talk is difficult for everyone because nobody understands the whole thing, but it’s especially difficult for undergraduates because they still expect to.” Information at a variety of levels of abstraction are presented in the same talk, so that audience members with widely varying backgrounds can all get something from it. An undergraduate student might understand only the opening slide, and a graduate student the first ten minutes, while that one inquisitive faculty member will be the only person who understands the cryptic throwaway remarks about connections to class field theory at the very end. Filter aggressively for the parts of the talk aimed at you in particular. 

Remember that the topic of the talk itself is rarely as interesting as the background material mentioned in passing in the first ten minutes. These classics – the core theorems and examples mentioned time and time again, the simplest proof techniques that appear over and over – are the real gems if you don’t know them already. Sometimes you can learn a whole new subfield by sitting in on a number of talks in the area and only listening to the first ten minutes of each. Bring something discreet to keep yourself occupied for the other fifty.

Remember also: information you never act on is useless information. A couple years back, I was taking a nap in a computer science lecture about a variation on an Important Old Theorem. As I was nodding off, I noticed to my surprise that my adviser, sitting nearby, was quite engaged with the talk. I was very curious what caught his attention, and he enlightened me on our walk back to the math department: instead of listening to the talk, he’d spent most of the hour looking for a better proof of the Important Old Theorem introduced in the first five minutes. From this, I learned that the most important information in a talk might be an unsolved problem, because it is certainly the easiest information to act on.

[![](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/jordan_meme.jpg?w=800)](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/jordan_meme.jpg)

_My PhD adviser after a seminar talk._

This conversation with my adviser had a great effect on me, and every so often I practice this perspective by going to a talk for the sole purpose of hearing new problems. As soon as I hear an interesting problem, I zone out and try to solve it immediately. Anecdotally, it worked a couple times.

### Papers

Most of this section was already covered in Of Math and Memory ([part I](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2019/11/13/of-math-and-memory-part-1/), [part II](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2020/01/27/of-math-and-memory-part-2/), [part III](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2020/05/16/of-math-and-memory-part-3-final/)), but I’ll reiterate here the relevant bits. Mathematical proofs are rarely meant to be written or read linearly. Instead, they are ideally arranged as a collection of outlines of increasing detail: a five-word title, a paragraph-long abstract, two pages of introduction, a four page technical outline, and only then the complete 20-page proof. Each outline is higher-resolution than the next, giving readers the chance to pick the level of understanding that suits their needs.

This organization is meant to solve one basic difficulty: it’s very hard to follow a proof without knowing where it’s going. Without reading the proof outline, you can’t tell which of the ten lemmas are boilerplate and which are critical innovations. Without running through a calculation at a high level, there’s no way to know which of alpha, n, epsilon, x, and y are important to track, and which are throwaway error terms. Reading through a paper line-by-line without knowing where you’re going, it’s easy to get lost in the weeds and dragged down endless rabbit holes – black boxes from previous papers to unpack, open problems to mull over, lightly explained computations which might contain typos – and while these rabbit holes might be worth exploring, you would do well to map them all out before picking one to dive into. 

When reading a paper, orient your eyes towards the future whenever possible, like reading several words ahead in a game of TypeRacer. Scan the paper at a high level to understand the big picture, then read all the theorem and lemma statements to see how they fit together, and only then decide which weeds to get into. Only check a difficult computation once you already know what its payoff will be.

### Meetings

In research, the vast majority of your time is spent in one of two ways: bashing your head against a wall alone, or bashing your head against a wall with company. These two activities can be more or less traded freely for each other to suit your level of introversion, and I’ve found that I usually prefer meeting with others to work on research together over working alone.

One of the pitfalls of working with others, especially when you are young and underconfident, is that you can naturally slide into the role Richard Hamming calls a “sound absorber”:

> For myself I find it desirable to talk to other people; but a session of brainstorming is seldom worthwhile. I do go in to strictly talk to somebody and say, “Look, I think there has to be something here. Here’s what I think I see …” and then begin talking back and forth. But you want to pick capable people. To use another analogy, you know the idea called the \`critical mass.’ If you have enough stuff you have critical mass. There is also the idea I used to call \`sound absorbers’. When you get too many sound absorbers, you give out an idea and they merely say, “Yes, yes, yes.” What you want to do is get that critical mass in action; “Yes, that reminds me of so and so,” or, “Have you thought about that or this?” When you talk to other people, you want to get rid of those sound absorbers who are nice people but merely say, “Oh yes,” and to find those who will stimulate you right back. 
> 
> ~ Richard Hamming, You and Your Research

On top of underconfidence, I suspect that the chief mistake “sound absorbers” make is that they have the wrong idea about where their eyes should be in a research meeting. I think a “sound absorber” is completely fixated on _personally solving the problem_. Not having generated interesting ideas for solving the problem, they contribute nothing at all. Again, this mistake is akin to being too self-conscious, and keeping your eyes on yourself when there is no useful information to be had there.

Personally solving the problem is certainly a great outcome for a research meeting, but it’s by no means the only goal. First of all, there’s a world of difference between _personally solving the problem_ and _getting the problem solved_. If your collaborators are any good, they are just as likely to come up with the next crucial idea as you are, so truly optimizing for _getting the problem solved_ involves spending a substantial fraction of your time supporting the thought processes of others. Repeat their thoughts back to them, write down and check their calculations, draw a nice picture or analogy for what they’re doing on the blackboard, project your enthusiasm for their insights. You can do all this without generating a single original thought, and still help with _getting the problem solved._

_Getting the problem solved_ is a higher value than _personally solving the problem_, but higher still is the value of _improving at problem-solving in general_, and this holds doubly if you’re still performing your [Gravity Turn](https://radimentary.wordpress.com/2021/08/16/gravity-turn/). Especially when meeting with your PhD adviser or another senior mentor, focus a substantial minority of your attention on modelling the thought processes of your mentor. Figure out and note down what examples and lemmas they pull out of their toolbox time and time again, what calculations and simplifications they do instinctively, and how they react when stuck on a problem. Learn the particularities of how they perform literature searches, who they ask for help about what, and how they decide if and when to give up. None of these decisions are arbitrary; they form an embodied model of the terrain in your field. Watching the other person can often be a better use of your time than staring blankly at the problem.

In hurried conclusion, research is like a simpler version of Dota: we are bombarded by information on all fronts, most of which we don’t even notice, and tasked to make complicated, heavy-tailed decisions. A fundamental skill in any such game is orienting your eyes – literally and figuratively – at the most valuable and decision-relevant information. Reacting to and executing on this information comes later, but you can never act properly if you don’t even see what you need to do.
