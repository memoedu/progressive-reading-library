# You don't need to work on hard problems

---

## 正文

_大学，2012_——又到了实习招聘季。招聘人员问：“你希望从实习中获得什么？”我回答：“我想 solve hard technical problems。”最后，我去了 [Jane Street](https://www.janestreet.com/)，在那里 writing software，来 calculate 一个 evaluate 起来 costly 的 function 的 numeric integrals。（他们不告诉我这个 function 是什么——对实习生来说，这太机密了。）

两周后，我想出了一些自己的 tweaks，让这个 algorithm 的 work 稍微好了一点。我高兴地在简历里加上：“built 了一个用于 numerical integration 的 state-of-the-art library，对 academic literature 中最好的 techniques 做出了 novel improvements。”

_回到校园_——该申报专业了。所有课我都听得昏昏欲睡，只有 math 课例外：课上用了足够多的 jargon，让我感到困惑，而不是无聊。看来就选 math 吧。申报之后，我终于分到了一位不会劝我选更容易的课程的导师。

_现实世界，2014_——Math 课并没有让我免于[厌倦大学](https://www.benkuhn.net/h)。我决定在大四休学一年，去一家很有吸引力的 machine learning startup 工作。我花了几个月 reading papers，并 optimizing 我们的 models。我们的 company 没有 grow，因为我们无法 close sales。（我们有三位 engineers，却没有 sales person。）

_现实世界，2015_——我的同学们已经毕业，去了 math grad school，或者 Google、Facebook。我则从 [earning to give](https://www.benkuhn.net/downgrading-confidence-earning-give) 转向了[在 Wave 直接尝试改善世界](https://www.benkuhn.net/status)。

Wave 正在帮 immigrants 省下很多钱。不幸的是，我们也在帮 fraudsters 省下很多钱：他们利用我们来 charge 别人的 debit cards。我花了三个小时 building 一个 trigram logistic regression，它能将 Kenyan names（真正的 users）与 non-Kenyan names（很可能是 fraudsters）进行 differentiates，这让我们的 fraud 在接下来一年里得到了遏制。

_现实世界，2016_——Wave 新的第二大 problem 是，我们已经 outgrown Quickbooks。我们_最大的_ problem 则是，我们雇来 fix 这件事的 accountant-cum-engineer，两样都不擅长。

最后只剩我来收拾烂摊子，用 raw Postgres queries 和粗制滥造、半成品似的 Twitter Bootstrap UIs 拼凑出的一套糟糕透顶的组合，来 closing 我们的 2015 books。我每周花 20 小时与我们的 auditors 通电话，解释我们那些糟糕的决定。不知怎么回事，尽管难以置信，我竟然不觉得无聊。

* * *

不知为什么，很多聪明的大学生最后都会觉得，“solving hard technical problems”是他们这辈子能做的最好的事。这种说法在 [Hacker News 评论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=9242217)、[招聘广告](https://stackoverflow.com/jobs?q=hard+problems)和[面试问题](https://stackoverflow.blog/2017/04/27/how-to-talk-about-yourself-in-an-interview/)中屡见不鲜。

为什么会这样？大概因为在过去 15 年里，这是他们唯一获得过奖励的事。

学校是一个 closed-world domain——你在 solving crisply-defined puzzles（对这两个 numbers 进行 multiply，对这个 algorithm 进行 implement，按照这份 rubric 写一篇读书报告），你的 solution 只在一个 dimension（letter grade）上被 evaluated，而且 performance ceiling（一个 A+）很 low。唯一的 progression 形式，就是选更难的课程。如果你试图在这个 reward function 下 maximize 你的 rewards，最终就会去寻找越来越棘手、又能让你拿到 A+ 的 puzzles。

现实世界则截然相反。你会有一个极其模糊的 end goal，例如“帮助 sub-Saharan Africa 的人们 solve 他们的 money problems”，你需要据此对许多不同的 sub-problems 进行 prioritize。一个 solution 的 performance 有许多不同的 dimensions（speed、reliability、usability、repeatability、cost，……）——你甚至可能连所有的 dimensions _是什么_都不知道，更不用说其中哪些最重要。plausible outcomes 的 range 横跨多个 [orders of magnitude](http://www.paulgraham.com/swan.html)，而 ceiling 则是[挽救数十亿人的生命](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Borlaug)。你在做 problem sets 时学到的习惯，在这里帮不上忙。

* * *

由于这些差异，名校的大多数毕业生——包括我——起初都完全无法 identify 哪些工作真正重要。（而且，即使某项重要工作真的摆在我们眼前，它也不会以附带 grading rubric 的 puzzle 的形式出现，所以我们也不知道该怎样把它做好。）相反，我们会继续试着沿用大学里的那套做法，寻找 hard problems。

我们往往会通过把 easy problems 变成 hard 来找到它们，结果令人啼笑皆非，或者沮丧。Big Tech 的高层里，满是靠 writing [bizarre](https://eng.uber.com/schemaless-part-two/) [custom](https://stripe.com/blog/announcing-mosql) [databases](https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/a/2014/manhattan-our-real-time-multi-tenant-distributed-database-for-twitter-scale.html)，或 building [用一台 laptop 就能替代的 Big Data infrastructure](https://adamdrake.com/command-line-tools-can-be-235x-faster-than-your-hadoop-cluster.html)而成就职业生涯的人。

刚毕业时，我担心，如果使用 [boring technology](http://boringtechnology.club/) 工作，我就会觉得无聊。结果我发现，在其他 dimensions 上进行 optimize，或者换一种玩法，不仅可行，而且很有趣。我不必为了在一个 hard problem 上拿到 A+ 而竞争，而可以尝试尽可能快地 solve 一个 easy problem（比如 Wave 的 accounting），或者寻找 solution 会有用的最容易的 problem（比如 identifying Kenyan names），或者雇一支团队，以比我自己所能达到的更快的速度 solve easy problems。

事实上，这些事最后反而更有趣！为什么？因为“hard technical problems”并不是我的 root goal——我的 root goal 是运用自己的 skills，在[改善世界](https://www.benkuhn.net/ea/)这件事上获得尽可能大的 leverage。

在学校里，如果你选一个 easy problem，而不是 hard 的，你就会失去 leverage，因为你多出来的 problem-solving ability 被浪费了。但在现实生活中，你可以把它转用于 prioritizing 要 solve 哪些 problems，或者更快地工作，或者 building 一台替你 solves 这些 problems 的 machine。

正因如此，我确信，我 solve “hard problems”的 ability 已经转化成了对 sub-Saharan Africa 人们的生活大得多的 impact，尽管从某种意义上说，“我所做的一切”不过是在帮忙 build 一个 CRUD app。

我不会声称，returns to intelligence 在任何地方都真的 equal。但只要 goal 足够棘手，它们的 distributed 就比你以为的要 evenly 得多。所以，不要去寻找 hard problems——重要的那些，归根结底更有趣！

## 原文

_College, 2012_—Internship recruiting season. “What are you looking for in your internship?” the recruiter asks. “I’d like to solve hard technical problems,” I reply. I end up at [Jane Street](https://www.janestreet.com/) writing software to calculate numeric integrals of a function that is costly to evaluate. (They don’t tell me what the function is—it’s too secret for interns.)

After two weeks, I come up with some of my own tweaks that make the algorithm work a bit better. I happily add “built a state-of-the-art library for numerical integration, with novel improvements on the best techniques in the academic literature” to my resume.

_Back to campus_—time to declare a major. I’m falling asleep in all my lectures, except the math ones, which use enough jargon that I am confused instead of bored. Guess it’s math then. After declaring, I finally get assigned an adviser who doesn’t tell me to take easier courses.

_Real world, 2014_—Math classes haven’t saved me from getting [bored of college](https://www.benkuhn.net/h). I decide to take senior year off to work for a sexy machine learning startup. I spend months reading papers and optimizing our models. Our company doesn’t grow because we can’t close sales. (We have three engineers and no sales person.)

_Real world, 2015_—My classmates have graduated and gone to math grad school or Google or Facebook. I’ve switched from [earning to give](https://www.benkuhn.net/downgrading-confidence-earning-give) to [directly trying to improve the world, at Wave](https://www.benkuhn.net/status).

Wave is saving immigrants a lot of money. Unfortunately, we’re also saving a lot of money for fraudsters, who are using us to charge other people’s debit cards. I spend three hours building a trigram logistic regression that differentiates Kenyan names (real users) from non-Kenyan names (likely fraudsters), which puts a lid on our fraud for the next year.

_Real world, 2016_—Wave’s new second-biggest problem is that we have outgrown Quickbooks. Our _biggest_ problem is that the accountant-cum-engineer we hired to fix this is not good at either.

I’m left holding the bag, closing our 2015 books with a god-awful combination of raw Postgres queries and janky half-baked Twitter Bootstrap UIs. I spend 20 hours a week on the phone with our auditors, explaining our terrible decisions. Somehow, improbably, I am not bored.

* * *

For some reason, a lot of smart college students end up with the idea that “solving hard technical problems” is the best thing they can do with their life. It’s a common refrain in [Hacker News comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=9242217), [job ads](https://stackoverflow.com/jobs?q=hard+problems) and [interview questions](https://stackoverflow.blog/2017/04/27/how-to-talk-about-yourself-in-an-interview/).

Why does this happen? Probably because that’s the only thing they’ve been rewarded for over the past 15 years.

School is a closed-world domain—you are solving crisply-defined puzzles (multiply these two numbers, implement this algorithm, write a book report by this rubric), your solution is evaluated on one dimension (letter grade), and the performance ceiling (an A+) is low. The only form of progression is to take harder courses. If you try to maximize your rewards under this reward function, you’ll end up looking for trickier and trickier puzzles that you can get an A+ on.

The real world is the polar opposite. You’ll have some ultra-vague end goal, like “help people in sub-Saharan Africa solve their money problems,” based on which you’ll need to prioritize many different sub-problems. A solution’s performance has many different dimensions (speed, reliability, usability, repeatability, cost, …)—you probably don’t even know what all the dimensions _are_, let alone which are the most important. The range of plausible outcomes covers [orders of magnitude](http://www.paulgraham.com/swan.html) and the ceiling is [saving billions of lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Borlaug). The habits you learn by working on problem sets won’t help you here.

* * *

Because of these differences, most graduates of elite schools—including me—start out being completely unable to identify which work is actually important. (And if some important work does happen to hit us over the head, it won’t come in the form of a puzzle with a grading rubric, so we won’t know how to execute it well.) Instead, we’ll keep trying to run our college playbook, and look for hard problems.

Frequently, we’ll find them by making easy problems hard, with hilarious/depressing results. The upper ranks of Big Tech are filled with people who made their careers writing [bizarre](https://eng.uber.com/schemaless-part-two/) [custom](https://stripe.com/blog/announcing-mosql) [databases](https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/a/2014/manhattan-our-real-time-multi-tenant-distributed-database-for-twitter-scale.html), or building [Big Data infrastructure that could be replaced with a laptop](https://adamdrake.com/command-line-tools-can-be-235x-faster-than-your-hadoop-cluster.html).

When I first graduated, I was afraid that if I worked with [boring technology](http://boringtechnology.club/), I’d get bored. Instead, I learned it was possible—and fun—to optimize on other dimensions, or play a different game. Rather than competing for an A+ on a hard problem, I could try to solve an easy problem as quickly as possible (like Wave’s accounting), or find the easiest problem whose solution would be useful (like identifying Kenyan names), or hire a team to solve easy problems faster than I ever could myself.

In fact, these turned out to be even more interesting! Why? Because “hard technical problems” wasn’t my root goal—my root goal was to use my skills to get the most possible leverage on [improving the world](https://www.benkuhn.net/ea/).

In school, if you pick an easy problem instead of a hard one, you lose leverage because your extra problem-solving ability goes to waste. But in real life, you can redirect it to prioritizing which problems to solve, or working more quickly, or building a machine that solves the problems for you.

Because of that, I’m confident that my ability to solve “hard problems” has translated into having way more impact on the lives of people in sub-Saharan Africa, even though there’s a sense in which “all I’m doing” is helping build a CRUD app.

I won’t claim that the returns to intelligence are literally equal everywhere. But given a hairy enough goal, they’re a lot more evenly distributed than you think. So don’t look for hard problems—important ones are ultimately more fun!
