# When simple econ models don't work

---

## 正文

最近在读：Dani Rodrik 的 [The Globalization Paradox](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/9303596-the-globalization-paradox)。

countries 应该对 imports impose taxes 吗？

最典型的 economist 回答是：不应该！[comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage) 的 theory 认为，trade 是 [positive sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Win-win_game)。Tariffs 是一种 [prisoner’s dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma)，我们应该用 [multilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Pacific_Partnership) 来 fix 这个 game theory。可惜，lobbyists 和 entrenched interests 会 [fight against it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_capture)。

这个回答遗漏了（至少）三件重要的事。

-   Tariff reductions 是 positive sum，但仍有 negative components。当我对 grain imports reduce taxes 时，这意味着 price signals 会使我的 production capacity 从 inefficient grain production 被 reallocated 到其他更 efficient 的事情上。太好了，我 improved 了 efficiency！但我也从我的 grain-producers 向我的 non-grain-producers transferred 了巨额 money。Rodrik estimates[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/econ101/#fn:1)，如果 US 今天转向 complete free trade，每 \$1 in efficiency gained by reducing tariffs would be accompanied by $50（！）的 wealth transfer，从 domestic producers（他们的 goods 是 less competitive）转向 importers（他们需要缴纳的 tax 更少）。

-   大多数 countries 都希望自己的 citizens 有事可做。无事可做真的很糟糕！处于 unemployed 状态会让人承受极大的压力、恐惧和沮丧；事实上，long-term unemployment 会 measurably decreases life expectancy。financial shock 不仅可能毁掉你的生活，也可能毁掉你所有 dependents 的生活。从 Socially 的角度看，high unemployment 往往会 cause political unrest、愤怒、xenophobia 和 crime。

-   大多数 countries 现在想要更多东西，以后也想要更多东西。这意味着它们关心 improving growth。做到这一点最容易的方式（如果你不是 richest countries 之一），就是让你的 citizens 的 human capital 赶上 rich-country levels。某些 production 的 types 在这方面远比其他 types 更好。

    如果你主要在 exporting minerals，你真正需要知道的就只有 pickaxe technique。这里的 human capital ceiling 相当 low。但如果你在 building cars，你就需要有人懂得如何把 cars 组装起来、每个 part 有什么作用、出现怪声时如何修理、如何确保 car plant 里的其他每个人都在做好自己的工作，等等。这些 skills 要 productive 得多，因此也更 valuable。

    如果你看看过去 50 年里 developed most quickly 的 countries——主要在 East 和 Southeast Asia——你就会发现，所有这些 countries 实际上都曾在不同时期实行相当 aggressive 的 tariffs 和 export subsidies，并且为了 gain 它们如今在 electronics、manufacturing 等领域的 advantages，做过一些并不十分 free-market-oriented 的事情。[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/econ101/#fn:2)

完全可以把这些 caveats incorporate 到 inter-country trade 的 naive model 中。但大多数时候，当你向 economist 询问 tariffs 时，他们不会尝试这样做；他们只会告诉你，tariffs 的 optimal level 是 zero。Rodrik 认为，大多数 economists 为这些省略辩护时会说，只要 implementing 从 free trade 的 beneficiaries（即 net importers）到 harmed parties（domestic producers、unemployed people，以及具有 positive growth externalities 的 sectors）的 redistribution，就能使 trade liberalization 成为一种 Pareto improvement。

但即使这些 redistributions 在原则上是 possible 的，似乎也很难恰当地确定它们的 size。没有人确切知道，如果你对 domestic producers 的 goods reduce import tariffs，会在多大程度上 harm 他们——更不用说会在多大程度上 affect unemployment（这取决于 skill transferability、union negotiations、未来的 economic conditions 等）或 growth（对此根本无人理解）。

既然如此，就实在很难看出你如何能促成任何涉及如此 large transfers 的 deal。如果你的 grain tariff reduction 拿走 \$50 from Pat and gives \$51 给 Dana，你可以尝试通过立法，另外安排一笔从 Dana 到 Pat 的 \$50 direct transfer，让这件事变得可以接受。但只要 Dana 对自己将从 tariff reduction 中 benefit 的程度 underestimates 哪怕 2%，他们就会反对说，第二笔 compensatory transfer 太大了！为了 fix 这个 redistribution，各方需要对 costs 和 benefits 的 magnitudes 达成非常精确的共识。

这里的模式是，naive model 通过 assumptions 排除了一大堆东西，然后当那些 assumptions 被 violated 时，又把它当作 second-order problem——但由于这些 assumptions 实际上涉及非常 large effects，只需稍微被 violated，就足以 dominate calculation。

如果你把这一点 generalize 到 economists 所说的其他事情上，会发生什么？

我认为，这说明你应该对 technological unemployment 担心得多。确实，historical track record 表明，technological improvements 在 long run 通常是值得的。但 historical track record 也表明，它往往伴随着非常痛苦的 short-term dislocations、人们在乡间四处奔走砸毁 looms，等等。我们似乎应该能够做得更好——但如果 economists 把自己的 policy suggestions 限于“是啊，应该可以靠一些 redistribution 来 fix 这个问题”，我们就不太可能做得更好。

更带推测性地说，我认为同样的 reasoning 表明，我们应该对 wage controls 困惑得多。这又是一个例子：naive economic analysis 强烈建议 liberalization，但也作出了许多 spherical-cow assumptions。在这个例子中，那些 assumptions 主要涉及相关 markets 的 efficiency。似乎不难想象，某些 scenarios 下 minimum wage 会 improves efficiency——例如，local employers 的 market power 远大于 employees，或者 changing jobs 的 transaction cost 高到使 job market 并不是很 efficient，或者人们出于 behavioral reasons 而 averse to changing jobs。类似的论证也 applies 于 rent controls。

## 原文

Reading recently: [The Globalization Paradox](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/9303596-the-globalization-paradox) by Dani Rodrik.

Should countries impose taxes on imports?

The bog standard economist answer is: no! The theory of [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage) says that trade is [positive sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Win-win_game). Tariffs are a [prisoner’s dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma) and we should fix the game theory with [multilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Pacific_Partnership). It’s too bad that lobbyists and entrenched interests [fight against it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_capture).

There are (at least) three major things this answer leaves out.

-   Tariff reductions are positive sum, but they still have negative components. When I reduce taxes on grain imports, it means that price signals cause my production capacity to be reallocated from inefficient grain production, to other more efficient things. Great, I’ve improved efficiency! But I’ve also transferred an enormous amount of money from my grain-producers to my non-grain-producers. Rodrik estimates[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/econ101/#fn:1) that if the US moved to complete free trade today, every $1 in efficiency gained by reducing tariffs would be accompanied by $50 (!) of wealth transfer from domestic producers (whose goods are less competitive) to importers (who have to pay less in tax).

-   Most countries want their citizens to have things to do. Not having stuff to do is really bad! Being unemployed is incredibly stressful, scary, and depressing; in fact, long-term unemployment measurably decreases life expectancy. The financial shock can mess up not only your life but also all your dependents’. Socially, high unemployment tends to cause political unrest, anger, xenophobia and crime.

-   Most countries want more stuff now, but also want more stuff later. That means they care about improving growth. The easiest way to do that (if you’re not one of the richest countries) is to catch up your citizens’ human capital to rich-country levels. Some types of production are much better for that than others.

    If you’re mostly exporting minerals, all you really need to know is pickaxe technique. The human capital ceiling here is pretty low. But if you’re building cars, you need people who understand how to put the cars together, what each part does, how to fix it if it’s making weird noises, how to make sure everyone else in the car plant is doing their job, and so on. Those are much more productive, hence valuable, skills.

    If you look at countries that have developed most quickly in the last 50 years—mainly East and Southeast Asia—you see that all these countries actually had fairly aggressive tariffs and export subsidies at various points, and did things that weren’t very free-market-oriented, in order to gain the advantages they now have in electronics, manufacturing, etc.[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/econ101/#fn:2)

It’s perfectly possible to incorporate these caveats into the naive model of inter-country trade. But most of the time you ask an economist about tariffs they don’t try to do that; they just tell you that the optimal level of tariffs is zero. Rodrik thinks that most economists justify these elisions by saying that you can make trade liberalization a Pareto improvement by implementing redistribution from the beneficiaries of free trade (i.e., net importers) to the harmed parties (domestic producers, unemployed people, and sectors with positive growth externalities).

But even if these redistributions are possible in principle, it seems very hard to size them properly. No one knows exactly how much it will harm domestic producers if you reduce import tariffs on their goods—let alone how much it will affect unemployment (which depends on skill transferability, union negotiations, future economic conditions, etc.) or growth (which nobody understands at all).

Given that, it’s actually hard to see how you could broker any deal involving such large transfers. If your grain tariff reduction takes away $50 from Pat and gives $51 to Dana, you could try to make it palatable by legislating a separate direct transfer of $50 from Dana to Pat. But if Dana underestimates how much they’ll benefit from the tariff reduction by even 2%, they’ll object that the second compensatory transfer is too big! In order to fix the redistribution, the parties need to agree really precisely on the magnitudes of the costs and benefits.

The pattern here is that the naive model assumes a way a bunch of stuff, and then treats it as a second-order problem when those assumptions are violated—but, because the assumptions are actually about very large effects, they only need to be violated a little bit in order to dominate the calculation.

What happens if you generalize this to other things that economists say?

I think this suggests you should freak out a lot more about technological unemployment. It’s true that the historical track record suggests that technological improvements are usually worth it in the long run. But, the historical track record also suggests that it’s often accompanied by very painful short-term dislocations, people running around the countryside smashing looms, and so on. It seems like it should be possible to do better—but it’s unlikely that we will do better if economists limit their policy suggestions to “yeah, it should be possible to fix that with some redistribution.”

More speculatively, I think it the same reasoning suggests that we should be a lot more confused about wage controls. This is another example where the naive economic analysis strongly suggests liberalization, but also makes a lot of spherical-cow assumptions. In this case, those assumptions are mostly about the efficiency of the relevant markets. It seems like it’s not too hard to imagine scenarios where a minimum wage improves efficiency—for instance, if local employers have much more market power than employees, or if the transaction cost of changing jobs is high enough that the job market isn’t very efficient, or if people are averse to changing jobs for behavioral reasons. An analogous case also applies for rent controls.
