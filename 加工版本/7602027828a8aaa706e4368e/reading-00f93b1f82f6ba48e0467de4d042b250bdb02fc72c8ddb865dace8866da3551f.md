# Things I learned from Toby Ord

## 正文

[Toby Ord](http://www.amirrorclear.net/academic/) 最近为 [Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy](http://harvardhip.org/) 做了一场关于 foreign aid 的演讲，题为“Aid Works (On Average)（援助有效，平均而言）”。这场演讲在许多方面都很有启发。以下是演讲本身的一些精彩要点：

-   Eradicating smallpox 所 saved 的 lives 如此之多，以至于即使 assuming 其他所有 foreign aid interventions 都是 neutral，它仍然 averages out 到远低于 US 愿意为 save a life 支付的金额。

-   这次 eradication 还 paid for itself，也就是说，存在一条 direct 且 obvious 的 causal path，使它 paid for itself（大多数 health interventions 很可能通过 increased GDP 来 pay for themselves，但这一点总是很难确定）：smallpox inoculation 不再是 necessary，每年 saving 数十亿的 vaccination costs。

-   此外，还有其他几项 public health foreign-aid efforts，其 effects 处于 the same order of magnitude，包括 fighting cholera 和 malaria。这些 efforts 中的每一项所 yielded 的 utility，都可能比人类迄今做过的任何其他事情更多。

-   这些 efforts 主要是“vertical”的，也就是 focused on eliminating a single disease。尽管它们取得了巨大成功，vertical interventions 在 public health 圈子里却已不再流行，可能是因为没有人 run the numbers 并意识到它们有多么 effective。Dr. Ord 提出，overspecialization 可能是其他人没有做过这个相对 simple 的 calculation 的原因。

在之后的问答环节中，Dr. Ord 又提到了几个非常有趣的观点。

-   至少据他所说，他做的那些听起来很了不起的事情——与 Prime Minister 的 special advisers 交往、帮助 set disease control priorities、吸引大量 donors——其实并不需要超能力，只需要愿意大胆行动并 network/make connections。他声称，任何有足够动力且相当聪明的人都能做这类事情，对在场听他演讲的许多学生而言，这些事情也可能很适合他们。我觉得，这可能是演讲中最值得本科生听到的部分——对我来说当然很鼓舞人心！

-   （在我看来，最重要的一点是：）一些 preliminary modeling results 表明，meta-research（研究哪些 interventions 最 effective）与 direct interventions 之间的 impact differential 可能是 _a factor of 1,000 or higher._ 不出所料，这对于你所 assume 将被 redirected 到更 cost-effective 的 interventions 的金额相当 sensitive，所以我猜他们在这里采用了相当 optimistic 的 figures（我记得 Toby 提到过数十亿左右）。不过，如果你 assume 它对于 money moved 是 linear 的，并从十亿降到仅仅 GiveWell 的 money moved（今年为 \$10 million），仍然至少有 a factor of 10。

    我 assume 这个 figure 还非常 tentative，否则我肯定早就听说过了；但即使只是关于这种 differential 的传闻，也足以让我觉得，在 making a donation 之前，我或许应该再等等。

## 原文

[Toby Ord](http://www.amirrorclear.net/academic/) recently gave a talk about foreign aid to [Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy](http://harvardhip.org/), titled “Aid Works (On Average)”. It was really enlightening in many ways. Here are a few juicy points from the talk itself:

-   Eradicating smallpox saved so many lives that, assuming all other foreign aid interventions were neutral, it still averages out to well below the amount the US is willing to pay to save a life.

-   The eradication also paid for itself, as in, there’s a direct and obvious causal path through which it paid for itself (most health interventions probably pay for themselves through increased GDP, but that’s always hard to be sure about): smallpox inoculation was no longer necessary, saving billions in vaccination costs each year.

-   Also, there were several other public health foreign-aid efforts with effects on the same order of magnitude, including fighting cholera and malaria. Each of these efforts may have yielded more utility than anything else humanity has done to date.

-   These efforts were largely “vertical”, that is, focused on eliminating a single disease. Despite their massive successes, vertical interventions are no longer in vogue in public health circles, possibly because nobody has run the numbers and realized how effective they are. Dr. Ord suggested overspecialization as a potential reason why nobody else did this relatively simple calculation.

During the Q&A afterwards, Dr. Ord brought up a few other really interesting points.

-   According to him, at least, the impressive-sounding things that he does—hobnobbing with special advisers to the Prime Minister, helping set disease control priorities, attracting tons of donors—don’t actually require superpowers, just a willingness to be bold and network/make connections. He claims they’re the kind of thing that any sufficiently motivated and reasonably bright person could do, and could be a good fit for many of the students listening to him. I think this may have been the part of the talk that was best for the undergrads to hear—certainly heartening for me!

-   (The big one, in my opinion:) some preliminary modeling results show that the impact differential between meta-research (on which interventions are most effective) and direct interventions could be _a factor of 1,000 or higher._ Unsurprisingly, this is quite sensitive to the amount of money you assume will be redirected to more cost-effective interventions, so I’m guessing they used pretty optimistic figures for this (I think Toby mentioned something in the billions). Nevertheless, if you assume it’s linear in money moved and you go down from a billion to just GiveWell’s money moved ($10 million this year), you still have at least a factor of 10.

    I assume that this figure is very tentative because otherwise I would surely have heard it before, but even rumors of that kind of differential are enough to suggest that I might want to wait before making a donation.
