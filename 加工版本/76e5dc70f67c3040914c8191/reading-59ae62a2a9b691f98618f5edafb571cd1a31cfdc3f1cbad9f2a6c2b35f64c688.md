## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

Michael Nielsen

首次发布：2022年6月2日

本次发布：2022年6月3日（小幅修改）

_关于 Effective Altruism (EA) 的冗长而粗略的笔记。写下来是为了帮助我弄清几个问题：我喜欢 EA 的哪些方面，又认为哪些方面很重要？为什么我觉得这种 mindset 如此陌生？为什么我不是一名 EA？也为了让我开始思考：EA 的 alternatives 会是什么样？这些笔记并不是写给 effective altruists 的，不过 EA-adjacent 的人或许会感兴趣。欢迎深思熟虑、有所了解的评论和指正（尤其是详细、具体的指正！）——请见底部的评论区。_

_“运用 evidence 和 reason，弄清如何尽可能多地 benefit others，并在此基础上 taking action”_：这就是 Effective Altruism (EA) 这一 ideology 和 movement 的基础理念[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn1)。过去二十年间，它从少数 moral philosophers 反复讨论的一个想法，变成了数千乃至数万人 life philosophy 的核心部分，其中包括世界上几位最有权势、最富有的人。这些是我关于 EA 的粗略工作笔记。笔记很长，写得也很仓促：是尚未组织好的粗略思考，而不是一篇精心打磨的文章。

我写这些笔记有几个原因。一个纯粹出于社交：我的许多朋友对 EA 都有强烈的看法（有些支持，有些反对，还有些更为中立）。另一个原因是，我觉得 EA 作为一种 social movement，以及（或许）一组 ideas，是重要的。有这么多聪明、怀抱理想的青少年和二十多岁的年轻人对 EA 产生如此强烈的反响，这件事意义重大。许多人说，他们彻底改变了自己的生活：换了职业；改变了日常行为；承诺把 income 的很大一部分捐给他们称为“effective”的 charities。EAs[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn2) 还共享许多不寻常的 language 和看待世界的方式，其中很大一部分改编自 welfare economics 和 moral philosophy。

人们很容易把这一切斥为“不过”是一种风潮，或者认为这只是 EA 获得的 funding（飞速）增长的结果。但我不认同。许多 EAs 极其真诚，并在 EA 中找到了强大的 conviction 和 meaning。它正在为他们发挥某种非常重要的作用，远远不只是一种小众风潮。

我最初了解 EA 时，本能而未经深思的反应相当负面。我经常半开玩笑地说，自己是个 ineffective altruist，或者 chaotic altruist。我喜欢把自己称为 mutilitarian，用 Zen Buddhist 的“mu”作为我的 utility function（也就是说，否定这个想法）。然而，深入审视后就会发现，这些都是轻率的否定。

2011 年，我的一位 EA 朋友接受了手术，向一位陌生人 donating a kidney。他向我解释说，他：

> 偶然看到了一些关于 donate 有多 safe 的 stats，这彻底改变了我的看法。我想，surgery 中 1/3,000 的 risk of death，就像牺牲自己来 save 3,000 people。我想成为愿意那样做的人，而你只需要遵循这几个步骤。

我有一些 EA 朋友，会把 income 的很大一部分 donate 给 charitable causes。有些人的做法是，把超过某个相当低的 threshold（以富裕的 developed world 的标准而言）的 income _全部_捐出，比如 $30k 以上的部分。有些情况下，我们有理由相信，他们的 personal donations 已经 saving dozens of lives，帮助许多人 lift out of poverty，并 preventing 许多 debilitating diseases，而且往往是在世界上一些最贫穷、最 underserved 的地区。他们之中有些人已经直接帮助 save many lives。这句话很简单，却非同寻常，所以我要再说一遍：他们已经直接帮助 save many lives。

这一切令我震撼而敬畏。我也为自己那些关于 ineffective altruism 的玩笑感到有点不好意思，并感谢 EA 朋友们对我的包容。我一直努力把自己的 personal skills 和 interests 与那些对世界有 good 的事情结合起来，作为自己的生活方式。我希望自己做出了一些真正的 good，同时也享受着生活。但据我所知，我从未直接 saved a life。我不认为自己_能够_ donate a kidney：这会过分触犯我的 somatic integrity 感。在个人层面上，我喜爱我的 EA 和 near-EA 朋友们的真诚与真正的 goodness。和他们相处之后，我就是会_觉得_自己变得更 wholesome。我往往会更加 honest；有时会更加 kinder 或 more open-minded。这些都是非常好的事情。

因此，接下来是一组关于 EA 的 observations。其中一部分是 appreciation：要 critique EA，你也必须理解它好在哪里。其他 ideologies 也能从中学到很多东西。但我也会深入探究，试着理解 EA 的哪些方面让我不安，我认为哪些地方有问题，以及我认为可以如何对 EA 进行富有成效的 modified。

这些笔记缺少一项内容：关于 EA 所做的 good 的直接、first-person account。我从朋友那里间接感受到了一些，但希望自己知道得更多。没有这些，就不可能真正 appreciate EA。Malaria bed nets、direct cash transfers、de-worming _等等_并不是 abstractions：事实上，它们是现实世界中规模巨大的事件，为许多人的生活带来了巨大改变。这里缺少这些内容，仅仅是因为我的无知。阅读时，请尽量记住这一事实；写作时，我也一直努力这样做。

提醒一下：我对“EA 做什么”作了许多 generalizations。但 EA 并不是 monolithic。这使得我很难在不加入大量 qualifiers 的情况下写作。我本可以通过说“多数 EAs believe”，或者引用 leading EAs 的话等方式来处理。但我（大体上）选择了使用概括性的语言，并默认读者理解：往往会有 EAs 不同意其中某个具体部分。不过，当 EA community 对某一点存在 widespread disagreement 时，我尽量作了说明。

这些笔记开头采用了一个广泛使用的 EA 描述，出自 philosopher Will MacAskill，他是 EA 的 founders 之一：“运用 evidence 和 reason，弄清如何尽可能多地 benefit others，并在此基础上 taking action”。实际上，我经常听到人们把它简略地说成：“运用 evidence 和 reason，做到尽可能多的 good”。我通常会用后者来简要表达 EA 的宗旨，但也会记住那个较长的描述。这两个描述都有一点需要注意：它们本质上都是 maximizing 的，即“尽_可能多_地 benefit others”，“做到尽可能_最多_的 good”。事实上，许多 EAs 主张相当大程度地退出这种 maximizing frame。因此，根据一个人接受这种 maximization approach 的程度（或者是否接受），可以把 EA 看成具有不同的“strengths”。我们还会回到这一点，因为这是 EA community 尚未解决的一个重要问题。而当我采用“the most good”这一 framing 时，也隐含着一个 caveat：许多 EAs 在实践中并不坚持“most”。

## EA 作为 moral invention 的来源[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn3)

我在上文提到过那位在 2011 年 donated a kidney 的朋友。moral philosopher Peter Singer 是 EA 中许多 ideas 的提出者之一，他描述了自己在（2004 年）听说 Zell Kravinsky 的故事时有多么惊讶[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn4)。Kravinsky 是一位富有的 real estate investor，几乎 donated 了自己的全部 fortune，即 \$45 million, living on $每年 60 thousand。但还有更不寻常的事。乍看之下，它与上文我朋友的 kidney donation 故事非常相似。但它在一个重要方面有所不同：

> 他仍然不认为自己为 help others 所做的已经足够，于是与附近一家 hospital 安排向一位陌生人 donate a kidney……他 Quoting 了 scientific studies，表明因 kidney donation 而 dying 的 risk 只有 1 in 4,000。他说，不 making the donation 就意味着他把自己的 life valued 为陌生人的 4,000 times，而他认为这样的 valuation 完全是 unjustified。

尽管我朋友的 generosity 非同寻常，这里仍然还有更进一步的东西。Kravinsky 的 act 体现了 moral imagination——竟然想到 donating a kidney——继而又体现了 moral conviction，将它付诸行动。这是一个令人惊叹的 moral invention act：有人（想必就是 Kravinsky）第一个既设想这样做，又真的做到了。这一 moral invention 随后又激励其他人做同样的事。它确实 expanded 了 human moral experience 的 range，其他人可以从中学习，继而 emulate。从这个意义上说，Kravinsky 这样的人可以被视为 moral pioneer 或 moral psychonaut[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn5)，inventing 新的 moral experience forms。

当然，这样的 moral pioneers 并不只来自 EA。远非如此！他们奠定了我们 civilization 的基础。我个人敬仰的许多英雄都是 moral pioneers，包括 Sermon on the Mount 的作者[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn6)、abolitionist movement、suffragettes 和 feminist movement，以及 Martin Luther King 和 civil rights movement 中的其他 leaders。所有这些人或 movement（以及更多其他人）都做出了 moral imagination 的 acts，expanded 了我们其余人可以 emulate 的 moral experience 的 range。我们未必总是同意他们：例如，我并不确定自己是否认同 Peter Singer 关于 rights of animals 的 views。Singer 在这一点上也许是错的。但作为 expanded 我们潜在 moral experience range 的 moral invention act，它仍然有价值。

EA 有意思的一点在于，它鼓励了许多 moral pioneers：这些人愿意 rethink fundamental moral questions，并且（有时）expand 我们的 moral experience 的 range。他们认真提出过的问题包括（在某些情况下，还根据答案采取了行动）：“如果 animal lives 确实 mattered，会怎样？”“如果世界另一端的一条 life，与在你眼前 drowning 的一个孩子的 life mattered just as much，会怎样？”“如果 intelligent machine 的‘life’与 human being 的 life mattered just as much，会怎样？”“我们应该如何 value 一百万年后 living 的 human being 的 life？”认真对待这些问题，可以让他们 expand 我们的 moral horizons。

moral pioneering 也有黑暗的一面，political philosopher Hannah Arendt 在 _Eichmann in Jerusalem_ 中曾令人难忘地指出这一点。这本书记录了对 Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann 的 trial。在 Arendt 的叙述中，Nazi's（在某种意义上）也是 moral pioneers，inventing 了新的 crime kinds，从而 expanded 了未来 crime 可能出现的 range：

> 对于理解这些新的 crimes，或对于能够处理它们的 international penal code 的出现，没有什么比一种常见的 illusion 更为 pernicious，或更为 obstructive：认为 crime of murder 与 crime of genocide essentially the same，因而后者“严格说来并不是什么新的 crime”。后者的关键在于，一个截然不同的 order 被 broken，一个截然不同的 community 被 violated……人类事务的 nature 本就如此：每一个曾经出现、并被 recorded 在 history of mankind 中的 act，在其 actuality 早已成为过去之后，仍会作为一种 potentiality 与人类同在。从来没有任何 punishment 具有足够的 power of deterrence，能够 prevent crimes 的 commission。恰恰相反，无论 punishment 如何，一旦某种 specific crime 首次出现，它的 reappearance 就比它最初 emergence 时所可能具有的任何 likelihood 都更高。

Moral reasoning 如果被认真对待并付诸行动，就是极其重大的事情，部分原因在于存在犯下可怕错误的危险。Nazi 的例子过于戏剧化：首先，我很难相信 Nazi ideas 的提出者没有意识到这些是极其 evil 的 acts。但一个更日常、也应该让任何 ideology 停下来想一想的例子，是过度 self-righteous 的人，投身于他们“知道”是 good cause 的事情，实际上却在 doing harm。我对 EA 的 moral pioneering 抱有审慎的热情。但它也可能是一片雷区，同样需要谨慎对待。

## EA judo：对任何特定“most good”strategy 的 strong critique 都会 improves EA，而不会 discredit 它

对 EA 最常见的一种“攻击”路线，是不认同 EA 对 do the most good 意味着什么的常见 notions。“你是 EA 吗？”“哦，就是那些认为你需要为 malaria bed nets \[或 AI safety，或 de-worming，/等等等等/\] 捐钱的人，但这是错的，因为 $$…$$。”或者：“Will MacAskill 说 EAs 应该考虑 earning-to-give，但这是错的，因为 $$…$$。”或者：“Science、social justice、creativity 和 \[等等等等\]，远比 QALYs 之类的东西更难 measure，因此 EAs 往往 undervalue 或 ignore 它们。”或者：“EAs 对 RCTs 和 meta-analysis 的 value 相当轻信[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn7)，你应该改为 $$…$$。”或者：“听着，你可以随心所欲地 directly increase QALYs，但这不会让你从 low-growth economy shift 到 high-growth economy。这两者处于 causal abstraction 的不同 levels。”

这些说法可能是对的，也可能不对。无论如何，其中没有任何一个是对 EA 的 fundamental critique。相反，它们是 EA thinking 的例子：当你发表这样的评论时，你实际上正在参与 EA project。EAs 一直都在激烈争论 do the most good 究竟意味着什么。把他们联结起来的，是他们认同自己应该“运用 evidence 和 reason，弄清如何 do the most good”；如果你不认同当前占主流的 EA 对 most good 的 notions，并且有 evidence 可以贡献，那么你就是在为推动 EA 改善其对 good 的理解的磨坊提供原料。

无论如何，在我听过的对 EA 的 external criticism 中，这种“critique”至少占一半——很可能更多。大多数以为自己正在 critiquing EA 的 external critics，实际上 critiquing 的是一个幻影。从这个意义上说，EA 有一个巨大的 surface area，它只会因 critique 而得到 improved，而不会被 weakened。我把这一模式称为 _EA judo_。在与“EA critics”的讨论中，你经常能看到它。一个令人愉快、也能增长见识的例子，是 EA Rob Wiblin [采访](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/russ-roberts-effective-altruism-empirical-research-utilitarianism/) Russ Roberts；后者自称不认同 EA。但在采访的（大部分）过程中，Roberts 默许了 EA 的 basic ideas，只是不同意某些 particular instantiations。而 Wiblin 一遍又一遍地施展 EA judo，把采访变成了一场非常典型的 EA-type debate，讨论如何 do the most good。这很有意思，两位参与者也都思考深入，但它实际上并不是一场关于 EA 的 merits 的 debate。

对我而言，这是 EA 最有吸引力、也最有力量的 features 之一。它使 EA 与大多数 ideologies 很不一样，后者往往相当 static。从某种意义上说，EA 试图对“什么是 good？”这个问题，做出 science 已经对“世界如何运作？”这个问题所做的事情。它不是在提供一个答案，而是在发展一个 community，力求不断 improve 这个答案[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn8)。

因此，值得把 EA-in-practice（一种 social movement）与 EA-the-intellectual-project 区分开来。如果你希望触及 fundamental issues，最终就需要关注后者，而不只是前者。正如我说过的：对 EA-in-practice 的许多 critiques，本来就是推动 improvement 的核心引擎的一部分。但这并不意味着，不值得花时间 critiquing EA-in-practice 的 surface area。“凭着他们的果子，就可以认出他们来”，不仅适用于人，也适用于 intellectual principles。如果一组 principles 结出了许多烂果子，那就说明这些 principles 有问题，构成一种 _reductio ad absurdum_。你大概听过 communists 和 libertarians 为失败的 communist 和 free-market experiments 辩护，说：“那不是真正的 communist / free-market experiment。”有时候，他们说得有道理。但如果这种模式持续出现，如果 fundamental principles 并不 resilient，或者需要大量 special pleading，那就意味着这些 principles 存在严重问题。

换句话说：当 EA judo 被施展得太多时，就值得寻找更 fundamental 的 problems。EA judo 的 basic form 是：“听着，对什么是 good 的 disagreement，并不能直接触及 EA。事实上，这样的 disagreement 正是推动我们改善对 good 的 notion 的引擎。”在某种 God's-eye、omniscient、in-principle 的 philosopher 意义上，这也许是对的。但 EA community 和 organizations 与其他任何 community 和 organization 一样，都会受时尚、power games、shortcomings 和 biases 的影响。仅凭 Good intentions，不足以确保就 effectiveness 做出 effective decisions[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn9)。许多人对 EA 感到不安，并不是因为他们认为“do good better”是个坏主意，而是因为他们怀疑 EA institutions 和 community 是否有能力实现这些抱负。

这些 critiques 可以来自许多方向。我听关心 identity politics 的人说过：“听着，这些 EA organizations 中有许多由有权势的 white men 掌管，reproducing 现有的 power structures，biased toward technocratic capitalism 和 status quo，并且 ignoring 许多真正重要的事情。”我听 libertarians 说过：“听着，EA 不过是 leftist collective utilitarianism。它过度 centralizes decision-making，既 ignores price signals，也 ignores 许多人各自为自身 self-interest 努力所带来的巨大力量，尽管他们置身其中的 system 被设计为让 self-interest（往往）能够 collectively 帮助每个人[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn10)。”我听 startup 从业者和 inventors 说过：“EAs 不就是在从事 public goods 吗？如果你想 do the most good，为什么不转而做一家 startup？我们完全可以 invent 和 scale 新的 technology（或新的 ideas），以 improve 世界！[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn11)”我听熟悉老化 organizations 和 communities 的 pathologies 的人说过：“听着，任何 rapidly grows 的 movement，也都会开始 decay。它会逐渐被野心勃勃的 careerists 和 principal agent problems 所 dominated，并 lose 那些 pioneers 和 early adopters 所具有的 sincerity 和 agility[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn12)”[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn13)。

所有这些 critiques 都有一些道理；它们也存在显著问题。不深究那些枝节，眼下要说的是，它们看起来都“仅仅”是 practical problems，可以对其施展 EA judo：“如果我们没有做好，那我们就会改进，我们只需要你提供 evidence 和一个更好的 alternative。”但这些 organizational patterns 如此强固，以至于在我看来，这些 criticisms 更属于 in-principle 层面。再说一遍：如果你的 social movement “works in principle”，但 practical implementation 存在太多问题，那么它其实也并没有在 principle 层面奏效。“我们能够在 practice 中 effectively 做到这一点”这一 quality，是一项重要的、implicit 的 in-principle quality。

## “Bad” EAs，陷入 misery trap

让我们回到 EA principle：“Using evidence 和 careful reasoning，来 do the most good possible”。它在许多方面都是一个非常有吸引力的 principle。它极其 clear。它具有很强的 orienting 和 meaning-giving 作用，尤其是在它 embedded 于一个 social and organizational context，而这个 context 对如何 do the most good 提出令人信服的 recommendations 时。这些 recommendations 不必是 perfect：它们只需要比你预期自己在大多数其他 community contexts 中能做到的更好。

这个 principle 的部分吸引力在于，它剥夺了 choice。modernity 的一项伟大成就，是给人越来越多的 choice，直到他们可以 choose（看起来）一切[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn14)。但大量的 choice 也令人困惑、难以应对。EA（以及许多 ideologies）的力量，很大一部分就在于剥夺其中许多 choice，并说：不，你有 duty[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn15)，要在世界上 do the most good you can。此外，EA 提供 institutions 和一个 community，帮助指引你如何 do that good。由此，它为你提供 orientation、meaning，以及一个说明你_为何_做自己正在做的事的 narrative。

在 Twitter 上，前 EA Nick Cammarata 发表了下面这条[评论](https://twitter.com/nickcammarata/status/1528005615095799808)；我在与许多 EAs 和前 EAs 的一对一交谈中，都听到过类似说法：

> 在 2016 年初，我内心的声音会 automatically convert 我花掉的所有钱（比如吃晚饭的钱），将其变成一个 fractional “death counter”，计算如果我把钱 donated 给 good charities，本可以在 expectation 中 saved 的 lives 数量。当时，我向大多数 EAs 提起这件事，他们的反应都是：啊，对啊，似乎很合理

或者看看 Twitter 上，一个非 EA 与一个 EA 之间的下面这段[令人惊讶的交流](https://twitter.com/TheDrewRat/status/1525558537467969536)：

> “optimal charity 的 optimal amount 不是 100%”
> 
> “但 good EAs 会把这一点考虑进去”
> 
> “是的，但 bad EAs 会陷入 misery trap”
> 
> “没错，但那不是 EA 的 flaw，而是那些人的 flaw。”

或者看看 Peter Singer 的书《The Most Good You Can Do》中的下面这段话：

> 当 \[pioneering EA\] Julia \[Wise\] 还年轻时，她如此强烈地觉得，自己选择 donate 或不 donate，就意味着另一个人 living 或 dying 的 difference，以至于她决定，自己生孩子会是 immoral 的。孩子会占用她太多时间和金钱。她把自己的决定告诉父亲，他回答说：“听起来，这种生活方式不会让你幸福。”她回应道：“我的 happiness 不是重点。”后来，当她与 \[她的丈夫\] Jeff 在一起时，她意识到父亲是对的。她不生孩子的决定让她痛苦。她与 Jeff 谈了谈，两人决定，他们负担得起养育一个孩子，同时仍然能够 give 很多。能够期待成为母亲，让 Julia 重新对未来感到兴奋。她猜想，与成为一个“broken-down altruist”相比，对生活的 satisfaction 让她对世界更有用。
> 
> 每个人都有 boundaries。如果你发现自己正在做的事让你满怀怨怼，那就该重新考虑了。你能否更积极地看待它？如果不能，综合考虑一切，它真的就是最好的吗？
> 
> …
> 
> Julia 承认自己犯过错误。购物时，她会不断问自己：“我对这份冰激凌的需要，是否和世界上其他地方一位 living in poverty 的女性让孩子 vaccinated 的需要一样强烈？”这让买日用品成为一种令人抓狂的体验，因此，她和 Jeff 决定了接下来六个月要 give away 的数额，然后根据剩余的钱制定 budget。在这个 budget 内，他们把钱视为自己的，可以花在自己身上。现在，Julia 不再省冰激凌的钱，因为正如她对课堂上的学生所说：“冰激凌对我的 happiness 真的很重要。”
> 
> …
> 
> Julia 和 Jeff 决定生孩子，表明他们划定了一条界限：超出这条界限，他们就不会让 maximizing 其 giving 的 goal，阻止他们拥有对自己非常重要的东西。Toby Ord 的伴侣 Bernadette Young 也曾以类似方式描述他们生孩子的决定：“我乐于在一生中 donating 自己 income 的 50 percent，但如果我还选择不生孩子，仅仅是为了把这个数额提高到 55 percent，那么最后这 5 percent 给我带来的代价，就会超过其余所有部分。……我正在决定满足一个重要的 psychological need，并规划一种自己能够长期继续过下去的生活。”无论出于什么原因，无法生孩子都让 Julia 和 Bernadette 深感痛苦，这种体验并不罕见。生孩子无疑既花钱又花时间，但与此相对，Bernadette 指出，effective altruists 可以 reasonably hope，生孩子会 benefit the world。cognitive abilities 和 empathy 之类的 characteristics，都有显著的 inherited component，我们也可以预期，孩子会受到父母在日常生活中秉持和践行的 values 的影响。虽然无法 certainty 地断言，effective altruists 的孩子在一生中会 do more good than harm，但他们这样做的 probability 是 reasonable 的，而这有助于 offset 养育他们的 extra costs。我们可以换一种说法：如果所有关心 do the most good 的人都决定不生孩子，而那些不关心任何其他人的人继续生孩子，我们真的能够期待，几代人之后，这个世界会比那些关心他人的人生了孩子的情况下更好吗？

EA 中普遍存在一种对 the arts 的相关 attitude。Singer 对此直言不讳：你其实无法 justify the arts：

> promoting the arts 能否成为“你能够 do 的 the most good”的一部分？
> 
> 在一个已经 overcome extreme poverty 以及我们目前面临的其他重大问题的世界里，promoting the arts 会是一个 worthy goal。然而，在我们生活的这个世界里，出于将在第 11 章探讨的原因，向 opera houses 和 museums donating，不太可能是在 doing the most good you can。

我听过几位 EAs 说，他们认识多位 EAs，因为觉得自己对世界的 impact 不够大，而变得非常低落，甚至 depressed。作为一个纯粹的 intellectual project，从“use reason 和 evidence 来 figure out 如何在世界上 do the most good”这样的 principle 出发，并尝试把“照料孩子”“享受吃冰激凌”或“engage in 或 support the arts”[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn16)之类的事 derive 为这个 overarching principle 的 special cases，是很吸引人的。但尽管这在 intellectually 层面很有趣，作为生活的直接指引，它却是一个可怕的错误。照料孩子（_等等_）的原因，不是它有助于你 do the most good。而是因为_我们就是绝对应该照料自己的孩子_。art、music 和冰激凌之所以重要，不是因为它们有助于你 do the most good。而是因为我们是人——不是没有灵魂的自动机——我们会以自己并不完全理解的方式，对一些事物作出回应，而这些事物对我们_自身_的 impact，我们既没有、也无法完全理解。

如今，EA 所选择的 pattern 是插入 escape clauses。许多人会谈到，设立一个“warm fuzzies” budget，用于仅仅让自己感觉良好的“ineffective” giving。他们还划出一些 _ad hoc_ extension clauses，例如关于生孩子、预留冰激凌 budget 或晚餐 budget 的条款，等等[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn17)。在我看来，这一切像是频繁到足以表明某处有问题的 special pleading。你从一个看似极具吸引力的 single overarching principle 出发。但现在，你要么接受全部 consequences，让自己痛苦不堪；要么就得开始以个人身份，嫁接上 _ad hoc_ extension clauses。而事实证明，后者本身就让人承受极大的压力。于是，你会看到像 Nick Cammarata 这样深思熟虑的人，为自己的晚餐纠结不已。问题不在晚餐：问题在于 Cammarata 陷入了纠结。或者是 Julia Wise，纠结要不要吃冰激凌——或者要不要生孩子。

这并不奇怪：一边是一个非常 clear、非常 powerful 的 principle，以及 superhuman entities（EA organizations + collective community），它们就如何 do the most good，发出极其 clear 且 compelling 的信息。但人们却是在 individual level 上，努力发现和设定 boundaries。难怪这会让人压力重重。

这对 EA 来说是个非常大的问题。当人们认真对待这样一个 overarching principle 时，结果就是一群压力重重、紧张不安的人，一群担心自己活错了的人。对这种情形的正确 critique，并不是 Singer 提出的那个：它阻止了他们 doing the most good。应有的 critique 是：_这是一种错误的生活方式_。他们需要一个不同的生活 foundation。它或许可以包含那个 principle 的某种 variation，作为一个大得多、也完善得多的 life philosophy 中的_一小部分_。但它必须受到某个或某些其他 principle 的强力制衡；那些 principles 必须具有同样的 clarity 和 force；所有部分如何 fit together，必须清楚明白，从而让“most good” principle 受到其他 principles 的牢固 bounded。而且，这种 balancing 很可能需要（部分地）delegated 给 superhuman institutions，因为要求大多数个人完成它，却不让他们承受巨大压力，实在要求太高了。但如果把“the most good”用作一个 life philosophy 的 foundation tentpole，再往上 _ad hoc_ 嫁接 additional clauses，在我看来，这就是滋生问题的做法。

另一种 solution——我相信，也是许多 EAs 采用的 solution——是某种形式的 weak-EA。Strong-EA 把“do the most good you can do”当作 life philosophy 的 central aspect，极其认真地对待。Weak-EA 则更多地把那个 principle 用作 guidance。Donate 你 income 的 1%。Donate 你 income 的 10%，前提是这不会让你生活困难。认真考虑你的工作对世界的 impact，并参考许多不同的 sources。这些都是值得做的好事！对这种形式的 critique 是：它固然很好，但也很难与许多人早就持有的常见想法区分开来：“好好生活，并努力在世界上 do some good”。正如 Amia Srinivasan 所说[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn18)：

> 但 figures 越 uncertain，calculation 就越不 useful，我们最终也就越依赖对什么值得做的 commonsense understanding。我们真的需要一个 sophisticated model 来告诉我们不应该 deal in subprime mortgages \[/ed: 是的/\]，或者 American prison system 需要 fixing，或者如果能确信自己并非仅仅出于 self-interest，那么投身 electoral politics 或许值得吗？effective altruism 试图 address 的 problem 越 complex——也就是说，它越 deeply 地与作为 political entity 的世界 engages——它的 contribution 就越不 distinctive。Effective altruists 和其他所有人一样，都遇到了世界是 messy 这一事实；而且，和其他所有想让世界变得更好的人一样，他们必须做自己看来 best 的事，却无法最终确定那究竟是什么，也无法保证自己做得对。
> 
> 一旦我们走出 controlled intervention 的 circumscribed realm，model 就无法告诉我们什么很有用的东西；比这更令人担忧的是，它很容易被用来告诉我们恰好想听到的话。
> 
> …
> 
> Effective altruism 接过了 Singer 的 argument 的精神，却使我们免受其 conclusion 的全部冲击……我们受到的鼓励不是把生活降到 subsistence levels，而是从传统的 10 per cent 的 tithe 开始，此后每年再多做一点。因此，effective altruism 避开了针对 utilitarianism 的一个 standard objections：它对我们的要求太多。但这种回避究竟如何成立，并不清楚。MacAskill 告诉我们，effective altruists 和 utilitarians 一样，都 committed to doing the most good possible；但他也告诉我们，只要向 charity 捐了很多钱，享受一种“舒适的生活方式”也没问题。effective altruism 要么像 utilitarianism 一样，要求我们 do the most good possible，要么只是要求我们尝试让事情变得更好。第一种想法是真正 radical 的，要求我们以大多数人无法想象的方式彻底改变日常生活。（Singer 在他最近的书 The Most Good You Can Do 中再次呼吁进行这样的彻底改变，而 Larissa MacFarquhar 的 Strangers Drowning 则是一组响应了这一呼吁的“extreme altruists”的人物画像。）第二种想法——我们尝试让事情变得更好——则为每一种 plausible moral system 和每一个正派的人所共有。如果 effective altruism 只是要让我们在尝试帮助别人时更加 effective，那么很难反对它。但在这种情况下，也很难看出它提供了什么新的 moral insight，更不用说它怎么可能成为我们此后唯一需要的 social movement。

这段摘录中有很多内容我都同意。但我认为，对于 Srinivasan 最后的评论，有一个相当有力的回应：“在这种情况下，也很难看出 \[EA\] 提供了什么新的 moral insight，更不用说它怎么可能成为我们此后唯一需要的 social movement。”如果这只是一个纯粹的 intellectual argument，我会同意她。但 EAs 确实已经付诸行动：他们 created 了真正以这个想法为 _centered_ 的 institutions。这是有价值的，也是一种 innovation。

## 对 EA principle 的 Internal criticisms

让我们再次回到 EA principle：“Effective altruism 意味着 using evidence and reason 来 do the most good possible in the world。”我已经讨论了这个 principle 的 implicit issues 的一些 in-practice symptoms；也讨论了为这个 principle setting boundaries 时的问题。现在让我们转向直接 critique 这个 principle 本身。

许多 issues 不过是人们用来 attack moral utilitarianism 的那些 standard issues。遗憾的是，我远非这些 arguments 的专家。所以，我只非常简短地说说自己的看法：“good”并不是 fungible，因此任何 quantification 都是一种 oversimplification。事实上，不仅仅是 oversimplification：它有时简直是 wrong，而且 badly misleading。当然，在 making tradeoffs 时，这种 quantification 往往是一种 practical convenience；它也可能有助于提出 suggestive（但并非 dispositive）的 moral arguments。但它没有 fundamental status。因此，“increasing good”或“most good”之类的 notions 是有用的便利手段，但将它们视为 fundamental 则是一个严重错误。此外，单一的“the” good 这一 notion 也很可疑。存在许多 plural goods，它们是 fundamentally immeasurable 和 incommensurate 的，而且无法被 combined。

我觉得这些 attacks 很有说服力。作为一种 practical convenience 和 generative tool，utilitarianism 是有用的。但我并不是把它视为关于世界的 fundamental fact 的那种 utilitarian。

（说句题外话：前 UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld 曾说：“全心全意地奉献给一个人，比为大众的救赎而勤奋劳作更高尚。”思考这句话究竟有多少道理，是一件有意思的事。说得委婉些，这并不是一种 EA point of view。但我仍相信，它颇有道理。）

另一个不那么核心、却很引人注意的方面，是这个 principle 中关于“using evidence and reason”的部分。人类对“evidence and reason”的理解一直在变化，偶尔还会发生 sharp jumps。事实上，人类许多最伟大的成就，就是对 evidence and reason 的理解发生了 radical changes。11th century 的 evidence and reason 的 standards 与今天非常不同；我预计，31st century 的 standards 又会非常不同。当然，这一点可以通过一些小修补来处理。或许可以把这个 principle 改成：“以我们目前最好的 evidence and reasoning 的 standards 来 do the most good possible in the world”，以强调我们意识到这些事物确实会变化。

## 杂项

有四个主题，我本想详细讨论，但最后决定将它们留在这份笔记的范围之外。我只想在这里提一下，尽管过于简短的说明很容易被误解，反而可能把问题弄得更混乱。这四个主题确实都需要长篇讨论：

-   **Illegibility：**反对 EA 的一种常见 argument 是，它 undervalues illegible activity。典型的 EA response 是另一种形式的 EA judo，也就是 bureaucrat 的呐喊：让我们把它变得 legible[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn19)！我们只需弄清 early-stage science / 孩子的生日聚会 / 新类型的 sculpture 究竟 do 了多少 good。然而，我们使之变得 legible 的 activity 形式越多，illegibility 的 penumbra 就越会变化、扩大：而许多最深刻的 creative work 和最具 transformative 性的 life changes，正是由身处那片 penumbra 的人做出的[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn20)。在许多类型的工作中，如果你得到的 outcomes 正是你想要的 outcomes——甚至，如果这些 outcomes 是你能够理解的——你就已经错失了一个巨大的机会。根据定义，在 illegibility 的 penumbra 中，“Evidence and reason”开始 break down。我也怀疑，作为一种 basic personality trait，我在那片 illegible penumbra 中最快乐，这正是我一直难以 grokking EA 的原因：它感觉像一门外语，其中有某个 starting assumption，我就是无法理解。反过来，当我和 EAs 谈论 illegibility 时，他们常常像看着我多长了一个脑袋一样看着我。他们把 illegibility 视为需要 conquered 和 minimized 的东西；我则把它视为关于世界如何运作的 fundamental、immovable fact。事实上，你 conquer 的 illegibility 越多，就越有更多 illegibility 涌现，对这种工作的需要也就越大。

-   **“EA-is-a-cult / EA-is-a-religion：”**这些是常见的说法，通常被用作批评攻击的一部分。我认为，人们使用这些说法时往往不是未经思考，就是不够诚实，依赖的是“cult”的贬义联想。确实，EA-the-movement 与 cults 有一些 overlapping features；登山、欣赏 Bob Dylan 的音乐，以及许多其他活动也一样。值得注意的实质部分是：和任何强大、有吸引力、不断发展的 movement 一样，EA 可能吸引一些 charismatic scoundrels，企图利用别人。这是一个真实的问题，也非常值得防范。但与其他任何强大的 ideology 相比，我不认为 EA 特别容易出现这种问题。

-   **Long-termism / x-risk / AI safety：**这需要单独写一份笔记。总体而言，我对 x-risk 方面的工作持积极态度；例如，我很欣赏 Toby Ord 最近讨论这个主题的书。我对目前大多数 AI safety 工作评价不高，不过，有少数人在做出色的工作，也有一些非常有价值的 adjacent work（关于 fairness、interpretability、explainability，_等等_）。

-   **Vibe 和 aesthetics：**一位朋友指出，EA 有一种非常独特而且相当不寻常的 vibe，与许多其他 cultures 很不一样。这似乎既属实，也很有意思。我不确定该如何理解它。aesthetic 也是如此：EA 倾向于一种非常 instrumental 而且独特的 aesthetic。放在 art 的框架中考虑这一点很有意思：从历史上看，以 instrumental 为主的 art approaches 几乎总会产生糟糕的 art。如果能看到一个源自某种 non-instrumental 事物的 EA arts movement，那该多好！

## 总结

EA 是一种鼓舞人心、meaning-giving 的 life philosophy。它鼓励人们与某种 greater good 的 notion 建立紧密联系，为那个 greater good 作出贡献，并让它成为生活的中心。EA-in-practice 已经在世界上做出了数量惊人的 direct good，让人们的生活变得更好。让“如何 do the most good”这一 conversational frame 随时可用，并预先承认它的价值，是件极好的事。EA-in-practice 也为许多人提供了强大的 community、sense of belonging 和 shared values。作为 moral pioneers，EA 正在提供一组令人瞩目的新 public goods。

这一切使 EA 作为一种 life philosophy 很有吸引力：它提供 orientation、meaning，以及一个 clear 而 powerful 的 core，并有 institutions 提供支持。不幸的是，strong-EA 是一种糟糕的 life philosophy，其 boundaries 不完善，可能让人极为痛苦，而且 underserves core needs。EA-in-practice 过于 centralized，过于 focused on absolute advantage；在 providing 某些种类的 private（或 privatizable）good 方面，market 往往做得好得多。不过，在 providing 某些种类的 public good 方面，EA-in-practice 很可能比许多现有 institutions 做得更好。EA 过分依赖 online charisma：围绕 simulation argument、x-risk 和 AI safety 等 topics 的讨论华而不实，却往往 dominate conversation，而不是让更扎实的工作占据中心。（这并不是说这些 topics 没有好的讨论。）EA-in-practice 与现有 systems of power 结盟过深，却很少 question 或 change 它们。Appropriating “effective”这个 term，是聪明的 marketing 和 movement-building，但在 intellectual 层面并不诚实。EA 把 illegibility 看作一个有待 solved 的 problem，而不是一种 fundamental condition。因此，它在某些种类的 creative 和 aesthetic work 上表现不佳。Moral utilitarianism 是一种有用但 limited 的 practical tool，却把有助于 making tradeoffs 的 quantification 误当成了关于世界的 fundamental fact。

我在这些笔记中强烈 criticized 了 EA。但我没有提供一个表述清楚、有力的 alternative。这相当于说，某个人以冰淇淋和巧克力棒为食的饮食并不理想，却没有提供更好的食物；这话也许正确，却不能立即付诸行动。考虑到人们对于强大 meaning-giving system 的巨大 emotional need，我不指望这些笔记能对他们产生多大影响。人们太容易用空泛的说辞把这些 issues 打发掉，或忽略它们，认为只要嫁接一些 exception clauses 就能解决。不过，写这些笔记既帮助我更好地理解了自己为什么不是 EA，也帮助我理解了为什么我认为，EA principle 在经过非常大的 modification 之后，会成为某种更宏大的 life philosophy 中有价值的一部分。但我还不明白那种 life philosophy 是什么。

Initial release: June 2, 2022

This release: June 3, 2022 (minor modifications)

_Long and rough notes on Effective Altruism (EA). Written to help me get to the bottom of several questions: what do I like and think is important about EA? Why do I find the mindset so foreign? Why am I not an EA? And to start me thinking about: what do alternatives to EA look like? The notes are not aimed at effective altruists, though they may perhaps be of interest to EA-adjacent people. Thoughtful, informed comments and corrections welcome (especially detailed, specific corrections!) - see the comment area at the bottom._

_"Using evidence and reason to figure out how to benefit others as much as possible, and taking action on that basis"_: that's the idea at the foundation of the Effective Altruism (EA) ideology and movement[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn1). Over the past two decades it has gone from being an idea batted about by a few moral philosophers to being a core part of the life philosophy of thousands or tens of thousands of people, including several of the world's most powerful and wealthy individuals. These are my rough working notes on EA. The notes are long and quickly written: disorganized rough thinking, not a polished essay.

I wrote the notes for a few reasons. One is purely social: many of my friends have strong opinions on EA (some pro-, some anti-, others more neutral). Another is a sense that EA is important as a social movement and (perhaps) as a set of ideas. It's significant that so many smart, idealistic teenagers and 20-somethings respond so strongly to EA. Many report radically changing their lives: changing careers; changing their day-to-day behavior; committing to give a large proportion of their income to charities they describe as "effective". EAs[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn2) also share a lot of unusual language and ways of seeing the world, much of it adapted from welfare economics and moral philosophy.

It's tempting to dismiss this as all "just" fashion, or as a consequence of the (meteoric) rise in funding to EA. But I don't buy it. Many EAs are extraordinarily sincere, and have found tremendous conviction and meaning in EA. It's doing something very important for them, something well beyond being a niche fashion.

When I first learned about EA, my instinctive and poorly thought through response was fairly negative. I've often half-joked that I'm an ineffective altruist, or a chaotic altruist. I like to describe myself as a mutilitarian, using the Zen Buddhist "mu" as my utility function (i.e., a denial of the idea). And yet upon deeper inspection these are cheap dismissals.

In 2011 an EA friend of mine went under the knife, donating a kidney to a stranger. He explained to me that he:

> came across some stats on how safe it was to donate and it totally changed my picture. I thought, 1/3,000 risk of death in surgery is like sacrificing yourself to save 3,000 people. I want to be the kind of person who'd do that, and you just have to follow these few steps.

I have EA friends who donate a large fraction of their income to charitable causes. In some cases it's _all_ their income above some fairly low (by rich developed world standards) threshold, say $30k. In some cases it seems plausible that their personal donations are responsible for saving dozens of lives, helping lift many people out of poverty, and preventing many debilitating diseases, often in some of the poorest and most underserved parts of the world. Some of those friends have directly helped save many lives. That's a simple sentence, but an extraordinary one, so I'll repeat it: they've directly helped save many lives.

I'm in stunned awe of all this. And feel a little sheepish about my jokes about ineffective altruism, and grateful that EA friends put up with me. I've tried to live a life matching my personal skills and interests to things which are good for the world. I hope I've done some genuine good, while also enjoying my life. But I've never directly saved a life, as far as I know. I do not think I _could_ donate a kidney: it would violate my sense of somatic integrity too much. At a personal level, I love the sincerity and genuine goodness of my EA and near-EA friends. I simply _feel_ more wholesome after spending time with them. I'm often more honest; I'm sometimes kinder or more open-minded. These are all very good things.

What follows, then, is a collection of observations about EA. It's in part an appreciation: to critique EA you must also understand some of what is good about it. And there is a great deal that can be learned from it by other ideologies. But I'll also dig down and try to understand what bothers me about EA, what I think is wrong, and how I think EA might fruitfully be modified.

Something that's missing from the notes: a direct, first-person account of the good that EA does. I have some reflected sense of this from friends, but wish I knew more. It's impossible to genuinely appreciate EA without it. Malaria bed nets, direct cash transfers, de-worming _etc_ aren't abstractions: they are, in fact, an enormous real world event, making a huge difference in the lives of many people. And that's missing here, just due to my ignorance. Try to keep the fact of this in mind as you read; I've tried to do so as I write.

A caution: I make a lot of generalizations about "what EA does". But EA is not monolithic. This makes it difficult to write without inserting lots of qualifiers. I could do it by saying "Most EAs believe", or quoting leading EAs, and so on. I've instead (mostly) opted to use the general language, with the implicit understanding that there are often EAs who disagree with that particular piece. However, I've tried to note when there is widespread disagreement about a point among the EA community.

I began the notes with a widely-used description of EA, taken from philosopher Will MacAskill, one of the founders of EA: "Using evidence and reason to figure out how to benefit others as much as possible, and taking action on that basis". In practice, I've often heard this abbreviated along the lines of: "Using evidence and reason to do the most good possible". I'll usually use the latter as a shorthand for what EA is about, but keeping in mind the longer description. One caveat about both these descriptions: note that they are inherently maximizing, "benefit others _as much_ as possible", "do the _most_ good possible". In fact, many EAs advocate backing away quite a bit from this maximizing frame. As a result, it makes sense to think of different "strengths" of EA, according to how much a person accepts this maximization approach (or not). We'll return to this, as it's a significant issue not settled by the EA community. And when I use "the most good" framing, it's with the implicit caveat that many EAs back off from "most" in practice.

## EA as a source of moral invention[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn3)

I mentioned above my friend who donated a kidney in 2011. The moral philosopher Peter Singer, one of the originators of many ideas in EA, describes his amazement[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn4) at learning (in 2004) the story of Zell Kravinsky, a wealthy real estate investor who had donated almost his entire fortune of $45 million, living on $60 thousand a year. But there's something even more remarkable. At first sight it will look very similar to my friend's kidney donation story above. But it's different in an important way:

> He still did not think he had done enough to help others, so he arranged with a nearby hospital to donate a kidney to a stranger… Quoting scientific studies that show the risk of dying as a result of making a kidney donation to be only 1 in 4,000, he says that not making the donation would have meant he valued his life at 4,000 times that of a stranger, a valuation he finds totally unjustified.

As extraordinary as my friend's generosity was, there is something further still going on here. Kravinsky's act is one of moral imagination, to even consider donating a kidney, and then of moral conviction, to follow through. This is an astonishing act of moral invention: someone (presumably Kravinsky) was the first to both imagine doing this, and then to actually do it. That moral invention then inspired others to do the same. It actually expanded the range of human moral experience, which others can learn from and then emulate. In this sense a person like Kravinsky can be thought of as a moral pioneer or moral psychonaut[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn5), inventing new forms of moral experience.

Of course, such moral pioneers don't only come from EA. Far from it! They are at the foundation of our civilization. Many of my personal heroes are moral pioneers, including the author of the Sermon on the Mount[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn6), the abolitionist movement, the suffragettes and feminist movement, Martin Luther King and other leaders in the civil rights movement. All these (and many more) engaged in acts of moral imagination that expanded the range of moral experience available to the rest of us to emulate. We may not always agree with them: I don't know, for instance, that I agree with Peter Singer's views on the rights of animals. Singer may be wrong on that. But it's valuable nonetheless as an act of moral invention expanding our potential range of moral experience.

One of the things that's interesting about EA is that it has encouraged many moral pioneers: people willing to rethink fundamental moral questions, and (sometimes) to expand the range of our moral experience. Questions they've seriously asked (and in some cases, acted on the answers): "What if animal lives truly mattered?" "What if a life on the other side of the world mattered just as much as that of a child drowning before your eyes?" "What if an intelligent machine's 'life' mattered just as much as a human being's?" "How should we value the life of a human being living a million years from now?" By taking these questions seriously they can expand our moral horizons.

There's a dark flipside to moral pioneering, memorably pointed out by the political philosopher Hannah Arendt in _Eichmann in Jerusalem_, her account of the trial of the Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann. In Arendt's account, the Nazi's were (in some sense) also moral pioneers, inventing new kinds of crime, which then expanded the likely range of future crime:

> Nothing is more pernicious to an understanding of these new crimes, or stands more in the way of the emergence of an international penal code that could take care of them, than the common illusion that the crime of murder and the crime of genocide are essentially the same, and that the latter therefore is “no new crime properly speaking.” The point of the latter is that an altogether different order is broken and an altogether different community is violated… It is in the very nature of things human that every act that has once made its appearance and has been recorded in the history of mankind stays with mankind as a potentiality long after its actuality has become a thing of the past. No punishment has ever possessed enough power of deterrence to prevent the commission of crimes. On the contrary, whatever the punishment, once a specific crime has appeared for the first time, its reappearance is more likely than its initial emergence could ever have been.

Moral reasoning, if taken seriously and acted upon, is of the utmost concern, in part because there is a danger of terrible mistakes. The Nazi example is overly dramatic: for one thing, I find it hard to believe that the originators of Nazi ideas didn't realize that these were deeply evil acts. But a more everyday example, and one which should give any ideology pause, is overly self-righteous people, acting in what they "know" is a good cause, but in fact doing harm. I'm cautiously enthusiastic about EA's moral pioneering. But it is potentially a minefield, something to also be cautious about.

## EA judo: strong critique of any particular "most good" strategy improves EA, it doesn't discredit it

One of the most common lines of "attack" on EA is to disagree with common EA notions of what it means to do the most good. "Are you an EA?" "Oh, those are the people who think you need to give money for malaria bed nets \[or AI safety, or de-worming, /etc etc/\], but that's wrong because \[…\]." Or: "Will MacAskill says that EAs should consider earning-to-give, but that's wrong because \[…\]". Or: "Science and social justice and creativity and \[etc etc etc\] are much harder to measure than things like QALYs, so EAs tend to undervalue or ignore them." Or: "EAs are rather credulous[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn7) about the value of RCTs and meta-analysis, you should instead \[…\]". Or: "Look, you can directly increase QALYs all you want, it won't shift you from a low-growth to a high-growth economy. The two are at different levels of causal abstraction".

These statements may or may not be true. Regardless, none of them is a fundamental critique of EA. Rather, they're examples of EA thinking: you're actually participating in the EA project when you make such comments. EAs argue vociferously all the time about what it means to do the most good. What unites them is that they agree they should "use evidence and reason to figure out how to do the most good"; if you disagree with prevailing EA notions of most good, and have evidence to contribute, then you're providing grist for the mill driving improvement in EA understanding of what is good.

In any case, this kind of "critique" accounts for at least half – probably more – of the external criticism of EA I've heard. Most external critics who think they're critiquing EA are critiquing a mirage. In this sense, EA has a huge surface area which can only be improved by critique, not weakened. I think of the pattern as _EA judo_. And you see it often in discussions with "EA critics". A pleasant, informative example is EA Rob Wiblin [interviewing](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/russ-roberts-effective-altruism-empirical-research-utilitarianism/) Russ Roberts, who presents himself as disagreeing with EA. But through (most of) the interview, Roberts tacitly accepts the basic ideas of EA, while disagreeing with particular instantiations. And Wiblin practices EA judo, over and over, turning it into a very typical EA-type debate over how to do the most good. It's very interesting and both participants are very thoughtful, but it's not really a debate about the merits of EA.

This is, to me, one of the most attractive and powerful features of EA. It makes it very different to most ideologies, which are often rather static. EA is, in some sense, an attempt to do for the question "what is the good?" what science has done for the question "how does the world work?". Instead of providing an answer it is developing a community that aims to continually improve the answer[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn8).

Because of this, it's worth separating EA-in-practice (a social movement) from EA-the-intellectual-project. If you wish to get at fundamental issues, you ultimately need to focus on the latter, not just the former. As I said: many critiques of EA-in-practice are just part of the core engine of improvement. This does not mean, however, that it's not worth spending time critiquing the surface area of EA-in-practice. "By their fruits ye shall know them" holds for intellectual principles, not just people. If a set of principles throws off a lot of rotten fruit, it's a sign of something wrong with the principles, a _reductio ad absurdum_. You've probably heard communists and libertarians defend failed communist and free-market experiments by saying: "It wasn't a true communist / free-market experiment". Sometimes they have a point. But if the pattern persists, if the fundamental principles aren't resilient or need a lot of special pleading, it means those principles have something badly wrong with them.

Put another way: when EA judo is practiced too much, it's worth looking for more fundamental problems. The basic form of EA judo is: "Look, disagreement over what is good does nothing directly to touch EA. Indeed, such disagreement is the engine driving improvement in our notion of what is good." This is perhaps true in some God's-eye, omniscient, in-principle philosopher's sense. But EA community and organizations are subject to fashion and power games and shortcomings and biases, just like every other community and organization. Good intentions alone aren't enough to ensure effective decisions about effectiveness[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn9). And the reason many people are bothered by EA is not that they think it's a bad idea to "do good better". But rather that they doubt the ability of EA institutions and community to live up to the aspirations.

These critiques can come from many directions. From people interested in identity politics I've heard: "Look, many of these EA organizations are being run by powerful white men, reproducing existing power structures, biased toward technocratic capitalism and the status quo, and ignoring many of the things which really matter." From libertarians I've heard: "Look, EA is just leftist collective utilitarianism. It centralizes decision-making too much, and ignores both price signals and the immense power that comes from having lots of people working in their own self-interest, albeit inside a system designed so that self-interest (often) helps everyone collectively[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn10)." From startup people and inventors I've heard: "Aren't EAs just working on public goods? If you want to do the most good, why not work on a startup instead? We can just invent and scale new technology (or new ideas) to improve the world![11](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn11)." From people familiar with the pathologies of aging organizations and communities, I've heard: "Look, any movement which grows rapidly will also start to decay. It will become dominated by ambitious careerists and principal agent problems, and lose the sincerity and agility that characterized the pioneers and early adopters[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn12)"[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn13).

All these critiques have some truth; they also have significant issues. Without getting into those weeds, the immediate point is that they all look like "merely" practical problems, for which EA judo may be practiced: "If we're not doing that right, we shall improve, we simply need you to provide evidence and a better alternative". But the organizational patterns are so strong that these criticisms seem more in-principle to me. Again: if your social movement "works in principle" but practical implementation has too many problems, then it's not really working in principle, either. The quality "we are able to do this effectively in practice" is an important (implicit) in-principle quality.

## "Bad" EAs, caught in a misery trap

Let's go back to the EA principle: "Using evidence and careful reasoning to do the most good possible". It's a very attractive principle in many ways. It's extremely clear. It's highly orienting and meaning-giving, especially if embedded in a social and organizational context that makes convincing recommendations about how to do the most good. Those recommendations don't need to be perfect: they need merely be better than you expect you could do in most other community contexts.

Part of the attraction of the principle is that it takes away choice. One great achievement of modernity is to give people more and more choice, until they get to choose (seemingly) everything[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn14). But vast choice is also bewildering and challenging. Much of the power of EA (and of many ideologies) is to take away much of that choice, saying: no, you have a duty[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn15) to do the most good you can in the world. Furthermore, EA provides institutions and a community which helps guide how you do that good. It thus provides orientation and meaning and a narrative for _why_ you're doing what you're doing.

On Twitter, ex-EA Nick Cammarata made the following [comment](https://twitter.com/nickcammarata/status/1528005615095799808), which I've heard echoed one-on-one with many EAs and ex-EAs:

> my inner voice in early 2016 would automatically convert all money I spent (eg on dinner) to a fractional “death counter” of lives in expectation I could have saved if I’d donated it to good charities. Most EAs I mentioned that to at the time were like ah yeah seems reasonable

Or consider the following [remarkable exchange](https://twitter.com/TheDrewRat/status/1525558537467969536) on Twitter, between a non-EA and an EA:

> "the optimal amount of optimal charity is not 100%"
> 
> "But good EAs take this into account"
> 
> "Yes but bad EAs get caught in a misery trap"
> 
> "True, but that's not a flaw of EA, that's a flaw of those people."

Or consider the following passage in Peter Singer's book "The Most Good You Can Do":

> When \[pioneering EA\] Julia \[Wise\] was young she felt so strongly that her choice to donate or not donate meant the difference between someone else living or dying that she decided it would be immoral for her to have children. They would take too much of her time and money. She told her father of her decision, and he replied. “It doesn’t sound like this lifestyle is going to make you happy,” to which she responded, “My happiness is not the point.” Later, when she was with \[her husband\] Jeff, she realized that her father was right. Her decision not to have a child was making her miserable. She talked to Jeff, and they decided they could afford to raise a child and still give plenty. The fact that Julia could look forward to being a parent renewed her sense of excitement about the future. She suspects that her satisfaction with her life makes her of more use to the world than she would be if she were “a broken-down altruist.”
> 
> Everyone has boundaries. If you find yourself doing something that makes you bitter, it is time to reconsider. Is it possible for you to become more positive about it? If not, is it really for the best, all things considered?
> 
> …
> 
> Julia admits to making mistakes. When shopping, she would constantly ask herself, “Do I need this ice cream as much as a woman living in poverty elsewhere in the world needs to get her child vaccinated?” That made grocery shopping a maddening experience, so she and Jeff made a decision about what they would give away over the next six months and then drew up a budget based on what was left. Within that budget, they regarded the money as theirs, to spend on themselves. Now Julia doesn’t scrimp on ice cream because, as she told the class, “Ice cream is really important to my happiness.”
> 
> …
> 
> Julia’s and Jeff’s decision to have a child shows that they drew a line beyond which they would not let the goal of maximizing their giving prevent them from having something very important to them. Bernadette Young, Toby Ord’s partner, has described their decision to have a child in a similar way: “I’m happy donating 50 percent of my income over my life, but if I also chose not to have a child simply to raise that amount to 55 percent, then that final 5 percent would cost me more than all the rest. … I’m deciding to meet a major psychological need and to plan a life I can continue to live in the long term.” Neither Julia nor Bernadette is unusual in experiencing the inability to have a child—for whatever reason—as deeply distressing. Having a child undoubtedly takes both money and time, but against this, Bernadette points out, effective altruists can reasonably hope that having a child will benefit the world. Both cognitive abilities and characteristics like empathy have a significant inherited component, and we can also expect that children will be influenced by the values their parents hold and practice in their daily lives. Although there can be no certainty that the children of effective altruists will, over their lifetimes, do more good than harm, there is a reasonable probability that they will, and this helps to offset the extra costs of raising them. We can put it another way: If all those who are concerned to do the most good decide not to have children, while those who do not care about anyone else continue to have children, can we really expect that, a few generations on, the world will be a better place than it would have been if those who care about others had had children?

There is a related attitude toward the arts common in EA. Singer is blunt about this: you can't really justify the arts:

> Can promoting the arts be part of “the most good you can do”?
> 
> In a world that had overcome extreme poverty and other major problems that face us now, promoting the arts would be a worthy goal. In the world in which we live, however, for reasons that will be explored in chapter 11, donating to opera houses and museums isn’t likely to be doing the most good you can.

I've heard several EAs say they know multiple EAs who get very down or even depressed because they feel they're not having enough impact on the world. As a purely intellectual project it's fascinating to start from a principle like "use reason and evidence to figure out how do the most good in the world" and try to derive things like "care for children" or "enjoy eating ice cream" or "engage in or support the arts"[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn16) as special cases of the overarching principle. But while that's intellectually interesting, as a direct guide to living it's a terrible mistake. The reason to care for children (_etc_) isn't because it helps you do the most good. It's because _we're absolutely supposed to care for our children_. The reason art and music and ice cream matter aren't because they help you do the most good. It's because we're human beings – not soulless automatons – who respond in ways we don't entirely understand to things whose impact on our _selves_ we do not and cannot fully apprehend.

Now, the pattern that's been chosen by EA has been to insert escape clauses. Many talk about having a "warm fuzzies" budget for "ineffective" giving that simply makes them feel good. And they carve out _ad hoc_ extension clauses like the one about having children or setting aside an ice cream budget or a dinner budget, and so on[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn17). It all seems to me like special pleading at a frequency which suggests something amiss. You've started from a single overarching principle that seems tremendously attractive. But now you've either got to accept all the consequences, and make yourself miserable. Or you have to start, as an individual, grafting on _ad hoc_ extension clauses. And that turns out to be terribly stressful in its own right. You have thoughtful people like Nick Cammarata in a spin over their dinner. It's not the dinner that's the problem: it's the fact that Cammarata is in a spin. Or Julia Wise, deciding whether to have ice cream – or children.

And it's not surprising: on one side you have a very clear, very powerful principle and superhuman entities (EA organizations + the collective community) sending extremely clear and compelling messages about how to do the most good. But it's an individual level at which people are trying to discover and set boundaries. It's no wonder it's stressful.

This is a really big problem for EA. When you have people taking seriously such an overarching principle, you end up with stressed, nervous people, people anxious that they are living wrongly. The correct critique of this situation isn't the one Singer makes: that it prevents them from doing the most good. The critique is that _it is the wrong way to live_. They need a different foundation for their life. It may be that it includes some variation of that principle, as a _small part_ of a much larger and very well developed life philosophy. But it must be sharply tempered by some other principle or principles; those principles must have the same kind of clarity and force; it must be apparent how all the parts fit together, so the "most good" principle is firmly bounded by the other principles. And it may well be that the balancing needs to be (in part) delegated to superhuman institutions, that it's too much to ask of most individuals without causing them tremendous stress. But if "the most good" is used as the foundation tentpole for a life philosophy, onto which you _ad hoc_ graft additional clauses, that seems to me a recipe for problems.

An alternate solution, and the one that has, I believe, been adopted by many EAs, has been a form of weak-EA. Strong-EA takes "do the most good you can do" extremely seriously as a central aspect of a life philosophy. Weak-EA uses that principle more as guidance. Donate 1% of your income. Donate 10% of your income, provided that doesn't cause you hardship. Be thoughtful about the impact your work has on the world, and consult many different sources. These are all good things to do! The critique of this form is that it's fine and good, but also hard to distinguish from the common pre-existing notion many people have, "live well, and try to do some good in the world". As Amia Srinivasan puts it[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn18):

> But the more uncertain the figures, the less useful the calculation, and the more we end up relying on a commonsense understanding of what’s worth doing. Do we really need a sophisticated model to tell us that we shouldn’t deal in subprime mortgages \[/ed: yes/\], or that the American prison system needs fixing, or that it might be worthwhile going into electoral politics if you can be confident you aren’t doing it solely out of self-interest? The more complex the problem effective altruism tries to address – that is, the more deeply it engages with the world as a political entity – the less distinctive its contribution becomes. Effective altruists, like everyone else, come up against the fact that the world is messy, and like everyone else who wants to make it better they must do what strikes them as best, without any final sense of what that might be or any guarantee that they’re getting it right.
> 
> More worrying than the model’s inability to tell us anything very useful once we move outside the circumscribed realm of controlled intervention is its susceptibility to being used to tell us exactly what we want to hear.
> 
> …
> 
> Effective altruism takes up the spirit of Singer’s argument but shields us from the full blast of its conclusion… Instead of downgrading our lives to subsistence levels, we are encouraged to start with the traditional tithe of 10 per cent, then do a bit more each year. Thus effective altruism dodges one of the standard objections to utilitarianism: that it asks too much of us. But it isn’t clear how the dodge is supposed to work. MacAskill tells us that effective altruists – like utilitarians – are committed to doing the most good possible, but he also tells us that it’s OK to enjoy a ‘cushy lifestyle’, so long as you’re donating a lot to charity. Either effective altruism, like utilitarianism, demands that we do the most good possible, or it asks merely that we try to make things better. The first thought is genuinely radical, requiring us to overhaul our daily lives in ways unimaginable to most. (Singer repeats his call for precisely such an overhaul in his recent book The Most Good You Can Do, and Larissa MacFarquhar’s Strangers Drowning is a set of portraits of ‘extreme altruists’ who have answered the call.) The second thought – that we try to make things better – is shared by every plausible moral system and every decent person. If effective altruism is simply in the business of getting us to be more effective when we try to help others, then it’s hard to object to it. But in that case it’s also hard to see what it’s offering in the way of fresh moral insight, still less how it could be the last social movement we’ll ever need.

There's much I agree with in that excerpt. But I think there's a pretty good retort to Srinivasan's final comment: "in that case it's also hard to see what \[EA is\] offering in the way of fresh moral insight, still less how it could be the last social movement we'll ever need." Now, if it were a purely intellectual argument, I'd agree with her. But: the EAs have actually gone and done it: created institutions that are actually _centered_ around the idea. And that's valuable and an innovation.

## Internal criticisms of the EA principle

Let's return again to the EA principle: "Effective altruism means using evidence and reason to do the most good possible in the world." I've discussed some in-practice symptoms of implicit issues with this principle; I've also discussed problems setting boundaries on the principle. Let's shift to directly critique the principle itself.

Many of the issues are just the standard ones people use to attack moral utilitarianism. Unfortunately, I am far from an expert on these arguments. So I'll just very briefly state my own sense: "good" isn't fungible, and so any quantification is an oversimplification. Indeed, not just an oversimplification: it is sometimes downright wrong and badly misleading. Certainly, such quantification is often a practical convenience when making tradeoffs; it may also be useful for making suggestive (but not dispositive) moral arguments. But it has no fundamental status. As a result, notions like "increasing good" or "most good" are useful conveniences, but it's a bad mistake to treat them as fundamental. Furthermore, the notion of a single "the" good is also suspect. There are many plural goods, which are fundamentally immeasurable and incommensurate and cannot be combined.

I find these attacks compelling. As a practical convenience and as a generative tool, utilitarianism is useful. But I'm not a utilitarian as a fundamental fact about the world.

(Tangentially: it is interesting to ponder what truth there is in past UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld's statement that: "It is more noble to give yourself completely to one individual than to labor diligently for the salvation of the masses." This is, to put it mildly, not an EA point of view. And yet I believe it has some considerable truth to it.)

Less centrally, the part of the principle about "using evidence and reason" is striking. There is ongoing change in what humanity means by "evidence and reason", with occasional sharp jumps. Indeed, many of humanity's greatest achievements have been radical changes in what we mean by evidence and reason. 11th century standards of evidence and reason are very different from today's; I expect 31st century standards will be very different again. Of course, this point can be addressed with some minor patching. It can perhaps be dealt with by changing the principle to: "our best current standards of evidence and reasoning to do the most good possible in the world", to emphasize awareness of the fact that these things do change.

## Miscellanea

These are four subjects I'd like to treat at length, but decided to leave as outside the scope of the current notes. I want to just mention them here, at the risk of confusing the issue with a too-easily-misinterpreted brief account. All four subjects really need a lengthy account:

-   **Illegibility:** A common argument against EA is that it undervalues illegible activity. The typical EA response is another form of EA judo, the bureaucrat's war cry: let's make it legible[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn19)! We'll just figure out how much good early-stage science / a children's birthday party / new types of sculpture really do. And yet the more forms of activity we make legible, the more the penumbra of illegibility changes and grows: and much of the deepest creative work and the most transformative life changes are made by people in that penumbra[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/eanotes/#fn20). In many types of work, when the outcomes you get are the outcomes you want – indeed, when they're outcomes you can even understand – you've missed an enormous opportunity. "Evidence and reason" begin to break down, by definition, in the penumbra of illegibility. I also suspect that as a basic personality trait I am happiest in that illegible penumbra, and this is why I've had so much trouble grokking EA: it feels like a foreign language, where there's some starting assumption I just don't get. Conversely, when talking about illegibility with EAs they often look at me like I've grown an extra head. They view illegibility as something to be conquered and minimized; I view it as a fundamental, immovable fact about the way the world works. Indeed, the more illegibility you conquer, the more illegibility springs up, and the greater the need for such work.

-   **"EA-is-a-cult / EA-is-a-religion:"** These are common statements, usually used as part of critical attacks. I believe they're often used either thoughtlessly or disingenuously, relying on the perjorative connotations of "cult". It's true, EA-the-movement does have some overlapping features with cults; so does mountaineering, appreciation of the music of Bob Dylan, and many other activities. The substantive part that is worth paying attention to is: as with any strong, attractive, growing movement, EA may attract charismatic scoundrels looking to take advantage of others. That is a genuine issue. And it's well worth guarding against. But I don't think EA is unusually prone to it when compared to any other strong ideology.

-   **Long-termism / x-risk / AI safety:** This requires a set of notes of its own. I'm broadly positive about work on x-risk in general; I admire, for instance, Toby Ord's recent book about it. I think little of most work being done on AI safety, although there are a few people doing good work, and adjacent work (on fairness, interpretability, explainability, _etc_) that is very valuable.

-   **Vibe and aesthetics:** A friend points out that EA has a very particular and quite unusual vibe, very different from many other cultures. This seems both true and interesting. I'm not sure what to make of it. This is also true of aesthetic: EA tends toward a very instrumental and particular aesthetic. Interesting to consider in the frame of art: historically, primarily instrumental approaches to art nearly always result in bad art. It'd be lovely to see an EA arts movement that sprang from something non-instrumental!

## Summing up

EA is an inspiring meaning-giving life philosophy. It invites people to strongly connect with some notion of a greater good, to contribute to that greater good, and to make it central in their life. EA-in-practice has done a remarkable amount of direct good in the world, making people's lives better. It's excellent to have the conversational frame of "how to do the most good" readily available and presumptively of value. EA-in-practice also provides a strong community and sense of belonging and shared values for many people. As moral pioneers EA is providing a remarkable set of new public goods.

All this makes EA attractive as a life philosophy, providing orientation and meaning and a clear and powerful core, with supporting institutions. Unfortunately, strong-EA is a poor life philosophy, with poor boundaries that may cause great distress to people, and underserves core needs. EA-in-practice is too centralized, too focused on absolute advantage; the market often does a far better job of providing certain kinds of private (or privatizable) good. However, EA-in-practice likely does a better job of providing certain kinds of public good than do many existing institutions. EA relies overmuch on online charisma: flashy but insubstantial discussion of topics like the simulation argument and x-risk and AI safety have a tendency to dominate conversation, rather than more substantial work. (This does not mean there aren't good discussions of such topics.) EA-in-practice is too allied with existing systems of power, and does little to question or change them. Appropriating the term "effective" is clever marketing and movement-building, but intellectually disingenuous. EA views illegibility as a problem to be solved, not as a fundamental condition. Because of this it does poorly on certain kinds of creative and aesthetic work. Moral utilitarianism is a useful but limited practical tool, mistaking quantification that is useful for making tradeoffs with a fundamental fact about the world.

I've strongly criticized EA in these notes. But I haven't provided a clearly and forcefully articulated alternative. It amounts to saying that someone's diet of ice cream and chocolate bars isn't ideal, without providing better food; it may be correct, but isn't immediately actionable. Given the tremendous emotional need people have for a powerful meaning-giving system, I don't expect it to have much impact on those people. It's too easy to arm-wave the issues away, or ignore them as things which can be resolved by grafting some exception clauses on. But writing the notes both helped me better understand why I'm not EA, and also why I think the EA principle would, with very considerable modification, make a valuable part of some larger life philosophy. But I don't yet understand what that life philosophy is.
