## 正文

许多 effective altruists 想要 spread EA ideas。在过去两年担任 [Harvard Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/) 的 co-president 期间，我在这方面积累了一些经验，觉得分享出来可能会有帮助。我的经验仅限于 Harvard Effective Altruism，但其中的 lessons 可以 generalize 到从零开始 creating 其他由志同道合者组成的 groups。

## 口口相传（Word of mouth）

刚接手 HEA 时，我希望我们能通过 word-of-mouth 来 spread——我会找到几个看起来很合适的朋友，让他们和我一样对 HEA 感到兴奋，然后他们会 recruit 自己的朋友，如此循环往复。

但这并不怎么奏效——我们的 members 不够多，无法靠这种 strategy 来 grow quickly。而且，即使人数足够，其中大多数人可能也没有投入或热情到足以 recruit 其他人加入 club 的程度。总的来说，许多通过 word-of-mouth 对 EA 产生兴趣的人，似乎都不如向他们介绍 EA 的那个人热情，因此随着传播相隔的 degrees 增多，人们的热情很快就会减退。

未来几年，或者有更多人参与后，我们完全有可能让 word-of-mouth 更好地发挥作用，但至少在 create 最初的 seed group 时，它并没有多大帮助。

## 亲自招募朋友（Personally recruiting friends）

我曾亲自尝试让好几个朋友对 effective altruism 产生兴趣。起初，效果很差。人们要么只来参加一次 HEA 的 meeting，之后就不再来了，要么一直找借口，直到我不再邀请他们。

然而，最终我的许多朋友确实对 EA 更感兴趣了，而我似乎并没有给予太多鼓励。这让我觉得，在谈论 effective altruism 时表现得“咄咄逼人”，实际上会 counterproductive；相比之下，我不主动找人谈它，但在话题出现时也不羞于提起，并且通常以身作则，让人看到 effective altruism 有多棒、多有趣，这样取得的 results 要好得多。

## 被动可发现性（Passive discoverability）

我们还尝试使用更 passive 的 outreach channels：在年初的“课外活动展”上设立 booth，请 [THINK](http://thehighimpactnetwork.org/) 在其 website 上将我们列为一个 chapter，把我们的 events 列入各种 directories，等等。

我对这些 strategies 相当满意。也许有些出人意料，我们正是通过课外活动展上的 booth 找到了两位最投入的 members，他们如今都在 HEA 的 board 任职。THINK 也为我们带来了几位热情的人，而且从我们的角度看，这相当 low-effort。我们还在 Harvard 面向高中毕业班学生的到访周末期间举办了一场 event，只通过周末开始时发给他们的一本手册进行 advertised，却吸引来了一群出乎意料地 engaged 且 throughtful 的人。

## Philanthropy Fellowship 项目

过去一年，我们的主要 project 是 running 一个名为 [Philanthropy Fellowship](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/01/29/apply/) 的 speaker series。Fellows 通过申请参加与不同 speakers 共进的晚餐，其中一些 speakers 来自 Harvard 校内，但很多来自校外。学期结束时，我们选了一个 project 共同完成：秋季，我们为 GiveWell 的 three top charities 进行 fundraised；春季，我们就让人们 engaged 的最佳方式开展了一项 small randomized controlled trial。（这项 trial 的 Data 将于近期公布。）

虽然我们 accepted 了几乎所有 applicants，但我认为，设置 application 有助于 screening 出那些至少已经对 EA 有些兴趣的 fellows。这也让我们 accepted 的人觉得 fellowship 更 legitimate、更 official。（而且，这还帮助我们 screen out 了几位显然只是为了 promote 自己最喜欢的 organization 才来参与的人。）

这个 fellowship 激发了一些很好的 discussions，并为 fellows 提供了固定的时间和 context，让他们思考 effective altruism，这似乎有助于人们 stay engaged。而学期末的 fundraiser 和 research project 无疑既有趣又有用。

另一方面，尽管我们尽了最大努力，两个学期参加 weekly meetings 的 fellows 人数都出现了 substantial attrition。我认为，随着学期推进，人们越来越忙，一定程度的 attrition 是不可避免的，但我们在这方面显然仍有工作要做。

## 知名演讲者（High-profile speakers）

我们还邀请了几位非常 high-profile 的 speakers。其中最有名的是 philosopher [Peter Singer](https://www.princeton.edu/~psinger/) 和 Skype 的 cofounder [Jaan Tallinn](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaan_Tallinn)。虽然这些 speakers 吸引了大量 audience（Prof. Singer 的听众多到一个 350-seat 的房间都容纳不下），但我的感觉是，大多数 audience 对 HEA 的其他 activities 并不太感兴趣，因此我们可能没有由此获得很多 followers。

这并不是说，我认为这些 talks 不值得举办。邀请 high-profile speakers 为我们的 [philanthropy fellowship](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/01/29/apply/) 增添了 credibility，而后者是一个很好的 outreach tool。这也提高了我们作为一个 group 的声望，并为 core group members 提供了一项值得齐心协力完成的 task。

## 捐赠游戏（Giving Games）

在我们的许多 passive-outreach events 中，我们都开展了 [giving games](http://www.apaththatsclear.com/)：给感兴趣的路人 $1，让他们从 three charities 中选择一家来 donate。我的意思是，我们真的会走上前，向他们递出一张一美元纸币，问他们是否愿意把它 donate 给一家 charity。这种 approach 在让人们 participate 我们的 giving game 并 sign up 我们的 email list 方面极其 effective——等我们公布前文提到的 HEA 本学期的 research 时，也会一并公布一些实际的 statistics。

我对我们所有 giving games 得到的 response 都相当满意。在需要争夺注意力的场合，它们提供了一个 hook，让许多人至少短暂地 engage。显然，通过 giving games 接触到的人中，进一步 involved 的并不多，但这没关系，因为我们通过它们 reached 的每个人所耗费的精力并不多，而且我们还获得了一些有意思的 data，涉及哪些 charities 胜出，以及 presentation 如何 affected 这一结果。

除了上述 setup，我们还在一场面向到访高中毕业班学生的 event 中尝试了另一种 format：participants 先 discussed 这 three options，然后 voted，winner 获得 $20。这同样取得了非常好的效果，激发了一场热烈而深思熟虑的 discussion。

## 我们需要更多办法来让人们保持 engaged

秋季的 philanthropy fellowship 结束后，我们基本上与许多 fellows 失去了联系。我想，如果我们能提供更多让他们继续 involved 的方式，并且更努力地让他们参与那个学期的 events，就会有更多人一直 involved 下去。（我们实际做的，基本上仅限于向他们 advertising 我们的 talks 和其他 events，以及让他们在愿意的情况下来参加春季的 fellowship dinners。）

但很难找到适合 college students 做、又能满足以下所有 desirable criteria 的事情：

1.  能在 extracurricular activity 的范围内完成，每周只需 reasonable time commitment
2.  对 college students 来说，有理由认为是 effective use of time
3.  在 casual observer 看来，像是 college students 对时间的 reasonable use

我们在学期末进行了大量 brainstormed，并想出了一些 candidate activities，因此希望到下学期我们就能解决这个问题。不过，如果早点想到这些就好了，而且我们仍在寻找更令人兴奋的活动。

## 最后的一些想法

我们那些 best-performing outreach methods 有什么共同之处？我猜，它们都在 select for 人们对 effective altruism 的 latent interest。我们做过的最好的 advertising 是 _passive_ 的：我们把 blurbs 放在合适的地方，希望它们能激起人们的 interest。尽管从数量上看，这样做带来的 response 可能更少，但我们吸引到的人对 EA 更 enthused。

相比之下，在做 active outreach 时，我们很容易想要尽可能地_让_ EA 听起来 attractive——例如，夸耀它获得了哪些 high-profile endorsements、有多少 smart people involved，或者它有多 interesting/fun/fulfilling。但实际上，如果人们原本并没有这种 interest，我不确定试图在他们身上 arouse interest 到底有多大用处。

## 原文

Many effective altruists want to spread EA ideas. As co-president of [Harvard Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/) for the last two years, I have some experience in this area that I thought might be helpful to share. My experience is specific to Harvard Effective Altruism, but the lessons generalize to creating other groups of like-minded people from scratch.

## Word of mouth

When I first took over HEA, I hoped that we’d be able to spread by word-of-mouth—that I’d find a few friends who seemed like good fits and get them as excited about HEA as I was, and then they’d recruit their own friends and the cycle would repeat.

But that didn’t work very well—we didn’t have enough members for us to grow quickly using this strategy. And even if we did, most of them probably wouldn’t have been dedicated or enthusiastic enough to recruit other people to the club. In general, it seems like many people who get interested in EA through word-of-mouth show a lower level of enthusiasm than the person they heard about it from, so as you go more degrees away people’s enthusiasm tapers off quickly.

It’s quite possible that in future years or with more people we could make word-of-mouth work better, but at least to create the initial seed group it wasn’t very helpful.

## Personally recruiting friends

I tried personally to get a number of my friends interested in effective altruism. Initially, this worked pretty poorly. People would either come to one meeting of HEA and then stop going, or would continuously make excuses until I stopped asking them.

However, eventually many of my friends did get more interested in EA, seemingly without too much encouragement from me. This leads me to think that being “pushy” about talking about effective altruism is actually counterproductive; I’ve had much better results from not actively talking to people about it, but not being shy about mentioning it when it comes up, and generally showing by example to how awesome and fun effective altruism is.

## Passive discoverability

We also tried using more passive outreach channels: we set up a booth at the “extracurriculars fair” at the beginning of the year, asked [THINK](http://thehighimpactnetwork.org/) to list us as a chapter on their website, had our events listed in various directories, and so on.

I’m quite happy with these strategies. Perhaps surprisingly, our booth at the extracurriculars fair is how we found two of our most dedicated members, who both now sit on HEA’s board. THINK also sent a few enthusiastic people to us, and was fairly low-effort from our point of view. And an event we held during Harvard’s visit weekend for high school seniors, which was advertised only through a handbook given to them at the start of the weekend, attracted a surprisingly engaged and throughtful group.

## The Philanthropy Fellowship

Our main project for the past year was running a speaker series called the [Philanthropy Fellowship](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/01/29/apply/). Fellows applied to attend dinners with various speakers, some from within Harvard but many from outside. At the end of the semester we picked a project to do as a group: in the fall we fundraised for GiveWell’s three top charities, and in the spring we did a small randomized controlled trial on the best ways to get people engaged. (Data for the trial will be forthcoming shortly.)

Although we accepted almost all applicants, I think that having an application was helpful in screening for fellows who were at least somewhat interested in EA already. It also made the fellowship feel more legitimate and official to those we accepted. (And it also helped us screen out a few people who were obviously participating only to promote their favorite organization.)

The fellowship sparked some good discussions and provided a regular time and context for fellows to think about effective altruism, which seemed to help people stay engaged. And the end-of-term fundraiser and research project were definitely fun and useful.

On the other hand, both semesters saw substantial attrition in the number of fellows attending weekly meetings, despite our best efforts. I think some attrition is inevitable since people get busier over the semester, but we certainly still have work to do in that regard.

## High-profile speakers

We also brought in a few very high-profile speakers. The most famous were philosopher [Peter Singer](https://www.princeton.edu/~psinger/) and Skype cofounder [Jaan Tallinn](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaan_Tallinn). While these speakers drew large audiences (Prof. Singer overflowed a 350-seat room), my sense was that most of the audience wasn’t very interested in the rest of HEA’s activities, so we may not have gained very many followers from it.

That’s not to say that I think the talks weren’t worth it. Having high-profile speakers lent credibility to our [philanthropy fellowship](http://harvardea.org/news/2014/01/29/apply/), which was a good outreach tool. It also gave us more cachet as a group and provided a good task for the core group members to rally around accomplishing.

## Giving Games

At many of our passive-outreach events, we ran [giving games](http://www.apaththatsclear.com/) in which we presented interested passers-by with $1 to donate to a choice of three charities. By that, I mean that we literally walked up, held out a dollar bill to them, and asked if they’d like to donate it to a charity. This approach was incredibly effective at getting people to participate in our giving game and sign up for our email list—we’ll have some actual statistics up when we publish HEA’s research for the semester, mentioned above.

I’m pretty pleased with the response at all of our giving games. In situations where we were competing for attention they provided a hook that got a lot of people to engage at least briefly. Obviously, not very many people from the giving games got involved further, but that was OK, since we didn’t spend very much effort per person we reached with them, and we also got some interesting data on which charities won and how presentation affected it.

In addition to the setup described above, we tried a different format during an event for visiting high school seniors, in which the participants discussed the three options and then voted, with the winner receiving $20. This also turned out remarkably well, inspiring a lively and thoughtful discussion.

## We need more ways to keep people engaged

After the fall philanthropy fellowship ended, we essentially lost touch with many of the fellows. I think a lot more of them would have stayed involved if we’d had more ways for them to do so, and if we’d worked harder to include them in the semester’s events. (What we actually did was basically limited to advertising our talks and other events to them and letting them come to the spring fellowship dinners if they wanted.)

But it’s hard to find things for college students to do that satisfy all of the following desirable criteria:

1.  can be done in the context of an extracurricular activity with a reasonable time commitment per week
2.  are plausibly an effective use of time for college students
3.  look like a reasonable use of time for college students to a casual observer

We brainstormed a lot at the end of the semester and came up with some candidate activities, so hopefully we’ll have solved this problem next semester. But it would have been helpful to have thought of them earlier, and we’re still looking for more exciting ones.

## Closing thoughts

What’s the common thread among our best-performing outreach methods? I’m guessing it’s that they select for latent interest in effective altruism. The best advertising we did was _passive_: we put blurbs in the right places and hoped they piqued peoples interest. Although this probably resulted in a smaller response by the numbers, the people we got were more enthused about EA.

By contrast, our temptation when doing active outreach is to _make_ EA sound as attractive as possible—for instance by bragging about high-profile endorsements, how many smart people are involved, or how interesting/fun/fulfilling it is. But actually, I’m not sure how useful it is to try to arouse interest in people when it’s not already there.
