## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

2023年11月29日

Vitalik Buterin 最近发表了一篇[深思熟虑的文章](https://vitalik.eth.limo/general/2023/11/27/techno_optimism.html)，阐述了他对于 technology 和 techno-optimism 的一些总体立场。下面是我就 Buterin 的文章随手写下的几则笔记，目的是帮助自己更深入地理解它。这些确实是随手写下的笔记：如果我花的时间更多，笔记反而会更简短。如果你对 technology 对 humanity 的 impact 感兴趣，我强烈建议你仔细阅读他的文章。

Buterin 的文章阐明了一种对于 technology 的广泛立场，将许多通常被认为处于 tension 之中的 ideas 进行了 synthesizes。它并不只是以下某一种主张：“stop”、“slow down”、“go faster”、“maximize profit”、“maximize equity and fairness”、“maximize safety”、“reduce growth”、“eliminate inequality”，或其他任何常见说法。它尝试 harness market forces，却不将其奉为圭臬；它认真看待 safety 和 equity，却不认为它们是唯一值得争取的 goals；它理解 technology 所具有的让 humanity 变得更好的巨大 power，却不接受愚蠢的 technological utopianiasm[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn1)。换句话说：Buterin 的 synthesis 将许多通常被认为处于 opposition 之中的 elements 结合了起来。由此，他深思熟虑地阐明了认真看待 differential technology development 意味着什么。人们完全可以有理由地不同意文章中的许多内容，但它比许多类似的 visions 深入得多，考虑也周全得多。虽然我对其中许多观点相当赞同，但我的笔记自然也会部分着重于分歧之处。

下面进入详细笔记！

> 我相信，得益于 radically transformative technology，未来会比现在光明得多；我也相信 humans 和 humanity。我拒绝这样一种 mentality：我们所应努力做到的最好结果，就是让世界与今天大致相同，只是少一些 greed，多一些 public healthcare。不过，我认为，不仅 magnitude 重要，direction 也重要。某些 types 的 technology 比其他 types 的 technology 能够可靠得多地让世界变得更好。某些 types 的 technlogy 如果得到 developed，就可能 mitigate 其他 types 的 technology 的 negative impacts。世界对 tech development 的某些 directions 存在 over-indexes，而对另一些则存在 under-indexes。我们需要 active human intention，来 choose 我们想要的 directions，因为“maximize profit”这一 formula 不会自动将我们带到那里……我们在 technology 方面的 rapid advances 很可能会成为二十一世纪最重要的 social issue，因此，仔细思考它们十分重要。

1.  我很喜欢 Buterin 文章的一点是：它不是一种 totalizing vision。这一点在开头就已经显现。Totalizing visions 作为简单的 models of principle，往往很有吸引力（甚至有用），但在实践中往往表现得糟糕透顶。从 Marxism-Leninism 到 e/acc 的 techno-capitalism，一系列 ideologies 都有这一点令我不喜欢。相比之下，Buterin 的文章是在 manages tensions。它没有 e/acc 或 AI doomerism 那样的 simplicity，但在我看来，它为 optimism 提供了合理得多的 frame。我尤其留意到他所说的：“‘maximizing profit’这一 formula 不会”必然带来 tech development 中良好的 investment allocation。那么，我们需要怎样的 market alterations？Buterin 无论在此处还是在文章后面，都没有真正说明，而是将其留作一个关键问题。不过，可以参照 price of carbon 之类的事物，以及 supplying public goods 这一 genral problem——事实上，safety 和 security 往往就是这种 undersupplied public goods 的例子[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn2)。
2.  我很喜欢的一个问题是：technology _为什么_能如此有力地帮助我们过上更好的生活？这个问题非常简单，但我听过（或自己想到过）的所有回答往往都相当浅薄，而且是 question-begging。Technology 是一种逐渐利用相同的 resources 做到更多事情的方式，是真正的 free lunch[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn3)：它确确实实能够 creates wealth from nothing！这份 free lunch 从何而来？它的 upper limits 是什么？我们知道一些非常基本的东西——例如 Bekenstein bound 和 Einstein's mass-energy relation。但对于 technology 的 ultimate limits，我们并没有很好的 theories。人们有时倾向于给出 doctrinaire 的回答——想想 Julian Simon 的书《The Ultimate Resource》，人们有时会搬出它来表示 innovation 是 unbounded——但尽管这种观点有其吸引力，它仍然是 doctrine，而不是经过周全思考的回答。话虽如此，尽管 limits to growth 确实**存在**，它们也远远超出了 Malthus、Ehrlich 和 Club of Rome 这些 pessimists 曾经的设想。
3.  许多 progress 并不是由 technology 产生的，而是由 social ideas 产生的——新的 norms、新的 forms of organization，等等。Voting、civil rights、suffrage 等等——它们是 human civilization 有史以来最大的一些 increments。Technology 有时 helps（有时也 hinders）这些 norms 的 development——它往往会 creates abundance，从而帮助促成这样的 social advances。Buterin 当然明白这一点，但我想把它说得更明确些。
4.  关于我如何思考这里讨论的所有问题，先交代一些总体背景 framing：我相信，向 posthumanity 的 transition 是 likely-but-not-certain 会成为 21st century 最重要的事件。它不会只是单一的 posthumanity，而会是 plurality 的 posthumanities。因此，重大问题是如何避免把这件事搞砸。请注意，这种 framing 并没有 discount climate change、AI safety、inequality 等事物的 challenge；相反，我把它们看作这个问题的早期 manifestations。

> 在某些圈子里，downplay technology 的 benefits，并主要将其视为 dystopia 和 risk 的来源，是很常见的。在过去半个世纪里，这往往源于 environmental concerns，或源于这样的担忧：benefits 只会 accrue 给 rich，而他们会 entrench 自己对 poor 的 power……我担心我们已经矫枉过正，许多人忽略了论证的另一面：technology 的 benefits 真的大得惊人，在那些我们能够 measure 的 axes 上，好处远远压过坏处，而哪怕 delay 十年，其 costs 也高得不可思议……“limits to growth”这一 thesis 是在 1970s 提出的 idea，主张 growing population 和 industry 最终会 deplete Earth 的 limited resources，结果却启发了中国的 one child policy 和印度大规模的 forced sterilizations。在更早的时代，对 overpopulation 的担忧曾被用来 justify mass murder。而这些自 1798 年起就被提出的 ideas，有着被 proven wrong 的漫长历史。正是出于这类原因，作为一个出发点，我对主张 slow down technology 或 human progress 的论点深感不安。

1.  我同样对主张 slow down technology 的论点感到不安。
2.  将 technology 的 progress 与 human progress 等同起来是一个错误。我当然不认为 Buterin 相信这一点，但它在某些圈子里普遍得令人惊讶。有时，progress 可能意味着 giving up 或 restricting technology——想想 asbestos、lead-in-oil、CFCs、DDT，以及许多其他例子。我们 humans 实际上相当擅长决定 give up dangerous technologies，即使它们与极其 profitable 的 industries 相关联。
3.  Buterin 举出了相当多关于 technology 的 benefits 的例子，既有详细描述，也有 statistical aggregates（increased longevity、child mortality 的 decreases，等等）。我不会引述这些例子，但当然：这就是 technology 的奇迹。这些例子的一个问题是，这样的 statistical aggregates 很难让人直接感受到。但当你与失去过孩子、或遭受过 polio、malnutrition 的人交谈，并意识到数百万乃至数十亿人都曾有过这样的处境，而其 proportion 却一直在下降、下降、再下降时，你就会深切地感受到它们。总体而言：Buterin 如此倚重 Our World in Data，令我印象深刻。这是一个极好的资源，却深受 underfunding 之苦。你可以[在这里捐款](https://ourworldindata.org/donate)（我与团队中的几位成员关系友好，也捐过款，但没有直接关联）。
4.  我们极有可能仍处在 technology 的非常早期。今天，有些出生在 poor households 的孩子，其生活在某些——虽然远非所有——重要方面比 John Rockafellar 还好。这简直不可思议；如果我们能够妥善应对未来，我相信，_mutatis mutandis_，同样的事情未来还会发生。我非常希望，一个在 2100 年出生的 poor child，能够拥有充满 opportunity 和 plenty 的生活，在许多方面超过今天 billionaire 所能拥有的生活。并且_不会_ threatening Earth 的 environment 或其他人。这听上去或许不切实际，但我相信 historical precedent 很有力。
5.  对于 environmental 和 inequality concerns，_应当_极其认真地对待。这或许是（？）我与 Buterin 意见不同的一点。我喜欢、却没有好答案的一个问题是：distribution of wealth _应该_是什么样的？inequality 达到多大程度，就开始需要 rebalancing？这些问题是 underspecified 的，但我注意到，当我向人们提出这个问题时，那些更感到不自在的人会以这种 underspecification 为借口，避免回答。我觉得这种不自在很有意思。

> AI 与其他 tech 是 fundamentally different 的，值得格外谨慎对待
> 
> 我见过的许多关于 AI 的轻蔑看法，都来自这样一种视角：它“不过是另一种 technology”……但对于 AI 是什么，还有一种不同的思考方式：它是一种新的 mind，正在 rapidly gaining in intelligence，而且很有可能 overtaking humans' mental faculties，成为这个星球上新的 apex species。属于那个 category 的事物要少得多……感觉我们正在走进一片远没有那么多人走过的领域。
> 
> AI 出了问题后，可能以一种（几乎是）最糟糕的方式让世界变得更坏：它可能真的 cause human extinction。这是一个 extreme claim……这些全都是 speculative hypothesis，而对于涉及 complex multi-step stories 的 speculative hypotheses，我们都应当保持警惕。但即使你不担心真正的 extinction，也还有其他令人害怕的理由……即使我们 survive，一个有 superintelligent AI 的未来，会是我们想生活其中的世界吗？许多现代 science fiction 是 dystopian 的，把 AI 描绘得很糟糕。即使不是 science-fiction，试图 identify possible AI futures 的探索，也常常给出相当不吸引人的答案。因此，我四处询问这样一个问题：有没有某种描绘，无论是不是 science fiction，呈现了一个包含 superintelligent AI、而我们又愿意生活其中的未来……。
> 
> \[关于 Iain Banks 的 /Culture/\] 然而，进一步看，就会发现一个问题：似乎完全是 Minds 在掌权，而 humans 在故事中的唯一 role，就是充当 Minds 的棋子，替它们 performing tasks。
> 
> 引自 Gavin Leech 的《Against the Culture》：
> 
> humans 并不是 protagonists。即使这些书看起来有一位 human protagonist，在做重大而严肃的事情，他们实际上也是某个 AI 的 agent。（Zakalwe 是仅有的几个 exceptions 之一，因为他能做 Minds 不愿做的 immoral things。）“Culture 中的 Minds 不需要 humans，但 humans 却需要被需要。”（我认为，只有少数 humans 需要被需要——或者说，只有少数人的这种需要强烈到足以让他们放弃诸多舒适。大多数人并不生活在这样的尺度上。这仍然是一项很好的 critique。）

1.  今天就已经如此：我们所有人都在相当大程度上受到远比自身庞大的 causal forces 的 dominated。William Whyte 的《Organization Man》精彩地阐明了这一点。我们对 systemic forces 作出 respond，而它们的 causal origin 往往位于远比任何 human being 庞大的 systems 之中。有时，把那些 systems 看作 agency 所在之处，似乎是合理的；有时则不然，但那个 level 似乎仍然是 causal origin。事实上，或许有些讽刺的是，支持不受限制地开展 AGI 和 ASI 工作的最常见 arguments 之一，就是这在 historically 是 inevitable 的。任何用“但如果 China 抢先做出来呢？”来为自己的 AGI 工作辩护的人，都已经把大量 agency 让给了一个 superhuman entity。
2.  说个题外话：我觉得一个有趣的问题，是 narrative plausibility 与 correctness 之间的 relationship。好故事往往是 false 的；true 的东西往往成不了好故事。在 science fiction 里写 faster-than-light travel、perpetual motion machines，或者能 solve the halting problem（或 equivalents）的 machines 等等，都很容易。而许多 true 的事情，却很难写得令人信服，甚至很难写得让人理解——例如，relativity 的许多 consequences 就是如此。每当看到描绘假想的未来 space stations 或未来城市中田园诗般生活的画面，我总会想到这一点：那些描绘也许是 narratively plausible 的，但在许多情况下，我怀疑它们 violate 了 human psychology 或 economics 中相当 basic 的 principles。你能把它画出来，并不意味着你就能过上那样的生活。总之，我只是想指出，我希望自己能更好地理解 story 与 truth 之间的 relationship。我有一个坚定的信念：nature 比我们更有想象力。那些 govern the universe 的 deep principles，似乎比我们贫乏的 minds 编出来的故事更令人惊讶、更有趣，除非那些故事本身就 rooted in 那些 principles。我并不十分理解这究竟是_为什么_，我只是在观察到，事实确实如此。

> 一个向 superintelligent machine 发号施令的 human，其 intelligent 程度会远低于那台 machine，而且能 access 的 information 也更少。在一个存在任何程度 competition 的 universe 中，那些 humans 退居次要位置的 civilizations，会 outperform 那些 humans 顽固坚持 control 的 civilizations。此外，computers 自己也可能夺取 control。要理解为什么，请想象你在法律上确实是一个八岁孩子的 slave。如果你能和这个孩子长时间交谈，你觉得自己能说服他签下一张让你获得自由的纸吗？我没有做过这个 experiment，但我的直觉回答是非常肯定的。因此，总的来说，humans 成为宠物似乎是一个很难 escape 的 attractor。

这似乎是 plausible 的，但我认为其中遗漏了一些 caveats。Chess computers 远胜于 humans；chess 在许多方面都非常 competitive；然而 humans 仍掌握着 control，而且迄今为止，chess computers 没有表现出接管 world championship 的迹象。我认为，引文那一段确实包含一些核心 truth，但还需要进一步推敲，而且其 scope 比看起来更窄。

> 在二十世纪，modern transportation technology 使 limitations of distance 对 centralized power 的 constraint 远弱于从前；1940s 那些庞大的 totalitarian empires，部分就是由此而来。在二十一世纪，scalable information gathering 和 automation 可能意味着，attention 也将不再构成 constraint。government 的 natural limits 完全消失，可能带来可怕的 consequences。Digital authoritarianism 已经兴起了十年，而 surveillance technology 也已经为 authoritarian governments 提供了强大的新 strategies，用来 crack down on opposition：先让 protests 发生，事后再 detect 并悄悄追查 participants。更广泛地说，我基本的恐惧是：让 OpenAI 能够用 500 名 employees 为超过一亿 customers 提供服务的同类 managerial technologies，也会让一个 500 人的 political elite，甚至一个 5 人的 board，对整个国家维持铁腕统治。有了 modern surveillance 来 collect information，再由 modern AI 来 interpret 它，可能就无处可藏了。

这有些离题，但一个让我十分困扰的问题是，为什么 Apple-Google-Microsoft 没有比现在糟糕得多。它们都在 operating 非同寻常的 panopticons[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn4)。当我停下来想想，自己的生活有多少处在 Apple 的注视之下，就会感到恐惧。我不会假装，它们利用对我生活的了解所做的一切都是善意的；但它们也不是 Stasi，尽管它们对我的了解，远超过 Stasi 对 East Germans 的了解。

对此，有一些惯常的反驳——比如“Apple 没有 state force”，以及“哦，你是自愿与 Apple 达成那个 agreement 的，不喜欢就换别的”。但这些回答在许多（并非所有）方面都相当缺乏说服力。Apple 确实不能把我关进监狱，但它们可以合法地做一些相当糟糕的事情。而且，虽然原则上我可以换用其他东西，或者 opt out，但实际上，我们 society 的运作方式，使得完全 opt out of AGM 非常困难[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn5)。总之，我只是想记下，这对我来说仍是一个持续存在的谜。

> 如今，“human in the loop”对 dictator 发动 wars、或在国内 oppress 其 citizens 的 power，构成了重要的 check……如果 armies 都是 robots，这种 check 就会彻底消失。一个 dictator 可能在 10 PM 喝醉，在 11 PM 因为 twitter 上有人对自己刻薄而发怒，而一支 robotic invasion fleet 就能在午夜之前越过 border，向邻国的 civilians 和 infrastructure 倾泻地狱般的炮火。

关于发动 wars 的这一点，我此前基本忽略了：这样的 actions 在历史上始终需要（某种程度的）political legitimacy，尤其是持续数月或数年时。consent of the governed（大体上？）是一件好事，即使对于 dictators 的 actions，它也施加着相当大的影响。这一点可能会消失。

接下来是 Buterin 对其所提出的 philosophy of technology 的核心 summary。顺便说一句，虽然我不会把它复制粘贴过来，但其中包含一幅有趣的 solarpunk-style 图像。

> d/acc：Defensive（或 decentralization，或 differential）acceleration……纵观各处，我看到太多拯救世界的 plans，都涉及把 extreme 且 opaque 的 power 交给一小群人，然后希望他们能明智地使用它。因此，我发现自己被另一种 philosophy 所吸引：它对如何应对 risks 有详细的想法，但它寻求 create 和 maintain 一个更 democratic 的世界，并试图避免把 centralization 当作解决我们问题的首选 solution。这种 philosophy 的范围也远比 AI 广泛；我认为，即使在 AI risk concerns 最终被证明大体缺乏依据的世界里，它也很适用。我将把这种 philosophy 称为 d/acc。这里的“d”可以代表许多东西；尤其是 defense、decentralization、democracy 和 differential。首先，从 defense 的角度理解它，然后我们就可以看看，这如何与其他 interpretations 联系起来。

1.  这个 labelling 很有趣：“d/acc”。所有存续下来的 visions，都得到了很好的 labelled。它们也往往会聚拢 tribes；我不太确定自己对此怎么看——tribe 往往是良好思考的敌人。一个很有意思的问题是，它们在多大程度上_是_ tribal affiliations，又在多大程度上是 coherent philosophies，或 areas of study。
2.  让我印象深刻的是，近来（以及不那么近来）的许多 visions，全都是 technocratic capitalism 的不同版本：EA、e/acc、d/acc、techno-optimism、progress studies 都属于此列。正如 Neil Postman 所说（当时谈的是 C. P. Snow 的《Two Cultures》）：“这场 argument 并不是发生在 humanists 与 scientists 之间，而是发生在 technology 与其他所有人之间。”H. G. Wells 对 Eloi 和 Morlocks 的构想，也相当精彩地表达了这一点。人们可能从 technology 中获益巨大，但最终也可能 _subject_ 于 technology。Technology 是施加于他们的东西，无论多么有益，都不是他们自己做的东西。所有那些 visions 都有这一共同点。不过，Buterin 的 vision 有所不同，它远没有那么 High Modernist。

> Defense-favoring worlds 有助于 healthy 和 democratic governance thrive 思考 technology 的 macro consequences 的一种 frame，是考察 defense vs offense 的 balance。有些 technologies 让 attack 他人变得更容易，这里的 attack 取其广义：做出违背他们的 interests、让他们觉得需要 react 的事情。另一些则让 defend 变得更容易，甚至可以在不依赖 large centralized actors 的情况下 defend。
> 
> 一个 defense-favoring world 是更好的世界，原因有很多。首先当然是 safety 的 direct benefit：更少的人 die，更少的 economic value 被 destroyed，更少的时间被浪费在 conflict 上。不过，较少得到重视的是，一个 defense-favoring world 更容易让 healthier、更 open、更 freedom-respecting 的 governance forms thrive。
> 
> 一个明显的 example 是 Switzerland。Switzerland 常被认为是现实世界中最接近 classical-liberal governance utopia 的地方……
> 
> 事实上，Switzerland 和那些 island states 所共有的 voluntary trade 的 ease 与 involuntary invasion 的 difficulty，两者结合起来似乎是 human flourishing 的理想条件。
> 
> 我在为 Ethereum ecosystem 内的 quadratic funding experiments 提供建议时，发现了一个相关的 phenomenon：具体来说，就是 Gitcoin Grants funding rounds。在 round 4 中，一场小风波出现了，因为某些 highest-earning recipients 是 Twitter influencers，而他们的 contributions 在一些人看来是 positive，在另一些人看来却是 negative。我自己对这个 phenomenon 的 interpretation 是，其中存在一种 imbalance：quadratic funding 允许你 signal 你认为某样东西是一种 public good，却没有提供办法来 signal 某样东西是一种 public bad。在 extreme 情况下，一个 fully neutral quadratic funding system 会 fund 一场 war 的双方。因此，在 round 5，我提议 Gitcoin 应当纳入 negative contributions：你支付 $1，来 reduce 某个给定 project receives 的 amount of money（并 implicitly redistribute 给所有其他 projects）。结果是：很多人讨厌它。
> 
> 在我看来，这像是一个更大 pattern 的缩影：creating decentralized governance mechanisms 来处理 negative externalities，从社会层面看是一个非常困难的 problem。decentralized governance 出错时，人们首先想到的 example 是 mob justice，这是有原因的。human psychology 中的某些东西，使得 responding to negatives 比 responding to positives 棘手得多，也更有可能严重出错。这也是为什么，即便在其他方面 highly democratic 的 organizations 中，如何 respond to negatives 的 decisions 也往往被交给一个 centralized board。
> 
> 在许多情况下，这个难题是 "freedom" 这个 concept 如此有价值的深层原因之一。如果有人说了冒犯你的话，或者有一种你觉得恶心的生活方式，你感受到的痛苦和厌恶都是真实的，你甚至可能觉得挨上一拳也比接触这些东西好受一些。但试图就哪些 offense 和 disgust 是 socially actionable 达成一致，可能带来远多于以下做法的 costs 和 dangers：只是提醒自己，某些怪人和混蛋，是我们生活在 free society 中所付出的代价。

1.  我想知道，innovation/creativity 与 defense 之间是如何 trade off 的？
2.  我很喜欢自己几次去 Switzerland 的经历，对这个国家的印象也非常好。
3.  过去一年左右，Crypto 遭到了很多嘲讽。但这个 example 很好地展示了它的 power：它正在使针对 designing markets 的不同方式的 experiments（以及 learning）成为可能。
4.  Market shorts 是关于 negative externalities 的这一点的另一个 example。我不知道对 shorts 的 negative view 是否不可避免：shorts 可是 "The Big Short" 中的英雄。但这需要 Michael Lewis 运用大量 narrative skill。我认为 Buterin 说得对，human nature 总体上厌恶那些把东西拿走的人。
5.  关于 "freedom" 这一 notion 的 value，这一点说得极好，而我对此的理解仍然很浅。
6.  Buterin 列出了一长串 defensive technology 的具体 examples，我在这里（大多）不会 quote——当然，这份 list 可以变得 arbitrarily long！但作为对广义 "defensive technology" 的唤起，这份 list 极其有趣，也很能启发思考。

> 把 technologies classifying 为 offensive、defensive 或 neutral，难免会有 imperfections。就像 "freedom" 一样，人们可以争论 social-democratic government policies 究竟是通过 levying heavy taxes 和 coercing employers 来 decrease freedom，还是通过 reducing 普通人担忧多种 risks 的需要来 increase freedom；对于 "defense"，同样有些 technologies 可能落在 spectrum 的两侧。Nuclear weapons 是 offense-favoring，但 nuclear power 是 human-flourishing-favoring，而且是 offense-defense-neutral。

在我看来，这种 classification 将非常棘手。许多拥有 nuclear weapons 的 countries，在描述它们时都说得仿佛它们是用于 defensive use 的。这个 problem 看起来似乎很容易 solve："如果拥有 technology T 的 group A 能够 kill country B 中的人，那么 T 就是 offensive"；"如果拥有 technology T 的 group A 能够 save 人们的 lives，那么它就是 defensive"。但 group A 可以 **withold** 一种 vaccine（比如说），从而 kill group B 中的人。这会让 vaccine 变成 offensive 吗？显然不会。但我认为 classification 的困难程度出人意料，因为大多数 technologies 在某种意义上都是 dual use。"这符合谁的 interest？" 这个问题往往更清楚，尽管即便如此也很棘手。我们在 misinformation、conspiracy theories 等事情上就能看到这一点："我把这些告诉他们，是为了他们好"，是人们常常真心相信的。而今天的 misinformation，可能成为明天的 conventional wisdom。

> 那么，superintelligence 的前进道路有哪些？
> 
> 以上这些都很好，也可能让下个世纪的世界变得更加 harmonious、safer 和 freer。然而，它尚未处理那个无法回避的重大问题：superintelligent AI。
> 
> 许多担忧 AI 的人所建议的默认前进道路，实质上通向一个 minimal AI world government。其 Near-term versions 包括建立一个 "multinational AGI consortium"（"MAGIC"）的 proposal。这样的 consortium 如果得到建立，并且 succeeds at 其 creating superintelligent AI 的 goals，就会自然走上成为 de-facto minimal world government 的道路。从更长期来看，还有像 "pivotal act" theory 这样的 ideas：我们 create 一个 AI，由它 performs 一个 single one-time act，把世界 rearranges 成一个 game；从那一刻起，humans 仍然掌权，但 game board 会以某种方式更加 defense-favoring，也更加适合 human flourishing。
> 
> 到目前为止，我看到的主要 practical issue 是，人们似乎并不真正信任任何一个具体 governance mechanism，愿意把 build 这种东西的 power 交给它。看看我最近 Twitter polls 的 results，这个事实就很鲜明了：我问人们，是更愿意看到 AI 被一个拥有十年 head-start 的 single entity monopolized，还是让所有人的 AI 都 delayed 十年……在九个 cases 中，九个 cases 的 majority 都宁愿让 highly advanced AI 干脆 delayed 十年，也不愿它被一个 single group monopolized，无论这个 group 是 corporation、government 还是 multinational body。在九个 cases 中，有七个 cases 的 delay 以至少二比一胜出。对于任何致力于 AI regulation 的人来说，这似乎都是一个需要理解的重要事实。

我相当认同这种看法。但是：这里存在很大的 selection effect。Buterin 的 Twitter followers 往往是对 state-run currency 的 decentralized alternatives 感兴趣的人。他们反对 centralized solutions，当然是意料之中的事。

> 反对“让一个 global org 来做 AI，并把它的 governance 做得非常非常好”这条路线的人，主要偏好的 approach 是 polytheistic AI：有意确保有许多人和 companies 在 developing 许多 AIs，从而让它们之中没有任何一个变得远比其他的 powerful。按照这个 theory，这样一来，即使 AIs 变得 superintelligent，我们也能 retain 一种 balance of power。
> 
> 这种 philosophy 很有趣，但我在 Ethereum ecosystem 中试图确保 "polytheism" 的经历，确实让我担忧这是一种 inherently unstable equilibrium……我在 Ethereum 内的经历，也与从整个更广阔世界获得的认识相呼应，其中许多 markets 已被证明是 natural monopolies。当 superintelligent AIs independently of humans 地 acting 时，局面就更加 unstable。由于 recursive self-improvement，最强的 AI 可能很快领先，而一旦 AIs 比 humans 更 powerful，就没有任何 force 能把局面推回 balance。

在我看来，这很可能是对的，而且很重要。我对 "multiple poles" arguments 非常怀疑。Information economies 往往是 winner-take-all 或 winner-take-most 的。

> 一条 happy path：与 AIs merge？
> 
> 我最近听到的另一种 option，是不再那么侧重于把 AI 视为与 humans 分离的东西，而是更多关注那些 enhance human cognition、而非 replacing 它的 tools……像 \[brain-computer interface\] 这样的 directions 有时会引起担忧，一部分是因为它们是 irreversible，另一部分是因为它们可能让 powerful people 比我们其他人拥有更多 advantages。Brain-computer interfaces 尤其有 dangers——毕竟，我们说的是切实地对人们的 minds 进行 reading and writing。正是这些 concerns，让我认为，理想情况下，这条道路上的 leading role 应由一个 security-focused open-source movement 来承担，而不是 closed 和 proprietary corporations 以及 venture capital funds。此外，与紧密依附于 humans 的 augmentations 相比，在 independently from humans 地 operate 的 superintelligent AIs 身上，所有这些 issues 都更严重。由于谁能、谁不能 use ChatGPT 的 limitations，"enhanced" 与 "unenhanced" 之间的 divide 在今天就已经存在。
> 
> 如果我们想要一个既 superintelligent 又是 "human" 的未来，一个 human beings 不只是 pets、而是真正 retain 对世界有意义的 agency 的未来，那么类似这样的东西似乎就是最自然的 option。也有充分的 arguments 说明，为什么这可能是一条 safer AI alignment path：通过在 decision-making 的每一步都纳入 human feedback，我们 reduce 了把 high-level planning responsibility offload 给 AI 本身的 incentive，从而 reduce 了 AI 自行做出与 humanity's values totally unaligned 的事情的 chance。

我想，我比 Buterin 更 pessimistic。在我看来，这又回到了 technocracy-versus-everyone-else。**Technology 把对 humanity 的 control centralizes 到 toolmakers 手中**。即使对于 open source approaches，这也成立。有人可能会说：“任何人都能 hack on \[open source project\]，并按照自己的意愿 modify 它，这多好啊”；但在实践中，世界 population 中只有极小一部分原则上能做到这一点，而 resource limitations 意味着，世界 population 中实际上能做到这一点的人更少。原则上，我的 browser 是 open source，我可以 modify 它；但实际上，我只能接受给我的东西。technology 对我们生活的 influences 越大——我们变得越 posthuman——这一点就越成立。Humans 最终成为 toolmakers 的 techno-chattel，无论后者多么仁慈。

不过，一个有趣的反驳是，也许这正是我们能 _从_ ASI 那里拿回来的东西。让你的 ASI rewrite 你的 browser，使它能够 _等等_。这是一种有趣的可能性。我猜我们 _确实会_ 拿回一部分，但不会那么多。不只是 implementing，甚至仅仅是 _specifying_ 你想要什么，都是一种深具 technical 性质、需要高超技艺的 skill，任何 Movie Director 或 Art Director 都能告诉你这一点。因此，这将 limit 我们 use ASI 去 modify 那些 defines 我们的 extended selves 的 technology。

不过，我确实还没有足够认真地看待这个想法。下面这段话颇有说服力：

> 支持这个方向的另一个 argument 是，比起只是高喊“pause AI”，却没有配套信息提供 alternative path forward，它可能更为 socially palatable。这将需要一次 philosophical shift，摆脱当前这种心态：触及 humans 的 tech advancements 是 dangerous，而与 humans 分离的 advancements 则是 by-default safe。但它有一个巨大的 countervailing benefit：它让 developers 有事可做。如今，AI safety movement 向 AI developers 传达的主要信息似乎是“你们应该停下来”。人们可以从事 alignment research，但如今这缺乏 economic incentives。相比之下，e/acc 常见的那句“你现在这样就已经是英雄了”极具吸引力，这是可以理解的。一种 d/acc 信息或许能够胜出，它说的是：“你们应该 build，而且要 build profitable things，但要更加 selective 和 intentional，确保自己正在 building 的东西有助于你们和 humanity thrive”。

我不确定这是否足够有说服力。这主要是一个 values-based argument，试图处理一个 public goods problem（safety 和 security）。而这些东西依然是 undersupplied。回到我先前的问题：market 究竟需要_如何_被 modified？我认为，Covid-19 的 case 很有启发性：vaccines 的 makers 几乎没有 captured 任何 value。如果他们获得了 10x 的 profits，世界实际上会过得更好（long run）。

我很喜欢 Buterin 的 conclusion：

> 我们是最明亮的星
> 
> 我热爱 technology，因为 technology expands human potential。一万年前，我们能够 build 一些 hand tools，改变一小块土地上生长的 plants，并 build basic houses。如今，我们能够 build 800-meter-tall towers，把全部 recorded human knowledge 都 store 在一个我们能够拿在自己土地里的 device 中，在全球范围内 communicate instantly，让自己的 lifespan double，并且过上幸福而充实的生活，不必害怕最好的朋友经常因 disease 突然死去。
> 
> 我相信这些事情是深深美好的，而且，把 humanity 的 reach 进一步 expanding 到 planets 和 stars 也是深深美好的，因为我相信 humanity 是深深美好的。在某些圈子里，对此持怀疑态度是一种风尚：voluntary human extinction movement 主张，如果 humans 根本不存在，Earth 会过得更好；还有更多人希望，在未来几个世纪里，能够见到这个世界光明的 human beings 的数量要少得多。人们经常主张 humans 是坏的，因为我们欺骗、偷窃，参与 colonialism 和 war，并且 mistreat 和 annihilate 其他 species。我对这种思路的回应只有一个简单问题：跟什么相比？
> 
> 是的，human beings 常常很刻薄，但我们展现善意和仁慈、为共同利益携手合作的时候要多得多。即便在 wars 期间，我们也常常注意 protect civilians——当然，做得还远远不够，但也远比 2000 年前多。下一个世纪很可能带来广泛可得的 non-animal-based meat，从而 eliminating 如今可以正当地归咎于 human beings 的最大 moral catastrophe。Non-human animals 并非如此。没有哪只猫会出于 ethical principle，采取一种完全拒绝吃老鼠的生活方式。Sun 每年都在 growing brighter，而据预计，大约十亿年后，这将使 Earth 热到无法 sustain life。Sun 甚至会想一想它将造成的 genocide 吗？
> 
> 因此，我坚信，在我们的 universe 中我们所知、所见的一切事物里，我们 humans 是最明亮的星。在我们所知的事物中，唯有我们，即便做得不完美，有时也会真诚地努力关心“the good”，并 adjust 自己的 behavior，以便更好地服务于它。二十亿年后，如果 Earth 或 universe 的任何部分依然承载着 Earthly life 的美丽，那将是 space travel 和 geoengineering 这样的人类 artifices 促成的。
> 
> 我们需要 build，并且 accelerate。但有一个非常切实的问题需要提出：我们正在 accelerating towards 的究竟是什么？21st century 很可能是 humanity 的 pivotal century，是我们未来数千年的命运得到决定的世纪。我们会落入某个无法逃脱的陷阱，还是会找到一条通向未来的道路，在那里保有我们的 freedom 和 agency？这些是充满挑战的问题。但我期待着见证并参与我们 species 为寻找答案而付出的宏大集体努力。

我认为这是一个出色的 vision。再说一遍我的一些担忧：我认为它低估了 technocracy 已经夺走 agency 的程度；它没有 specify market 要_如何_被 altered；我认为它低估了 posthumanities 的 plurality，而这种 plurality 也许能造就一颗更明亮的星；我认为它低估了日常的人类行动、理想和经验。我想更具体地思考一些问题：例如，OpenAI、Anthropic、Alphabet、VC、EA，以及更广义的 philanthropy 等 institutions，究竟在多大程度上确实与这个 vision aligned？它们在哪些方面 differ？如何才能让它们进入 alignment？现有 institutions 中，又有哪些确实是 aligned 的？

**更新，2023年11月30日：** 对自己如何回应 Buterin 的笔记又思考了一番后，我发现自己有一个重大的总体担忧，它让我觉得，d/acc 还需要一些 creative extension，我才愿意接受它。在[先前的笔记](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html)中，我讨论过我们过去应对 threatening technologies 的方式：

> $$…$$ 我们使用 governance feedback loops 来应对这一点。举个例子：在成长过程中，我一直以为 humanity 很可能会因以下问题遭受巨大痛苦：acid rain；ozone hole；nuclear war；peak oil；climate change；等等。但在每一种情况下，humanity 都以非凡的 ingenuity 作出了回应——Vienna and Montreal protocols 等帮助我们应对了 ozone hole；许多想法（Swanson's Law、carbon taxes 等）正在帮助我们应对 climate change；诸如此类。threat 越严重，response 就越强烈；结果便是一种 co-evolution：我们在 develop destructive technologies 的同时，也在寻找 governing 它们并 blunting 其 negative effects 的方式。

这个 governance feedback loop 是 distinguishing 我们与其他 animals、并使我们的 extinction 更不可能发生的一个重要因素。你可以把 Buterin 的 vision 看作是在寻找 strengthen governance feedback loop 的方法。当然，我非常赞同把这作为一个 goal，并认为这是一个很好的 call-to-action。我的担忧是，这还远远不够。具体来说，我曾在别处讨论过 [recipes for ruin](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html)，也就是那些 civlization-threatening、trivial-to-make 的 technologies：我们如今因太过无知而无法 make 它们，但它们是 inherently ungovernable 的，除非依靠 ignorance。如果这样的 recipes for ruin 不存在——如果我们生活在一个 fundamentally friendly world 中——那么在我看来，d/acc 非常明智。但如果这样的 recipes for ruin 确实存在——如果我们生活在 Nick Bostrom 所称的 vulnerable world 中——那么在我看来，d/acc 是个好的开端，但它_并没有_处理一个核心 long-run issue：如何避免 developing 关于那些 recipes for ruin 的 knowledge。在我看来，这种 ignorance 很可能是应对这类 recipes 唯一明智的 governance strategy。

总结一下我从 Buterin 的文章中得到的主要收获：

1.  d/acc 为 technology 未来的 development 提供了一个有吸引力且 optimistic 的 vision，试图 manage 许多 genuine tensions，而许多其他 visions 并不承认、或者否认这些 tensions。它也 harnesses 了 humanity 许多最好的 institutional、social 和 technological inventions。
2.  d/acc 留下了一个关于 differential technology development 的 fundamental question：如何让 safety 和 security 变得 relatively more profitable。二者（在某些方面）都具有 public goods 的 character，而且一直以来都是 undersupplied。
3.  d/acc 从根本上 grounded in technocratic capitalism，并没有处理那些对 technology 拥有 agency 的人与不拥有这种 agency 的人之间日益扩大的 division。我想知道 ASI 将如何改变这种 division。
4.  d/acc 没有处理 inherently ungovernable technologies 的 (non)-development，也就是：如果我们生活在一个 fundamentally vulnerable world 中，如何避免 recipes for civilizational ruin。
5.  我还不太理解，d/acc 与许多现有 issues 具体如何 relates——例如与特定 companies 的关系，或者与 wealth inequality 之类 issues 的关系，等等。这些值得进一步思考。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

[Astera Institute](https://astera.org/)

November 29, 2023

Vitalik Buterin has recently published a [thoughtful essay](https://vitalik.eth.limo/general/2023/11/27/techno_optimism.html) describing some of his general stance toward technology and techno-optimism. The following are a few quick notes on Buterin's essay, written to help me process it more deeply. They really are quick notes: if I'd taken more time, my notes would be briefer. If you're interested in the impact of technology on humanity then I strongly suggest reading his essay carefully.

Buterin's essay articulates a broad stance toward technology that synthesizes many ideas often thought to be in tension. It is not solely one of: "stop", "slow down", "go faster", "maximize profit", "maximize equity and fairness", "maximize safety", "reduce growth", "eliminate inequality", or any of the other usual messages. It attempts to harness market forces, without lionizing them; it takes both safety and equity seriously, without regarding them as the sole goals worth fighting for; it understands the enormous power technology has to make humanity better, but does not embrace a foolish technological utopianiasm[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn1). Put another way: Buterin's synthesis combines many elements often taken to be in opposition. The result is a thoughtful articulation of what it means to take differential technology development seriously. One can reasonably disagree with much in the essay, but it's much deeper and better thought through than many other visions in a similar vein. While I have many points of considerable sympathy, naturally my notes focus in part on points of difference.

Onto the detailed notes!

> I believe in a future that is vastly brighter than the present thanks to radically transformative technology, and I believe in humans and humanity. I reject the mentality that the best we should try to do is to keep the world roughly the same as today but with less greed and more public healthcare. However, I think that not just magnitude but also direction matters. There are certain types of technology that much more reliably make the world better than other types of technology. There are certain types of technlogy that could, if developed, mitigate the negative impacts of other types of technology. The world over-indexes on some directions of tech development, and under-indexes on others. We need active human intention to choose the directions that we want, as the formula of "maximize profit" will not arrive at them automatically… Our rapid advances in technology are likely going to be the most important social issue in the twenty first century, and so it's important to think about them carefully.

1.  Something I really enjoy about Buterin's essay: it's not a totalizing vision. This is already visible in this opening. Totalizing visions are often attractive (and even useful) as simple models of principle, but they tend to work horribly in practice. This is something I find myself disliking about a range of ideologies from Marxism-Leninism to e/acc's techno-capitalism. By contrast, Buterin's essay manages tensions. This doesn't have the simplicity of either e/acc or AI doomerism, but it seems to me a much more sensible frame for optimism. I particularly note his comment that "the formula of 'maximizing profit' will not" necessarily result in a good allocation of investment in tech development. What, then, are the market alterations we need? Buterin doesn't really specify, either now or later in the essay, leaving it as a key problem. But, c.f. things like the price of carbon, and the genral problem of supplying public goods – indeed, safety and security are often examples of such undersupplied public goods[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn2).
2.  A question that I really enjoy is _why_ technology does so much to help us live better lives. It's a very simple question, but all the answers I've heard (or have thought of) tend to be rather shallow and question-begging. Technology is a way of gradually doing more with the same resources, an actual free lunch[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/vbto/index.html#fn3): it really, genuinely, creates wealth from nothing! Where is that free lunch coming from? What are its upper limits? We know a few very basic things – things like the Bekenstein bound and Einstein's mass-energy relation. But we don't have very good theories on the ultimate limits of technology. People sometimes tend toward doctrinaire answers – consider Julian Simon's book "The Ultimate Resource", which is sometimes trotted out to mean that innovation is unbounded – but while this point of view has its attractions, it's doctrine, not a well thought through answer. That said, while there really **are** limits to growth, they're far, far beyond what pessimists like Malthus, Ehrlich, and the Club of Rome ever thought.
3.  Much progress is produced not by technology, but by social ideas – new norms, new forms of organization, and so on. Voting, civil rights, suffrage, and so on – these were some of the biggest increments ever in human civilization. Technology sometimes helps (and sometimes hinders) the development of such norms – often it creates abundance that helps enable such social advances. Buterin certainly understands this, but I wanted to make it more explicit.
4.  Some broad background framing about how I think about all the issues under discussion: I believe the transition to posthumanity is likely-but-not-certain to be the most important occurrence of the 21st century. It won't just be a single posthumanity, but a plurality of posthumanities. And so the big question is how to avoid messing it up. Note that this framing does not discount the challenge of things like climate change, AI safety, inequality, and others; rather, I view them as early manifestations of this problem.

> In some circles, it is common to downplay the benefits of technology, and see it primarily as a source of dystopia and risk. In earlier eras, concerns about overpopulation were used to justify mass murder. And those ideas, argued since 1798, have a long history of being proven wrong. 1.  I am similarly uneasy about arguments to slow down technology.
2.  It's a mistake to equate progress in technology to human progress. I certainly don't think Buterin believes this, but it's surprisingly common in certain circles. Sometimes, progress may consist of giving up or restricting technology – think of asbestos, lead-in-oil, CFCs, DDT, and many other examples. We humans are actually quite good at deciding to give up dangerous technologies, even when those are associated to enormously profitable industries.
3.  Buterin gives quite a number of examples of the benefits of technology, both in detail, and in statistical aggregates (increased longevity, decreases in child mortality, and so on). I won't quote them, but of course: this is the miracle of technology. One problem with the examples is that such statistical aggregates are hard to directly feel. But you feel them keenly when you talk with someone who has lost a child, or who suffered polio, or malnutrition, and realize that millions or billions of people have been in this position, but the proportion has just kept going down, down, down. In general: I'm struck by how much Buterin leans on Our World in Data. It's a fantastic resource, and suffers badly from underfunding. You can [donate here](https://ourworldindata.org/donate) (I'm friendly with several members of the team, and have donated, but have no direct connection).
4.  It seems extremely likely that we're still in the very early days of technology. There are children born into poor households today whose lives in some-though-far-from-all important respects are better than John Rockafellar. This is simply incredible; if we manage the future well, I believe the same thing will apply, _mutatis mutandis_ in the future. I very much hope that a poor child born in 2100 will have a life of opportunity and plenty that in many ways exceeds what is available to a billionaire today. _Without_ threatening the Earth's environment or other people. I find that discomfort interesting.

But even if you're not worried about literal extinction, there are other reasons to be scared as well… Even if we survive, is a superintelligent AI future a world we want to live in? A lot of modern science fiction is dystopian, and paints AI in a bad light. Even non-science-fiction attempts to identify possible AI futures often give quite unappealing answers. > \[On Iain Banks' /Culture/\] When we look deeper, however, there is a problem: it seems like the Minds are completely in charge, and humans' only role in the stories is to act as pawns of Minds, performing tasks on their behalf.

> Quoting from Gavin Leech's "Against the Culture":
> 
> The humans are not the protagonists. Even when the books seem to have a human protagonist, doing large serious things, they are actually the agent of an AI. (Zakalwe is one of the only exceptions, because he can do immoral things the Minds don't want to.) "The Minds in the Culture don't need the humans, and yet the humans need to be needed." (I think only a small number of humans need to be needed - or, only a small number of them need it enough to forgo the many comforts. Most people do not live on this scale. 1.  This is already true today: we are all to a considerable extent dominated by causal forces much larger than ourselves. William Whyte's "Organization Man" made the point beautifully. We respond to systemic forces whose causal origin is often in systems much larger than any human being. Sometimes it seems reasonable to think of those systems as being where agency resides; sometimes it does not, but that level still seems the causal origin. Indeed, perhaps ironically, one of the most common arguments in favour of unfettered work on AGI and ASI is that it historically inevitable. Anyone justifying their work on AGI with the argument "but what if China did it first?" has already ceded a lot of agency to a superhuman entity.
2.  Tangentially: a question I find interesting is the relationship between narrative plausibility and correctness. Good story is often false; what is true often makes for poor story. It's easy to write science fiction with faster-than-light travel or perpetual motion machines or machines that can solve the halting problem (or equivalents), and so on. And many true things are hard to write convincingly or even in a way that is comprehensible – this is true of many consequences of relativity, for instance. I always think of this when I see idyllic pictures of life on hypothetical future space stations or in future cities: those depictions may be narratively plausible, but in many cases I suspect they violate fairly basic principles of human psychology or economics. Just because you can paint it doesn't mean you can live it. Anyways, this is just to note that I wish I understood better the relationship between story and truth. I will say one strong conviction I have: nature is more imaginative than we. The deep principles that govern the universe seem to be more surprising and interesting than the stories our poor minds come up with, except insofar as those stories are rooted in those principles. I don't understand _why_ this is the case very well, I'm just observing that it is.

> A human giving orders to a superintelligent machine would be far less intelligent than the machine, and it would have access to less information. This is plausible, but I believe there's some caveats omitted. Chess computers are vastly better than humans; chess is very competitive in many ways; and yet humans are still in control, and so far the chess computers show no signs of taking over the world championship. I think the quoted paragraph does have some core truth to it, but it needs more work, and has a narrower scope than it seems.

> In the twentieth century, modern transportation technology made limitations of distance a much weaker constraint on centralized power than before; the great totalitarian empires of the 1940s were in part a result. In the twenty first, scalable information gathering and automation may mean that attention will no longer be a constraint either. The consequences of natural limits to government disappearing entirely could be dire. Digital authoritarianism has been on the rise for a decade, and surveillance technology has already given authoritarian governments powerful new strategies to crack down on opposition: let the protests happen, but then detect and quietly go after the participants after the fact. More generally, my basic fear is that the same kinds of managerial technologies that allow OpenAI to serve over a hundred million customers with 500 employees will also allow a 500-person political elite, or even a 5-person board, to maintain an iron fist over an entire country. With modern surveillance to collect information, and modern AI to interpret it, there may be no place to hide.

It's tangential, but a question that bothers me a lot is why Apple-Google-Microsoft aren't a lot worse than they are. Anyway, I just want to note this as an ongoing puzzle to me.

> Today, the "human in the loop" serves as an important check on a dictator's power to start wars, or to oppress its citizens internally… If armies are robots, this check disappears completely. I'd largely missed this point about starting wars: such actions have historically always required (some degree of) political legitimacy, especially when continued over months or years. On to Buterin's core summary of the philosophy of technology he's proposing. Incidentally, while I won't cut-and-paste it, it includes an interesting solarpunk-style image.

> d/acc: Defensive (or decentralization, or differential) acceleration… Across the board, I see far too many plans to save the world that involve giving a small group of people extreme and opaque power and hoping that they use it wisely. And so I find myself drawn to a different philosophy, one that has detailed ideas for how to deal with risks, but which seeks to create and maintain a more democratic world and tries to avoid centralization as the go-to solution to our problems. This philosophy also goes quite a bit broader than AI, and I would argue that it applies well even in worlds where AI risk concerns turn out to be largely unfounded. I will refer to this philosophy by the name of d/acc. The "d" here can stand for many things; particularly, defense, decentralization, democracy and differential. First, think of it about defense, and then we can see how this ties into the other interpretations.

1.  The labelling is interesting: "d/acc". All the visions which survive have been well labelled. They've also tended to accrue tribes; I don't know quite what I think about that – tribe is often the enemy of good thought. One thing which is fascinating is the extent to why they _are_ tribal affiliations versus coherent philosophies versus areas of study.
2.  I am struck by how many recent (and not-so-recent) visions are all versions of technocratic capitalism: EA, e/acc, d/acc, techno-optimism, progress studies all fit. As Neil Postman put it (in reference to C. P. Snow's "Two Cultures"): "the argument is not between humanists and scientists but between technology and everybody else". This point was also made rather well by H. G. Wells in his vision of the Eloi and the Morlocks. People may benefit enormously from technology, but they may also end up _subject_ to technology. Technology is something done to them, no matter how beneficial, not something they do. This is common across all those visions. Still, Buterin's vision is different in being much less High Modernist.

> Defense-favoring worlds help healthy and democratic governance thrive One frame to think about the macro consequences of technology is to look at the balance of defense vs offense. Some technologies make it easier to attack others, in the broad sense of the term: do things that go against their interests, that they feel the need to react to. Others make it easier to defend, and even defend without reliance on large centralized actors.
> 
> A defense-favoring world is a better world, for many reasons. First of course is the direct benefit of safety: fewer people die, less economic value gets destroyed, less time is wasted on conflict. What is less appreciated though is that a defense-favoring world makes it easier for healthier, more open and more freedom-respecting forms of governance to thrive.
> 
> An obvious example of this is Switzerland. Switzerland is often considered to be the closest thing the real world has to a classical-liberal governance utopia…
> 
> In fact, the combination of ease of voluntary trade and difficulty of involuntary invasion, common to both Switzerland and the island states, seems ideal for human flourishing.
> 
> I discovered a related phenomenon when advising quadratic funding experiments within the Ethereum ecosystem: specifically the Gitcoin Grants funding rounds. In round 4, a mini-scandal arose when some of the highest-earning recipients were Twitter influencers, whose contributions are viewed by some as positive and others as negative. My own interpretation of this phenomenon was that there is an imbalance: quadratic funding allows you to signal that you think something is a public good, but it gives no way to signal that something is a public bad. In the extreme, a fully neutral quadratic funding system would fund both sides of a war. And so for round 5, I proposed that Gitcoin should include negative contributions: you pay $1 to reduce the amount of money that a given project receives (and implicitly redistribute it to all other projects). The result: lots of people hated it.
> 
> This seemed to me to be a microcosm of a bigger pattern: creating decentralized governance mechanisms to deal with negative externalities is socially a very hard problem. There is a reason why the go-to example of decentralized governance going wrong is mob justice. There is something about human psychology that makes responding to negatives much more tricky, and much more likely to go very wrong, than responding to positives. And this is a reason why even in otherwise highly democratic organizations, decisions of how to respond to negatives are often left to a centralized board.
> 
> In many cases, this conundrum is one of the deep reasons why the concept of "freedom" is so valuable. If someone says something that offends you, or has a lifestyle that you consider disgusting, the pain and disgust that you feel is real, and you may even find it less bad to be physically punched than to be exposed to such things. But trying to agree on what kinds of offense and disgust are socially actionable can have far more costs and dangers than simply reminding ourselves that certain kinds of weirdos and jerks are the price we pay for living in a free society.

1.  I wonder how innovation/creativity versus defense trade off?
2.  I've loved my visits to Switzerland, and have a very positive impression of the country.
3.  Crypto has come in for a lot of derision over the past year or so. But this example nicely illustrates its power: it's enabling experiments (and learning) about different ways of designing markets.
4.  Market shorts are another example of the point about negative externalities. I don't know that the negative view of shorts is inevitable: the shorts were the heroes of "The Big Short". But that took a lot of narrative skill on Michael Lewis's part. I think Buterin is right about human nature in general abhorring people who take stuff away.
5.  This point about the value of the notion of "freedom" is marvelous, and something I understand only very poorly.
6.  Buterin gives a long list of specific examples of defensive technology, which I (mostly) won't quote here – the list could be made arbitrarily long, of course! But the list is extremely interesting and stimulating as an evocation of "defensive technology", broadly construed.

> There are inevitably going to be imperfections in classifying technologies as offensive, defensive or neutral. Like with "freedom", where one can debate whether social-democratic government policies decrease freedom by levying heavy taxes and coercing employers or increase freedom by reducing average people's need to worry about many kinds of risks, with "defense" too there are some technologies that could fall on both sides of the spectrum. Nuclear weapons are offense-favoring, but nuclear power is human-flourishing-favoring and offense-defense-neutral.

This classification is going to be very tricky, in my opinion. Many countries with nuclear weapons described them as though they were for defensive use. It might seem as though you can solve this problem pretty easily: "if group A with technology T can kill people in country B, then T is offensive"; "if group A with technology T can save people's lives, then it is defensive". But group A can **withold** a vaccine (say) and kill people in group B. Does that make the vaccine offensive? Obviously not. But I think classification is surprisingly difficult, because most technologies are in some sense dual use. The question "whose interest is this in?" is often clearer, although even there it's tricky. We see this with misinformation, conspiracy theories and so on: "I'm telling them this for their own good" is something people often genuinely believe. And today's misinformation can be tomorrow's conventional wisdom.

> So what are the paths forward for superintelligence?
> 
> The above is all well and good, and could make the world a much more harmonious, safer and freer place for the next century. However, it does not yet address the big elephant in the room: superintelligent AI.
> 
> The default path forward suggested by many of those who worry about AI essentially leads to a minimal AI world government. Near-term versions of this include a proposal for a "multinational AGI consortium" ("MAGIC"). Such a consortium, if it gets established and succeeds at its goals of creating superintelligent AI, would have a natural path to becoming a de-facto minimal world government. Longer-term, there are ideas like the "pivotal act" theory: we create an AI that performs a single one-time act which rearranges the world into a game where from that point forward humans are still in charge, but where the game board is somehow more defense-favoring and more fit for human flourishing.
> 
> The main practical issue that I see with this so far is that people don't seem to actually trust any specific governance mechanism with the power to build such a thing. This fact becomes stark when you look at the results to my recent Twitter polls, asking if people would prefer to see AI monopolized by a single entity with a decade head-start, or AI delayed by a decade for everyone… In nine out of nine cases, the majority of people would rather see highly advanced AI delayed by a decade outright than be monopolized by a single group, whether it's a corporation, government or multinational body. In seven out of nine cases, delay won by at least two to one. This seems like an important fact to understand for anyone pursuing AI regulation.

I have quite a bit of sympathy for this point of view. But: there's a big selection effect. Buterin's Twitter followers tend to be people interested in decentralized alternatives to state-run currency. The fact they're opposed to centralized solutions is of course what one would expect.

> The main approach preferred by opponents of the "let's get one global org to do AI and make its governance really really good" route is polytheistic AI: intentionally try to make sure there's lots of people and companies developing lots of AIs, so that none of them grows far more powerful than the other. This way, the theory goes, even as AIs become superintelligent, we can retain a balance of power.
> 
> This philosophy is interesting, but my experience trying to ensure "polytheism" within the Ethereum ecosystem does make me worry that this is an inherently unstable equilibrium… My experience within Ethereum is mirrored by learnings from the broader world as a whole, where many markets have proven to be natural monopolies. With superintelligent AIs acting independently of humans, the situation is even more unstable. Thanks to recursive self-improvement, the strongest AI may pull ahead very quickly, and once AIs are more powerful than humans, there is no force that can push things back into balance.

This seems to me likely to be true, and important. I am very skeptical of "multiple poles" arguments. Information economies are very often winner-take-all or winner-take-most.

> A happy path: merge with the AIs?
> 
> A different option that I have heard about more recently is to focus less on AI as something separate from humans, and more on tools that enhance human cognition rather than replacing it… Directions like \[brain-computer interface\] are sometimes met with worry, in part because they are irreversible, and in part because they may give powerful people more advantages over the rest of us. Brain-computer interfaces in particular have dangers - after all, we are talking about literally reading and writing to people's minds. These concerns are exactly why I think it would be ideal for a leading role in this path to be held by a security-focused open-source movement, rather than closed and proprietary corporations and venture capital funds. Additionally, all of these issues are worse with superintelligent AIs that operate independently from humans, than they are with augmentations that are closely tied to humans. The divide between "enhanced" and "unenhanced" already exists today due to limitations in who can and can't use ChatGPT.
> 
> If we want a future that is both superintelligent and "human", one where human beings are not just pets, but actually retain meaningful agency over the world, then it feels like something like this is the most natural option. There are also good arguments why this could be a safer AI alignment path: by involving human feedback at each step of decision-making, we reduce the incentive to offload high-level planning responsibility to the AI itself, and thereby reduce the chance that the AI does something totally unaligned with humanity's values on its own.

I'm more pessimistic than Buterin, I think. To me, this goes back to technocracy-versus-everyone-else. **Technology centralizes control over humanity in the hands of the toolmakers**. This is true even of open source approaches. Someone may say "How neat that anyone can hack on \[open source project\] and modify it to their will"; in practice, only a tiny fraction of the world's population can do this in principle, and resource limitations mean that an even smaller fraction of the world's population can do it in practice. In principle my browser is open source and I can modify it; in practice, I'm stuck with what I'm given. The more technology influences our lives – the more posthuman we become – the more true this is. Humans ending up as techno-chattel to toolmakers, no matter how benevolent.

Now, an interesting retort to this is that maybe this is something we get back _from_ ASI. Tell your ASI to rewrite your browser so _etc_. It's an interesting possibility. I suspect we _will_ get some of it back, but not that much. Not just implementing but merely _specifying_ what you want is a deeply technical and virtuosic skill as any Movie Director or Art Director can tell you. And so this will limit our use of ASI to modify the technology that defines our extended selves.

Still, I certainly haven't taken this idea seriously enough yet. The following is pretty compelling:

> One other argument in favor of this direction is that it may be more socially palatable than simply shouting "pause AI" without a complementary message providing an alternative path forward. It will require a philosophical shift from the current mentality that tech advancements that touch humans are dangerous but advancements that are separate from humans are by-default safe. But it has a huge countervailing benefit: it gives developers something to do. Today, the AI safety movement's primary message to AI developers seems to be "you should just stop". One can work on alignment research, but today this lacks economic incentives. Compared to this, the common e/acc message of "you're already a hero just the way you are" is understandably extremely appealing. A d/acc message, one that says "you should build, and build profitable things, but be much more selective and intentional in making sure you are building things that help you and humanity thrive", may be a winner.

It's not clear to me it's compelling enough. It's largely a values-based argument, trying to address a public goods problem (safety and security). And those things are still undersupplied. To come back to my earlier question: _how_ does the market need to be modified, exactly? The Covid-19 case is, I think instructive: the makers of the vaccines captured almost none of the value. The world would actually be better off (long run) if they'd made 10x the profits.

**Update, November 30, 2023:** Reflecting some more on how I respond to Buterin's notes, it strikes me that I have one major overall concern, a concern which makes me thinks d/acc needs some creative extension before I'd be willing to embrace it. In [prior notes](https://michaelnotebook.com/ti/index.html) I have discussed the ways we've dealt with threatening technologies in the past:

> \[…\] we deal with this using governance feedback loops. As an example: I grew up thinking humanity would likely suffer tremendously from: acid rain; the ozone hole; nuclear war; peak oil; climate change; and so on. But in each case, humanity responded with tremendous ingenuity – things like the Vienna and Montreal protocols helped us deal with the ozone hole; many ideas (Swanson's Law, carbon taxes, and so on) are helping us deal with climate change; and so on. The worse the threat, the stronger the response; the result is a co-evolution: while we develop destructive technologies, we also find ways of governing them and blunting their negative effects.

This governance feedback loop is a major thing distinguishing us from other animals, and making our extinction less likely. You can view Buterin's vision as one of finding ways to strengthen the governance feedback loop. Of course, I highly approve of this as a goal, and think it's a great call-to-action. My concern is that it's not nearly sufficient. In particular, elsewhere I've discussed [recipes for ruin](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html), civlization-threatening, trivial-to-make technologies which we are too ignorant to make today, but which are inherently ungovernable except through ignorance. If such recipes for ruin don't exist – if we live in a fundamentally friendly world – then d/acc seems very sensible to me. But if such recipes for ruin do exit – if we live in what Nick Bostrom has called a vulnerable world – then d/acc seems to me a good start, but does _not_ address a core long-run issue: how to avoid developing knowledge of those recipes for ruin. That ignorance seems likely to me to be the only sensible governance strategy for such recipes.

Summing up my main takeaways from Buterin's essay:

1.  d/acc offers an attractive and optimistic vision of the future development of technology, setting out to manage many genuine tensions which are not acknowledged or denied in many other visions. It also harnesses many of humanity's best institutional, social, and technological inventions.
2.  d/acc leaves open a fundamental question about differential technology development, which is how to make safety and security relatively more profitable. Those both have (in some respects) the character of public goods, and have been historically undersupplied.
3.  d/acc is fundamentally grounded in technocratic capitalism, and does not address the increasing division between those who have agency over technology, and those who do not. I wonder how ASI will change that division.
4.  d/acc does not address the (non)-development of inherently ungovernable technologies, how to avoid recipes for civilizational ruin if we live in a fundamentally vulnerable world.
5.  I don't understand very well how d/acc relates concretely to many existing issues – to particular companies, for instance, or to issues like (say) wealth inequality, and so on. These are worth further reflection.
