# 为什么人们在[糟糕的平台]上发帖，而不是在[好的平台]上？｜Why do people post on [bad platform] instead of [good platform]?

---

## 正文

当有人在 Mastodon/Twitter/Threads/等平台上发布一篇热门 thread 时，你经常会看到一类评论，在 videos 下也能看到，大意是：“为什么要发 Twitter thread？写成 blog post 不是更好吗？”或者“为什么要做 video？写成 blog post 不是更好吗？”不过，这些评论的措辞往往更激烈，例如：

> [我读不下去那些跨好几页的 tweets，因为 users 在每条 reply 里只放 5 个词。我觉得常见的 internet 简直蠢透了：Twitter、tiktok、Instagram 等等。真是极大的精力浪费](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31311904)。

或者

> [当有人选择在 twitter 上 blog 时，你就知道，那充其量也只是肤浅，更可能根本就是愚蠢（就像这次一样）](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31312967)

这类评论相当常见，例如，我找出了 Foone 最近在 HN 上获得 200 points 或更多的 10 篇 Twitter threads，其中 9 篇都有这样的评论，抱怨为什么使用 Twitter。

人们常常对居然有人能找到使用 \[bad platform\] 的理由感到困惑，比如：“[他到底发了多少条 tweets 才说清自己的意思？200 条？就没人想过‘也许放在 single page 上会更连贯’？我搞不懂 social media](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=33234817)”或者“[拜托，typing 一段简短的 description，再 uploading 一张 picture，重复 100 次，会比把所有内容 typing 在一个 block 里，再在各处加上几个连接语更容易？……客观地说，这需要做更多的事](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31723037)”。

就我个人而言，我不太喜欢 video 这种 format；对于我看到的 95% 的 youtube videos，我宁愿通过 blog post 而不是 video 获取 information（如果 Google 真的严厉打击 ad blocking，这一点就更是如此）。而且我认为，对于关心其中 information 的 reader 来说，long-form blog posts 基本上严格优于 \[bad platform\] 上的 long threads。但我也承认，如果没有 \[bad platform\] 之类的东西，我想读的许多 content 根本就不会存在。

退后一步，从整体来看，我所见过的人们使用 \[bad platform\] 的主要理由有四个：它能获得更多 engagement，朋友们都在那里，它的 friction 更低，以及它能更好地 monetizes。

### Engagement（互动）

engagement 这个理由最简单，所以我们先来看它。只看人们把时间花在哪里，Twitter、Instagram 等 short-form platforms 就完全 dominate 了 Medium、Blogspot 等 longer form platforms；从这些公司的 valuations、survey data 等方面都能看出这一点。Substack 是目前最热门的 long-form content platform，它上一次的 valuation 是 ~$600M，与 short-form platforms 的 value 相比，基本上只是个 rounding error（我没有把 Wordpress 和或 Squarespace 之类的公司算进去，它们的 valuation 有很大一部分来自 articles 和 posts 以外的东西）。资金跟着人走，而大多数人已经不再关注 long-form content。而且，如果你问使用 substack 的人，他们的 readers 和 growth 来自哪里，答案就是 Twitter 之类的 platforms，因此，那些制作 long-form content、为 engagement 或 revenue 而 optimize 的人，仍然会 produce 大量 short-form content[1](https://danluu.com/why-video/#fn:O)。

### 朋友

朋友这个理由大概是第二简单的。很多人会使用身边人在用的东西。说实话，如果我年轻十岁，不是在 2013 年，而是在 2023 年开始在网上做点什么，我很可能会先尝试 streaming，再尝试 blogging。但是，作为一个年纪大了、跟不上时代的人，即使知道相对于 video 而言，blogging 是一种正在衰落的 medium，我还是在 2013 年尝试开了一个 blog。它对我来说似乎还算管用，所以我一直坚持了下来，但这似乎与代际有关。虽然有人比我年长却做 video，也有人比我年轻却写 blogs，但从 distribution of ages 来看，我距离人们压倒性地转向 video 的那个年龄段并没有那么远；如果我当时真的打算长期做点什么，而不只是做[我刚开始时 friction 最低的事](https://danluu.com/writing-non-advice/#friction)，我就会从 video 开始。如今，对那些刚开始把自己的想法放到网上的人来说，做 video 是很自然的事。

### Friction（阻力）

当 \[bad platform\] 是 Twitter、Mastodon、Threads 等 microblogging platform 时，朋友这个理由仍然常常成立——这些 platforms 上的人往往属于一个他们会与之 interact 的 community，对他们来说，把 content 留在满是 community members 的 platform 上，比把 content 放到别处更合理。但对于那些 content 被广泛阅读的人来说，更重要的理由是，很多人觉得这些 platforms 的 friction 比写 blog posts 低得多。当有人指出这一点时，讨厌 \[bad platform\] 的人往往会感到困惑，作出这样的回应：

> 拜托，typing 一段简短的 description，再 uploading 一张 picture，重复 100 次，会比把所有内容 typing 在一个 block 里，再在各处加上几个连接语更容易？……客观地说，这需要做更多的事

首先，在我有联系的那些被广泛阅读的 programmer/tech bloggers 中，大多数人使用的 platforms 实际上有更高的 friction（例如，[Jekyll](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1204576631890698240) 的 [friction](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1244050309648797697)，以及 [Hugo](https://mastodon.social/@danluu/109542037575670321) 的 [fric](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1244050309648797697)[tion](https://commaok.xyz/post/on_hugo/)）。不过，原则上，他们可以使用 substack、hosted wordpress，或者这位评论者认为 friction“客观上”更低的其他 platform，但这种看法从根本上误解了 friction 的来源。人们说 \[bad platform\] 的 friction 更低时，通常指的是 writing 和 publishing 某些东西时的 emotional barriers，而不是 publish 某些东西实际需要多少次 clicks。我们可以争论这是否 rational、“客观上”是否说得通，等等，但归根结底，一个事实就是，很多人觉得，在让你写 short chunks of text 而不是 single large chunk of text 的 platform 上写东西，心理上更容易。

我[有时会在 Mastodon 上写东西](https://mastodon.social/@danluu/)，因为对我来说，它似乎是适合某些 content 的 platform。当然，既然问题不在于需要多少次 clicks，而是存在某种深层的 emotional motivation，其他人就会有不同的理由。例如，[Foone 说](https://nitter.net/Foone/status/1066548390517854208)：

> 不是想故作谦虚地炫耀什么，但我的东西被发到 hackernews 或 reddit 上之后，我最喜欢的环节就是，每一次都会有一条排名很高的 reply 说：“天啊，老兄，这明明可以写成一篇 blog post！能写一篇 blog post，为什么还要发 20 条 tweets？”
> 
> 因为我写不了 BLOG POST，妈的。我有 ADHD。我有严重的 ADHD，正在接受 treated，而 treatment 的效果并不好。我无法 focus 于写 blog posts。这事不会发生的
> 
> 如果我尝试写一篇 blog post，它最终会被丢下，无法完成，因为我没法把它 edit 成 readable、postable 的东西。所以，如果我 100% 转向 blogs：你们会得到：没有 content；我会得到：一大堆没写完的 drafts，以及觉得自己是个没用的废物的感觉
> 
> 但我能写絮絮叨叨的 tweet threads。它们不需要长时间投入大量 attention，也不会让我陷入写 blog posts 时那种没完没了的 editing，我能写它们。我写了很多！它们只是絮叨，而且发在 twitter 上，有些人不喜欢这样

Foone 所说的问题甚至并不少见——我最喜欢的 bloggers 中有三位提到，他们实际上只能一口气把东西写完，所以，要么有足够的劲头写完整篇 blog post，要么就写不了。只能让自己一次写一条 tweet，与只能写出一次 writing session 中能完成的内容，两者在规模上有所不同，但这些是程度上的差异，而不是性质上的差异。

### Revenue（收入）

而且，无论一个人出于什么原因觉得 \[bad platform\] 比 \[good platform\] 更 lower friction，让人们使用适合自己的 platform，就意味着我们能得到更多 content。对于 video，同样如此，因为 [video 的 monetizes 比 text 好得多](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1588318512258650112)，而且有很多在 video 上 monetizes well 的 content，如果采用 text，可能就不会 monetize well。

随便举个例子，automotive content 就是这样的领域之一。例如，如果你正在 buying a car，希望看到关于某辆 car 的 detailed、practical 的 reviews，以及它与考虑这辆 car 时可能也会考虑的其他 cars 的 comparisons，那么据我所知，在 YouTube 出现之前，没有人做过任何在 depth 上接近 [Alex Dykes 在 Alex on Autos 上所做的内容](https://www.youtube.com/@AAutoBuyersGuide)的东西。如果你翻开 car magazines 鼎盛时期的一本 car magazine，比如 1997 年的 Car and Driver 或 Road and Track，其中没有任何内容的 depth 能达到 Alex 所做的哪怕 1/10th，而今天的 modern car magazines 也仍然如此。不少 automotive content 的 sub-categories 也是这样，比如 [Jonathan Benson 在 Tyre Reviews 上的内容](https://www.youtube.com/@tyrereviews)。在 Jonathan 之前，没有人以同样的 breadth 和 depth 来 testing tires，并将其写出来（tire companies 的 engineers 做过这类 testing，而且做得多得多，但你必须直接与他们交谈才能获得这些信息）[2](https://danluu.com/why-video/#fn:V) 。在 automotive content 之外的许多领域，你也能发现类似的 patterns。虽然这要看具体领域，但在许多情况下，如果没有 video，这些 content 就不会存在。一般而言，人们不仅更愿意 watch videos，而不是 read text，而且 video 的 monetizes 比 text 好得多，这使人们能够以 text 所无法实现的方式，把 providing in depth information 作为自己的工作。在某些领域，你可以通过 paywalled newsletter 赚到可观的钱，但 car magazines 本质上就是这样的东西，而它们从未能够 support 任何类似 Alex Dykes 所做的内容；你能够 support 类似 Jonathan Benson 在 YouTube 上所做的内容，似乎也不太可能。

或者，举一个 tech 世界的例子：Lucy Wang 创建自己的 YouTube channel——[Tech With Lucy](https://www.youtube.com/@TechwithLucy)——后不久，当她拥有 50k subscribers、通常的 videos 有几千到几万 views、偶尔有一个 video 达到十万 views 时，[她提到，自己的收入已经超过了在 AWS 工作时的收入（其中大部分钱大概来自 sponsorships）](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1589775359243091969)。相比之下，我的 blog posts 每篇都有远超一百万的 hits，而我的收入绝对远远比不上 Lucy 在 AWS 时的收入；相反，我的 blog 只能勉强支付房租。如果你把大部分 text 放在 paywall 后面，确实有可能相当不错地 monetize 一些 text，例如，[Gergely Orosz 就是通过他的 newsletter 这样做的](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/)，但如果你希望大部分或全部 content 都是 freely available 的，那么 video 通常会 dominates text。

### Non-conclusion（非结论）

虽然我更希望自己在 YouTube/Twitter/Threads/Mastodon/等地方看到的大部分 content 都 hosted 在 text blog 上，但现实是，如果这些 content 必须写成 long-form text，而不能是分成小块的 short-form text 或 video，那么其中大部分根本就不会存在。也许再过几年，summary tools 会好到足以让我阅读这些 translations，但如今，我尝试过的所有 tools 都经常把关键细节弄得大错特错，所以我们只能接受 content 被创作出来时采用的 form。

## 原文

There's a class of comment you often see when someone makes a popular thread on Mastodon/Twitter/Threads/etc., that you also see on videos that's basically "Why make a Twitter thread? This would be better as a blog post" or "Why make a video? This would be better as a blog post". But, these comments are often stronger in form, such as:

> [I can't read those tweets that span pages because the users puts 5 words in each reply. I find common internet completely stupid: Twitter, tiktok, Instagram, etc. What a huge waste of energy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31311904).

or

> [When someone chooses to blog on twitter you know it's facile at best, and more likely simply stupid (as in this case)](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31312967)

These kinds of comments are fairly common, e.g., I pulled up Foone's last 10 Twitter threads that scored 200 points or more on HN and 9 out of 10 had comments like this, complaining about the use of Twitter.

People often express bafflement that anyone could have a reason for using \[bad platform\], such as in "[how many tweets are there just to make his point? 200? nobody thinks 'maybe this will be more coherent on a single page'? I don't get social media](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=33234817)" or "[Come on, typing a short description and uploading a picture 100 times is easier than typing everything in one block and adding a few connectors here and there? ... objectively speaking it is more work](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31723037)".

Personally, I don't really like video as a format and, for 95% of youtube videos that I see, I'd rather get the information as a blog post than a video (and this will be even more true if Google really cracks down on ad blocking) and I think that, for a reader who's interested in the information, long-form blog posts are basically strictly better than long threads on \[bad platform\]. But I also recognize that much of the content that I want to read wouldn't exist at all if it wasn't for things like \[bad platform\].

Stepping back and looking at the big picture, there are four main reasons I've seen that people use \[bad platform\], which are that it gets more engagement, it's where their friends are, it's lower friction, and it monetizes better.

### Engagement

The engagement reason is the simplest, so let's look at that first. Just looking at where people spend their time, short-form platforms like Twitter, Instagram, etc., completely dominate longer form platforms like Medium, Blogspot, etc.; you can see this in the valuations of these companies, in survey data, etc. Substack is the hottest platform for long-form content and its last valuation was ~$600M, basically a rounding error compared to the value of short-form platforms (I'm not including things like Wordpress and or Squarespace, which derive a lot of their valuation from things other than articles and posts). The money is following the people and people have mostly moved on from long-form content. And if you talk to folks using substack about where their readers and growth comes from, that comes from platforms like Twitter, so people doing long-form content who optimize for engagement or revenue will still produce a lot of short-form content[1](https://danluu.com/why-video/#fn:O).

### Friends

The friends reason is probably the next simplest. A lot of people are going to use whatever people around them are using. Realistically, if I were ten years younger and started doing something online in 2023 instead of 2013, more likely than not, I would've tried streaming before I tried blogging. But, as an old, out of touch, person, I tried starting a blog in 2013 even knowing that blogging was a dying medium relative to video. It seems to have worked well enough for me, so I've stuck with it, but this seems generational. While there are people older than me who do video and people younger than me who write blogs, looking at the distribution of ages, I'm not all that far from the age where people overwhelmingly moved to video and if I were really planning to do something long-term instead of just doing [the lowest friction thing when I started](https://danluu.com/writing-non-advice/#friction), I would've started with video. Today, doing video is natural for folks who are starting to put their thoughts online.

### Friction

When \[bad platform\] is a microblogging platform like Twitter, Mastodon, Threads, etc., the friends reason still often applies — people on these platforms are frequently part of a community they interact with, and it makes more sense for them to keep their content on the platform full of community members than to put content elsewhere. But the bigger reason for people whose content is widely read is that a lot of people find these platforms are much lower friction than writing blog posts. When people point this out, \[bad platform\] haters are often baffled, responding with things like

> Come on, typing a short description and uploading a picture 100 times is easier than typing everything in one block and adding a few connectors here and there? ... objectively speaking it is more work

For one thing, most widely read programmer/tech bloggers that I'm in touch with use platforms that are actually higher friction (e.g., [Jekyll](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1204576631890698240) [friction](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1244050309648797697) and [Hugo](https://mastodon.social/@danluu/109542037575670321) [fric](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1244050309648797697)[tion](https://commaok.xyz/post/on_hugo/)). But, in principle, they could use substack, hosted wordpress, or another platform that this commenter considers "objectively" lower friction, but this fundamentally misunderstands where the friction comes from. When people talk about \[bad platform\] being lower friction, it's usually about the emotional barriers to writing and publishing something, not the literal number of clicks it takes to publish something. We can argue about whether or not this is rational, whether this "objectively" makes sense, etc., but at the end of the day, it is simply true that many people find it mentally easier to write on a platform where you write short chunks of text instead of a single large chunk of text.

I [sometimes write things on Mastodon](https://mastodon.social/@danluu/) because it feels like the right platform for some kinds of content for me. Of course, since the issue is not the number of clicks it takes and there's some underlying emotional motivation, other people have different reasons. For example, [Foone says](https://nitter.net/Foone/status/1066548390517854208):

> Not to humblebrag or anything, but my favorite part of getting posted on hackernews or reddit is that EVERY SINGLE TIME there's one highly-ranked reply that's "jesus man, this could have been a blog post! why make 20 tweets when you can make one blog post?"
> 
> CAUSE I CAN'T MAKE A BLOG POST, GOD DAMN IT. I have ADHD. I have bad ADHD that is being treated, and the treatment is NOT WORKING TERRIBLY WELL. I cannot focus on writing blog posts. it will not happen
> 
> if I try to make a blog post, it'll end up being abandoned and unfinished, as I am unable to edit it into something readable and postable. so if I went 100% to blogs: You would get: no content I would get: lots of unfinished drafts and a feeling of being a useless waste
> 
> but I can do rambly tweet threads. they don't require a lot of attention for a long time, they don't have the endless editing I get into with blog posts, I can do them. I do them a bunch! They're just rambly and twitter, which some people don't like

The issue Foone is referring to isn't even uncommon — three of my favorite bloggers have mentioned that they can really only write things in one sitting, so either they have enough momentum to write an entire blog post or they don't. There's a difference in scale between only being able to get yourself to write a tweet at a time and only being able to write what you can fit into a single writing session, but these are differences in degree, not differences in kind.

### Revenue

And whatever the reason someone has for finding \[bad platform\] lower friction than \[good platform\], allowing people to use a platform that works for them means we get more content. When it comes to video, the same thing also applies because [video monetizes so much better than text](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1588318512258650112) and there's a lot of content that monetizes well on video that probably wouldn't monetize well in text.

To pick an arbitrary example, automotive content is one of these areas. For example, if you're buying a car and you want detailed, practical, reviews about a car as well as comparisons to other cars one might consider if they're looking at a particular car, before YouTube, AFAIK, no one was doing anything close to the depth of what [Alex Dykes does on Alex on Autos](https://www.youtube.com/@AAutoBuyersGuide). If you open up a car magazine from the heyday of car magazines, something like Car and Driver or Road and Track from 1997, there's nothing that goes into even 1/10th of the depth that Alex does and this is still true today of modern car magazines. The same goes for quite a few sub-categories of automotive content as well, such as [Jonathan Benson's on Tyre Reviews](https://www.youtube.com/@tyrereviews). Before Jonathan, no one was testing tires with the same breadth and depth and writing it up (engineers at tire companies did this kind of testing and much more, but you had to talk to them directly to get the info)[2](https://danluu.com/why-video/#fn:V) . You can find similar patterns in a lot of areas outside of automotive content as well. While this depends on the area, in many cases, the content wouldn't exist if it weren't for video. Not only do people, in general, have more willingness to watch videos than to read text, video monetizes much better than text does, which allows people to make providing in depth information their job in a way that wouldn't be possible in text. In some areas, you can make good money with a paywalled newsletter, but this is essentially what car magazines are and they were never able to support anything resembling what Alex Dykes does, nor does it seem plausible that you could support something like what Jonathan Benson does on YouTube.

Or, to pick an example from the tech world, shortly after Lucy Wang created her YouTube channel, [Tech With Lucy](https://www.youtube.com/@TechwithLucy), when she had 50k subscribers and her typical videos had thousands to tens of thousands views with the occasional video with a hundred thousand views, [she noted that she was making more than she did working for AWS (with most of the money presumably coming in from sponsorships)](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1589775359243091969). By comparison, my blog posts all get well over a million hits and I definitely don't make anywhere near what Lucy made at AWS; instead, my blog barely covers my rent. It's possible to monetize some text decently well if you put most of it behind a paywall, e.g., [Gergely Orosz does this with his newsletter](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/), but if you want to have mostly or exclusively have freely available content, video generally dominates text.

### Non-conclusion

While I would prefer that most content that I see on YouTube/Twitter/Threads/Mastodon/etc. were hosted on a text blog, the reality is that most of that content wouldn't exist at all if it had to be written up as long-form text instead of as chunked up short-form text or video. Maybe in a few years, summary tools will get good enough that I can consume the translations but, today, all the tools I've tried often get key details badly wrong, so we just have to live with the content in the form it's created in.
