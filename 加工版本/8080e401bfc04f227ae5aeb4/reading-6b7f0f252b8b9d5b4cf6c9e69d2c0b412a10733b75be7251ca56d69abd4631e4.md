## 正文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_关于 self-acceptance 以及人如何 change 的个人随手笔记。主要是在梳理各种 therapeutic traditions 中常见的 ideas。不过，我一直难以深入 understand 这些 ideas，因此写下了这篇文章。其中一些 examples 和 framing 不太寻常，或许也会让更多人感兴趣。_

下面是电视剧《Breaking Bad》中的一个震撼场景（涉及剧透）。两位主角之一 Jesse Pinkman 是一名 drug dealer，此时正在参加 therapy group。Jesse 是个迷失的人，真心想过上好日子，人生却反而坠入了 violence 的地狱。他 murdered 了 drug trade 中的另一个人，不知道该如何应对。但他不能向 therapy group 说出这件事，于是告诉他们，他杀了一条狗[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn1)：

accepting 自己这个 idea 很流行。但它可能显得太廉价，像是让自己逃避责任的一种方式。“我爱自己，也就是说，我 accept 自己的 overweight / laziness / cowardice / _等等_。”Jesse 把这个 issue 说得很尖锐：

> Counsellor：“我们来这里不是为了 sit in judgement。”
> 
> Jesse：“为什么不是？……也许我当时就该做点不一样的事……要是做了什么都没事，那这一切还有什么意思，有什么意义？哦，对了，这整套东西讲的都是 self acceptance。”
> 
> Counsellor：“把自己骂个狗血淋头，并不会赋予任何事情意义。”
> 
> Jesse：“所以我应该停止 judging，然后 accept？”
> 
> Counsellor：“这算是个开始。”
> 
> Jesse：“所以不管我做了什么，都该为我欢呼，因为我是个大好人？一切都没问题。不管我杀了多少条狗，我只要怎么着，做个 inventory，然后 accept。我是说，你倒车把自己的孩子轧了，然后你就这么 accept。真他妈扯淡。”

这个场景让我不安。它在 harsh self-judgement 与 unconditional acceptance 之间呈现了一种 false dichotomy。Jesse 说得没错，光是微笑着说‘我是个好人’并不够。但他多年来一直在 self-hating，这并没有 improving 他的生活。事实上，反而让他的生活变得更糟。

这个场景难以理解，部分原因在于其中 issues 的沉重。Murder。Addiction。Deception。Self-hatred。它们在情感上如此强烈，让人很难好好思考。考虑一个没那么沉重的情境，会有所帮助。

过去七年来，我时不时就会想起下面这个例子，觉得颇为有趣。我把它叫作‘纸杯蛋糕事件’。2019 年 5 月，我去探望一位住在英国 Oxford 附近的朋友。我们愉快地聊了几个小时。他请我吃纸杯蛋糕。我没有理智地只拿一个，而是狼吞虎咽地吃下了多得过分的数量，幸好我已经记不清到底有多少了。

后来，我变得极其 self-critical。在成年后的大部分时间里，我一直在与 weight 作斗争。我为什么吃了那么多？我曾发誓这趟旅行要好好吃饭，结果却在这里胡吃海塞。我是个 self-indulgent 的蠢货，毫无 self-control。我肯定会患上 diabetes 和 heart disease，生活也会变得更糟。那也是我活该。诸如此类，没完没了。

如果只发生一次，那不过是一段糟糕的插曲。问题在于，纸杯蛋糕事件并不是这种事情第一次发生，甚至也不是第十四次。

我们大多数人都有一种 implicit theory of personal change：你 identify 某种‘错误的’behaviour，弄清自己本来应该怎么做，然后下次做对的事。这似乎是 self-evident，而且往往确实管用！但有时它 _并不_ 管用。你 self-criticise……然后下次又做同样的事。再下次也是。再下次还是。即使知道‘正确的’做法，有些 problems 依然挥之不去。年轻时，你会纳闷自己到底出了什么毛病。年纪渐长，你会意识到，许多人都有同样的经历，只是各自难以摆脱的 problems 不同。一个人觉得停止 overeating 很容易，却无法停止 biting their nails；另一个人则恰好相反。

面对这些难以摆脱的 problems，许多人的做法是不断加码，让 self-judgement 变得越来越 harsh。仿佛 _intensity of self-judgement_ 就是 causing change 的关键 input。

但 intensity of self-judgement 只是一个 input。另一种很不一样的 input，是你对自身 behaviour 的 understanding 有多深。就纸杯蛋糕事件而言，我最初的 observations 是这样的：由于旅行，我当时极其 jetlagged。我很 tired，而这往往会 reduce self-control。诸如此类。这些 observations 是一个好的开始，但并没有告诉我可以在哪里 intervene；孤立来看，它们更像是借口。我需要进一步深入，找到可以尝试的 interventions[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn2)，以进一步 improve 我的 understanding。

我尝试了几种办法。我试着 develop 一个 habit：每到一个新地方，就买我惯常选择的 healthy snack——香蕉。这往往不管用：到达之后，我通常累得没力气去找超市。于是，我试着趁精力充沛时提前找好超市，这样真正到达时就容易多了。有时我成功了！但有时我还是会忘记，或是太累了。而且，即使成功，也会出现新问题。旅行时，香蕉容易在包里被压烂，我又不想吃放久了、软烂的香蕉。于是我改吃苹果和坚果。苹果更好，因为它们比坚果更能 stabilize 我的 appetite。不过，与苹果和香蕉不同，坚果通常在机场很容易买到。所以我往往还是选坚果。到目前为止，这个 strategy 还算管用——它不是一个 full solution，但确实能让我在长途旅行后到达新地方时吃得稍微好一些。

这只是整个故事的一小部分；我也尝试过许多其他办法。这是 iterative problem-solving，重点是 improving 我旅行时的饮食方式——不是靠 self-criticism 和 willpower，而是反复尝试，观察发生了什么 changes，并在过程中不断 improving 我的 understanding。

关键在于：2013 年，我被 diagnosed 为患有一种 mild arthritis，叫作 ankylosing spondylitis (AS)。开始 treatment 后，我的 weight 减轻了不少，但我当时完全不知道为什么。直到 2020s，我才知道原因：untreated AS 会让人 tiring，而人们往往会 overeat 来 compensate，因而变得 overweight。2019 年纸杯蛋糕事件发生时，我仍然不知道这一点。因此，我当时形成的 understanding 确实有助于让我的 weight 保持较低，但它也并不 complete。世界很复杂，你不应指望在 intervening 之前就拥有 full understanding。你远比你对自己的 model 更为庞大。而且有时候，生活中其他的 changes，或者仅仅是 real-world circumstances 的 change，就能带来出乎意料的 impact。

这第二种 theory of change 很显然：‘Understand 你为什么会那样 behave，并找到 interventions，以获得你 desired outcomes。’它是一种 criticise-understand-experiment loop。但尽管这很显然，许多人还是会跳过 understanding，或满足于 shallow 的 understanding，并把 self-criticism 当作更好的替代品。这也难怪：与真正 understanding 所需的艰苦努力相比，不断加码 self-criticism 要容易得多。这种态度往往相当于：‘得了吧，你就是没有足够的 willpower。你需要的是在自己的 commitment 上 shut-the-fuck-up-and-be-more-intense。你要是认真的，就会好好吃饭，根本不需要这么折腾。你就是把事情搞得太复杂了！’

几十年来，我自己大体上也持这种态度。的确，有时 understanding 的关键一步 _就是_ 注意到‘我在这件事上没有认真对待’，然后 just-doing-the-right-fucking-thing。但当你长期抱持 harsh self-judgement——就像我在好好吃饭这件事上一样——它往往会 inhibits understanding。‘我是个一无是处的 lazy 蠢货，连……都做不到’，成了 avoiding the problem 的一种方式，实际上让更深入的 understanding 变得 _更加困难_。

其中很大一部分，是 understanding dark patterns 如何 contribute to 你的 behaviour。也许你有很高的 need for control。或者，你有想要富有或出名的 ambition，却觉得这很 shameful。也可能是其他什么。问自己这个问题往往很有帮助：‘有哪些可能 relevant 的事，是我不愿向自己 admitting 的？’同样还有：‘我在 afraid 什么？’以及：‘有什么是我 want，却又 ashamed to admit 自己 want 的？’诸如此类[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn3)[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn4)。

在进行这种 understanding 时，很容易 blame the external world，觉得自己是那些超出自身 control 的 forces 的 victim。‘都是我的 upbringing / genetics / illness / 刻薄的人造成的’，是一种很诱人的推脱。这很可能是部分 truth，但它的用处主要在于作为 understanding 过程中的 intermediate point。如果你 accept 它就是那个 reason，你就把 power 交给了自己 control 之外的东西。这一点需要格外小心。当然，有时候你需要 accept 自己的 situation。患有 ALS 的人不可能立志成为 100-metre runner。但 Stephen Hawking 尽管身患 illness，还是找到了 contribute 的办法。你也许会觉得，一个身高 5 feet 3 inches 的人不可能打 men's basketball。但 Muggsy Bogues 找到了在最高 level 打球的办法。你也不会觉得，一个出生于 poverty 和 prostitution 环境中的人，能够建立世界上规模最大的 armadas of ships 之一。但 Zheng Yi Sao 做到了。也许这些人有时都曾自怜，对世界的不公平感到愤怒。但他们也找到了生活中可以 intervene 的地方，来 overcome external circumstances。

再回到 Jesse。他其实已经走了很长一段路。在《Breaking Bad》早期，他是 irresponsible 的，把自己的 problems 都 blaming 到别人身上。到了这时，他至少在尝试 take responsibility。不幸的是，他的 theory of change 建立在 totalizing self-criticism 和 self-hatred 之上。这并没有帮助他 change effectively。他依然是个困在地狱里的怪物。作为不在他所造成 damage 的直接范围内的人，我们很可能会同情他——事实上，我认为这种同情是可贵的。但作为一个 society，我们必须阻止他。这个支离破碎的人，正在对身边的人造成可怕的 damage。这并不是说怪物永远不可能得到救赎。但只要他还认为出路是更加用力地 hate 自己，救赎就不会发生。

Jesse 后来的一句台词也很震撼。Counsellor 说：‘嘿，Jesse，我知道你很 pain。’Jesse 回答：‘不，你知道吗？我一开始为什么来这儿？就是为了卖给你们 meth。对我来说，你们什么都不是，只是 customers。我把你们耍得像狗一样。’

这话听起来很糟糕。他说得无比笃定，而且毫无疑问，Jesse 在那一刻相信自己说的是真的。但 Jesse 也许是房间里唯一没有意识到这话不 true 的人。如果它是 true，他为什么会为杀了‘那条狗’如此 upset？为什么要跟这个 group 谈这件事？又为什么会对自己作出如此 harsh 的 judgement？

那么，他为什么要这样说？这是 Jesse 出于 instinctive 的做法：把自己说得如此卑劣，好让自己得到他觉得应得的 negative judgement 和 shunning。这是 self-punishment：他如此 hates 自己，以至于他 _想要_ sabotage 自己与这个 group 的 relationship。

这也说明了这种 harsh self-criticism 的 dangers。它可能 distort self-understanding，进而导致 counterproductive actions。在这个例子中，他 isolates 自己，远离 support group，又被逼回那些 drug dealer ‘朋友’那里，寻求他们的‘support’。但他们对他有害极了。

话虽如此，Jesse 有一点是对的：pure unconditional self-acceptance 并不是答案。只是 uncritically accept 自己的 weight problem、laziness、cowardice 或其他什么，并为之‘欢呼’，是错误的。更好的做法是 observe 到它 _确实是_ 一个 problem。而且，你想要有所不同。

但他需要一个更深入的想法：一种 provisional acceptance，使你能够 see 和 understand 你目前是怎样的人。你对这一点 understand 得越好，就越能 effective 地 intervening。这样的 understanding 需要 suspending 那种否则会令人 paralyzing 的 judgement。不是永久如此，而是暂时如此。你需要让 understanding 和 experimentation 的时期，与退后一步、judge 并决定自己愿意 accept 什么、又将集中精力 changing 什么的时期交替进行。

这是任何 creative worker 都熟悉的一种 pattern。你不能一边让自己有太多 judgement，一边 paint、write 或 compose。“这就是垃圾。这太糟糕了。这简直糟透了。”嗯，没错，它往往确实如此：它还是一个 work in progress。但一直这样想，对作品并没有帮助。作家 Anne Lamott 曾令人难忘地将其描述为 Radio Station KFKD (K-Fucked)，也就是 creatives 在工作时经常在脑海里听到的声音，你必须想办法把它的音量调低。但在适当的时候，你也确实 _需要_ criticism，甚至是 harsh criticism。

这一点不仅适用于 art、writing 或 music 的 creativity，也适用于你自己人生的 creation。你不能仅靠 self-criticise 就走进天堂。事实上，你甚至不能主要靠 self-criticise 走进天堂。反过来也同样如此：self-acceptance 作为一种 temporary stance 极有帮助，但作为一种 unconditional stance，却会令人 debilitating。

我在这里逐渐形成的看法，一直着重于 fixing bad patterns。你有必要用人生中的一部分时间来做这件事。但我有时会遇到一些有才华的人，他们把自己看作一堆有待 solve 的 problems，而不是一组有待 use 的 abilities。更好的做法是花更多时间 developing 自己的 strengths，并 using 它们来 contribute 于世界。你首先不是一个有待 debug 的 system；你是一个能够 contribute 的人。

这里的许多论述还包含一个 tacit assumption：我们的 conscious mind 最终应该 dictate 我们的 behaviour，而它所“知道”的、我们应该做的事就是 right 的——我们应该更 thinner、更不 lazy，等等。但有时，我们的 unconscious mind 才是 right 的[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn5)。正如我上面所说，我们比自己关于自身的 model 要大得多，也 complex 得多。对于自己的 conscious understanding，我们需要有相当的谦逊，也需要尊重我们的 unconscious self 和 external world。有时，需要 adapt 于它们的是我们的 conscious mind，而不是反过来。

让我总结一下。多年来，我个人的 theory of change 都 implied，我的 overeating 主要是由 lack of willpower 所 caused 的。我是个坏人。这种 harsh self-criticism assumed 我已经 understood 发生了什么，使我看不见实际正在发生的事情——从细小、容易 improved 的 factors，一直到 chronic inflammation 和 exhaustion。不停的 self-loathing 并不会 produce understanding，廉价的 self-acceptance 也不会带来美好的人生。Change 需要你 make judgements，但也需要你把它们暂时放下足够长的时间，以便 understand 自己面对的是什么，然后尝试 improving 的方法。也许很简单，也很显然。但这是一种很难坚持的 discipline，我仍在学习。

_Personal working notes on self-acceptance and how people change. Mostly working through ideas which are standard in various therapeutic traditions. I've struggled to understand those ideas deeply, though, so I wrote this. Some of the examples and framing are unusual, and may be of broader interest._

The following is a striking scene from the television series "Breaking Bad" (spoilers). One of the two main characters, Jesse Pinkman, a drug dealer, is in a therapy group. Jesse is a lost soul, a person who genuinely wants to live a good life, but whose life has instead spiralled into a hell of violence. He's murdered someone else in the drug trade, and doesn't know how to deal with it. But he can't say this to his therapy group, so instead he tells them he killed a dog[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn1):

 Your browser doesn't support embedded video. [Download the clip](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/assets/problem-dog.mp4).

The idea of accepting yourself is a popular one. But it can seem too cheap, a way of letting ourselves off the hook. "I love myself, which means I accept my being overweight / laziness / cowardice / _etc_". Jesse states the issue starkly:

> Counsellor: "We're not here to sit in judgement."
> 
> Jesse: "Why not?… Maybe I shoulda done something different… If you just do stuff and nothin' happens, what's it all mean, what's the point? Oh right, this whole thing is about self acceptance."
> 
> Counsellor: "Kicking the hell out of yourself doesn't give meaning to anything."
> 
> Jesse: "So I should stop judging and accept?"
> 
> Counsellor: "It's a start."
> 
> Jesse: "So no matter what I do, hurray for me because I'm a great guy? It's all good. No matter how many dogs I kill I just what, I do an inventory, and accept. I mean, you back your truck over your own kid and you like accept. What a load of crap."

The scene bothers me. It presents a false dichotomy between harsh self-judgement and unconditional acceptance. Jesse's right that smiling and saying "I'm a good person" isn't enough. But he's been self-hating for years, and it isn't improving his life. In fact, it's making it worse.

Part of the difficulty in understanding the scene is the gravity of the issues. Murder. Addiction. Deception. Self-hatred. They're so emotionally intense that it's hard to think well about them. It helps to consider a less loaded situation.

For the past seven years it has amused me, on and off, to think about the following example. I call it "the cupcake incident". In May of 2019 I visited a friend living near Oxford in the UK. We spent a couple of enjoyable hours chatting. He offered me cupcakes. Rather than take a sensible single cupcake, I gobbled down some immoderate number that I fortunately cannot remember.

Later, I was extremely self-critical. I have struggled with weight through much of my adult life. Why had I eaten so much? I'd vowed to eat well on this trip, and here I was stuffing myself. I was a self-indulgent fool with no self-control. I would no doubt suffer diabetes and heart disease, and my life would be worse. I would deserve it. And so on, at length.

As a one-off that's just a bad episode. The trouble is the cupcake incident wasn't the first or even the fourteenth time this kind of thing had happened.

Most of us have an implicit theory of personal change: you identify some "wrong" behaviour, figure out what you should have done, and then next time you do the right thing. This seems self-evident, and it often works! But sometimes it _doesn't_. You self-criticise… and then do the same thing the next time. And the next time. And the next time. Some problems stick despite knowing the "right" thing to do. When you're young you wonder what's wrong with you. As you get older you realize many people have the same experience, with different sticky problems. One person finds it easy to stop overeating, but can't stop biting their nails; another finds the reverse.

What many people do with these sticky problems is ratchet up harsher and harsher self-judgement. It's as though _intensity of self-judgement_ is the key input in causing change.

But intensity of self-judgement is only one input. A very different input is your depth of understanding of your behaviour. In the case of the cupcake incident, this began with observations like: I was extremely jetlagged, due to travel. I was tired, which tends to reduce self-control. And so on. Such observations are a good start, but don't give me places where I can intervene; in isolation they're closer to excuses. I needed to go deeper to find interventions[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn2) I could try out, in order to improve my understanding further.

I tried a few things. I attempted to develop a habit of buying my go-to healthy snack, bananas, whenever I arrived in a new place. This often didn't work: after arrival I was usually too tired to find a supermarket. So I tried identifying supermarkets in advance, when I was fresh, making it much easier when I actually arrived. Sometimes I succeeded! But sometimes I'd still forget or be too tired. And even when I succeeded there was a new problem. Bananas tend to get squashed in my bag when travelling, and I didn't want to eat squishy old bananas. So I switched to apples and nuts. Apples are better, since they stabilize my appetite better than nuts. But unlike apples and bananas, nuts tend to be easy to find in airports. So nuts it often is. So far this strategy is working okay – it's not a full solution, but it does help me eat somewhat better when I arrive in a new place after a long trip.

This is just a small part of the story; I've tried many other things too. It's iterative problem-solving, focused on improving how I eat when I travel – not through self-criticism and willpower, but by trying things out over and over, seeing what changes, and improving my understanding as I go.

Crucially: in 2013 I was diagnosed with a mild arthritis called ankylosing spondylitis (AS). After I began treatment I lost a fair bit of weight, though I had no idea why. It was only in the 2020s that I learned the reason: untreated AS is tiring, and people often overeat to compensate, and so become overweight. At the time of the cupcake incident, in 2019, I still didn't know. So the understanding I was generating was genuinely useful in keeping my weight lower, but was also incomplete. The world is complicated, and you shouldn't expect a full understanding before intervening. You are much larger than your model of yourself. And sometimes, making other changes in your life, or simply a change in real-world circumstances, can have a surprising impact.

This second theory of change is obvious: "Understand why you behave the way you do, and find interventions so as to get your desired outcomes". It's a kind of criticise-understand-experiment loop. But while it's obvious, many people skip the understanding or settle for a shallow one, and treat self-criticism as a better substitute. No wonder: escalating self-criticism is easy compared to the hard work of actual understanding. The attitude often amounts to: "Nah, you just don't have enough willpower. What you need is to shut-the-fuck-up-and-be-more-intense in your commitment. If you were serious, you'd eat well, no need for all this fuss. You're just over-complicating it!"

I largely held this attitude myself for decades. Indeed, sometimes the key step in understanding _is_ noticing that "I'm not being serious here", and just-doing-the-right-fucking-thing. But when you hold harsh self-judgement over a long time – as I did with eating well – it often inhibits understanding. "I'm a no-good lazy fool who can't even…" becomes a way of avoiding the problem, actually making it _harder_ to go deeper in understanding.

A big part of this is understanding how dark patterns contribute to your behaviour. Maybe you have a high need for control. Or an ambition to be rich or famous that you feel is shameful. Or whatever it might be. It's often helpful to ask: "What am I not admitting to myself that might be relevant?" So too: "What am I afraid of?" So too: "What do I want, but am ashamed to admit I want?" And so on[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn3)[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn4).

In doing this understanding, it's easy to blame the external world, to feel a victim of forces beyond our control. "It was my upbringing / genetics / illness / mean people" is a tempting cop-out. It may well be part of the truth, but it's mostly useful as an intermediate point in understanding. If you accept it as the reason, then you cede power to things outside your control. That's something to be very careful about. Of course, sometimes you need to accept your situation. Someone with ALS can't aspire to be a 100-metre runner. But Stephen Hawking found ways to contribute, despite his illness. And you might think someone 5 feet 3 inches tall couldn't play men's basketball. But Muggsy Bogues found a way to play at the highest level. And you wouldn't think someone born into poverty and prostitution could build one of the world's largest armadas of ships. But Zheng Yi Sao did. Perhaps all these people sometimes felt sorry for themselves and mad at the unfairness of the world. But they also found places they could intervene in their lives to overcome external circumstances.

Let's come back to Jesse. He's actually come a long way. Early on in "Breaking Bad" he was irresponsible, blaming others for his problems. By this point he's at least trying to take responsibility. Unfortunately, his theory of change is based on totalizing self-criticism and self-hatred. It's not helping him change effectively. He's still a monster, trapped in hell. As people outside the immediate radius of damage he's causing, we may well feel sorry for him – indeed, I think such pity is admirable. But as a society we must stop him. This broken man is doing terrible damage to the people around him. That's not to say monsters can't ever be redeemed. But it's not going to happen while he believes the way out is to hate himself harder.

Jesse's later line is also striking. The Counsellor says: "Hey, Jesse, I know you're in pain". And Jesse replies: "No, you know what? Why I'm here in the first place? Is to sell you meth. You're nothin' to me but customers. I made you my bitch".

This sounds terrible. It's delivered with tremendous conviction, and there's no doubt Jesse believes it in the moment. But Jesse may be the only person in the room who doesn't realize it's untrue. If it were true, why would he be so upset about killing "the dog"? Why would he be talking about it with the group? And why would he be so harsh in judgement of himself?

So why does he say it? It's Jesse's instinctive way of making himself seem so despicable that he will get the negative judgement and shunning he feels he deserves. It's self-punishment: he hates himself so much he _wants_ to sabotage his relationship with the group.

And it illustrates the dangers of such harsh self-criticism. It can distort self-understanding in a way that leads to counterproductive actions. In this case: he isolates himself from the support group, and is driven back to his drug dealer "friends" for their "support". But they're terrible for him.

With that said, Jesse is right that pure unconditional self-acceptance isn't the answer. It's a mistake to just uncritically accept and say "hurray" to your weight problem, laziness, cowardice, or whatever. It's better to observe that it _is_ a problem. And that you want something different.

But he needs a deeper idea: a provisional acceptance which enables you to see and understand the person you currently are. The better you understand that, the more effective you can be in intervening. Such understanding requires suspending what would otherwise be a paralyzing judgement. Not permanently. But for some time. You need to alternate periods of understanding and experimentation, with periods where you step back and judge and decide what you're willing to accept, and what you will focus on changing.

This is a pattern familiar to any creative worker. You can't paint or write or compose while allowing yourself too much judgement. "This is crap. This is terrible. This is awful". Well, yes, it often is: it's a work in progress. But thinking this way all the time doesn't help the work. The writer Anne Lamott memorably described this as Radio Station KFKD (K-Fucked), the voice creatives often hear in their head as they work, and which you must find ways of turning down. But you also, at suitable times, _need_ criticism, even harsh criticism.

This holds not just for the creativity of art or writing or music. It holds for the creation of your life. You can't solely self-criticise your way into heaven. Indeed, you can't even mainly self-criticise your way into heaven. The reverse is also true: while self-acceptance is wonderfully helpful as a temporary stance, it's debilitating as an unconditional stance.

The view I've developed here has been focused on fixing bad patterns. It's necessary to spend some of your life doing this. But I sometimes meet talented people who think of themselves as bundles of problems to solve, not bundles of abilities to use. It's better to spend more time developing your strengths and using them to contribute to the world. You are not primarily a system to debug; you're a person to contribute.

There's also a tacit assumption in much of this: that our conscious mind should ultimately dictate our behaviour, and that what it "knows" we should be doing is right – we should be thinner, less lazy, and so on. But sometimes our unconscious mind is right[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/cupcake/#fn5). We are, as I said above, much larger and more complex than our own model of ourselves. We need considerable humility about our conscious understanding, and respect for both our unconscious self and the external world. Sometimes it's our conscious mind that needs to adapt to them, not the other way around.

Let me sum up. For years, my personal theory of change implied that my overeating was mostly caused by lack of willpower. I was a bad person. This harsh self-criticism assumed I already understood what was going on, blinding me to what was actually happening – ranging from small, easily improved factors, through to chronic inflammation and exhaustion. Relentless self-loathing doesn't produce understanding, and cheap self-acceptance doesn't produce a good life either. Change needs you to make judgements, but also to put them aside long enough to understand what you're dealing with, and then to try out ways of improving. Simple and obvious, maybe. But a hard discipline to keep, and one I'm still learning.
