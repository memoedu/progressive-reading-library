## 牢记目标

到 college 结束时，你大概应该已经比较清楚接下来要做什么。

弄清这个计划的默认做法是：(1) 三年里都不去想它；(2) 到 senior year 时，因为不知道自己想做什么而焦虑不堪；(3) 注意到朋友们都在 interviewing，争取 consulting、finance 和 MicroFaceGoogAzon 的 jobs；(4) 接受 consulting、finance 或 MicroFaceGoogAzon 的一份 job。

这个过程……还有待改进。提前规划，不要因为 social defaults，或害怕找不到 job，就做出一个“安全”的决定。最重要的待答问题是：“有哪些 jobs，既 (a) 是我会喜欢的，又 (b) 是我有合理机会得到的？”

没错，直面这个问题（尤其是 b！）会让人害怕、倍感压力，但把多年时间浪费在安全而非精彩的事情上，应该要可怕得_多_。

这并不是说，你需要有多大把握地知道自己想做什么。（那不合理，而且反正也会变。）只是说，如果你在 college 期间的选择，能建立在对这些选择将为日后做何准备的_某种_大致认识上，那会很好。“计划毫无用处；规划不可或缺。”

从 college 获得的另一件有用的东西，是一群出色、聪明又能干的朋友。我建议为此 optimizing，可能应该把它放在 educational quality 或 status 之前。

几点补充：

-   关于“擅长 STEM 的人的 density”，我的印象是，research universities 的表现比小型 liberal arts colleges 好得多。我没有申请 Caltech，不过按某些（lagging）metrics 来看，它的[有成就者的 density 甚至更高](https://infoproc.blogspot.com/2015/09/colleges-ranked-by-nobel-fields-turing.html)，超过其他顶尖学校。

-   能自己决定和谁住在一起、住在谁附近，是一个巨大的吸引力，因为这在很大程度上决定了你的 social circle。例如，Harvard 会 randomly 给各组 upperclassmen 分配 dorms——你可以选择 roommates，却不能选择住在附近的其他 roommate groups。这让最出色的人很难找，因为他们混在无聊的群体里。MIT 等其他学校则往往有着不同 cultures、住着不同类型的人的 dorms，因此更容易找到出色的人聚在一起的地方。所以，如果今天让我选 college，我可能会优先考虑 housing policies 更好的学校。

-   如果你去的学校有强烈的 subculture，要确保自己仍然能够与“正常人”好好相处。比如，人们对 MIT alums 有一种刻板印象：他们余生都只和其他 MIT students 来往；这种情况确实会发生在一些人身上。这是合理的个人选择（正常人有时确实很烦！），但如果你在为对世界其他部分的 impact 而 optimizing，这就会成为一种障碍。

## 更善于决定做什么

我希望自己在 college 时能更多地拥有一种类似 _taste_ 的东西：不是 aesthetic taste，而更像是“对精彩事物的 taste”或“对好 projects 的 taste”。（在好的 project-taste 方面，我的榜样是朋友 [Adrian](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Adrian_Veres)，他在 graduating 之前就已在 _Nature_、_Science_ 和 _Cell_ 上 coauthored articles。）

好的 taste 会引导你在 college 做 effective 的事，而不是 pointless 的事。但在 college 期间，_develop_ 好的 taste 也真的很难，因为除了 academics，你没有其他好的 taste 榜样。Academics 的 taste 往往非常古怪，驱动它的常常是什么 niche 能让他们获得 tenure，而不是什么令人兴奋或有用，所以也有其他 taste 榜样会很好。

关于如何做到这一点，我最好的猜测是休一个 gap year，在此期间，你可以 front-load 对现实世界如何运转的学习。academia 之外的 Summer internships 也很好。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:1)

## 休一个 gap year

没错，上面我刚提到过这一点，但它足够具体、足够重要，值得单独用一节来说。我自己没有休过 gap year，但我认识的所有休过的人，都发现这极大地改善了他们利用 college 时间的方式，而且没有人后悔。

如果 gap year 的意义在于改善你在 college 如何利用时间，那么你就应该利用这一年，尽可能多地了解 college 之外的世界如何运转，尤其是那个世界认为什么有价值。试着寻找能让你与 non-academics 共事，并 produce 真正有用的东西的 projects。

（人们常在 gap years 里 volunteering 或旅行。稍微做一些，能开阔眼界；但把整年都花在这上面，虽然好玩，却不会 maximize 你的 learning。）

## 更倾向于行动

大学生活最大的变化之一，是你拥有了多得多的独立性。由于父母不再监管你的日常生活，你要做些随性或出人意料的事，阻力就小得多了。

许多大学生利用这种自由，把日程填满：(a) 不是聚会就是 problem sets，再加上 (b) 午餐约会，边吃边抱怨自己有多忙。其实还有更令人兴奋的选择！下面是我做过的一些事；我觉得，从 on the margin 来看，它们比额外做些 problem sets 更有价值，尽管其中很多最终“没成”：

-   在 GiveWell 实习（最终没有在那里工作，但接触到各种各样的人和想法，非常有价值）
-   经营[一个学生团体](http://www.harvardea.org/)（我不太擅长，但结果还是相当不错）
-   尝试担任 math teaching assistant（结果发现 grading:fun ratio 实在太高）
-   尝试 research（没能找到让我兴奋的 projects）
-   加入合唱团（特别好玩，但日程太紧，一年后我就退出了）
-   writing 一个 course catalog website（现在已经 unmaintained，但这是我从头参与直到 shipping 的第一个 project）
-   writing blog posts

当然，problem sets 也很重要！但如果你发现自己离不开它们，那好，你还可以再读六年 grad school，尽情做这些。如果你发现自己离不开聚会，那我就不是适合给你建议的人。

## 获取好建议

获取好建议，是你能做的影响最大的事情之一！和一个善于给建议的人聊一小时，就可能彻底改变你接下来几个月的时间安排。大多数人都大大低估了好建议的价值。

这是因为大多数建议都很糟糕。[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:2) 比如，如果大学给你分配 advisers，他们大概会很不靠谱。

大一时，学校给我分配了一位 dental student 做 adviser。我相信她已经尽力了，但她完全不知道任何一门 introductory courses 到底有多 easy 或 hard。（她能告诉我的，大概是“那门 course 以 hard 出名”之类的话，但学生入学时的基础差别如此之大，抽象地说“hard”，完全是毫无意义的描述。）她对我的 education 唯一的贡献，就是说服我不要在第一学期选[那门 hardest intro math course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Math_55)。我不觉得这让我的生活糟糕了_那么_多，但我确实觉得自己错过了一段很棒的时光！

如果那时我还没吸取教训，大二的 adviser 本来会更糟：我把 study card 交给她时，她警告我说，“introductory theoretical statistics”可能会把我毁掉，想必是因为她没听说过 algebraic topology。（她也没理由应该听说过——她学的是 education。）幸好，我成功说服她，我不会有事的。

（当然，我真正需要听到的建议是，[“hard”和“important”并不 related](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/)，但指望在 10 分钟的谈话时间里听到这个，也实在太奢侈了！）

问题在于，到了大学，好建议就已经_极其_取决于你的具体情况——你喜欢什么、什么让你受挫、你有什么样的背景、你可能有什么样的目标——而大多数正式的 advisers 根本不会有时间去了解这些。

因此，你得到的最好建议，大概多数都来自和朋友交流想法——最好是比你年长一点的朋友，不过，同届的人也很可能比随便一位 faculty 更合适。我觉得自己在大学时远远没有充分利用这一点；如果当时多联系一些我知道和自己想法相近的人，我应该会受益不少。

（你也有可能从大学里的权威人士那里得到好建议，但你需要认识到他们的局限和 biases——没有哪个 dental student 能对 math courses 给你好建议，也很少有 professors 会告诉你 [grad school 很烂](https://www.benkuhn.net/grad/)，等等。如果你对他们保持怀疑，并且自己下功夫，让他们只在真正熟悉的领域提供建议，那么你的运气或许会比我好。）

## 坚决无视狗屁

刚入学时，你可能会以为，你的大学经历经过了精心设计，目的是为你提供尽可能好的 education。（哈哈哈。[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:3)）所以，当学校要求你做什么事时，你会倾向于认真对待。

这是个错误。你的大学会向你喷出大量狗屁。能无视就无视；不能无视时，就尽量限制它渗入你生活的程度。

在我看来，以下这些事情最终被证明都是狗屁（当然，具体情况会因学校、field、世界观等而异）：

-   亲自到场听 lectures（用 2x speed 听就行）
-   [所谓“general education”（通识教育）](https://www.benkuhn.net/gened)
-   强制参加的 writing seminar（我的 writing 变差了，而不是变好）
-   文书手续（让我给你讲讲，我为什么没有 computer science 的 minor！）
-   courses 的 Prerequisites（注意：并非总是如此！）
-   许多 courses（但绝对不是所有 courses！）

免得这听起来太愤世嫉俗，我想指出：(a) 大学依然很棒；(b) 无视狗屁能赋予你_不可思议的超能力_，让你专注于那些真正很棒的部分，比如和聪明人相处、学习有难度的东西、尝试不同的工作和活动，等等。

## 别把这些看得太认真

最后，我想说几个你不该把这些建议看得太认真的理由：

-   这些建议非常取决于我的背景——不仅是教育背景，也包括文化和经济背景——以及我的整体看法、人生目标等。如果你在其中任何一个方面和我不同，这些建议对你的适用程度就会降低。

-   我很幸运，上了[一所非常好的高中](https://www.commschool.org/)，所以入学时已经在各门 subjects 上有了扎实的基础。[4](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:4) 这意味着，那些普遍适用的 math/science courses——calculus、linear algebra、programming、algorithms 等——带给我的收获，没有在缺少这些基础的情况下那么大。（我的确从 statistics 和 machine learning courses 中学到了很多东西，而且我觉得，自己选过的另一些 classes 大大提高了我的 general problem-solving ability。）

    这也意味着，我基本上没能从必修的 writing 和 humanities courses 中得到什么，因为它们还不如我高中的课（显然，这种情况并不少见；guidance counselor 提醒过我，但我一直把这当成夸口，直到它真的发生）。

    总之，如果你入学时的基础没有那么多，instruction 的质量对你的重要性，可能会比对我更大；你也可能有机会选更多最终会与你日后生活相关的 courses。

-   我离开学校还没有很久：比我多 10-20 年人生阅历的人，对于什么有价值、什么没有价值，会有不同的看法。遗憾的是，我不认识这个年龄段里和我足够相似、让我觉得其建议会很适用的人。

It’s college decision season! To celebrate, I’ve been thinking about what I would have told myself in 2011 when I was deciding where to attend. Here’s a stitching-together of several emails I sent to friends who asked for college advice, plus a few additional thoughts.

As a caveat: I really do mean that this advice is for people who are _exactly like me_! So please don’t take my word for things—lots won’t apply to you because we’re different. As with all advice: think about it, see what resonates with you, consider [reversing it](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/24/should-you-reverse-any-advice-you-hear/), and generally take it as one of many data points.

## Keep the goal in mind

At the end of college you should probably have a good idea about what to do next.

Here’s the default way to figure that plan out: (1) don’t think about it for three years; (2) get really stressed during your senior year because you have no idea what you want to do; (3) notice that all your friends are interviewing for jobs in consulting, finance, and MicroFaceGoogAzon; (4) accept a job from consulting, finance, or MicroFaceGoogAzon.

This process… could stand to be improved. Plan ahead and don’t make a “safe” decision based on social defaults or fear of not having a job. The most important question to answer is “what kind of jobs exist that (a) I would enjoy and (b) I have a reasonable shot at getting?”

Yes, it is scary and stressful to stare this question (especially b!) in the face, but it should be a _lot_ scarier to waste years doing something safe instead of something awesome.

That isn’t to say you need to know with any amount of certainty what you want to do. (That’s unreasonable and it will change anyway.) Just that it’s good if your choices during college are informed by _some_ broad sense of what they’re setting you up for later. “Plans are useless; planning is indispensable.”

The other useful thing to get out of college is a bunch of awesome, smart and competent friends. I’d recommend optimizing for that, probably more than educational quality or status.

Sub-points:

-   On “density of people who are great at STEM,” my impression is that research universities score a lot better than small liberal arts colleges. I didn’t apply to Caltech, but by some (lagging) metrics it has an [even higher density of accomplished people](https://infoproc.blogspot.com/2015/09/colleges-ranked-by-nobel-fields-turing.html) than other top schools.

-   Control over who you live with/near is a huge selling point, since it mostly determines your social circle. For example, Harvard assigns dorms to groups of upperclassmen randomly—you can pick your roommates but not the other roommate groups that you live near. This makes it hard to find the most awesome people because they’re mixed into boring groups. Other schools like MIT will often have dorms with different cultures and types of people, which makes it easier to find clusters of awesome people. For that reason, if I were choosing colleges today I might have prioritized schools with better housing policies.

-   If you go to a school with a strong subculture, make sure you retain your ability to interact well with “normal people.” The stereotype of, e.g., MIT alums that end up spending the rest of their life only hanging out with other MIT students, does happen to some people. It’s a reasonable personal choice (normal people can be annoying!), but if you’re optimizing for impact on the rest of the world, it will be a handicap.

## Get better at deciding what to do

One thing I wish I’d had more of in college is something like a sense of _taste_: not aesthetic taste, but more “taste for what’s awesome” or “taste in good projects.” (My role model for good project-taste was my friend [Adrian](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Adrian_Veres), who coauthored articles in _Nature_, _Science_ and _Cell_ before graduating.)

Good taste is what will guide you to doing effective things, instead of pointless things, in college. But it’s also really hard to _develop_ good taste while in college, because you have no models of good taste except for academics. Academics’ taste is often deeply weird and driven by what niche will get them tenure rather than what is exciting or useful, so it’s good to have other models of taste too.

My best guess for how to do that is to take a gap year, during which you can front-load learning how the real world works. Summer internships outside academia are also good.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:1)

## Take a gap year

Yes, I just mentioned this above, but it’s concrete and important enough to merit its own section. I didn’t personally take a gap year, but everyone I know who took one found that it enormously improved how they used their time in college, and nobody regretted it.

If the point of a gap year is to improve how you spend your time in college, then you should spend it trying to learn as much as possible about how the world outside college works, and in particular, what that world considers valuable. Try to find projects that let you work with non-academics and produce something genuinely useful.

(It’s common for people to spend their gap years volunteering or traveling. A bit of that will expand your horizons, but spending the whole year on it, while fun, won’t maximize your learning.)

## Bias towards action

One of the biggest new things about college is that you have way more independence. Because your parents no longer oversee your day-to-day life, it takes a lot less friction to do something random or unexpected.

Many college students take advantage of this by filling their schedule with (a) either parties or problem sets, plus (b) lunch dates where they complain about how busy they are. There are more exciting options! Here are some things I did that I think were more valuable on the margin than additional problem sets, even though a lot of them “didn’t work out:”

-   interning at GiveWell (didn’t end up working there, but super valuable for exposure to various people/ideas)
-   running [a student group](http://www.harvardea.org/) (I wasn’t very good at it but it turned out pretty well anyway)
-   trying out being a math teaching assistant (turned out the grading:fun ratio was way too high)
-   trying out research (couldn’t find projects I was excited about)
-   joining a choir (was incredibly fun but I dropped after a year because the schedule was intense)
-   writing a course catalog website (now unmaintained but was the first project I worked on from the beginning to shipping)
-   writing blog posts

Of course, the problem sets are also important! But if you discover you can’t live without them, well, you can have six more years of grad school for that. If you discover you can’t live without parties, I’m not your guy for advice.

## Get good advice

Getting good advice is one of the highest-impact things you can do! A one-hour conversation with a good advice-giver can totally change how you spend months of your time. Most people underrate good advice by a lot.

This is because most advice is bad.[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:2) For instance, if your college assigns you advisers they will probably be terrible.

My freshman year I was assigned a dental student as an adviser. I’m sure she was doing her best, but she had absolutely no idea how easy or hard any of the introductory courses were. (She could tell me things like “that course a reputation for being hard,” but students come in with such different backgrounds that “hard” in the abstract is a completely meaningless description.) Her only contribution to my education was to convince me not to take [the hardest intro math course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Math_55) during my first semester. I don’t think that made my life _that_ much worse, but I do think I missed out on a great time!

My sophomore adviser would have been worse if I hadn’t learned my lesson by that time: when I handed her my study card she warned me that “introductory theoretical statistics” might ruin me, presumably because she hadn’t heard of algebraic topology. (There’s no reason she should have—she was studying education.) Fortunately, I successfully convinced her I’d be fine.

(Of course, the advice I really needed to hear was that [“hard” and “important” aren’t related](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/), but that would have been way too much to ask for a 10-minute conversation slot!)

The problem is that by college, good advice is _incredibly_ context-specific to you—what you enjoy, what frustrates you, what your background is like, what kind of goals you’re likely to have—that most formal advisers won’t ever have the time to understand.

Because of this, most of your best advice will probably come from bouncing things off friends—optimally friends who are a bit older than you, but people in your year are also probably better than random faculty. I think I under-utilized this a lot in college and would have benefited from reaching out more to people who I knew had a similar outlook.

(It’s possible to get good advice from authority figures at your college, but you’ll need to recognize their limitations and biases—no dental student will give you good advice about math courses, few professors will tell you that [grad school sucks](https://www.benkuhn.net/grad/), etc. If you treat them skeptically and do the work yourself to focus their advising on the areas where they’re well-informed, then you might have better luck than I did.)

## Aggressively ignore bullshit

Coming in, you will probably think that your college experience has been carefully designed to provide you the best possible education. (HA HA HA.[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:3)) As a result, when your college asks you to do things, you will feel inclined to take them seriously.

This is a mistake. Your college will spew lots of bullshit at you. Ignore it when you can; when you can’t, try to limit the degree to which it seeps into your life.

Examples of things that, in my opinion, turned out to be bullshit (of course this will vary by school, field, worldview, etc.):

-   Physically attending lectures (just listen to them at 2x speed)
-   [“general education”](https://www.benkuhn.net/gened)
-   Mandatory writing seminar (my writing got worse, not better)
-   Paperwork (let me tell you the story of why I don’t have a minor in computer science!)
-   Prerequisites for courses (note: not always!)
-   Many courses (but very much not always!)

Lest this sound too cynical, let me point out that (a) college is still awesome and (b) ignoring bullshit gives you _crazy superpowers_ to focus on the actually awesome parts, like spending time with smart people, learning difficult things, trying out different jobs and activities, etc.

## Don’t take this too seriously

I’ll close with some reasons you shouldn’t take this advice too seriously:

-   This advice is very specific to my background—not only educational but also cultural/economic—as well as my general outlook, life goals, etc. If you’re different from me on any of these axes it will become less relevant to you.

-   I was lucky to go to [a very good high school](https://www.commschool.org/), so I started out with a strong background in various subjects.[4](https://www.benkuhn.net/college/#fn:4) That meant I didn’t benefit as much from the generally-applicable math/science courses—calculus, linear algebra, programming, algorithms, etc.—as I would have otherwise. (I did learn a bunch from my statistics and machine learning courses, and I think some other classes I took improved my general problem-solving ability a lot.)

    It also meant that I pretty much bounced off of the mandatory writing and humanities courses because they were worse than my high school’s (which apparently isn’t uncommon; the guidance counselor warned me about it, but I wrote it off as bluster until it actually happened).

    Anyway, if you’re coming in with less background, the quality of instruction might matter more than it did for me, and you might be able to take more courses that turn out to be relevant to your later life.

-   I haven’t been out of school for that long: someone with 10-20 years more life experience would have a different perspective on what was valuable and what wasn’t. Unfortunately, I don’t know anyone in that age bracket who was similar enough to me that I think their advice would apply well.
