# Time Management 的迷思

---

## 正文

我时不时会收到一封学生的邮件，对方正在准备一场很难的 exam。他们已经安排好自己的 schedule，计划好了要如何 study，并在邮件末尾紧张地问：“你觉得怎么样？”

我的回答总会让人失望：“嗯，这得看情况……”

有几个显而易见的原因：我不了解这场 exam。我不了解他们的 ability level。我不知道他们已经掌握了什么。

但我的犹疑还有一个更深层的原因：我不知道他们能否 stick to 自己给自己设定的 schedule。

设定一个 schedule 很容易。Sticking to 它——尤其当其中的 work 是 cognitively demanding 时——却极其困难。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-1.jpg)

## Time Management 的迷思

几年前，我开始撰写一篇深入的、research-based 的文章，讨论 productivity 的 history，类似于我的其他 [Complete Guides](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/subscription-complete/)。后来，我放下了这个 project，转而撰写自己的[第二本书](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/getbetter/)，但我至今仍记得，当时读过许多 19th century 的书，讨论正在兴起的 productivity science。

早期关于 productivity 的思考由 factory work 主导。人们以 second 而不是 hour 来理解 Time management；像 [Frederick Winslow Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor) 这样的 theorists，真的会手持 stopwatch，对 worker’s movements 进行 timing，试图从碰巧处于他注视之下的 laborers 身上榨取越来越多的 efficiency。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-2.jpg)

这种对 time 的 focus 一直延续下来，即使“work”越来越多地从 manufacturing parts 转向 manipulating symbols。提出“knowledge worker”这一 term 的 management 大师 [Peter Drucker](https://www.amazon.ca/Effective-Executive-Definitive-Getting-Things/dp/0060833459)，也把 productivity 的问题明确归入了 [managing one’s time](https://www.amazon.com/Effective-Executive-Definitive-Harperbusiness-Essentials/dp/0060833459/ref=sr_1_1?keywords=the+effective+executive&qid=1656002721&sprefix=the+effect%2Caps%2C153&sr=8-1) 的范畴：

> 据我观察，Effective executives 不会从自己的 tasks 入手。他们从自己的 time 入手。他们也不会一开始就进行 planning。他们首先弄清自己的 time 究竟花在了哪里。

对 Drucker 笔下的 executives 而言，work 是由 tasks、interruptions、appointments 和 meetings 汇成的无休止的洪流。他们面临的问题，是设法对大量占用其 time 的 demands 施加 control，而不是我收到的许多学生诉苦邮件中所描述的、stick to 一个 studying schedule 所需的 endurance。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-3-1.jpg)

然而，根据我自己的经历，以及几十年来与读者的通信，我觉得我们大多数人既不像 Taylor 及其同道研究的那些简单的“human machines”，也不像 Drucker 所指导的产业领袖。相反，我们大多数人更像我收件箱里的那些学生：我们想 manage 自己的 time，但真正 sticking to 一个 schedule，远比把它计划出来困难。

我自己也遇到过这个问题。无论是整天做 research 和 writing，还是在 [MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/) 这样的 projects 中努力 stick to 自己设定的 pace，scheduling 各项事情并不难——但真正 doing the work 却很难。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-3-2.jpg)

## Energy 是缺失的关键

近二十年前，我最早几篇受到欢迎的文章中，有一篇是[书评](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2006/07/14/energy-management/)，评介的是 [The Power of Full Engagement](https://www.amazon.ca/Power-Full-Engagement-Managing-Performance/dp/0743226755/)。这是一本出色的书，有助于解释我们许多人都感受到的 gap：根据 available time，我们觉得自己理应能够 accomplish 的事情，与我们日常实际 accomplish 的事情之间的差距。

作者 Tony Schwartz 和 Jim Loehr 认为，high-productivity work 与 athletics 类似：intense effort 之后接着 recovery，这样的 cycles 会 produce training gains，而 non-stop work 和 non-stop rest 都会 result in 更差的 outcomes。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-4.jpg)

Schwartz 和 Loehr 发现，他们典型的 client 在 mentally 方面是 overworked，在 physically 方面却是 underworked。他们长时间 work，却很少 sleep 和 exercise。因此，他们进行 productive work 的 capacity 会 falls；而当他们越来越偏离由 time management 所 defined 的“theoretical” productivity goals 时，他们就决心更加拼命地 work，以弥补这个 gap——这又进一步加深了他们的 exhaustion，并 decreasing 他们的 capacity。

我认为，这类 productivity trap 的普遍存在，有助于解释为什么那么多 productivity advice 会让人反感。听到“productivity”时，我们首先想到的通常是催促自己更加拼命地 work。想到自己的忙碌和不断加重的 exhaustion，我们的 subconscious 一想到额外的 pressure 就会退缩。

但仔细想想，我们最 productive 的时刻其实并不是这样。相反，当我们最 productive 时，work 会带着某种轻盈感。Focus 很容易，work 很有趣，即使结束时觉得 tired，也不会觉得 exhausting。

当你的 energy——而不是你的 time——得到妥善 managed 时，work 就不会让人觉得那么困难。它甚至可以令人愉快。

当我重新讲述自己一些 intensive learning projects 的经历时，人们觉得奇怪的一点是：总体而言，它们并没有那么 effortful。虽然每周 studying 60+ hours 需要 focus，但在进行那些 challenges 时，我大多数日子都觉得相当满足。

这并不是因为我始终都极其 productive（我并不是）。我也经历过许多完成起来十分艰难的 projects，哪怕只完成一两个小时的 work，都感觉像在拔自己的指甲。

Productivity advice 若要真正有帮助，就应该像一张路线图，说明如何让 work 更像那些令人愉快的 projects，而不是那些苦差事。它应该教你如何创造更多这样的情境：即便你正在无比努力地 working，work 本身仍然让人觉得轻松。

## Energy 不只是一种 Fuel

遗憾的是，easy productivity 并没有什么秘诀。Energy 本身可能是一个有些 misleading 的 metaphor。“Fuel” 暗示，受到 constrained 的是某种 internal resource，它可以被 deployed 来应对任何 task，并具有 equal efficacy。

实际上，我们 stick to 自己制定的 schedules 和 plans 时的 ease（或缺乏 ease），会随着 biological 和 psychological 两方面错综复杂的 factors 而 varies。

这些 factors 正是我接下来几周要在一系列文章中 explore 的内容。我正在考察 best-available research 对于如何思考这个问题有何见解。我也会分享，自我第一次写这个话题以来近二十年间，通过自己的 experiments 发现哪些 strategies 最为有效。

此刻，我想用一个 thought experiment 来结束本文：**如果做起 work 来很 easy，你会想做什么 work？** 这个 ideal 或许无法在所有情况下都完全 achievable，但它至少让我们看到，自己想要 achieve 的事情，与自己觉得有 energy 去 accomplish 的事情之间存在怎样的 gap。请在评论中写下你的答案！

## 原文

Every now and then I get an email from a student preparing for a hard exam. They have their schedule planned out for how they’re going to study, and they end the email with a nervous “What do you think?”

Invariably, my answer disappoints: “Well, it depends …”

There are some obvious reasons: I don’t know the exam. I don’t know their ability level. I don’t know what they already know.

But there’s a deeper reason for my ambivalence: I don’t know if they can stick to the schedule they’ve set for themselves.

Setting up a schedule is easy. Sticking to it—especially when the work involved is cognitively demanding—is incredibly hard.

## The Myth of Time Management

A few years ago, I started working on a deep, research-based essay on the history of productivity, akin to my other [Complete Guides](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/subscription-complete/). I eventually pivoted away from the project to work on my [second book](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/getbetter/), but I can still recall reading many 19th century books about the emerging science of productivity.

Early thinking on productivity was dominated by factory work. Time management was understood by the second, not the hour, with theorists like [Frederick Winslow Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor) literally timing worker’s movements with a stopwatch in hand, attempting to squeeze ever-more efficiency out of the laborers who happened to be under his gaze.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-2.jpg)

This focus on time continued, even as “work” increasingly moved from manufacturing parts to manipulating symbols. [Peter Drucker](https://www.amazon.ca/Effective-Executive-Definitive-Getting-Things/dp/0060833459), the management guru who coined the term “knowledge worker” also put the problem of productivity squarely in the realm of [managing one’s time](https://www.amazon.com/Effective-Executive-Definitive-Harperbusiness-Essentials/dp/0060833459/ref=sr_1_1?keywords=the+effective+executive&qid=1656002721&sprefix=the+effect%2Caps%2C153&sr=8-1):

> Effective executives, in my observation, do not start with their tasks. They start with their time. And they do not start out with planning. They start by finding out where their time actually goes.

To Drucker’s executives, work was an unceasing flow of tasks, interruptions, appointments and meetings. The issue they faced was trying to assert control over the torrent of demands on their time, rather than the endurance required to stick to a studying schedule described in so many of the plaintive student emails I’ve received.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-3-1.jpg)

However, based on both my own experience and my correspondence with readers over the decades, I feel like most of us don’t resemble either the simple “human machines” studied by Taylor and his ilk, nor the captains of industry advised by Drucker. Instead, most of us are more like the students in my inbox: we want to manage our time, but actually sticking to a schedule is much harder than planning it out.

I’ve faced this problem myself. Whether it’s doing research and writing all day or trying to stick to the pace I set for myself during projects like the [MIT Challenge](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/), scheduling things isn’t hard—but actually doing the work is.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-3-2.jpg)

## Energy is the Missing Key

One of my first popular essays I wrote nearly two decades ago was [a review](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2006/07/14/energy-management/) of [The Power of Full Engagement](https://www.amazon.ca/Power-Full-Engagement-Managing-Performance/dp/0743226755/), an excellent book that helps explain the gap so many of us feel between what we feel we ought to be able to accomplish given our available time and how much we actually accomplish on a routine basis.

The authors, Tony Schwartz and Jim Loehr, argue that high-productivity work is similar to athletics, where cycles of intense effort followed by recovery produce training gains, and both non-stop work and non-stop rest result in poorer outcomes.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Energy-management-4.jpg)

Schwartz and Loehr found that their typical client is overworked mentally and underworked physically. They work long hours, but sleep and exercise little. As a result, their capacity for productive work falls, and as they slip further from their “theoretical” productivity goals, as defined by time management, they resolve to work ever harder to make up the gap—further entrenching their exhaustion and decreasing their capacity.

I think the prevalence of this type of productivity trap helps to explain why so much productivity advice rubs people the wrong way. When we hear “productivity”, the first thing to come to mind is usually a push to work even harder. Reflecting on our own busyness and mounting exhaustion, our subconscious recoils at the thought of added pressure.

But, if you reflect on it, our most productive moments aren’t actually like that. Instead, when we’re at our most productive, the work has a certain lightness. Focus is easy, the work is interesting, and even if we feel tired at the end, it doesn’t feel exhausting.

When your energy—rather than your time—is managed well, work doesn’t feel all that hard. It can even be joyful.

One of the things people find strange when I’m retelling the stories of some of my intensive learning projects is that they were, on the whole, not so effortful. While studying 60+ hours per week required focus, I felt pretty content most days when doing those challenges.

That wasn’t because I’m always extremely productive (I’m not). I’ve had plenty of projects that were slogs to complete, where getting even an hour or two of work done felt like pulling my fingernails out.

Productivity advice, if it is to be helpful, should be a road map explaining how to make work more like those joyful projects and less like the slogs. It should teach you how to create more situations where, even if you’re working incredibly hard, the work itself feels easy.

## Energy is More Than a Fuel

Unfortunately, there is no secret to easy productivity. Energy itself may be a somewhat misleading metaphor. “Fuel” implies that what’s constrained is some internal resource that can be deployed against any task with equal efficacy.

Instead, the ease (or lack thereof) with which we stick to the schedules and plans we make varies according to a complicated mess of factors both biological and psychological.

These factors are what I’m going to explore over the coming weeks in a series of essays. I’m looking at what the best-available research says about how to think about this problem. I’m also sharing what strategies I’ve found work best, from my own experiments in the nearly two decades since I first wrote about this topic.

Right now, I’d like to end with a thought experiment: **What would you work on if doing the work was easy?** This ideal may not be entirely achievable in all cases, but it at least exposes us to the gap between the things we’d like to achieve and what we feel we have the energy to accomplish.
