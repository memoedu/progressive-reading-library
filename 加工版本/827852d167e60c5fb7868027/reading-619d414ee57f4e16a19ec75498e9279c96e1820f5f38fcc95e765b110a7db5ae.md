# Valuing intelligence

---

## 正文

Scott Alexander 最近写了一篇文章，谈论[为什么不该因为自己不如别人 smart 而难过](http://slatestarcodex.com/2015/01/31/the-parable-of-the-talents/)。

> ……上周的一条评论：
> 
> > 很抱歉留下这样一条自我关注过度的评论，但读到这些真的让我很难受，我实在需要一吐为快……在一种把 intelligence 看得比什么都重要的 culture 中，尤其是如果正如 Scott 所说，Human Intelligence Really Is the Key to the Future，而一个人自己并不特别 intelligent，而且显然也没有任何变得 intelligent 的 potential，那么这个人究竟要怎样才能保持 sane？此刻我基本上觉得自己就是池塘里的浮渣。
> 
> 有些人认为 body weight 是 biologically/genetically determined。另一些人认为它完全取决于 willpower——你 diet 得有多严格，你能让自己进行多少 exercise。这些人会陷入相当激烈的争论。……尽管这里的 science 存在许多争论，但双方似乎普遍同意，更 compassionate、sympathetic、progressive 的 position，也就是真正担忧 stigma 和 self-esteem 的那类人所倡导的 position，是 weight 是 biologically determined。
> 
> mental illness 也是如此。……同样，争论中 compassionate/sympathetic/progressive 的一方认为，depression 是某种 biological 的东西，不能轻易靠 willpower 和 hard work 来 overcome。……显而易见的 pattern 是，把 outcomes 归因于 genes、biology 和 accidents of birth 之类的东西，是 kind 而 sympathetic 的。把它们归因于谁 works harder、谁在“really trying”，可能会 stigmatize 最终遭遇 bad outcomes 的人，而且通常被认为是不厚道的做法。
> 
> 奇怪的是，也是我始终不理解的是，intellectual achievement 是唯一打破这一 pattern 的 domain。
> 
> 在这里，是那些自诩头脑冷静的 conservatives 在主张 intellectual greatness 来自 genetics 和 accidents of birth，并要求我们“接受”这一“令人不快的事实”。
> 
> 而那些自诩 compassionate 的 progressives 却坚持说，不，它取决于谁 works harder；他们声称，只要真的努力，任何人都可以 brilliant，并警告我们，不要把较不 intelligent 的人“stigmatize”为“genetically inferior”。
> 
> ……我选择通过保留这样一种信念来 maintain consistency：overweight 的人、depressed 的人和 poor 的人，并不应当为自己的处境承担全部责任——unintelligent 的人也一样。归根结底，全都是 accidents of birth。Intelligence 在很大程度上是 genetic 且 determined at birth 的——而在其他每个领域，我们早已认定，“在很大程度上是 genetic 且 determined at birth 的”意味着，如果你运气不好，你不必因此难过。

（我不想让这段摘录变得更长，但你真的应该[直接读读原文](http://slatestarcodex.com/2015/01/31/the-parable-of-the-talents/)。）

* * *

在对此发表看法之前，我应该先作一个声明。我在这里谈到“intelligence”时，指的是在一团似乎彼此都 correlate 的 cognitive tasks 上的 performance。这些 scores 似乎也与“intelligence”的各种日常含义 correlate——比如 solve math problems 或 make puns 的 ability，或者，对 Scott 的那位评论者来说更切题的，write witty blog comments 的 ability。这一团东西似乎也与 income、educational attainment 等重要的 life outcomes correlate。我们目前对这些 traits 的了解，也还不足以为人们 reliably improve 它们，除了一些显而易见的事情，比如不要[poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_poisoning) [他们](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline#Tetraethyllead)。我们可以教人们如何 solve 特定 classes 的 math problems，或 make 特定 types 的 puns，但对于整个 correlation-blob，我们无能为力。

我要强调，我_并不是_在声称 intelligence 这个东西具有任何 [causal relevance](http://bactra.org/weblog/523.html)。例如，一个合理的 alternate hypothesis 是，intelligence 这个 idea 是编造出来的，目的是通过给人们 low scores 来 oppress 他们，而 oppression 也往往会沿着许多 dimensions depress 人们的其他 outcomes，因此便有了这种 correlation。我也_并不是_在声称 intelligence 这个东西是 fixed；同样 plausible 的是，我们只是还不明白怎样把它教好。但无论这些问题最终如何解决，有一点肯定是真的：很多人在这团 correlated metrics 上最终所处的位置，低于他们希望达到的位置，而我们还不真正理解他们怎样才能 improve。

* * *

好，回到 Scott 的文章：看来，你对自己在“intelligence”这个东西上的表现，其实并没有那么多 control，所以你不该拿自己与那些在这方面走运的人 compare，然后感到难过。

Ruthie 指出，如果你难过是因为希望自己更 smarter，那么这番话其实帮不上你多少。那更多是 emotional 层面的事。如果你的 social groups 重视 intelligence，那么，听别人说你的 intelligence 是 accident of birth，可能比听别人说你只需要 work harder 还要_更_伤人——因为其中隐含着这样的意思：“你无法 change 自己有多 intelligent……所以 society 永远不会重视你。”当然，Scott 这样的人并不是这个意思，但那层潜台词仍然存在。

* * *

Scott 的文章是写给那些因为觉得自己不如所在 social groups 中的人 smart 而难过的人的。但也许，写给那些_没有_这种感受的人会更好。

如果大多数 overweight 的人对自己的 weight 没有多少 control，你就不该_只是_告诉 overweight 的人，没关系，因为他们对此无能为力。你确实要这样说，因为这比反过来说更好，但这并不能改善他们的处境。要改善他们的处境，你得让 media 不再把 physical attractiveness 看得那么重要，也不再把 weight 当作 physical attractiveness 中如此重要的一部分——也就是普遍创造出这样的 social spaces：overweight 的人可以进入其中，而不觉得自己的 personal worth 与某种自己无法 change 的东西相联系。

同样，如果 Scott 的那位评论者因为自己并不特别 intelligent 而觉得 worthless，你就不该_只是_告诉对方：“别担心，你对此无能为力。”这样的安慰可真够冷冰冰的！你还需要解决他们不断被告知自己 worthless 的问题。你需要解决人们因为自己 control 之外的东西而受到重视的那个问题。

* * *

但对于 intelligence 这样的东西，这_真的很难_。

原因在于，“achieving personal goals”正是构成“intelligence”的那个庞大 correlation-blob 的一部分。事实上，有些人把 [intelligence 定义](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence#Definition)为“一个 agent 在 wide range of environments 中 achieve goals 的 ability”。而大多数人都有一个 goal，就是感到自己受到重视。所以，即使我们不再把人们的 personal worth 与其 intelligence 直接联系起来，无论我们用什么 channels 来取代它，smarter 的人仍然会更容易通过那些 channels 受到重视。

假设我们开始因为人们 kind，而不是 intelligent，就重视他们。（我倒希望如此！）Kindness 在你自己的 control 之下，对吧？嗯，算是吧。但那些更善于想出巧妙办法、公开表现得 kind 的人，仍然会更善于博取 social favor。

如果我们开始重视 honesty，那么人们就会形成让自己 honest 的 goals。而那些更善于找出令人不舒服的 truths 并说出来的人，就会胜出。以此类推。

事实上，这在某种程度上已经发生了。如果你问人们，他们是否认为 raw cognitive ability 是 intrinsically valuable 的，我相信大多数人都会说不是。只是碰巧，他们_确实_重视的许多东西，比如写出好的博客文章、拥有成功的事业，甚至讲出好笑的笑话，也都是 correlation-blob 的一部分。因此，要阻止 social respect 中最大的一份流向那些赢得 cognitive lottery 的人，似乎相当困难——似乎比在 gender、race 或 physical appearance 等方面做到这一点还要困难，而后面这些本来就已经够难了。

* * *

遗憾的是，我目前看不到什么很好的解决办法。我们最多只能试着弄清楚，什么事情_确实_让某个人付出了大量 effort，而不只是看起来对你而言会很难的事情。这基本上也是 Scott 对自己的 self-esteem 得出的结论：

> 这也是为什么，对我来说，相信 innate ability，尤其是相信 innate ability 的 differences，很重要。如果一切都归结为 hard work 和 positive attitude，那么 God 完全有权问我：“你为什么不是 Srinivasa Ramanujan？”或者“你为什么不是 Elon Musk？”
> 
> 如果每个人确实都是不同的人，有着不同的 brain、不同的 talents 和 abilities，那么 God 能问我的，就只有我到底有没有成为 Scott Alexander。
> 
> 这个门槛似乎低得令人欣慰。

我们只需要记住，_我们_也需要重视那些不是 Ramanujan 的人——以及那些不是 Scott 的人。这才是真正能改善人们处境的部分。

## 原文

Scott Alexander recently wrote about [why you shouldn’t feel bad for not being as smart as other people](http://slatestarcodex.com/2015/01/31/the-parable-of-the-talents/).

> …a comment from last week:
> 
> > I’m sorry to leave self a self absorbed comment, but reading this really upset me and I just need to get this off my chest… How is a person supposed to stay sane in a culture that prizes intelligence above everything else — especially if, as Scott suggests, Human Intelligence Really Is the Key to the Future — when they themselves are not particularly intelligent and, apparently, have no potential to ever become intelligent? Right now I basically feel like pond scum.
> 
> Some people think body weight is biologically/genetically determined. Other people think it’s based purely on willpower — how strictly you diet, how much you can bring yourself to exercise. These people get into some pretty acrimonious debates. … Although there’s a lot of debate over the science here, there seems to be broad agreement on both sides that the more compassionate, sympathetic, progressive position, the position promoted by the kind of people who are really worried about stigma and self-esteem, is that weight is biologically determined.
> 
> And the same is true of mental illness. … Once again, the compassionate/sympathetic/progressive side of the debate is that depression is something like biological, and cannot easily be overcome with willpower and hard work. … The obvious pattern is that attributing outcomes to things like genes, biology, and accidents of birth is kind and sympathetic. Attributing them to who works harder and who’s “really trying” can stigmatize people who end up with bad outcomes and is generally viewed as Not A Nice Thing To Do.
> 
> And the weird thing, the thing I’ve never understood, is that intellectual achievement is the one domain that breaks this pattern.
> 
> Here it’s would-be hard-headed conservatives arguing that intellectual greatness comes from genetics and the accidents of birth and demanding we “accept” this “unpleasant truth”.
> 
> And it’s would-be compassionate progressives who are insisting that no, it depends on who works harder, claiming anybody can be brilliant if they really try, warning us not to “stigmatize” the less intelligent as “genetically inferior”.
> 
> …I choose to maintain consistency by preserving the belief that overweight people, depressed people, and poor people aren’t fully to blame for their situation—and neither are unintelligent people. It’s accidents of birth all the way down. Intelligence is mostly genetic and determined at birth—and we’ve already determined in every other sphere that “mostly genetic and determined at birth” means you don’t have to feel bad if you got the short end of the stick.

(I don’t want to make the excerpt any longer, but you should really [just read it](http://slatestarcodex.com/2015/01/31/the-parable-of-the-talents/).)

* * *

Before I say things about this, I should make a disclaimer. When I talk about “intelligence” here, I’m referring to performance on a blob of cognitive tasks that all seem to correlate with each other. These scores also seem to correlate with various colloquial meanings of “intelligence”—like ability to solve math problems or make puns or, more relevantly for Scott’s commenter, write witty blog comments. The blob also seems to correlate with important life outcomes like income, educational attainment, and so on. We also don’t currently understand these traits well enough to reliably improve them for people, aside from obvious things like not [poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_poisoning) [them](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline#Tetraethyllead). We can teach people how to solve specific classes of math problems or make specific types of puns, but we can’t do anything about the whole correlation-blob.

I’m emphatically _not_ claiming that this intelligence thing has any [causal relevance](http://bactra.org/weblog/523.html). For instance, a reasonable alternate hypothesis is that the idea of intelligence was made up to oppress people by giving them low scores on it, and oppression also tends to depress people’s other outcomes along many dimensions, hence the correlation. I’m also _not_ claiming that the intelligence thing is fixed; it’s equally plausible that we just don’t understand how to teach it well yet. But regardless of how these questions resolve, it’s definitely true that there are a lot of people who end up below where they want to be on this blob of correlated metrics, and we don’t really understand yet how they could improve.

* * *

OK, so back to Scott’s post: it seems like you don’t actually have that much control over how well you do on this “intelligence” thing, so you shouldn’t compare yourself to other people who lucked out on it and feel bad.

Ruthie pointed out that this doesn’t really do much for you if you’re sad because you wish you were smarter. That’s more of an emotional thing. If your social groups value intelligence, then being told that your intelligence is an accident of birth can hurt _more_ than being told that you should just work harder—because there’s an undertone of, “you can’t change how intelligent you are… and therefore society will never value you.” Of course that’s not what people like Scott mean, but the undercurrent is there just the same.

* * *

Scott’s essay was addressed to people who felt bad because they felt less smart than their social groups. But it might have been better addressed to people who _didn’t_ feel that way.

If most overweight people don’t have much control over their weight, you don’t _just_ tell overweight people that it’s okay because they can’t help it. You do that, because it’s better than the alternative, but that’s not how you make things better for them. You make things better by getting the media to stop making physical attractiveness so important, and stop making weight such an important part of physical attractiveness—by generally creating social spaces where overweight people can go without feeling like their personal worth is connected to something they can’t change.

Similarly, if Scott’s commenter feels worthless for not being particularly intelligent, you shouldn’t _just_ tell them “don’t worry, there’s nothing you can do about it.” That’s some cold comfort! You need to fix the problem where they keep getting told they’re worthless, too. You need to fix the part where people are valued for things that aren’t under their control.

* * *

But for something like intelligence, that’s _really hard_.

The reason is that “achieving personal goals” is one of the parts of the massive correlation-blob that makes up “intelligence.” In fact, some people [define intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence#Definition) as “an agent’s ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments.” Well, most people have a goal of feeling valued. So even if we stop connecting people’s personal worth directly to their intelligence, smarter people are still going to have an easier time being valued through whatever channels we replace it with.

Suppose we start valuing people for being kind, instead of intelligent. (I wish!) Kindness is under your own control, right? Well, sort of. But people who are better at thinking up clever ways to be publicly kind will still be better at currying social favor.

If we start valuing honesty, then people will develop goals of being honest. And the people who are better at figuring out uncomfortable truths to tell will come out ahead. And so on.

In fact, this is already happening to some extent. If you asked people whether they think raw cognitive ability is intrinsically valuable, I’m sure most of them would say no. It just so happens that a lot of the stuff they _do_ value, like writing good blog posts or having a successful career or even making funny jokes, are also part of the correlation-blob. So it seems pretty hard to stop the lion’s share of social respect from going to people who won the cognitive lottery—harder than it seems to be for, say, gender or race or physical appearance, and those are quite hard enough.

* * *

Unfortunately, I don’t currently see a great solution. The best we can do is try to figure out what _really_ took someone a lot of effort, not just what seems like it would be hard for you. And that’s basically the conclusion that Scott comes to about his own self-esteem:

> And this is part of why it’s important for me to believe in innate ability, and especially differences in innate ability. If everything comes down to hard work and positive attitude, then God has every right to ask me “Why weren’t you Srinivasa Ramanujan?” or “Why weren’t you Elon Musk?”
> 
> If everyone is legitimately a different person with a different brain and different talents and abilities, then all God gets to ask me is whether or not I was Scott Alexander.
> 
> This seems like a gratifyingly low bar.

We just have to remember that _we_ need to value the non-Ramanujans—and the non-Scotts—too. That’s the part that actually makes things better for people.
