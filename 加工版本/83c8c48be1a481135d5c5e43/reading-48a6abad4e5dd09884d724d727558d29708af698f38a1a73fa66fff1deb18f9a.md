## 核心洞察

一、石英危机实为三重灾难叠加：瑞士钟表业在1970年代初遭遇的并非单一事件，而是日本竞争、布雷顿森林体系崩溃导致瑞郎升值、以及石英机芯普及三场独立灾难几乎同时爆发。即使没有石英机芯，前两者也足以摧毁该行业，石英只是让整个以精准计时为核心的竞争游戏变得无关紧要。
[[#^pr-78b4e8672383|正文跳转]]

二、从精密仪器到奢侈品牌的转型：幸存下来的瑞士钟表商将自身从精密仪器制造商转变为奢侈品牌，机械表的本质也随之改变：昂贵不再因为制造成本高和走时精准，而是因为品牌在广告上大量投入并人为限制供应，买家获得的是身份象征而非计时工具。
[[#^pr-3fb270fdea75|正文跳转]]

三、品牌是产品实质差异消失后的剩余物：品牌在产品的实质性差异消失时凸显，而技术天然倾向于消除这些差异。因此瑞士钟表业的经历并非孤例，而是当代许多行业的普遍故事：当性能趋同，竞争便转向品牌表达。
[[#^pr-074ca2d235ff|正文跳转]]

四、品牌与好设计存在根本冲突：品牌要求独特性，而好设计像数学或科学一样追求正确答案，正确答案倾向于趋同。因此品牌是离心力，设计是向心力。品牌化不仅与好设计正交，而且与之对立，这一冲突在宗教、艺术和手表设计中都有体现。
[[#^pr-002e9ee7d86f|正文跳转]]

五、黄金时代追求薄与准的权衡：1945至1970年的钟表黄金时代，制表师最追求薄度和精准度，其中薄度比精准度更难实现，因此更常区分昂贵表款。复杂功能除日期显示外基本是配角，顶级制表师专注于这一本质权衡并达到此后难以超越的完美。
[[#^pr-6a907a905b90|正文跳转]]

六、欧米茄与百达翡丽的不同应对：欧米茄坚持性能路线，制造更精准的机芯，结果因脆弱损害声誉，最终在1981年破产。百达翡丽则转向重新设计表壳，将品牌表达从表盘上的微小名字扩展到整个表壳，把品牌面积从8平方毫米扩大到800平方毫米。
[[#^pr-bee5c191fc5f|正文跳转]]

七、皇家橡树与鹦鹉螺的品牌化推进：爱彼1972年推出皇家橡树，用钢材质卖出黄金价格，并将表壳与金属表链整合，使品牌表达覆盖整个手腕。百达翡丽随后委托同一设计师推出鹦鹉螺，尺寸巨大且带有装饰性突起，虽超前于时代，却成为日后最受追捧的表款。
[[#^pr-f04d96e923c5|正文跳转]]

八、品牌时代中质量变成门槛：当产品转向因品牌而被购买时，质量并未停止重要，但形态改变：它变成门槛。产品不再需要好到能卖出自己，品牌负责销售，但质量必须足够好以维持品牌声誉，品牌绝不能破坏角色。
[[#^pr-aefcddd3f766|正文跳转]]

九、人造稀缺与资产泡沫管理：百达翡丽通过多层购买和等待名单制造人造稀缺，并在二手市场回购自家手表以追踪泄漏、惩罚违规买家和零售商。其目标是维持顶级表款二手价格高于零售价，从而小心管理一个持续的资产泡沫，避免价格崩溃引发连锁反应。
[[#^pr-9ae73bf5ec0e|正文跳转]]

十、品牌时代缺乏功能约束导致怪异：机械表在黄金时代是必需品，功能约束赋予其有意义的形态。品牌时代的手表没有实用功能，其功能是表达品牌，而品牌约束依赖人性中最糟糕的特征，因此只由品牌定义的世界会变得怪异而糟糕。
[[#^pr-9d8a2ddeaa97|正文跳转]]

十一、跟随问题而非寻找黄金时代：领域有自然节奏，个人难以抵抗。找到黄金时代的方法不是直接寻找它们，而是跟随有趣的问题。如果你聪明、有雄心且对自己诚实，对问题的品味就是最好的指引，去有趣问题所在之处，很可能发现其他同样的人，日后他们回顾时便会称之为黄金时代。

## 正文

# 231 The Brand Age

March 2026

在 1970 年代初，disaster 袭击了 Swiss watch industry。现在人们把它叫做 quartz crisis，但事实上它是三场各自独立的 disaster 的复合体，它们都大约在同一时间发生。

第一场是来自 Japan 的 competition。整个 1960 年代，the Swiss 一直从后视镜里观察 the Japanese，而他们一直在以惊人的速度进步。但即便如此，the Swiss 在 1968 年还是感到意外，当时 the Japanese 在 Geneva Observatory trials 上横扫了 mechanical watches 的所有 top spots。

The Swiss 知道什么即将到来。多年来 the Japanese 一直能够制造更便宜的 watches。现在他们也能制造更好的了。

更糟的是，Swiss watches 即将变得昂贵得多。The Bretton Woods agreement，自 1945 年以来一直固定着世界上大多数 currencies 的 exchange rates，曾把 Swiss Franc 设定在 .228 USD 的人为低 rate。当 Bretton Woods 在 1973 年崩溃时，the Franc 急剧上升。到 1978 年它达到了 .625 USD，这意味着 Swiss watches 对 Americans 来说现在贵了 2.7 倍。 [1](#fn1)

foreign competition 和失去其 protective exchange rate 的联合效应，即使没有 quartz movements，也会摧毁 Swiss watch industry。但 quartz movements 是最后一击。现在他们一直试图赢下的整个 game 变得无关紧要了。曾经昂贵的东西——知道 exact time——现在成了一种 commodity。

^pr-78b4e8672383

在 1970 年代初到 1980 年代初之间，Swiss watches 的 unit sales 下降了近三分之二。大多数 Swiss watchmakers 变得 insolvent 或接近如此，并被出售。但不是全部。少数作为 independent companies 幸存了下来。而他们做到这一点的方式，是把自己从 precision instrument makers 转变为 luxury brands。

在这个过程中，mechanical watch 的本质也被转变了。最昂贵的 watches 一直花费很多，但它们为什么花费很多以及 buyers 得到了什么回报已经完全改变了。在 1960 年，expensive watches 花费很多是因为它们制造成本很高，而 buyer 得到的回报是就其尺寸而言所能制造的最准确的 timekeeping device。现在它们花费很多是因为 brands 在 advertising 上花费很多并使用 tricks 来限制 supply，而 buyer 得到的回报是一个昂贵的 status symbol。

^pr-3fb270fdea75

不过事实证明这是一门有利可图的生意。Swiss watch industry 现在从 selling brand 中赚到的可能比他们如果还在 selling engineering 会赚到的更多。事实上，当你查看 Swiss watch sales by revenue 的 graph 时，它讲述的故事与 unit sales 的 graph 不同。revenue 数字没有跌落悬崖，而只是平缓了一段时间，然后在 1980 年代末像火箭一样起飞，因为幸存的 watchmakers 接受了他们的新命运。

watchmakers 花了大约 20 年才弄清楚 game 的新 rules。看着他们这样做很有趣，因为他们转变的彻底性使它成为我们时代最强大的力量之一的完美 case study：brand。

Brand 是当 products 之间的 substantive differences 消失时所剩下的东西。但让 products 之间的 substantive differences 消失正是 technology 自然倾向于做的事。所以发生在 Swiss watch industry 身上的事不仅仅是一个有趣的 outlier。它非常是我们时代的故事。

^pr-074ca2d235ff

Jaeger-LeCoultre 的 web site 说他们当前的一个 collections“takes its inspiration from the classic designs of the golden age of watchmaking。”在这样说时，他们隐含地说了一件 present-day watchmakers 都知道但很少如此接近直说的话：无论我们现在处于什么 age，它都不是 the golden age。

The golden age 是从 1945 年到 1970 年——从 watch industry 从战争的混乱中脱颖而出、the Swiss 居于顶端的那一刻起，直到从 60 年代末开始袭击它的 triple cataclysm。在 the golden age，watchmakers 最追求的两样东西是：thinness 和 accuracy。事实上这可以说是 watchmaking 中 essential tradeoff。A watch 是你随身携带用来告诉你时间的东西。所以有两种 fundamental ways 来改进它：让它更容易随身携带，以及让它更擅长 telling the time。

^pr-6a907a905b90

显然 accuracy 是有价值的，但在 the golden age，thinness 如果说有什么不同的话是更有价值。即使在 pocket watches 的时代，最好的 watchmakers 也试图把他们的 watches 做得尽可能薄。廉价、厚重的 pocket watches 被嘲笑为“turnips。”但当 men’s watches 在 World War I 期间移到手腕上时，thinness 有了新的紧迫性。而且由于 thinness 比 accuracy 更难实现，正是这种品质倾向于区分 the golden age 中更昂贵的 watches。

watchmakers 在某些时代还追求过另一件事：以通常方式之外的方式 telling more than the time。例如告诉你 the phase of the moon，或者用声音 telling the time。在行业中这些东西的术语是“complications。”它们在 19 世纪很流行，现在又流行起来，但除了一个 pragmatic complication（showing the date）之外，它们在 the golden age 只是 a sideshow。在 the golden age，一如在 golden ages 中总是如此，top watchmakers 专注于 essential tradeoff。而且，一如在 golden ages 中总是如此，他们做得非常漂亮。The golden age 中最好的 watches 有一种 [quiet perfection](https://goldammer.me/products/vacheron-constantin-18k-white-gold)，此后从未被超越。而且出于我即将解释的原因，可能永远不会被超越。

The golden age 中三个最有声望的 brands 是所谓的“holy trinity”：Patek Philippe、Vacheron Constantin 和 Audemars Piguet。他们的 prestige 大多是应得的；他们通过其工作的卓越品质赢得了它。到 1960 年代，他们站在两条腿上：prestige 和 performance。而他们在接下来二十年里学到的是，他们必须把全部重量放在第一条腿上，因为他们再也无法在 watchmakers 历史上一直努力实现的两件事中的任何一件上获胜。Quartz movements 不仅比任何 mechanical movement 更准确，而且也更薄。

The holy trinity 至少还有另一条腿可以站。大多数其他知名的 Swiss watchmakers 只卖 performance。那些公司没有一家完好地幸存下来。

Omega 展示了不该做什么。Omega 是 Swiss watchmakers 中的书呆子。他们制造了极其准确的 watches，但对于成为 luxury brand 的想法，他们充其量是矛盾的。当 the Japanese 在制造 accurate movements 方面变得和 the Swiss 一样好时，Omega 以 Omega 的方式回应：制造更准确的 movements。他们在 1968 年推出了一款新的 movement，以高出 45% 的 frequency 运行。理论上这应该使它更准确，但这款新 movement 如此脆弱，以至于毁掉了他们 reliability 的声誉。他们甚至试图制造更好的 quartz movement，但那条路上除了 a race to the bottom 什么都没有。到 1981 年他们 insolvent，被他们的 creditors 接管。

^pr-bee5c191fc5f

Patek Philippe 采取了相反的做法。当 Omega 在重新设计他们的 movements 时，Patek 在重新设计他们的 cases。或者更准确地说，是在设计他们的 cases，因为在那之前他们并没有。

这大概是提到 Swiss watch industry 在那些日子里是一头多么奇怪的野兽的合适时机。那是一种今天难以想象的 capitalism，即使在当时也只能在像 Switzerland 这样的国家里被弄得运转起来——一个由 regulation 锁定到位的小型、专业化公司的 network。我们为了方便一直称为 watchmakers 的那些公司，只是这个 network 面向消费者的一端。The holy trinity 不设计他们自己的 cases，大多数时候甚至不设计他们自己的 movements。

在 1968 年（又是那一年），Patek Philippe 推出了一款新的 watch，它改变了 case design 的重心。这一次他们把自己的 designs 拿给 casemakers 并说“这就是你们要为我们制造的。”结果是一款引人注目的新 model，叫做 [Golden Ellipse](https://collectability.com/learn/the-history-of-the-golden-ellipse/)。有点令人困惑，因为它并不是 elliptical。新的 case 更像是 UI designers 会称之为 round rect 的东西：一个带圆角的 rectangle。而这个新的 watches 家族相当成功。但它不止于此：它是未来的 pattern。 [2](#fn2)

仅仅设计一个独特的 case 怎么会如此重要？因为它把整个 watch 变成了 brand 的一种表达。

从想要用自己所戴 watch 的 brand 给人们留下印象的人的角度来看，the golden age 中最好的 watches 的麻烦在于，没有人能看出你戴的是什么 brand 的 watch。直到你离它们只有几英寸，所有 top makers 的 watches 看起来都一样。这就是 minimalism 的特点：往往只有一个答案。此外，the golden age 的 watches 按现在的标准来看很小。Watchmakers 花了几个世纪努力把它们做得更小，到 1960 年他们已经非常擅长于此。所以区分一个 top brand 与另一个的唯一东西是印在 dial 上的名字，而 dials 如此之小，以至于这些名字非常微小。The holy trinity 的 golden age watches 上的 manufacturers’ names 高度在半毫米到四分之三毫米之间。通过接管 case，Patek 把 brand 的尺寸从 8 square millimeters 扩大到 800。

为什么他们在低声细语了一个世纪之后突然决定让他们的 brand 大声喊叫？因为他们知道他们不会在 performance 上打败 the Japanese。从现在起他们必须更多地依赖 brand。

这样做是有代价的，即使在这个 case-as-brand 的早期例子中我们也能看到。Golden Ellipses 并不难看。在 1970 年代它们一定看起来更酷，当时 designers 把一切都变成 round rects。但 Golden Ellipse 并不是 case design 中的进化性进步。Watches 并没有全都变成 round rects。Watchmakers 早已发现了某种在旋转时描述一个 circle 的东西的 case 的最佳形状。

他们还发现了 crown 的最佳形状，crown 是 watch 侧面你转动来上链的那个 knob。但为了强调 Ellipse 独特的 profile，Patek 把 crown 做得太小，结果它们上链时难得令人分心。 [3](#fn3)

所以即使在这个早期例子中，我们也看到了关于 brand 和 design 之间关系的一个重要观点。Branding 不仅仅与 good design 正交，而是与它对立。Branding 按定义必须是 distinctive。但 good design，像 math 或 science 一样，寻求 right answer，而 right answers 倾向于 converge。

^pr-002e9ee7d86f

Branding 是 centrifugal；design 是 centripetal。

当然这里有一些回旋余地。Design 没有像 math 那样定义得那么清晰的 right answers，尤其是面向人类受众的 design。所以如果你有诚实的动机，做一些 distinctive 的事并不一定是 bad design。但你无法逃避 branding 和 design 之间的 fundamental conflict，就像你无法逃避 gravity 一样。

事实上，branding 和 design 之间的 conflict 如此 fundamental，以至于它远远超出了我们称之为 design 的东西。我们甚至在 religion 中也能看到它。如果你想让一个 religion 的 adherents 拥有使他们与其他人区别开来的 customs，你不能让他们做方便或合理的事，否则其他人也会做这些事。如果你想让你的 adherents 与众不同，你必须让他们做不方便且不合理的事。

如果你想让你的 designs 与众不同，也是一样。如果你选择 good options，其他人也会选择它们。

只有两种方式能把 branding 和 good design 结合起来。你可以在 possibilities 的空间极其 large 时做到这一点，比如在 painting 中就是如此。Leonardo 可以尽可能好地 paint，同时又以一种 distinctively 属于他自己的 style 来 paint。如果有 a million 个像 Bellini 和 Leonardo 一样好的 painters，这会更难做到，但既然实际上大约只有十个，他们彼此之间就没有太多 bump up against。 [4](#fn4)

另一种能把 branding 和 good design 结合起来的情形，是 possibilities 的空间相对 unexplored。如果你是第一个到达某个 new territory 的人，你既可以 find the right answer，又可以把它 claim 为 uniquely yours。至少一开始是这样；如果你真的找到了 the right answer，其他所有人的 designs 都不可避免地会 converge on yours，而你的 brand advantage 会随着时间 erode。

既然 watch design 的空间既不是 unexplored 也不是极其 large，branding 就只能以牺牲 good design 为代价来实现。而事实上，如果你想要一句话来描述 watchmaking 的当前 age，那一句就相当合适。

Patek Philippe 并不确定 making visibly branded watches 会 work。在当时，这甚至不是他们唯一的 strategy。他们是在 finding their way。但正是这个 strategy 确实 work 了，至少以 revenues 来衡量是这样。

要让它 work，customers 必须 meet them halfway。Patek 知道并非所有 customers 都是因为他们所交付的 performance——因为他们的 accuracy 和 thinness——而购买他们的 watches。他们知道至少有一些 customers 购买它们是因为它们 expensive。但究竟有多少人，或者他们能被 push 到多远，并不清楚。

为了 encourage 他们，Patek 做了一件 holy trinity 此前都没怎么做过的事：brand advertising。而他们谈论的是他们的 watches 有多么 expensive。一则 1968 年的 Patek ad 解释了“why you are well advised to invest perhaps half a month’s income”在一只 Ellipse 上。“Like every Patek Philippe，”这则 ad 继续说道，“this thin model is entirely finished by hand. Since a Patek Philippe is the costliest watch to make, production is severely limited: only 43 watches are signed out each day for delivery to prominent jewelers throughout the world.” [5](#fn5)

你可以看出这是一则早期的 ad，因为他们仍然提到 thinness。但完全没有提到 accuracy。想必 Patek 觉得那场 battle 已经输了。

The next move 是由 Audemars Piguet 做出的，他们在 1970 年委托著名 designer Gérald Genta 为他们设计自己的 iconic watch，这一只，大胆地，用 steel。结果，于 1972 年推出，就是 [Royal Oak](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2025/fine-%20watches-5/reference-5402st-jumbo-royal-oak-a-series-a)。而 Audemars Piguet 的 ads（因为他们现在也开始做 brand advertising 了）甚至更加戏剧性地强调了它的 high cost。“Introducing steel at the price of gold，”一则这样开头。“You’re looking at the costliest stainless steel watch in the world – the Audemars Piguet ‘Royal Oak’. What makes it even more precious than gold is the time that went into building it, by a vanishing breed of master watchmakers.”在 ad 的底部，他们把传统的公式颠倒过来，把他们的 watches 描述为“priced from $35,000 and down.”

The Royal Oak 在 devoted to brand 的 surface area 上也是一次进步。The Golden Ellipse 曾把 watch face 变成 brand 的一种 expression，但它使用的是普通的 straps 和 bracelets。在 the Royal Oak 中，watch face 与一条 metal bracelet 整合在一起，这条 bracelet 把它的 design 一直延续到整个 wrist 周围。当它说“You’re looking at the costliest stainless steel watch in the world”时，它是用每一 square millimeter 的 surface area 在说这句话。

^pr-f04d96e923c5

customers 会买这种 new approach 吗？最初的结果是适度令人鼓舞的。holy trinity 的 sales 没有 take off，但也没有 go down to zero。至少有一些人在那里 responding to the new message。也许如果他们 keep at it，这个 number 会 grow。

于是他们就这么做了。受到 the Royal Oak 的 success 的鼓励，Patek Philippe 在 1974 年委托 Gérald Genta 为他们设计一只类似的 watch。The Royal Oak 的 design 曾受到 a ship’s porthole 的启发，所以这只 new watch 的 design 将受到……a ship’s porthole 的启发。它被称为 [Nautilus](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2025/important-%20watches-2/reference-3700-11-nautilus-jumbo-a-stainless-steel)，并于 1976 年在 Basel Watch Fair 上推出。

在 the Nautilus 中，我们真正看到了 branding 和 design 的 incompatibility。它 huge。在 golden age 的巅峰时期，最 expensive 的 men’s watches 通常是 32 或 33 millimeters 的 diameter。The Nautilus 是 42 millimeters。而且除了 huge 之外，它在 face 的两侧还有 gratuitous knobs，像一对 ears。但你可以从房间的另一头 recognize 出一只。

在 Patek 现在制造的所有 watches 中，the Nautilus 是最 sought after 的。它与 present-day buyers 想要的东西完美 aligned——基本上，就是 brand 尽可能 loud 的 expression。但在 1976 年，它 ahead of its time。在 1976 年，它仍然有点 too much。

最终扭转 Patek 命运的 watch 是另一个 iconic design，the hobnail calatrava。The hobnail calatravas 之所以如此称呼，是因为它们装饰着 tiny pyramid-shaped spikes。这就足以让它们看起来 distinctive。但除了 hobnails 之外，它们基本上就是 golden age dress watches。

The hobnail calatrava 显然是 René Bittel 的 brainchild，他是 Patek Philippe 的 ad agency 的负责人。它并不是一个 new design。多年来许多 watchmakers 都用 hobnails 装饰他们的 cases，而且自 1968 年以来就有一款带 hobnails 的 Patek model。但在 1984 年，Bittel 对 Patek president Philippe Stern 说，实际上就是：把这个作为你的 standard design，而我会创建一个 ad campaign，在人们的头脑中把它与你的 brand 联系起来。 [6](#fn6)

它 work 得极其好。由此产生的 watch，the [3919](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2019/watches-online-6/patek-%20philippe-ref-3919j-a-yellow-gold-wristwatch/)，被称为“banker’s watch”，因为它在 80 年代和 90 年代纽约的 investment bankers 中变得如此 popular。到这时为止，Patek 一直在 hedging their bets，也制造 quartz watches，并在他们的 ads 中防御性地争辩说，装在 fancy cases 里的 quartz watches 制造起来几乎和 mechanical ones 一样 laborious。但 the ibankers 买下了完整的 mechanical story。他们甚至不需要 self-winding mechanical watches；the 3919 是 hand-wound。So be it。Patek 不再谈论 quartz movements。而他们的 sales，自 70 年代初以来一直 flat，到 1987 年已处于明显的 upward trajectory。

很难确定 critical ingredient 究竟是 Bittel 在 advertising 上的 skill，还是一个 receptive audience，但作为一个认识这些 investment bankers 的人，我会倾向于 audience。这些人正是“yuppy”这个词被创造出来所指的人。Living expensively 是他们最出名的事情之一。如果有什么人会 adopt 一种 display wealth 的新方式，那就会是他们。而如果 Bittel 在十年前发出同样的 message，可能根本没有人会听到。

无论原因是什么，在 1980 年代后半段发生了某些事情，因为正是在那时，所有的 numbers 终于又开始 go up。直到大约 1985 年，mechanical watches 会发生什么仍然不清楚。到 1990 年就清楚了。到 1990 年，把 expensive、highly-branded、conspicuously mechanical watches 用作 status symbols 的 custom 已经牢固地 established。 [7](#fn7)

Obsolete technologies 通常不会被 adopt 为 display wealth 的方式。为什么 mechanical watches 会这样？因为 the wristwatch 结果是做这件事的 perfect vehicle。还有哪里比就在你的 wrist 上更好，那里每个人都能看到它？而更关键的是，还有什么比它更适合用来做这件事？你可以戴一枚 diamond ring 或一条 gold chain，但那些对 investment bankers 来说会显得 socially dubious。他们也许是 barbarians，但他们不是 mafia。而没有什么能比一只 gold watch 更 legit 了。公司的 chairman 仍然戴着二十年前他妻子送给他的那一只，那时 quartz watches 甚至还不存在。如果 display wealth 的 increasing pressure 要在任何地方出现，这里就是那个地方。 [8](#fn8)

至少对 men 来说是这样。Women 从来没有真正接受戴 mechanical watches 这个想法。大多数 rich women 乐于戴一只带 quartz movement 的 Cartier tank。为什么会有这种差异？部分原因和大多数 steam engines 的 buyers 是 men 的原因相同。但主要原因是，expensive mechanical watches 现在充当 men 的 de facto jewelry，而 women 不需要 de facto jewelry，因为她们可以戴 actual jewelry。

不过，关键的是 mechanical watches 要 accurate _enough_。一只新的 3919 每天误差不会超过 5 seconds。那远不如 quartz。即使是最便宜的 mass market quartz watches 也 accurate 到每天半秒，而最好的则 accurate 到每年 3 seconds。但在实践中你并不需要那种 accuracy。如果 mechanical watches 只 accurate 到每天一分钟，它们就不可能完成从 keeping time 到 displaying wealth 的 leap。一只总是显示错误时间的 watch 会显得太明显地 unluxurious。但每天 5 seconds 已经足够接近了。 [9](#fn9)

这是关于 brand 和 quality 之间关系的一个重要 point。当一个 product 转向人们因其 brand 而购买的东西时，quality 并不会停止 mattering。但它 matter 的方式改变了 shape。它变成了一个 threshold。它不再需要好到能 sell the product；brand sells the product；但它确实必须足够好，以 maintain the brand’s reputation。The brand 绝不能 break character。

^pr-aefcddd3f766

对 watchmakers 来说，yuppies 恰好及时出现来拯救他们，是一件幸运的事。或者也许没那么幸运。因为 yuppies 所代表的 market 的 evolution 已经变本加厉地继续下去，而 watchmakers 也不得不被它拖着走。如果他们不为 Hong Kong 和 Dubai 的 buyers 制造 gigantic blingy watches，别人也会。所以这就是他们现在发现自己正在做的事。而最初只是 branding 和 design 之间冲突的几个相对 subtle 的例子，现在已变成一场对 design 的全面 [war](https://www.patek.com/en/collection/grand-complications/6300-400g-001)。

mechanical watchmaking 的 present era 还没有一个名字。但如果我们需要一个，它显然应该是什么：the brand age。The golden age 从 1945 年持续到 1970 年，随后是 1970 年到 1985 年的 quartz crisis。自 1985 年以来，我们一直处于 the brand age。

这不会是唯一的 brand age。事实上，它甚至不是第一个；fine art 自 1930 年代 Barr canon 建立以来就一直处于它自己的 brand age。而既然我们很可能会看到更多这类事情，花些时间看看 a brand age 是什么样子会是值得的。

现在的事情与 golden age 时的方式有何不同？回答这个问题的最好方式，也许是想象如果我们用 time machine 把某个来自 golden age 的人带到这里，他会注意到什么。

如果他走过一个 fancy shopping district，他注意到的第一件事会是，golden age 所有 prominent watchmakers 似乎都比以往做得更好。他们不仅全都仍然存在，而且大多数现在拥有自己的 boutiques，而不是像当年那样依赖 jewelers 来 sell their products。

事实上这是一种错觉。只有三家 watchmakers 作为 independent companies 挺过了 70 年代和 80 年代的 dark days：Patek Philippe、Audemars Piguet 和 Rolex。其余的全部归 six holding companies 所有，随着 mechanical watches 将作为 men 的 luxury accessories 获得 second life 变得明朗，这些 holding companies 让它们 reinflated。它们如今不再是 separate companies，而更像是被 rolled up 进 the big three American automakers 的那些 brands：它们是各自的 parent companies 用来 target 不同 market segments 的方式。所以，举例来说，Longines 不再与 Omega 竞争，因为拥有它们两者的那家公司已经把它 assigned 到了 market 的 lower tier。[10](#fn10)

Vacheron Constantin boutique 看起来如此像 IWC 和 Jaeger-LeCoultre boutiques，就此而言也像 Montblanc 和 Cartier boutiques，是有原因的。它们都归同一家公司所有。顺便说一句，clothing brands 也类似。当你走过一座城镇最 fancy 的 shopping district 时，看似属于许多不同 brands 的 shops 实际上归少数几家 conglomerates 所有。这就是这些 districts 显得如此 sterile 的一个原因；就像由单一 developer 建造的 suburbs 一样，它们有一种不自然的 variety 缺失。

当我们的 time traveler 窥视这些 shops 的橱窗时，他首先注意到的会是所有 watches 有多么 large。这会让他惊讶，因为在 golden age，正如在所有之前的世纪里一样，big 意味着 cheap。一只昂贵的 golden age men’s watch 可能是 33 millimeters in diameter、8 millimeters thick。今天一只 expensive watch 则更像是 42 millimeters in diameter、10 millimeters thick——size 翻了一倍还多。透过那些显然非常 fancy 的 shops 的橱窗，看到看似 cheap watches 的东西，会让我们的 visitor 大为震惊。[11](#fn11)

我们知道这是怎么发生的。当 watches 从 telling time 转向 telling brand 时，它们为了更擅长后者而 grew in size。而且不只是 size，shape 也是如此。那是我们的 time traveler 会注意到的另一件事：随着 branding 的 centrifugal tendency 展开，产生了令人惊讶的各式各样 strange case shapes 和 awkward protrusions。他会想，那些 Panerais 的 crowns 上巨大的 guards 是怎么回事？人们对这些 watches 做了什么，以至于 crown 需要这样的 protection？为什么一个 crown guard 上会 engraved 一条信息，说它是 registered trademark？对我们来说这里发生了什么显而易见，但想象一下这对一个来自 golden age 的人会有多困惑，那时 form followed function。[12](#fn12)

当他琢磨这一堆奇怪的 bulky watches 时，他会注意到一个进一步的 pattern。他会意识到，其中数量多得惊人的一部分看起来像他已经熟悉的一个特定 brand 的 bulky watch。

到目前为止我还没有谈到 Rolex，因为 Rolex 不需要做太多就能适应新时代。在 golden age 期间，他们就已经一只脚踩在 brand age 里了。在他们历史的早期，他们投入了大量精力让他们的 watches 更好，但他们“在 1950 年代末停止了参加 Geneva 和 Neuchâtel 的 competitions”，并且从大约 1960 年起“largely abandoned research into mechanical watchmaking。”[13](#fn13) 原因不是他们变得懒惰，而是他们发现通过把他们的 watches 作为 status symbols 来 marketing，可以让 sales 增长得更快。于是这成了他们在 1960 年代的 focus，等到十年后 quartz crisis 袭来时，他们的 customers 已经自我筛选成了那些不太在乎一只 watch 里面是什么的人，只要它可辨认地是一只 Rolex。

而他们在这方面远远领先于其他 watchmakers。他们在 1940 年代就已经拥有了我们看到的 Patek Philippe 和 Audemars Piguet 在 1970 年代和 80 年代苦苦试图创造的东西：一个能立刻宣告 maker 的 brand 的 case。Rolex look 似乎是 organically 演化出来的，但一旦它演化出来，他们就意识到它有多么重要。事实上他们把它作为他们 watches 的 features 之一来 pitch。一则 1960 年代的 Rolex ad 说：“你可以从 conference table 的另一端认出它那 classic shape，由一整块 solid gold carved out 而成。”

确实，Rolex 在两个维度上都领先于它的时代：他们的 cases 不仅 recognizable，而且 big，至少按 golden age standards 来说是这样。不过那不是 clever marketing 的结果。它是创始人 Hans Wilsdorf 对 building waterproof watches 的 obsession 的副产品。

正如其名字所暗示的，那就是 Rolex Oyster 的 raison d’etre。像 Oyster 这样的 watches 被设计得 tough，像 Jeeps 一样。在 golden age，watch design 有两个极点。一端是 tool watches，它们 thick、tough，通常由 steel 制成。另一端是 dress watches，它们 thin、elegant，通常由 gold 制成。但 Rolex 模糊了它们之间的界线。当他们制造 thick、tough watches 时，他们用 gold 也用 steel 来制造它们。结果是一种 luxury Jeep。如果这个说法没有在你脑中敲响警钟，停下来想一想，因为那正是现在每个人都在开的东西。SUVs 就是 luxury Jeeps。发生在 watches 身上的事，与发生在 cars 身上的事是同一件事。确实，如果我们的 time traveler 转过身，看到一辆 Porsche Cayenne 驶过并意识到它是什么——一辆巨大的、pseudo-offroad vehicle，意在让人想起 Porsche 911——他可能会比看到他一直注视的那些 watches 时更加震惊。[14](#fn14)

如果这位 time traveller 走进一家 Patek Philippe boutique，并且真的试图买一只 Nautilus，他会受到最大的 shock。他们不会卖给他一只。因为在 Patek 他会遇到最极端的 brand age phenomenon：artificial scarcity。你不能就这么买一只 Nautilus。你必须先花数年时间，通过买通多个 tiers 的其他 models 来证明你的 loyalty，然后再花数年在 waiting list 上。[15](#fn15)

显然这个 strategy 能卖出更多 watches。但它也通过让 watches 不进入 secondary market 来支撑 retail prices。一家利用 artificial scarcity 来驱动 sales 的公司，不能让太多 scarce models 泄漏进 secondary market，否则它们就不再 scarce。理想状态是相当于 carbon sequestration 的 watch 版本：让买他们 watches 的人把它们留到死。

为了把 market 推向这个理想，Patek 从 sale 的两侧挤压。他们通过让通往 scarce models 的路径在时间和金钱上都如此 costly——如此 inconvenient and unreasonable——以至于只有真正的 fan 才会忍受它，来 weed out flippers。lower tier watches 在 secondary market 上以低于 retail 的价格出售，因为 Patek _doesn’t_ restrict 它们的 supply，所以一个想当 flipper 的人应该得花数年做亏钱的 purchases，才能拿到一个他可以 flip 获利的东西。不过显然有些人仍然设法 beat 这个 system，所以 Patek 的 countermeasures 并不止于此。他们 vigilant 地盯住 secondary sales，看谁在卖他们的 watches。Auction listings 通常包含 serial numbers，所以那些很容易 trace，但如果有必要，他们会在 secondary market 上 rebuy 他们自己的 watches 来获取 serial number 并 trace 泄漏。他们一年买数百只。而当他们抓到有人在卖他们不希望被卖的 watches 时，他们不只是 cut off 那个 customer。如果一家 retailer 的 customers 要为太多这样的 leaks 负责，他们就会 cut off 整个 retailer。这自然让 retailers 急于帮他们 policing buyers。

当然，总会有一些 leaks 进入 secondary market。即使最 loyal 的 customers 也会以一定速率死去。而事实上，secondary market 继续存在对 Patek 至关重要，因为它是他们关于所面临最重要问题——以多快速度增加 top tier watches 的 supply——所拥有的最有价值的信息来源之一。它们的 scarcity 有助于驱动所有其他 watches 的 purchases，所以那些确实进入 secondary market 的应该总是以高于 retail 的价格出售。而且我确信 Patek 在增加 supply 时留了很大的 error 余地，因为如果这些 watches 的 secondary market prices 接近 retail prices，你就接近一场 price collapse——而由于人们现在把这些 watches 当作 investments 来买，那将产生与 asset bubble 破裂同样灾难性的 cascading effect。它不会只是像 asset bubble 破裂。它 _会_ 是 asset bubble 破裂。那就是一家 elite watchmaker 现在所处的 business：小心翼翼地管理一个持续的 asset bubble。[16](#fn16)

^pr-9ae73bf5ec0e

这是我称之为 comb-over effect 的一个实例：当一系列单独看来很小的 changes 把你从有点不对劲的东西带到 freakishly wrong 的东西。我确信 Patek 不是一口气想出这整个 scheme 的；我确信它是逐渐演化出来的。但看看我们最终落到了一个多么奇怪的地方。回到 golden age，你买一只 Patek Philippe 的方式是去一家 jeweler 那里给他们钱。现在 Patek 为了维持一个 asset bubble 而在 policing buyers。

对我来说，brand age 最引人注目的是它彻头彻尾的 strangeness。那些 zombie watch brands 看似 independent，甚至拥有自己的 retail stores，却全都归少数几家 holding companies 所有。那些 giant、awkwardly shaped watches 逆转了 500 年来把它们做得更小的进步。那种要求一家公司在 secondary market 上 rebuy 自己的 watches 以抓住 rogue customers 的 business model。rogue customers 这个概念本身。这一切都如此 strange。而它 strange 的原因是，没有 function 可供 form 去 follow。

直到 golden age 结束，mechanical watches 都是必要的。你需要它们来知道时间。而那个 constraint 赋予了 watches 和 watchmaking industry 一个有意义的 shape。在 golden age 期间当然也制造过一些 strange-looking watches。它们并非全都是 beautifully minimal。但当 golden age watchmakers 制造一只 strange-looking watch 时，他们知道自己在这么做。事实上他们给人的印象是把它当作一种 deliberate exercise 来做，以避免陷入 rut。

那不是 brand age watches 看起来 strange 的原因。brand age watches 看起来 strange，是因为它们没有 practical function。它们的功能是 express brand，而虽然那确实是一种 constraint，它不是那种能生成好东西的 clean constraint。brand 施加的 constraints 最终依赖于 human psychology 中一些最糟糕的特征。所以当你有一个只由 brand 定义的世界时，它将会是一个 weird、bad 的世界。

好吧，那可真 dark。我们能从这片 wreckage 中抢救出什么 edifying lesson 吗？

一个明显的 lesson 是远离 brand。确实，可能不仅避免 buying brand 是个好主意，避免 selling 它也是个好主意。当然，你也许能靠这种方式赚钱——尽管我打赌它比看起来更难——但 pushing people’s brand buttons 就不是一个好问题去 work on，而没有好问题就很难做出好工作。

更微妙的 lesson 是，fields 有 natural rhythms，超出 individuals 抵抗的能力。Fields 有 golden ages 和 not so golden ages，而你更有可能在一个正在上升的 field 里做出好工作。

当然，当它们正在发生时，人们并不称它们为 golden ages。“Golden age”是人们后来、在它们结束之后才使用的一个 term。这并不意味着 golden ages 不是真实的，而是说它们的 participants 在当时把它们视为理所当然。他们不知道自己拥有多好的条件。但虽然把一个人的好运视为理所当然通常是个错误，在这个情况下却不是。一个 golden age 在当时的感觉，就只是聪明人在努力 work on interesting problems 并取得 results。为比这更多的东西去 optimize 会是 overfitting。

事实上，有一条单一的原则既能让你免于去研究像 brand 这样的东西，也能自动为你找到 golden ages。Follow the problems。

找到 golden ages 的方法不是去寻找它们。找到它们的方法——历史上几乎所有参与者找到它们的方法——是 follow interesting problems。如果你聪明、有雄心，并且对自己诚实，那么没有什么比你对 problems 的 taste 更好的指引了。去 interesting problems 所在的地方，你很可能会发现其他聪明而有雄心的人也已经出现在那里。而后来，他们会回顾你们一起做过的事，并称之为一个 golden age。

## 原文

# 231 The Brand Age

March 2026

In the early 1970s, disaster struck the Swiss watch industry. Now people call it the quartz crisis, but in fact it was a compound of three separate disasters that all happened at about the same time.

The first was competition from Japan. The Swiss had been watching the Japanese in the rear view mirror all through the 1960s, and they’d been improving at an alarming rate. But even so the Swiss were surprised in 1968 when the Japanese swept all the top spots for mechanical watches at the Geneva Observatory trials.

The Swiss knew what was coming. For years the Japanese had been able to make cheaper watches. Now they could make better ones too.

To make matters worse, Swiss watches were about to become much more expensive. The Bretton Woods agreement, which since 1945 had fixed the exchange rates of most of the world’s currencies, had set the Swiss Franc at an artificially low rate of .228 USD. When Bretton Woods collapsed in 1973, the Franc shot upward. By 1978 it reached .625 USD, meaning Swiss watches were now 2.7 times as expensive for Americans to buy. [1](#fn1)

The combined effect of foreign competition and the loss of their protective exchange rate would have decimated the Swiss watch industry even if it hadn’t been for quartz movements. But quartz movements were the final blow. Now the whole game they’d been trying to win at became irrelevant. Something that had been expensive – knowing the exact time – was now a commodity.

Between the early 1970s and the early 1980s, unit sales of Swiss watches fell by almost two thirds. Most Swiss watchmakers became insolvent or close to it and were sold. But not all of them. A handful survived as independent companies. And the way they did it was by transforming themselves from precision instrument makers into luxury brands.

In the process the nature of the mechanical watch was also transformed. The most expensive watches have always cost a lot, but why they cost a lot and what buyers got in return have changed completely. In 1960 expensive watches cost a lot because they cost a lot to manufacture, and what the buyer got in return was the most accurate timekeeping device, for its size, that could be made. Now they cost a lot because brands spend a lot on advertising and use tricks to limit supply, and what the buyer gets in return is an expensive status symbol.

That turns out to be a profitable business though. The Swiss watch industry probably makes more now from selling brand than they would have if they were still selling engineering. And indeed, when you look at the graph of Swiss watch sales by revenue, it tells a different story than the graph of unit sales. Instead of falling off a cliff, the revenue numbers merely flatten out for a while, and then take off like a rocket in the late 1980s as the surviving watchmakers come to terms with their new destiny.

It took the watchmakers about 20 years to figure out the new rules of the game. And it’s interesting to watch them do it, because the completeness of their transformation makes it the perfect case study in one of the most powerful forces of our era: brand.

Brand is what’s left when the substantive differences between products disappear. But making the substantive differences between products disappear is what technology naturally tends to do. So what happened to the Swiss watch industry is not merely an interesting outlier. It’s very much a story of our times.

Jaeger-LeCoultre’s web site says that one of their current collections “takes its inspiration from the classic designs of the golden age of watchmaking.” In saying this they’re implicitly saying something that present-day watchmakers all know but rarely come so close to saying outright: whatever age we’re in now, it’s not the golden age.

The golden age was from 1945 to 1970 – from the point where the watch industry emerged from the chaos of war with the Swiss on top till the triple cataclysm that struck it starting in the late 60s. There were two things watchmakers sought above all in the golden age: thinness and accuracy. And indeed this was arguably the essential tradeoff in watchmaking. A watch is something you carry with you to tell you the time. So there are two fundamental ways to improve it: to make it easier to carry with you and to make it better at telling the time.

Obviously accuracy is valuable, but in the golden age thinness was if anything more valuable. Even in the days of pocket watches the best watchmakers tried to make their watches as thin as they could. Cheap, thick pocket watches were derided as “turnips.” But thinness took on a new urgency when men’s watches moved onto their wrists during World War I. And since thinness was more difficult to achieve than accuracy, it was this quality that tended to distinguish the more expensive watches of the golden age.

There is one other thing watchmakers have pursued in some eras: telling more than the time in the usual way. Telling you the phase of the moon, for example, or telling the time with sound. In the industry the term for these things is “complications.” They were popular in the nineteenth century and they’re popular again now, but except for one pragmatic complication (showing the date), they were a sideshow in the golden age. In the golden age, as always in golden ages, the top watchmakers focused on the essential tradeoff. And, as always in golden ages, they did it beautifully. The best watches of the golden age have a [quiet perfection](https://goldammer.me/products/vacheron-constantin-18k-white-gold) that has never been equalled since. And for reasons I’m about to explain, probably never will be.

The three most prestigious brands of the golden age were the so-called “holy trinity” of Patek Philippe, Vacheron Constantin, and Audemars Piguet. Their prestige was mostly deserved; they had earned it by the exceptional quality of their work. By the 1960s they stood on two legs, prestige and performance. And what they learned in the next two decades was that they had to put all their weight on the first leg, because they could no longer win at either of the two things watchmakers had historically striven to achieve. Quartz movements were not only more accurate than any mechanical movement, but thinner too.

The holy trinity at least had another leg to stand on. Most of the other well- known Swiss watchmakers sold only performance. None of those companies survived intact.

Omega showed what not to do. Omega were the nerds of Swiss watchmakers. They made wonderfully accurate watches, but they would have been ambivalent, at best, about the idea of being a luxury brand. When the Japanese got as good as the Swiss at making accurate movements, Omega responded in the Omega way: make even more accurate movements. They introduced a new movement in 1968 that ran at a 45% higher frequency. In theory this should have made it more accurate, but the new movement was so fragile that it destroyed their reputation for reliability. They even tried to make a better quartz movement, but there was nothing down that road but a race to the bottom. By 1981 they were insolvent and were taken over by their creditors.

Patek Philippe took the opposite approach. While Omega was redesigning their movements, Patek was redesigning their cases. Or more precisely, designing their cases, because until then they hadn’t.

This is probably the point to mention what a strange beast the Swiss watch industry was in those days. It was a kind of capitalism that’s hard to imagine today, and even then could only have been made to work in a country like Switzerland – a network of small, specialized companies locked into place by regulation. The companies that we for convenience have been calling watchmakers were merely the consumer-facing edge of this network. The holy trinity didn’t design their own cases, or even their own movements most of the time.

In 1968 (that year again) Patek Philippe launched a new watch that shifted the center of gravity of case design. This time they’d taken their own designs to the casemakers and said “this is what you’re going to make for us.” The result was a striking new model called the [Golden Ellipse](https://collectability.com/learn/the-history-of-the-golden-ellipse/). Somewhat confusingly, because it wasn’t elliptical. The new case was more of what UI designers would call a round rect: a rectangle with rounded corners. And this new family of watches was quite successful. But it was more than that: it was the pattern for the future. [2](#fn2)

How could merely designing a distinctive case be so important? Because it turned the entire watch into an expression of brand.

The trouble with the best watches of the golden age, from the point of view of someone who wanted to impress people with the brand of watch he was wearing, was that no one could tell what brand of watch you were wearing. Until you got within a few inches of them, the watches of all the top makers looked the same. That’s the thing about minimalism: there tends to be just one answer. Plus the watches of the golden age were small by present standards. Watchmakers had spent centuries working to make them smaller, and by 1960 they’d gotten very good at it. So the only thing distinguishing one top brand from another was the name printed on the dial, and dials were so small that these names were tiny. The manufacturers’ names on the holy trinity’s golden age watches are between half and three quarters of a millimeter high. By taking over the case, Patek expanded the size of the brand from 8 square millimeters to 800.

Why did they suddenly decide to make their brand shout, after a century of whispering? Because they knew they weren’t going to beat the Japanese on performance. From now on they’d have to depend more on brand.

There’s a cost to doing this, which we can see even in this early example of case-as-brand. Golden Ellipses are not bad looking. They must have looked even cooler in the 1970s, when designers were turning everything into round rects. But the Golden Ellipse was not an evolutionary step forward in case design. Watches didn’t all become round rects. Watchmakers had already discovered the optimal shape for the case of something that describes a circle as it rotates.

They had also discovered the optimal shape for the crown, the knob on the side of a watch that you turn to wind it. But to emphasize the distinctive profile of the Ellipse, Patek made the crown too small, with the result that they’re distractingly hard to wind. [3](#fn3)

So even in this early example we see an important point about the relationship between brand and design. Branding isn’t merely orthogonal to good design, but opposed to it. Branding by definition has to be distinctive. But good design, like math or science, seeks the right answer, and right answers tend to converge.

Branding is centrifugal; design is centripetal.

There is some wiggle room here of course. Design doesn’t have as sharply defined right answers as math, especially design meant for a human audience. So it’s not necessarily bad design to do something distinctive if you have honest motives. But you can’t evade the fundamental conflict between branding and design, any more than you can evade gravity.

Indeed, the conflict between branding and design is so fundamental that it extends far beyond things we call design. We see it even in religion. If you want the adherents of a religion to have customs that set them apart from everyone else, you can’t make them do things that are convenient or reasonable, or other people would do them too. If you want to set your adherents apart, you have to make them do things that are inconvenient and unreasonable.

It’s the same if you want to set your designs apart. If you choose good options, other people will choose them too.

There are only two ways to combine branding and good design. You can do it when the space of possibilities is enormously large, as it is in painting, for example. Leonardo could paint as well as he possibly could and yet also paint in a style that was distinctively his. If there had been a million painters as good as Bellini and Leonardo this would have been harder to do, but since there were more like ten they didn’t bump up against one another much. [4](#fn4)

The other situation when branding and good design can be combined is when the space of possibilities is comparatively unexplored. If you’re the first to arrive in some new territory, you can both find the right answer and claim it as uniquely yours. At least at first; if you’ve really found the right answer, everyone else’s designs will inevitably converge on yours, and your brand advantage will erode over time.

Since the space of watch design is neither unexplored nor enormously large, branding can only be achieved at the expense of good design. And in fact if you wanted one sentence to describe the current age of watchmaking, that one would do pretty well.

Patek Philippe didn’t know for sure that making visibly branded watches would work. It was not even their only strategy, at the time. They were finding their way. But it was the strategy that did work, at least as measured by revenues.

For it to work the customers had to meet them halfway. Patek knew that not all their customers were buying their watches for the performance they delivered – for their accuracy and thinness. They knew that at least some customers were buying them because they were expensive. But it was unclear how many, or how far they could be pushed.

To encourage them, Patek did something that none of the holy trinity had done much of before: brand advertising. And what they talked about was how expensive their watches were. A 1968 Patek ad explained “why you are well advised to invest perhaps half a month’s income” in an Ellipse. “Like every Patek Philippe,” the ad continued, “this thin model is entirely finished by hand. Since a Patek Philippe is the costliest watch to make, production is severely limited: only 43 watches are signed out each day for delivery to prominent jewelers throughout the world.” [5](#fn5)

You can tell this is an early ad because they still mention thinness. But there is no mention of accuracy. Presumably Patek felt that battle was already lost.

The next move was made by Audemars Piguet, who in 1970 commissioned the renowned designer Gérald Genta to design their own iconic watch, this one, daringly, in steel. The result, launched in 1972, was the [Royal Oak](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2025/fine-%20watches-5/reference-5402st-jumbo-royal-oak-a-series-a). And Audemars Piguet’s ads (for they too now started doing brand advertising) emphasized its high cost even more dramatically. “Introducing steel at the price of gold,” one began. “You’re looking at the costliest stainless steel watch in the world – the Audemars Piguet ‘Royal Oak’. What makes it even more precious than gold is the time that went into building it, by a vanishing breed of master watchmakers.” At the bottom of the ad they turn the traditional formula on its head and describe their watches as being “priced from $35,000 and down.”

The Royal Oak was also a step forward in surface area devoted to brand. The Golden Ellipse had turned the watch face into an expression of brand, but it used ordinary straps and bracelets. In the Royal Oak, the watch face was integrated with a metal bracelet that continued its design all the way around the wrist. When it said “You’re looking at the costliest stainless steel watch in the world,” it said it with every square millimeter of surface area.

Would customers buy this new approach? The initial results were moderately encouraging. The holy trinity’s sales didn’t take off, but they didn’t go down to zero either. There were at least some people out there responding to the new message. Perhaps if they kept at it the number would grow.

So they did. Encouraged by the success of the Royal Oak, Patek Philippe commissioned Gérald Genta in 1974 to design a similar watch for them. The design of the Royal Oak had been inspired by a ship’s porthole, so the design of this new watch would be inspired by… a ship’s porthole. It was called the [Nautilus](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2025/important-%20watches-2/reference-3700-11-nautilus-jumbo-a-stainless-steel), and it launched at the Basel Watch Fair in 1976.

In the Nautilus we really see the incompatibility of branding and design. It was huge. The most expensive men’s watches at the peak of the golden age were typically 32 or 33 millimeters in diameter. The Nautilus was 42 millimeters. And as well as being huge it had gratuitous knobs on either side of the face, like a pair of ears. But you could recognize one from across the room.

Of all the watches Patek makes now, the Nautilus is the most sought after. It’s perfectly aligned with what present-day buyers want – basically, the loudest possible expression of brand. But in 1976 it was ahead of its time. In 1976 it was still a little too much.

The watch that finally turned Patek’s fortunes around was another iconic design, the hobnail calatrava. The hobnail calatravas were so called because they were decorated with tiny pyramid-shaped spikes. That was enough to make them look distinctive. But except for the hobnails they were basically golden age dress watches.

The hobnail calatrava was apparently the brainchild of René Bittel, the head of Patek Philippe’s ad agency. It was not a new design. Many watchmakers had decorated their cases with hobnails over the years, and there had been a Patek model with them since 1968. But in 1984 Bittel told Patek president Philippe Stern, in effect: make this your standard design, and I’ll create an ad campaign to identify it in people’s heads with your brand. [6](#fn6)

It worked spectacularly well. The resulting watch, the [3919](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2019/watches-online-6/patek-%20philippe-ref-3919j-a-yellow-gold-wristwatch/), is known as the “banker’s watch” because it became so popular among investment bankers in New York in the 80s and 90s. Up to this point Patek had been hedging their bets, making quartz watches as well, and arguing defensively in their ads that quartz watches in fancy cases were almost as laborious to make as mechanical ones. But the ibankers bought the full mechanical story. They didn’t even need self- winding mechanical watches; the 3919 was hand-wound. So be it. Patek stopped talking about quartz movements. And their sales, which had been flat since the early 70s, were by 1987 on a clear upward trajectory.

It’s hard to say for sure whether the critical ingredient was Bittel’s skill at advertising or a receptive audience, but as someone who knew these investment bankers, I’d lean toward the audience. These were the people for whom the term “yuppy” was coined. Living expensively was one of the things they were best known for. If anyone was going to adopt a new way to display wealth, it would be them. Whereas if Bittel had sent the same message ten years earlier, there might have been no one to hear it.

Whatever the cause, something happened in the second half of the 1980s, because that’s when all the numbers finally start going up again. Up till about 1985 it was still not clear what would happen with mechanical watches. By 1990 it was. By 1990 the custom of using expensive, highly-branded, conspicuously mechanical watches as status symbols was firmly established. [7](#fn7)

Obsolete technologies don’t usually get adopted as ways to display wealth. Why did it happen with mechanical watches? Because the wristwatch turns out to be the perfect vehicle for it. Where better than right on your wrist, where everyone can see it? And more to the point, what better to do it with? You could wear a diamond ring or a gold chain, but those would have seemed socially dubious to investment bankers. They might have been barbarians, but they weren’t mafia. Whereas nothing could be more legit than a gold watch. The chairman of the company was still wearing one his wife gave him 20 years ago, before quartz watches were even a thing. If the increasing pressure to display wealth was going to emerge anywhere, this was the place. [8](#fn8)

For men, at least. Women never really went for the idea of wearing mechanical watches. Most rich women are happy wearing a Cartier tank with a quartz movement. Why the difference? Partly for the same reason that most buyers of steam engines are men. But the main reason is that expensive mechanical watches now serve as de facto jewelry for men, and women don’t need de facto jewelry because they can wear actual jewelry.

It was critical, though, that mechanical watches were accurate _enough_. A new 3919 would have been off by no more than 5 seconds a day. That was nowhere near as good as quartz. Even the cheapest mass market quartz watches were accurate to half a second a day, and the best ones were accurate to 3 seconds a year. But in practice you didn’t need that kind of accuracy. If mechanical watches had only been accurate to a minute a day they couldn’t have made the leap from keeping time to displaying wealth. It would have seemed too manifestly unluxurious to have a watch that always had the wrong time. But 5 seconds a day was close enough. [9](#fn9)

This is an important point about the relationship between brand and quality. Quality doesn’t stop mattering when a product switches to something people buy for its brand. But the way it matters changes shape. It becomes a threshold. It no longer has to be so great that it sells the product; brand sells the product; but it does have to be good enough to maintain the brand’s reputation. The brand must not break character.

It was a lucky thing for the watchmakers that yuppies arose just in time to save them. Or maybe not so lucky. Because the evolution of the market that yuppies represented has continued with a vengeance, and watchmakers have perforce been dragged along with it. If they don’t make gigantic blingy watches for buyers in Hong Kong and Dubai, someone else will. So that is what they now find themselves doing. And what began with a few comparatively subtle examples of the conflict between branding and design is now an all out [war](https://www.patek.com/en/collection/grand-complications/6300-400g-001) on design.

The present era of mechanical watchmaking doesn’t yet have a name. But if we need one, it’s obvious what it should be: the brand age. The golden age ran from 1945 to 1970, followed by the quartz crisis from 1970 to 1985. Since 1985 we’ve been in the brand age.

This won’t be the only brand age. Indeed, it’s not even the first; fine art has been in its own brand age since the establishment of the Barr canon in the 1930s. And since we’ll probably see more of this kind of thing, it would be worth taking some time to look at what a brand age is like.

How are things different now from the way they were in the golden age? The best way to answer that might be to imagine what someone from the golden age would notice if we brought him here in a time machine.

The first thing he’d notice, if he walked through a fancy shopping district, is that all the prominent watchmakers of the golden age seem to be doing better than ever. They’re not only all still around, but most now have their own boutiques instead of depending on jewelers to sell their products as they used to back in the day.

In fact this is an illusion. Only three watchmakers survived the dark days of the 70s and 80s as independent companies: Patek Philippe, Audemars Piguet, and Rolex. All the rest are owned by six holding companies, which reinflated them as it became clear that mechanical watches would have a second life as luxury accessories for men. Instead of separate companies they’re now more like the brands that got rolled up into the big three American automakers: they’re ways for their parent companies to target different segments of the market. So Longines, for example, no longer competes with Omega, because the company that owns them both has assigned it a lower tier of the market. [10](#fn10)

There’s a reason the Vacheron Constantin boutique looks so much like the IWC and Jaeger-LeCoultre boutiques, and for that matter the Montblanc and Cartier boutiques. They’re all owned by the same company. It’s similar with clothing brands, incidentally. When you walk through a town’s fanciest shopping district, what seem to be the shops of lots of different brands are actually owned by a handful of conglomerates. That’s one reason these districts seem so sterile; like suburbs built by a single developer, they have an unnatural lack of variety.

When our time traveler peered into the windows of these shops, the first thing he’d notice would be how large all the watches were. This would surprise him, because in the golden age, as indeed in all the preceding centuries, big meant cheap. An expensive golden age men’s watch might have been 33 millimeters in diameter and 8 millimeters thick. An expensive watch today will be more like 42 millimeters in diameter and 10 millimeters thick – more than double the size. It would astonish our visitor to look through the windows of what were clearly very fancy shops and see what seemed to be cheap watches. [11](#fn11)

We know how this happened. When watches switched from telling time to telling brand, they grew in size to be better at it. And not just in size, but in shape too. That’s another thing our time traveler would notice: the surprising variety of strange case shapes and awkward protrusions that have been produced as the centrifugal tendency of branding played out. What, he’d wonder, is going on with the huge guards on the crowns of those Panerais? What do people do with these watches that makes the crown need such protection? And why would a crown guard have a message engraved on it saying that it’s a registered trademark? It’s obvious to us what’s going on here, but imagine how confusing it would be to someone from the golden age, when form followed function. [12](#fn12)

As he puzzled over this strange assortment of bulky watches, he’d notice a further pattern. He’d realize that a surprisingly large number of them looked like a specific brand of bulky watch he was already familiar with.

I haven’t talked about Rolex so far, because Rolex didn’t have to do much to adapt to the new era. They already had one foot in the brand age during the golden age. Early in their history they put a lot of effort into making their watches better, but they “stopped taking part in competitions in Geneva and Neuchâtel at the end of the 1950s,” and from about 1960 “largely abandoned research into mechanical watchmaking.” [13](#fn13) The reason was not that they’d become lazy, but that they’d discovered they could make sales grow faster by marketing their watches as status symbols. So that became their focus during the 1960s, and by the time the quartz crisis hit ten years later, their customers were self-selected to be people who didn’t care that much what was inside a watch, so long as it was recognizably a Rolex.

And they were far ahead of other watchmakers in that department. They already had in the 1940s what we saw Patek Philippe and Audemars Piguet struggling to create in the 1970s and 80s: a case that immediately proclaimed the brand of the maker. The Rolex look seems to have evolved organically, but once it did, they realized how important it was. In fact they pitched it as one of the features of their watches. A 1960s Rolex ad says “You can recognize its classic shape, carved out of a block of solid gold, from the other end of the conference table.”

Indeed Rolex was ahead of its time in both dimensions: their cases were not merely recognizable, but big too, at least by golden age standards. That was not the result of clever marketing, though. It was a byproduct of the founder Hans Wilsdorf’s obsession with building waterproof watches.

As its name suggests, that was the raison d’etre of the Rolex Oyster. Watches like the Oyster were designed to be tough, like Jeeps. In the golden age there were two poles of watch design. At one end were tool watches, which were thick, tough, and usually made of steel. At the other end were dress watches, which were thin, elegant, and usually made of gold. But Rolex blurred the line between them. When they made thick, tough watches, they made them out of gold as well as steel. The result was a sort of luxury Jeep. And if that phrase didn’t ring a bell in your head, stop and think about it, because that is exactly what everyone is driving now. That’s what SUVs are, luxury Jeeps. What happened to watches is the same thing that happened to cars. And indeed if our time traveler turned and saw a Porsche Cayenne pass by and realized what it was – a huge, pseudo-offroad vehicle meant to recall the Porsche 911 – he might have been even more shocked than he was by the watches he’d been looking at. [14](#fn14)

If the time traveller walked into a Patek Philippe boutique and actually tried to buy a Nautilus, he’d get the biggest shock of all. They wouldn’t sell him one. Because at Patek he’d encounter the most extreme brand age phenomenon: artificial scarcity. You can’t just buy a Nautilus. You have to spend years proving your loyalty first by buying your way through multiple tiers of other models, and then spend years on a waiting list. [15](#fn15)

Obviously this strategy sells more watches. But it also supports retail prices by keeping watches off the secondary market. A company using artificial scarcity to drive sales can’t allow too many of the scarce models to leak into the secondary market, or they stop being scarce. The ideal is the watch equivalent of carbon sequestration: for the people who buy their watches to keep them till they die.

To push the market toward this ideal, Patek squeezes from both sides of the sale. They weed out flippers by making the path to the scarce models so costly in both time and money – so inconvenient and unreasonable – that only a genuine fan would endure it. The lower tier watches sell for below retail on the secondary market, because Patek _doesn’t_ restrict their supply, so a would-be flipper should have to spend years making money-losing purchases before he could even get something he could flip at a profit. Apparently some people still manage to beat this system though, so Patek’s countermeasures don’t end there. They keep a vigilant eye on secondary sales to see who’s selling their watches. Auction listings usually include serial numbers, so those are easy to trace, but if necessary they’ll rebuy their own watches on the secondary market to get the serial number and trace the leak. They buy hundreds a year. And when they catch someone selling watches they don’t want them to, they don’t just cut off that customer. If a retailer’s customers are responsible for too many such leaks, they’ll cut off the whole retailer. Which naturally makes retailers eager to help them police buyers.

There will of course always be some leaks into the secondary market. Even the most loyal customers die at a certain rate. And in fact it’s critical for Patek that the secondary market continue to exist, because it’s one of the most valuable sources of information they have about the most important question they face: how fast to increase the supply of the top tier watches. Their scarcity helps drive the purchases of all the others, so those that do make it into the secondary market should always sell for above retail. And I’m sure Patek leaves a large margin for error when increasing supply, because if secondary market prices for these watches get close to retail prices, you’re getting close to a price collapse – which, since people now buy these watches as investments, would have the same disastrous cascading effect as the bursting of an asset bubble. It wouldn’t just be like the bursting of an asset bubble. It would _be_ the bursting of an asset bubble. That’s the business an elite watchmaker is in now: carefully managing a sustained asset bubble. [16](#fn16)

This is an instance of what I call the comb-over effect: when a series of individually small changes takes you from something that’s a little bit off to something that’s freakishly wrong. I’m sure Patek didn’t cook up this whole scheme in one shot; I’m sure it evolved gradually. But look at what a strange place we’ve ended up in. Back in the golden age the way you bought a Patek Philippe was to go to a jeweler and give them money. Now Patek is policing buyers to maintain an asset bubble.

The most striking thing to me about the brand age is the sheer strangeness of it. The zombie watch brands that appear to be independent and even have their own retail stores, and yet are all owned by a few holding companies. The giant, awkwardly shaped watches that reverse 500 years of progress in making them smaller. The business model that requires a company to rebuy their own watches on the secondary market to catch rogue customers. The very concept of rogue customers. It’s all so strange. And the reason it’s strange is that there’s no function for form to follow.

Up to the end of the golden age, mechanical watches were necessary. You needed them to know the time. And that constraint gave both the watches and the watchmaking industry a meaningful shape. There were certainly some strange- looking watches made during the golden age. They weren’t all beautifully minimal. But when golden age watchmakers made a strange-looking watch, they knew they were doing it. In fact they give the impression of having done it as a deliberate exercise, to avoid getting into a rut.

That’s not why brand age watches look strange. Brand age watches look strange because they have no practical function. Their function is to express brand, and while that is certainly a constraint, it’s not the clean kind of constraint that generates good things. The constraints imposed by brand ultimately depend on some of the worst features of human psychology. So when you have a world defined only by brand, it’s going to be a weird, bad world.

Well that was dark. Is there some edifying lesson we can salvage from the wreckage?

One obvious lesson is to stay away from brand. Indeed it’s probably a good idea not just to avoid buying brand, but to avoid selling it too. Sure, you might be able to make money this way – though I bet it’s harder than it looks – but pushing people’s brand buttons is just not a good problem to work on, and it’s hard to do good work without a good problem.

The more subtle lesson is that fields have natural rhythms that are beyond the power of individuals to resist. Fields have golden ages and not so golden ages, and you’re much more likely to do good work in a field that’s on the way up.

Of course they don’t call them golden ages as they’re happening. “Golden age” is a term people use later, after they’re over. That doesn’t mean that golden ages aren’t real, but rather that their participants take them for granted at the time. They don’t know how good they have it. But while it’s usually a mistake to take one’s good fortune for granted, it’s not in this case. What a golden age feels like, at the time, is just that smart people are working hard on interesting problems and getting results. It would be overfitting to optimize for more than that.

In fact there’s a single principle that will both save you from working on things like brand, and also automatically find golden ages for you. Follow the problems.

The way to find golden ages is not to go looking for them. The way to find them – the way almost all their participants have found them historically – is by following interesting problems. If you’re smart and ambitious and honest with yourself, there’s no better guide than your taste in problems. Go where interesting problems are, and you’ll probably find that other smart and ambitious people have turned up there too. And later they’ll look back on what you did together and call it a golden age.
