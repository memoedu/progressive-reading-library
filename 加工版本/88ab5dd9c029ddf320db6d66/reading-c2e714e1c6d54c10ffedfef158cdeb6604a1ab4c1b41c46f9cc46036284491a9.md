# 附录：IQ 与 job performance

---

## 正文

_[Talent](https://www.amazon.com/Talent-Identify-Energizers-Creatives-Winners/dp/1250275814/ref=tmm_hrd_swatch_0?_encoding=UTF8&qid=1644014882&sr=8-1)_（Tyler Cowen & Daniel Gross, St. Martin's Press, 2022）是一本关于 discovering（以及 hiring）talent 的书。大多数关于 [hiring](https://www.amazon.com/gp/new-releases/books/2578/ref=zg_b_hnr_2578_1) 的畅销书其实讲的是 _being hired_，而 _Talent_ 主要讨论相反的 problem。Talent 各式各样，因此，这本书尝试做的事情，比一本试图帮助读者 prepare 某种特定 interview（例如 software engineering interview）的书更难；_Talent_ 希望它提出的 observations 具有相对 general 的性质。

(I)

为了实现这一目标，这本书 blends 了 academic research 与作者们的 personal experience。我最近一直在思考，academic research 对各个 domains 中 real-life practical decision-making 的 relevance（见[这里](https://nintil.com/notes-2021)的 section 1），所以我很想知道，如果作者们只根据自己 evaluating talent 的（丰富）experience 来写，再看看这些 experience 如何与 research 相吻合，这本书会是什么样子（我怀疑这本书不会有多大变化）。Personal experience 被 underrated 了，而作者们足够通情达理，不会因为自己的 personal knowledge 与某个 research literature conflicts，就将它抛弃。

这本书成功解释了在 finding top talent 时应当留意哪些东西，同时也成为一个 example，说明 [tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) 对 making 这些 decisions 具有 fundamental importance。Intelligence matters，但远非 exclusively！你会想要 conscientious candidates，但并非总是如此。你实际上可能想 hire 一个 subclinical schizoid 来担任 consultant，以 generate interesting ideas，但不会让其担任 manager。而且，不同的人在 online 与 in person interview 中可能 present 得不同。有些人在 speaking 时可能 express themselves poorly，却是 extremely clear writers 和 thinkers。

仅靠一本书无法 overcome tacit knowledge transmission 这一 problem；即使读上 10 遍，你仍然不会成为 skilled judge of talent。因此，可以把这本书看作是在指出应当留意哪些东西，就像一本 photography 书可能指出 color、perspective 和 composition 如何共同构成一幅看起来不错的 picture，但你仍然得走出去，拍一些 pictures，才能学会如何拍出 great photos。

\[1\]. Meta-game 指的是某个 social game 所处的 high level game。这个 term 通常用于 videogames：除了 game 本身，还存在一些关于如何 play the game 的细节，而这些细节取决于其他人往往如何 play the game。关于 Starcraft 这一 context 下更长的 discussion，见[这里](https://liquipedia.net/starcraft/Metagame)。

这本书还清楚意识到自己所处的 meta-game[1](https://nintil.com/talent-review/#sidenote-1)，这一点令人耳目一新：如果你告诉读者使用这个或那个 interview question，那么 candidates 就会了解这些 questions，并 craft polished answers，进而 hinder interviewer 看清实际情况的 ability。但 Cowen & Gross 看透了这一点：他们 discuss 了一些 tactics，例如反复提出同一个 question，不断要求更多 examples，直到穷尽为止。此外，他们提醒我们不要执着于 specific questions 或 interview formats；相反，这本书似乎是在 argument，促使我们成为那种自然而然就能在 talent 所在之处看见它的人。另一个 question——“你是如何为这次 interview 做 prepare 的？”——也是 breaking the fourth wall 这一做法在 interview setting 中的绝佳 example，有助于接触到可能经过 staged performance 背后的真实的人。

这本书的核心内容，在许多读者看来会是意料之中的正确；有意思的部分，是作者们指出的、talent-choosers 应当格外注意的一个人的 character 的各个 aspects：例如，我们不应该只看一个 candidate，还应该看其 trajectory。他们是在 learning fast，还是停滞在几年来一直维持的同一个 level of performance？两个目前拥有相同 job position 的 individuals，在未来几年值得期待的表现上，可能存在 radically different 的差别。

确实，这本书是一场漫长的“[overrated 还是 underrated](https://www.driverlesscrocodile.com/teams/tyler-cowen-on-talent-1-talent-spotting-as-art-and-overrated-underrated/)”的 exercise：

> **Gregory Washington**：society 如何才能更好地 finding talent，而这件事为什么如此困难？
> 
> **Tyler Cowen:** 我觉得它之所以如此困难，是因为它并不关乎 fixed rules，并不是“总是 hire 来自这所学校的人”，或“Hire 穿这种鞋的人”。我觉得它最接近 music 或 art appreciation——你也无法把它归结为 fixed rules，但如果你花很多时间 studying music 或 art，或者其他一些 areas，就能在这方面进步很多。但对于什么是 great symphony，或者什么是 good pop song，从来都没有 fixed rules。
> 
> **GW:** 真的是这样吗？
> 
> **TC:** 是的。
> 
> **GW:** 但还是有一些 rules……
> 
> **TC:** 是有……它们不是 rules。是 principles。它们都有 exceptions，但我认为，说到 talent，对于大多数 jobs，**我们实际上 overrate 了 intelligence 的 importance，我们 underrate 了 values 的 importance，也 underrate 了我所说的 durability 的 importance**：对 mission、对自身的 dedication，以及 keep on going 的 ability……这取决于你正在 hiring 的 job，但我试图教给人们的一些 lessons，就是要寻找这些 qualities……

最后这个 aspect，durability，值得展开谈谈。Durability 并不完全等同于 grit，后者[似乎只是 conscientiousness 的一个 strong correlate](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/)。相反，这个关键 idea 可以追溯到 Robin Hanson 的这篇[文章](https://www.overcomingbias.com/2019/09/stamina-succeeds.html)：stamina。你也许能够 work super hard，而且非常 focused，但能持续多久呢？我的一位 PhD student 朋友，除了长时间的 lab work，还有时间 run 一个由同样热爱 biology 的人组成的 community，与许多 prospective PIs 见面，以便 rotating into 他们的 labs，还能找到很棒的浣熊表情包。她睡 4-5 hours。另一些人觉得起床很困难，等他们起来时已经很晚了，或者还没到 10pm 就困得不行。如果你 compare 一个每天 works 8 hours、因为只能承受这么多的人，与一个因为不累而能投入 16 hours 的人，那么在 intelligence、creativity 以及其他条件相同的情况下，后者的 productive 程度就是前者的两倍。即使某人多了几个 IQ points，也很难与 working hours 是自己两倍的人 compete。这不是支持 burnout 的 argument，只是一个 observation：有些人的 work-personal life 天然就非常 well aligned，而从 employer 的角度看，这一点是 valuable 的。

(II)

\[2\]. Tyler 的影响通过那些极具 Cowen 风格的轶事体现在书中：它们讲述世界各地被 underrated 的 talent，从 South African opera singers，到致力于 criminal justice reform 的 nonprofit founders。

什么是 talent？书中并没有 defined，但我们只能假定，“你看见时自然就知道了”。这本书花了相当多的篇幅考察各种 variables 与 income 之间的 correlations。如果你认为 talent 会得到 market 的 rewarded，这样做就有道理（这本书在多大程度上聚焦于 finding 那些能让你在 financially 方面 succeed 的 talent？），但这远非 general：如果我们将 talent 理解为能够 generates 某种 high value 事物的人，那么那些有才华的 public goods 的 producers，就会在 earnings 方面处于 disadvantage。其中，academics 是一个极其 smart、但报酬却不算特别丰厚的 group 的典型 example（不过，这似乎正在改变：如今，如果你是 top-tier [CS](https://www.forbes.com/sites/samshead/2018/04/20/ai-geniuses-are-being-paid-over-1-million-at-elon-musks-openai/?sh=4590f073113b) 或 [life sciences](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1483910381751783426) researcher，就可能拿到 million-dollar salaries）。Academics 所 playing 的 game，与让 $ number 增长并不是一回事。如果能把这本书中带有 Gross 风格和 Cowen[2](https://nintil.com/talent-review/#sidenote-2) 风格的部分区分开来，我怀疑这是一个深受 Gross 启发的 section：Academics 与 great companies 的 founders 是两种不同的人。“[你有多强烈地想要 win？](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LH1bewTg-P4)”只有在 defining 你正在 playing 什么 game 之后才有意义。但有一类 talented person 更愿意[imagine 可以去 play 的 new games](https://www.notion.so/blog/a-roundtable-on-richard-hamming)，而不是寻找可以 beat 的 opponents。Michael Nielsen 有[这样一条](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1222685935290052608)令人难忘的 tweet，恰好为某些 chapters 对 earnings 的关注提供了 counterpoint。Michael 是 Emergent Ventures 会感兴趣的那类 talented person，但 Pioneer 作为 venture capital firm 则不会。

类似地，书中提到，hierarchy-savvy 的 Vox founders Ezra Klein 等人 launched 了一个 successful website，而其他 bloggers 则穿着运动裤待在父母的地下室里，写着引人入胜的 posts。然而，Daniel Gross 在 [Twitter](https://twitter.com/danielgross/following) 上 follows 的是 internet besserwisser Gwern，而不是 Vox 那群人。Gwern 正在 winning，但不是在 moneymaking game 中。Gwern plays 自己的 game，尽管[我曾努力改变这一点](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1397996144001642497)，他的 leaderboard 中仍然只有他一个人。

我刚才做出的这番 assessment 看起来似乎有些指责的意味，但我并没有那个意思，我只是想拓宽你对 talent 是什么的理解。而且，可以提出这样的 argument：对于一个人可能持有的许多组 values，打个比方说，taking over the world 是 actualize 这些 values 的最佳方式。如果你在乎 community，可以创办一个 grouphouse，也可以发起一场倡导 co-living 的 social movement，促成成千上万所新 houses 的建立。如果你在乎 learning，可以数年如一日地痴迷于写 blog，也可以尝试创办一个 research organization。你还可以在这期间创办一家不相关的 company，再用所得 proceeds 支持后者。也许 Gwern 应该尝试[become a billionaire](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/billionaire/)，然后 discover 并 enable 接下来的 billion 个 Gwern。

(III)

> 最近，我最喜欢拿来烦别人的 questions 之一是：“你做些什么来 train，能够与 pianist practicing scales 相 comparable？”如果你不知道这个 question 的答案，也许你做错了什么，或者做得还不够。又或者，你并不那么 ambitious（而这或许是 optimally 的选择？）？（Marginal Revolution，[2019](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2019/07/how-i-practice-at-what-i-do.html)）

书中有一个类似的 question。我读到它时，答不上来。如果是当面被问，我大概会耸耸肩，说“我不知道”（按照这本书的说法，这是个糟糕的回答）。我当然确实在做一些事情（没错，我读书，也写这个 blog），但并没有那么刻意地把它们当作 self-improvement 的手段。也许我已经如此深地 internalized 了 self-improvement 的 ethos，以至于尽管整天都在工作，也不会立刻把这看作 self-improvement 的一种途径。

Piano scales 是 pianists 的 finger workouts：不仅涉及 strength 和 dexterity，更涉及整个 muscle memory，或许也涉及对 sounds 如何 work together 的 understanding。

这对于 knowledge work 没有 direct analogue，brains 似乎不会像闲置的手指那样生疏。一旦你已经擅长，就会一直擅长。会 decays 的是 intelligence（除了尽量保持健康之外，对此也做不了多少），还有 memory（对此可以用 spaced repetition 或 take notes，但 Tyler 两者都不做）。Indian [ragas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R6Xye0ogJ5I) 很棒，但我并不那么确信它们对其他 kinds of skills 有帮助。Transfer learning 很难！

writing 本身也需要一定的 finger dexterity 和 speed at typing，但我发现，good writing 更多是受到 good thinking 而不是 speed of typing 的 constrained；而且，除了大量 typing 之外，我没有特意尝试把这件事练得特别好，就已经能够 type relatively fast（80-100wpm）。但这并不是我 writing 中的 constraint，所以我不尝试 practice typing。

有一件显而易见的事能让你 improve：去做你正在尝试做的事。training 之所以存在，是为了把你想要 get better at 的 main activity 进行 break down，分成更容易 practiced 的 chunks。[Chess players](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge/#chess-a-domain-where-we-have-solved-tacit-knowledge-transfer) 主要通过 solo practice 来 get better，而不是大量 playing chess（书中讨论了一个类似的 example）。我们能否从 chess players 的 learn 方式中 learn，并把它 apply 到 talent search 上？

正如所链接的博文中讨论的，chess learning 通过 patterns 的 memorization，以及在 memory 中 formation of chunks 来进行，以帮助 efficient recall。Playing chess 不如 practice 那么 effective，因为一旦读完一本 opening book，之后任何 chess games 的 starting 都不会再 adding 多少新的 learning。相比之下，通过 chess problems，人们可以 focus 于 specific weak areas，并在需要 learning 的 context 中 learn examples。我多少相信，大量阅读 business books（biographies、corporate histories，以及关于 management techniques 的书）能教会人一些 management 方面的东西。Interviewing 和结识 talented people，可以 improve 一个人 identifying talent 的 skill（书中建议通过观看合适的电视节目来 jumpstart 这个 process，我赞同这一点）。但这些与相当于 mental piano scales 的练习相距甚远，它们更接近整天 playing piano。

仔细想想，这个 self-improvement question 还触及另一项重要的 information：candidate 正在尝试做什么？他们是否有 clear aims？那些更多处于 "exploit"（相对于 "explore"）phase 的人，往往会对这个 question 给出更清楚的回答。

(IV)

把尝试 [crack cultural codes](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2018/12/deconstructing-cultural-codes.html) 作为实现 talent spotting excellence 的一种手段，是另一条让我比较怀疑的 recommendation，不过也有一些 caveats。尽管两位作者最终达成了足以合写一本书的共识，我还是不太看得出 Daniel Gross 会像 [Tyler](https://kulesa.substack.com/p/tyler-cowen-is-the-best-curator-of) 那样接受 cracking cultural codes 的劝告（不过 Daniel 也不怎么 [write](https://dcgross.com/posts)，所以谁知道呢）。这是因为他正在 cracking 另一套 cultural codes（videogames？），还是因为他不认为这对自己所做的事有用？我肯定不会尝试在 cultural code cracking 上做太多，尽管我有自己常听的一批 [niche](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FVC11hQNWE) [music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kk0WRHV_vt8)，也一直在寻找更多，但那只是为了自己享受。

对于这为什么可能有效，一种 interpretation 是，比如了解 Indian culture，可能有助于更好地理解 Indians，从而找到 talented Indians。同样，与来自其他 cultures 的人 working 时，了解他们的 culture 可能会让 communication 更容易。但这件事究竟要做多少？Tyler 确实在 cultural code cracking 上钻研得很深。这些时间也可以用来阅读 longevity research papers、做更多 interviews、阅读自己想 hire 的 talented people 写的博文，或者在本地活动中 networking。

## 附录：IQ 与 job performance

以前，我可能会逐一 checking 所有 references，但如今我更关心 argument 的 directionality：即使一篇 paper 是 wrong，overall argument 仍然可能是 correct。这就是为什么这一节放在附录里，而不加入主要的书评。只有一篇 paper 似乎与我的 priors 足够 contradict，让我想去 check 它：_[IQ 真的会 predict job performance 吗 (Richardson & Norgate; 2015)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/10888691.2014.983635)_。Cowen & Gross 说：

> 关于 intelligence 和 job performance，也有相当多的 academic literature。其中最审慎、最深思熟虑的 study 出自 Ken Richardson 和 Sarah H. Norgate，标题为“IQ 真的会 predict job performance 吗？”。IQ 不一定与 general intelligence 是一回事，但 researcher 的 conclusions 相当令人清醒：“在 primary studies 中，这些 correlations \[在 IQ 与 job performance 之间\] 通常使超过 95% 的 variance 仍处于 unexplained 状态”

此外，在相关 citation 中，作者指向了广泛的 literature（暗示已经做过 broad survey），又增加了一项 reference 来 interpret 现有的 studies：[Byington & Felps (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191308510000043?casa_token=PvWJfwPfLHoAAAAA:hOZtCYUWvTkI2r33GiPEkH7j15fEkJ4olQVJDufFvZEpx6eOP-MrXl6JzAoQGM0u_WNoMG9ZYpw)。它有一定启发性，但我认为最终是 wrong，因为它忽略了其他 sources of evidence；这些 evidence 会 reinforce 这样一种 model：IQ-performance correlation 不受 paper 中描述的 hypothetical mechanism 影响（例如，IQ 也 predicts [video game performance](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0186621)）。

我想先说明这些 priors，它们来自 [Stuart Ritchie](https://twitter.com/StuartJRitchie?ref_src=twsrc%5Egoogle%7Ctwcamp%5Eserp%7Ctwgr%5Eauthor) 的 work（在 intelligence 的一切问题上，我都信任他），而他前些时候曾 summarized 这一领域的 state of the art（[Ritchie, 2016](https://www.amazon.com/Intelligence-That-Matters-Stuart-Ritchie/dp/1444791877/ref=sr_1_1?crid=CWVIZA6FD3R4&keywords=intelligence+all+that+matters&qid=1643998084&sprefix=intelligence+all+that+matter%2Caps%2C129&sr=8-1)）。

![image-20220204100714075](https://nintil.com/images/2022-02-04-talent-review/image-20220204100714075.png)

Richardson 的 paper 写于那本书之前，所以我在这里的 prior 是，Stuart 知道它，但 discounted 了它；不过书中没有直接 reference 它。还要注意，即使在认为 IQ 确实 matters 的“聪明人共识”中，它 matters 的程度也是“很大”，而不是完全决定一切；而且对于 leadership，这个 correlation 更小，而这正是书中关注的 character type（founder），因此在实践中，这本书是在对一个 broadly valid point 作 minor correction。

Richardson-Norgate 的 paper 说了什么？首先，这篇 paper 确实指出，measuring job performance 以及它与 IQ 的 relation 存在多个问题。job performance 究竟是什么，我们能否跨 domains 进行 aggregate？如果使用 supervisor scores，它们可能是 biased。上图所依据的 meta-analyses 纳入的 original studies，也就是引文所说的“primary studies”，发现的 correlations 更小；随后，meta-analysis 对各种 potential sources of errors 进行 correct，才得到了 Cowen & Gross 所怀疑的 ~0.5 correlation。此外，paper 的末尾也指出，即便完全按 literature 的表面结果来理解，哪怕是 0.5 correlation，也仍然 implies job performance 中有 75% 的 variance 是 unexplained；那么问题就在于，是否存在我们应该关注的、与 IQ 一样好的 _其他_ traits，还是说这 75% 大多是 noise，我们仍应该 based on IQ 作出 best guess。_Talent_ 中实际引用的那段话，提到 95% 的 variance unexplained，指的是这些 primary studies 在 inclusion 于 meta-analysis 之前、且没有 corrections 的结果。这些 corrections 是否 fair？这些 original studies 又是什么样的？比如说，从“retail food manager、fish and game warden、biologist、city circulation manager”（Table 1）中的 IQ-job performance correlation，extrapolate 到 startup founders 中的 IQ-job performance correlation，似乎确实有些牵强。因此，人们可以接受 literature 的表面结果，但随后把它放在一边，因为它对眼下这个 domain 并没有那么 informative。

从这里出发，人们可以继续花几天深入相关 literature，来回进行 methodological 争辩，finding flaws，然后寻找说明这些 flaws 为什么并非 fatal 的 arguments，再提出 counter-counter arguments，与一些 experts 交谈并 assessing 谁是对的；几年前我会这么做，但现在我更务实：假如我在 hiring，correlation 是 0.5 而不是 0.2-0.3 这一事实，会改变我的行动吗？整个 range 0.2-0.5 都 maps to“Intelligence 对 job performance 很重要，但并非只有它重要”，所以我们可以继续相信这一点。exact number 似乎并不很重要。当然，如果 numbers 更 extreme，比如 <0.1 或 >0.8，那么我们或许会想要几乎完全 based on intelligence 来 select，但事实并非如此。同样，如果这些 research literature 更 specific（比如，我们能够确定，特别对于 founders，这个 number 是 0.3 而不是 0.5），那么我们会赋予它更大的 weight，弄清正确的 number 也会更加重要。

恰好，我能找到的最后一篇 review，即 [Sackett](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5dd4c2c45ccc387bd8ffb210/t/61c206cdaacb6502d241f04b/1640105679615/overcorrecting+for+range+restriction+10-13-21.pdf) 等人（2021）的 review，将 correlation 定为 .31（相比之下，original studies 中是 .51）。

![image-20220204143219986](https://nintil.com/images/2022-02-04-talent-review/image-20220204143219986.png)

考虑到即使 ranks 为最佳的 measure（structured interviews）也远非 ideal，我们可以在这里提出这样的 argument：与其费尽心思榨尽最后一点收益，不如放弃这种做法，对那些 above a certain threshold 的 candidates 进行 hire randomly，这可能反而是 efficient 的；其思路类似于通过 [lottery](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/) 来 fund science 的 proposals。

## 附录：Reference checks？

这本书有一节讨论如何获取有关你的 talent 的 references。许多 interview processes 的一个常见环节，是要求 candidate 提交 colleagues 的姓名，最好还有 managers 的姓名，以便向他们询问 candidate 的情况。这本书接受获取 references 的 premise（例如，[High Growth Handbook](https://growth.eladgil.com/book/recruiting/recruiting-best-practices/#check-references) 和 [Marc Andreessen](https://pmarchive.com/how_to_hire_the_best_people.html) 也是如此），并给出了一些具体做法上的建议，但我仍然确信，references 并没有那么有用。prior information（整个 interview）与 colleague 一方的 sugarcoating 结合在一起，让人很难确定究竟能从中获得什么 good quality information。有些情况下，references 可以帮助 spot 一个 bad candidate：确实，如果 candidate 无法提供任何 references，那就是一个 red flag。难道不能在 interview 中更早 spot 这些 candidates 吗？这样的人有多常见？referencing process 确实能带来一些 information，但等待 reference checks 也会损失一些 velocity，联系 referees 还会耗费时间。~我的 baseline recommendation 是跳过这些 checks，不过，它们也可能在 context-dependent 的情况下有意义。~理想情况下，我们应当多听听 reference checks 的 proponents 的看法；如果能有一些 case studies，或一些关于它们多大频率会 drive hiring decisions 的 statistics，就会很有用。

遗憾的是，我们不得不依靠 non-academic information 来做这些 decisions：关于 job performance prediction 的[最新 review](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5dd4c2c45ccc387bd8ffb210/t/61c206cdaacb6502d241f04b/1640105679615/overcorrecting+for+range+restriction+10-13-21.pdf) 因为缺乏 information，无法 assess reference checks 的效果，因而放弃了对它们的讨论。

\[编辑补充 2021/02/12\] Armand Cognetta 向我指出，reference checks 还会 serve other functions，即使我们同意这样一个 basic premise：reference 一旦获得，通常都会对 candidate 有利。要求提供 references，尤其是要求提供很多份时（比如 9 份！），可以 tests candidate 拥有多大的 network、他们有多 resourceful，以及他们对这份 job 有多 interested（他们能多快拿到那些 references）。另一个不那么明显的好处是，reference checks 本身就是 built-in networking！你最终会认识一些新的人，而你以后可能会想联系他们。关键是要 ask questions，以 prompt 一些 potential negatives（从而避免最初预期会出现的、对 candidate 的 positive bias）：例如这样一个 question：“在从 0 到 100 的 scale 上，如果 100 代表 doing this job perfectly, above and beyond，你会把 person X 放在哪里？”你大概会得到一个不错但不 perfect 的 answer，比如 85。然后就可以问，为什么不是 100，而这可能会引出一些 potential minor negatives。

## 原文

_[Talent](https://www.amazon.com/Talent-Identify-Energizers-Creatives-Winners/dp/1250275814/ref=tmm_hrd_swatch_0?_encoding=UTF8&qid=1644014882&sr=8-1)_ (Tyler Cowen & Daniel Gross, St. Martin's Press, 2022) is a book about discovering (and hiring) talent. Most highly sold books about [hiring](https://www.amazon.com/gp/new-releases/books/2578/ref=zg_b_hnr_2578_1) are rather about _being hired_, whereas _Talent_ is primarily about the opposite problem. Talent comes in all shapes and sizes, so this book is attempting something harder than a book that tries to prepare for a particular kind of interview (like a software engineering interview), _Talent_ aims to be relatively general in the observations it makes.

(I)

Working towards its goal, the book blends academic research and the personal experience of the authors. I've been thinking lately about the relevance of academic research for real-life practical decision-making in various domains (See section 1 [here](https://nintil.com/notes-2021)), so I'd be intrigued to know what the book would have looked like had the authors just written from their (extensive) experience evaluating talent, and seeing how that matches research (I suspect the book would be relatively unchanged). Personal experience is underrated, and the authors are reasonable enough not to discard their personal knowledge if it conflicts with a particular research literature.

In succeeding at explaining what sort of things to look for when finding top talent, the book also succeeds as an example of the fundamental importance of [tacit knowledge](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge) in making these decisions. Intelligence matters, but far from exclusively! You want conscientious candidates, but not always. You might actually want to hire a subclinical schizoid as a consultant to generate interesting ideas, but not as a manager. And different people may present differently in an online vs an in person interview. Some may express themselves poorly when speaking but be extremely clear writers and thinkers.

One can't overcome the problem of tacit knowledge transmission with a book, if one reads the book 10 times, one would still not become a skilled judge of talent, so think of this book as pointing out what sorts of things to pay attention to, as a book on photography might point out how color, perspective, and composition go into making a picture that looks good, but one still has to get out there and take some pictures to learn how to take great photos.

\[1\]. Meta-game refers to the high level game a given social game exists in. The term is usually applied to videogames, where there's the game itself, and then there are details about how to play the game that depend in how others tend to play the game. For a longer discussion in the context of Starcraft see [here](https://liquipedia.net/starcraft/Metagame).

It's also a book that is refreshingly aware of the meta-game[1](https://nintil.com/talent-review/#sidenote-1) it exists in: If you tell your audience to use this or that interview question then candidates will learn about those and craft polished answers that will hinder the interviewer's ability to see what's there. But Cowen & Gross see through this: they discuss tactics like repeating a question asking for more examples exhaustively. Moreover they remind us not to obsess about specific questions or interview formats, but rather it seems the book is an argument to get us to become the kind of person that just naturally sees talent there where it is. Another of the questions, "How did you prepare for this interview?" is also another great example of breaking the fourth wall of the interview setting, helping reach the actual person that's behind the potentially staged performance.

The core of the book will seem unsurprisingly correct to a lot of the audience; the interesting bits are various aspects of someone's character that the authors point to and that should be salient to talent-choosers: For example we shouldn't just look at a candidate, but at their trajectory. Are they learning fast, or are they stuck at the same level of performance they have been for a few years? Two individuals that have the same current job position can be radically different in what to expect from them in years to come.

Indeed, the book is a long exercise of "[overrated or underrated](https://www.driverlesscrocodile.com/teams/tyler-cowen-on-talent-1-talent-spotting-as-art-and-overrated-underrated/)":

> **Gregory Washington**: How can society get better and finding talent and why is it so difficult?
> 
> **Tyler Cowen:** I think it’s so difficult because it’s not about fixed rules, it’s not about “Always hire the person from this school,” or “Hire the person who wears this kind of shoes.” I think it’s closest to music or art appreciation – so you can’t boil that down to fixed rules either, but if you spend a lot of time studying music or art, or some other areas, you can become much better at it. But there are never fixed rules for what’s a great symphony or what’s a good pop song.
> 
> **GW:** Is that really true?
> 
> **TC:** Yes.
> 
> **GW:** But there are some rules…
> 
> **TC:** There are… they’re not rules. There are principles. They all have exceptions, but I think that when it comes to talent, for most jobs **we actually overrate the importance of intelligence, we underrate the importance of values, and we underrate the importance of what I call durability**: the dedication to mission, to one’s self, and the ability to keep on going… It depends on the job you’re hiring for, but some of the lessons that I try to teach people are to look for those qualities…

That last aspect, durability is one worth expanding on. Durability is not quite grit, which [seems to be just a strong correlate of conscientiousness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/). Rather the key idea goes back to this [post](https://www.overcomingbias.com/2019/09/stamina-succeeds.html) from Robin Hanson: stamina. You may be able to work super hard and in a very focused way, but for how long? A PhD student friend, besides the long hours of lab work, also has the time to run a community of fellow biology enthusiasts, meet with lots prospective PIs for rotating into their labs, and find excellent raccoon memes. She sleeps 4-5 hours. Other people find it hard to wake up, and when they do it is very late, or get very drowsy when it's not even 10pm. If you compare a person that works 8 hours a day because that's all they can manage with someone that can put 16 hours into it because they are not tired you have, for the same intelligence, creativity, and whatever else, someone who is twice as productive. Even if someone has a few extra IQ points, it is hard to compete with someone that puts in twice the working hours you do. This is not an argument for burnout, it is just the observation that some people have a naturally very well aligned work-personal life, and that is valuable from an employer's point of view.

(II)

\[2\]. Tyler's influence shows in the book via quintessentially Cowenian anecdotes about underrated talent around the world, from South African opera singers to nonprofit founders aiming working on criminal justice reform.

What is talent? This is not defined in the book, but one can only suppose that "you know it when you see it". The book spends a nontrivial amount of time looking at correlations between various variables and income. Doing this makes sense if you think that talent is rewarded by the market (To what extent is the book focusing on finding talent that can make you succeed financially?), but this is far from general: if we take talent to be someone that generates something of high value, those that are talented producers of public goods will be at a disadvantage regarding earnings. Among them, academics are the chief example of an extremely smart, and yet not particularly well paid group (Though this seems to be changing: If you are a top-tier [CS](https://www.forbes.com/sites/samshead/2018/04/20/ai-geniuses-are-being-paid-over-1-million-at-elon-musks-openai/?sh=4590f073113b) or [life sciences](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1483910381751783426) researcher you can get to million-dollar salaries these days). Academics are playing a game that's different from making the $ number go up. If one could separate the Gross and the Cowen[2](https://nintil.com/talent-review/#sidenote-2) -flavored parts of this book I suspect that this is a very Gross-inspired section: Academics and founders of great companies are two different kinds of people. "[How strongly do you want to win?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LH1bewTg-P4)" only makes sense after defining what game are you playing. But there's a certain kind of talented person that prefers to [imagine new games to play](https://www.notion.so/blog/a-roundtable-on-richard-hamming), rather than finding opponents to beat. Michael Nielsen has [this](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1222685935290052608) memorable tweet that perfectly serves as a counterpoint to the focus on earnings of some chapters. Michael is the kind of talented person Emergent Ventures would be interested in, but Pioneer qua venture capital firm would not.

Similarly, whereas the book observes that hierarchy-savvy Vox founders Ezra Klein et al. launched a successful website, other bloggers stayed in their parent's basement in sweatpants writing intriguing posts. And yet it is internet besserwisser Gwern, and not the Voxers who Daniel Gross follows on [Twitter](https://twitter.com/danielgross/following). Gwern is winning, but not at the moneymaking game. Gwern plays his own game and despite [my efforts to the contrary](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1397996144001642497), he exists in a leaderboard of one.

This assessment I just made here seems somewhat accusatory, but I don't mean it in that way, I just want to broaden your idea of what talent is. And there is a case to be made that for many sets of values a person may hold, metaphorically taking over the world is the best way to actualize those values. If one cares about community, one can start a grouphouse, or one can start a social movement for co-living leading to thousands of new houses starting. If one cares about learning, one can obsessively blog for years, or one can try to start a research organization. You can also start an unrelated company inbetween, then use the proceeds for the latter. Maybe Gwern should try to [become a billionaire](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/billionaire/) and then discover and enable the next billion Gwerns.

(III)

> Recently, one of my favorite questions to bug people with has been “What is it you do to train that is comparable to a pianist practicing scales?” If you don’t know the answer to that one, maybe you are doing something wrong or not doing enough. Or maybe you are (optimally?) not very ambitious? (Marginal Revolution, [2019](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2019/07/how-i-practice-at-what-i-do.html))

There's a similar question in the book. When I read it, I had no answer. I would have shrugged and say "I don't know" in person (which is a bad answer, according to the book). There are things I certainly do (sure I read, and write this blog) but not that purposefully as a means of self-improvement. Maybe I have internalized the ethos of self-improvement so much that despite being working all day I don't immediately think of that as a vehicle for self-improvement.

Piano scales are finger workouts for pianists: not only but strength and dexterity but in all muscle memory, and perhaps understanding of how sounds work together.

This doesn't have a direct analogue for knowledge work, brains don't seem to get as rusty as unused fingers. Once you're good, you're good. The one thing that decays is intelligence (Can't do much about it other than trying to stay healthy), and memory (Where you could use spaced repetition or take notes, none of which Tyler does). Indian [ragas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R6Xye0ogJ5I) are great but I'm not so convinced they help with other kinds of skills. Transfer learning is hard!

The act of writing itself also requires some finger dexterity and speed at typing, but good writing is more constrained by good thinking, I find, than speed of typing, and I can type relatively fast (80-100wpm) without having tried to get particularly good at it, besides typing a lot. But this is not the constraint in my writing, so I don't try to practice typing.

There's an obvious thing that gets you to improve: Doing the thing you're trying to do. The reason that training exists is to break down the main activity you want to get better at into chunks that can be more easily practiced. [Chess players](https://nintil.com/scaling-tacit-knowledge/#chess-a-domain-where-we-have-solved-tacit-knowledge-transfer) get better primarily via solo practice, not playing a lot of chess (The book discusses a similar example). Can we learn from the way chess players learn and apply that to talent search?

As discussed in the linked blogpost, chess learning proceeds by memorization of patterns and the formation of chunks in memory to aid efficient recall. Playing chess is less effective than practice because once one has worked through an opening book, the starting of any future chess games are adding little new learning. Instead, with chess problems, one can focus on specific weak areas and learn examples in a context where learning is needed. I somewhat believe that reading a lot of business books (biographies, corporate histories, and books on management techniques) can teach something about management. Interviewing and meeting talented people can improve one's skill at identifying talent (The book suggests to jumpstart this process by watching the right TV shows, which I endorse). But these are far removed from the equivalent of mental piano scales, they are very close to playing piano all day.

On reflection, the self-improvement question gets at another important piece of information: What is the candidate trying to do? Do they have clear aims? Those more in an "exploit" (vs "explore") phase will tend to have clearer answers to the question.

(IV)

Trying to [crack cultural codes](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2018/12/deconstructing-cultural-codes.html) is another recommendation I'm more skeptical of as a means of talent spotting excellence, with some caveats. As much as the authors ended up agreeing enough to write a book, I don't quite see Daniel Gross embracing the cracking cultural codes admonition to the extent [Tyler](https://kulesa.substack.com/p/tyler-cowen-is-the-best-curator-of) does (Though Daniel doesn't [write](https://dcgross.com/posts) that much so who knows). Is this because he's cracking a different set of cultural codes (videogames?), or because he does not think that is useful for what he does? I definitely don't try to do much in terms of cultural code cracking, though I have my set of [niche](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FVC11hQNWE) [music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kk0WRHV_vt8) I listen to, and I'm always looking for more, but just for my own enjoyment.

One interpretation of why this may work is that learning about, say, Indian culture could help better understand Indians, and thus find talented Indians. Likewise when working with people from other cultures, knowing about their culture may make communication easier. But how much of this does one have to do? Tyler does go very deep into cultural code cracking. That time could be also spent reading longevity research papers, doing more interviews, reading blogposts from talented people that one wants to hire, or networking at local events.

## Appendix: IQ and job performance

Back in the day I might have gone around checking all the references, but these days I care more about the directionality of the argument: even if a paper is wrong, the overall argument can still be correct. This is why this section is in the appendix and doesn't add to the main review. There was only one paper that seemed to contradict my priors sufficiently for me to go check it: _[Does IQ really predict job performance (Richardson & Norgate; 2015)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/10888691.2014.983635)_. Cowen & Gross say:

> There is also a sizable academic literature on intelligence and job performance. The most measured and thoughtful study is by Ken Richardson and Sarah H. Norgate and is titled "Does IQ really predict job performance?". IQ need not be the same thing as general intelligence but the researcher's conclusions are pretty sobering: "In primary studies such correlations \[between IQ and job performance\] have generally left over 95% of the variance unexplained"

Moreover, in the relevant citation, the authors point to the broad literature (implying a broad survey was done), adding one additional reference to interpret the existing studies: [Byington & Felps (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191308510000043?casa_token=PvWJfwPfLHoAAAAA:hOZtCYUWvTkI2r33GiPEkH7j15fEkJ4olQVJDufFvZEpx6eOP-MrXl6JzAoQGM0u_WNoMG9ZYpw), which is suggestive, but I think ultimately wrong, ignoring other sources of evidence that reinforce the model where the IQ-performance correlation is free from the hypothetical mechanism described in the paper (e.g. IQ also predicts [video game performance](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0186621)).

I wanted first to state those priors, derived from the work of [Stuart Ritchie](https://twitter.com/StuartJRitchie?ref_src=twsrc%5Egoogle%7Ctwcamp%5Eserp%7Ctwgr%5Eauthor) (whom I trust in all things intelligence), and who summarized the state of the art some time ago ([Ritchie, 2016](https://www.amazon.com/Intelligence-That-Matters-Stuart-Ritchie/dp/1444791877/ref=sr_1_1?crid=CWVIZA6FD3R4&keywords=intelligence+all+that+matters&qid=1643998084&sprefix=intelligence+all+that+matter%2Caps%2C129&sr=8-1)).

![image-20220204100714075](https://nintil.com/images/2022-02-04-talent-review/image-20220204100714075.png)

The Richardson paper was written before the book, so my prior here is that Stuart was aware of it, but discounted it; though the book does not make a direct reference to it. Note also that even in the "smart person consensus" where IQ does matter, the extent to which it matters is "a lot" but not totally, and that correlation is smaller for leadership, which is character type (the founder) that the books focuses on, so in practice the book is making a minor correction to a broadly valid point.

What does the Richardson-Norgate paper say? First, the paper does note that there are multiple issues with measuring job performance and its relation to IQ. What is even job performance, and can we aggregate across domains? If we use supervisor scores, maybe those are biased. The original studies that go into the meta-analyses that the figure above comes from, the "primary studies" the quote refers to find smaller correlations, and then the meta-analysis correct for a variety of potential sources of errors, arriving them at the ~0.5 correlation that Cowen & Gross are skeptical of. Moreover, at the end of the paper it is also noted that even taking the literature at face value, even the 0.5 correlation implies that 75% of the variance in job performance is unexplained anyway; the question would there be where there are _other_ traits that are as good as IQ that we should pay attention to, or whether that 75% is mostly noise, and we should still take our best guess based on IQ. The actual quote in _Talent_, referring to 95% of the variance unexplained refers to these primary studies prior to their inclusion in a meta-analysis, and without corrections. Are these corrections fair? And what do these original studies look like? It does seem a bit of a stretch to extrapolate from, say, the IQ-job performance correlation in "retail food manager, fish and game warden, biologist, city circulation manager" (Table 1) to the IQ-job performance correlation in startup founders. So one could accept the literature at face value, but then sweep it aside, because it's not that informative for the domain at hand.

From here, one could proceed to spend a few days delving into the relevant literature, playing methodological ping pong, finding flaws and then arguments for why those flaws are not fatal and then counter-counter arguments, talking to some experts and assessing who is right and that's what I would have done a few years ago but now I'm more pragmatic: Will the fact that the correlation is 0.5 instead of 0.2-0.3 change my actions if I were hiring? The entire range 0.2-0.5 maps to "Intelligence matters for job performance, but not only" so we can continue to believe that. The exact number doesn't seem very important. Certainly if the numbers were more extreme like <0.1 or >0.8 then maybe we would want to select almost exclusively based on intelligence in general, but this is not the case. Similarly, if this research literature was more specific (Say, we could be sure that the number is 0.3 instead of 0.5 for founders in particular) then we would put more weight on it and it would matter more to figure out the right number.

As it happens, the last review I could find, [Sackett](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5dd4c2c45ccc387bd8ffb210/t/61c206cdaacb6502d241f04b/1640105679615/overcorrecting+for+range+restriction+10-13-21.pdf) et al. (2021) puts the correlation at .31 (vs .51 in the original studies).

![image-20220204143219986](https://nintil.com/images/2022-02-04-talent-review/image-20220204143219986.png)

Given that even the measure that ranks as the best (structured interviews) is far from ideal, one could make here the argument that it may be efficient to give up on trying to squeeze the lemon too hard, and hire randomly candidates that are above a certain threshold, in the same vein to proposals to fund science by [lottery](https://nintil.com/funding-lotteries/).

## Appendix: Reference checks?

The book has a section on how to get references on your talent. A common part of many interview processes is asking for a candidate to submit the names of colleagues and ideally managers to ask them about the candidate. The book accepts the premise of getting references (As does say the [High Growth Handbook](https://growth.eladgil.com/book/recruiting/recruiting-best-practices/#check-references), and [Marc Andreessen](https://pmarchive.com/how_to_hire_the_best_people.html)), and has some tips on how to go about doing that, but I remain convinced that references are not that useful. The combination of prior information (the whole interview) with sugarcoating on the colleague's side makes it very uncertain what good quality information you get from these. There are situations where references can help spot a bad candidate: indeed if the candidate is unable to produce any, that's a red flag. Can't these candidates be spotted earlier in the interview? How common are these people? There is some information gained from the referencing process, but some velocity lost in waiting for the reference checks, as well as time consumed by reaching out to the referees. ~My baseline recommendation is to skip the checks, but they may make sense in a context-dependent way.~ Ideally we would hear more from proponents of reference checks, having a number of case studies, or some statistics on how frequently they drive hiring decisions would be useful.

Unfortunately we have to rely on non-academic information to make these decisions: The [latest review](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5dd4c2c45ccc387bd8ffb210/t/61c206cdaacb6502d241f04b/1640105679615/overcorrecting+for+range+restriction+10-13-21.pdf) on job performance prediction gives up on reference checks due to lack of information to assess how well they work.

\[EDIT 2021/02/12\] Armand Cognetta pointed out to me that reference checks serve other functions, even when one agrees with the basic premise that the reference, once obtained, will be usually favorable to the candidate. Asking for references, especially if one asks for many of them (Say 9!) tests how much of a network a candidate has, how resourceful they are, and how much they are interested in the job (How quickly can they get those references). Another, less obvious benefit, is that the reference checks are built-in networking! You end up meeting new people that you may want to reach out to at a later time. It is key to ask questions that prompt some potential negatives (To avoid the initial expected positive bias towards the candidate): A question like "On a scale from 0 to 100, where 100 is doing this job perfectly, above and beyond, where would you put person X" will probably get a nice but not perfect answer like say an 85. Then one can ask why not 100, and that may lead to some potential minor negatives.
