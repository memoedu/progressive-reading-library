# 要更加 skeptical！

---

## 正文

寒假期间，我在 [GiveWell](http://www.givewell.org/) 实习了大约四周；这是一家 nonprofit，通过 evaluates charities 来弄清哪些 charities 的工作最 effective。在那里工作让我学到了很多东西，其中包括许多关于 GiveWell 如何运作、似乎并不广为人知的情况。因此，我想分享一下自己的 findings。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/gw/#fn:1)

## 要更加 skeptical！

### a.

在 developing world，water quality 是一个很大的问题。许多 natural water sources 中 contain 会 cause diarrhea 的 bacteria，而 diarrhea 是一大致死原因。如果对 water 进行 filter 或 chlorinate，就能去除那些 pathogens。

所以，如果给人们 filters 或 chlorine dispensers，就会 reduce 他们患 diarrhea 的频率，对吧？先别急——这确实是一个 plausible theory，但我们需要核查它在实践中是否真的有效。好在我们知道该怎么做：进行 randomized controlled trial！

于是，我们做了许多 studies。我们给一些 villages 提供了 dispensers，而没有给其他 villages 提供，并定期就 diarrhea prevalence 对它们进行 surveyed。果然，与 controls 相比，intervention villages 的 diarrhea 减少了。太好了！让我们 scale it up，挽救一些生命吧！

只不过，我们漏掉了一件事：这些 trials 并不是 blinded。intervention group _知道_自己获得的 water 更 cleaner。更糟的是，outcome（diarrhea incidence）是 self-reported。后来，我们又做了更多 studies，这一次对它们进行了 blinded，结果 _整个 effect 都消失了_。原来，self-reported diarrhea incidence 并不是一个足够 reliable 的 diarrhea measure。

只不过，我们又漏掉了一件事！那些 blinded studies 都存在 methodologocal problems。但那些 unblinded 的 studies 也一样！因此，evidence 究竟指向哪一边，其实并不清楚。你可以在 [GiveWell 的 intervention report](http://www.givewell.org/international/technical/programs/water-quality) 中了解更多；下面引述他们的 summary：

> 总体而言，我们对 water quality interventions 对 diarrhea 的 effect 持 ambivalent 态度。无论是相信 water quality interventions 会 reduce diarrhea，还是得出更 pessimistic 的 conclusion，认为这些 interventions 并没有 effect，我们都能找到以现有 evidence 为依据的 plausible theories。我们也没有看到任何 analysis 能在不额外投入大量时间的情况下，带来更 definitive 的 answer。因此，我们在此 summarize 迄今的工作，提供我们从 scholars 那里收到的 feedback，并留下有待进一步 investigation 的 questions。

再重复一遍：_阻止人们每天随饮水 ingesting diarrhea-causing bacteria，可能并不会 decrease diarrhea prevalence。_为什么？嗯，我也没什么头绪。一个 speculative guess 是，diarrhea 属于 [simple contagion](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_contagion#Examples_of_simple_contagion)，那些从自家 wells 感染 diarrhea 的人，也在从其他地方感染它。不过，正如 GiveWell 所指出的，两个方向都有 plausible theories。

### b.

我在 GiveWell 的任务，是围绕 promote exclusive breastfeeding 的 interventions 做 research。Global Burden of Disease study 使用了 Lamberti et al. 针对许多 observational studies 所做的一项 [meta-analysis](http://www.biomedcentral.com/1471-2458/11/S3/S15/)，据此 conclude，proper breastfeeding practices 每年最多可以避免 800,000 deaths，这使其成为 developing world 中 death 的第三大 risk factor（[来源](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140673612617668)）。这项 meta-analysis 发现，在最初六个月里，partially breastfed 的 infants 比 exclusively breastfed 的 infants 更 likely to die，程度超过 _four times_；而完全没有 breastfed 的 infants，其情况竟达到 _fourteen times more likely to die_。即使 controlled for 所有 known confounders——maternal income、maternal health、job outside the home 等——这一点也没有改变。多大的 effect 啊！而且，它被 GBD program 使用，在 cost-effectiveness studies 中被 widely cited，并且似乎得到了 public health community 的充分认可。

不过，与 water quality 不同，并没有直接针对 breastfeeding 的 randomized trials，因为让 participants 不对自己的孩子 breastfeed 是 unethical 的——breastfeeding 具有已知的 health benefits。但取两个 random groups，给其中一组提供 breastfeeding 的 _support_——peer counselling、关于其 benefits 的 education 等——_是_ ethical 的。而这会 substantially raises breastfeeding rates，往往几乎使其 doubling。

这些 studies 大多并不直接 measure mortality，想必是因为它们没有足够的 statistical power 来 detect 一个 reasonably-sized difference。不过，其中许多 studies _确实_ record 了 deaths，因为当你的某位 subjects dies 时，你就必须将其从 study 中移除，而 record attrition 的数量和原因是一种良好做法。当我 pooled 所有以这种方式 recorded mortality 的 studies 后，得到的结果与 Lamberti et al. 所获得的 results _totally inconsistent_。mortality 不但没有出现你可能预期会发现的 drop，反而在 intervention groups 中几乎达到了 significantly _higher_ 的程度。哎哟！[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/gw/#fn:2)

那么，这到底是怎么回事？我还是不知道。不过，在读了大约 30 篇关于 breastfeeding 的 papers、处理了许多 numbers 后，我怀疑，究竟是谁 breastfeeds，以及什么因素 mediates 其 benefits，这两方面的 intra-country variation 实在太大，以至于无法对其 effects 作出非常 general 的判断。在一些国家，问题在于 children 过早 weaned；这可能使他们失去母乳中的 nutrition 或 antibodies。在另一些国家，mothers 进行 breastfeed 的时间足够长，但她们过早 introduce complementary foods（这些 foods 可能 contaminated with pathogens），从而 causing diarrhea、respiratory infections 和 allergies。而在另外一些国家，mothers 又往往过晚 introduce solid foods，这可能 cause stunting 或 underweight，因为 breastmilk 最终不再有足够的 nutrition 来满足 growing baby 的需要。似乎还存在某种 bonding effect，尽管 developing-world studies 通常并不讨论这一点。而 breastfeeding 最终也可能会、也可能不会对日后的 IQ 产生一个较大的 effect，或许是通过前面提到的那些相同的 nutritional 或 health effects。所以，这至少涉及四种不同的 mechanisms 和六种 effects。真够复杂的。

Lamberti et al. 的 meta-analysis 参考了许多 studies，但他们关于 all-cause mortality 的 results 仅基于其中三项。因此，可能只是那个 countries 的 sample 还不够 representative。实际上，data 是 highly clustered 的，而他们的 confidence intervals 本应宽得多。又或者，存在 unknown confounders，一旦对它们 controlled for，就会改变 analysis。谁知道呢！

## Public health 是_真的很难_

看过我刚才写的内容，这大概不会让人意外，但我此前并没有意识到，要了解任何有关 public health 的事情究竟有多难。比如，在读过大约十项不同的 breastfeeding promotion 的 randomized controlled trials 后，我才意识到，study designers 要把所有细节都处理妥当有多难。

事实上，根据一篇关于这个主题的 Cochrane review，关于 breastfeeding support 的不错的 randomized controlled trials 有超过 50 项。其中，只有 11 项 measured 了任何 secondary outcomes——某种 health outcome，比如 infections 或 hospitalizations 的 reports，这些能为 breastfeeding 的 actual effects 增添 evidence base。对于确实这样做了的那些 studies，并没有一套 standardized 的 metrics 可供 test，因此不可能对 secondary outcomes 进行 formally meta-analyze；最后，我只是把所有 effects 做成一张 table，盯着它看，直到对究竟发生了什么产生某种直觉。许多 studies 在 trial 的 mechanics 上存在问题，比如 allocation（原来，根据婴儿出生的周次交替 assigning 到 treatment group，嗯，并不怎么 random）和 concealment（不，你的 data collectors 不应该知道他们的 interviewees 属于哪个 group）。standardization 的缺乏以及 protocols 上的困难，让人更难理解 breastfeeding promotion 的全貌。

## GiveWell 的视野比我以为的更宽广

GiveWell 经常被指责对 charity 的看法过于狭隘。对 global health 的关注可能显得像是一种 measurability bias：专注于那些 best-understood、most-researched 的 charitable cause，而忽略所有太难 analyze 的东西。在 global health 内部，他们也被指责只顾 treating symptoms，而不处理 underlying issues，同样是因为 distributing bednets 这样的事情，比方说 changing laws 或 improving education 更容易理解。有时候，这也被称为“risk aversion”——GiveWell 不想冒着 making a bad recommendation 的风险。

但事实上，他们对 global health 中 symptom treatments 的关注，与其说源于 risk aversion 或 measurability bias，不如说源于[对所有东西都进行大量 regressing](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/08/18/why-we-cant-take-expected-value-estimates-literally-even-when-theyre-unbiased/)。GiveWell 已经有过好几次作出“bad recommendation”的经历，也就是说，他们的 top charity 最后并没有他们希望的那么好（比如，VillageReach 的 data issues，以及 AMF 的 room for more funding）。但我不认为这让他们变得更加谨慎（指 sacrificing mean for variance），只是改变了他们对某些类型的 arguments 的看法。

回头看，我有些惊讶，人们仍然觉得 GiveWell 是 conservative 的，关注的是 strength of evidence，而不是 strength of effect。毕竟，[GiveWell Labs](http://www.givewell.org/givewell-labs) 现在大概占用了 GiveWell 的 50% 的 resources，而他们的[shallow cause overviews 列表](http://www.givewell.org/shallow)中，有相当多的 non-mainstream-EA causes，比如 advocacy、existential risk、open borders、animal welfare 等。我猜，随着 Labs 越来越活跃，GiveWell 墨守成规或 risk averse 的误解会进一步消散，但眼下它仍然存在。

## 事情对细节的依赖程度高得离谱

GiveWell 受到的另一项指责是过于细致。而他们确实细致得离谱。一位同事讲到，她曾对 GiveDirectly 做一些 due diligence，检查他们是否正确地 audited 了 field officers 的工作；这些 field officers 会检查村庄，记录哪些房屋有茅草屋顶——这是 GiveDirectly 用来决定谁会收到 cash transfers 的 criteria 之一。这需要查看每个相关村庄的 aerial photo，并将它与 audit spreadsheet 进行 comparing，以确保 GiveDirectly 已经注意到 field workers 工作中的所有 discrepancies。她提到这件事时，我_想象不出_这样做怎么可能获得值得的 return on time。_（更新：同事在会上描述这个过程时，我迟到了，显然作出了错误的 inference。这个 auditing process 涉及 comparing GiveDirectly 的 spreadsheets，以确保那些被 GiveDirectly 发现存在 discrepancy——比如 roof materials 不符——的 households，被 marked 为需要 additional auditing。）_

但在过去，许多支持 top charities 的 cases，都取决于一些细枝末节，其琐碎程度令人惊讶。DCP2 中的 Errors [radically changed](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/09/29/errors-in-dcp2-cost-effectiveness-estimate-for-deworming/) 了 deworming 的 cost-effectiveness estimates。一张对 VillageReach 的 evaluation 很重要的 chart，[结果](http://www.givewell.org/international/top-charities/villagereach/pilot-project-re-analysis)因为没有提及 missing data，而大大 overstate 了其 case。一些支持 SCI、看起来很有力的 evidence，在一些含糊措辞被正确解读后，[远比它们看起来的要弱](http://blog.givewell.org/2013/11/29/rethinking-scis-evidence-of-impact-2/)。

事实上，就连那个 audit 的例子，最后也没有听起来那么极端。查看 spreadsheets 时，我的同事注意到，一个村庄中的每一所房屋都被 marked 为需要 audit。结果，这只是 copying data 时的一个 error，但它也可能像那些措辞问题一样严重，后者基本上 destroyed 了那些 studies 的 evidential value。所以，GiveWell 的细致是可以理解的；这是他们从艰难的经历中学到的。

## Research 比我以为的更难

去那里工作之前，我以为 GiveWell 的 research process 是相对直接的。我会提出一个 question（或者别人交给我一个），读一堆相关的 papers，弄清 answer 是什么，补上 reasoning 中的任何漏洞，然后我们就知道该如何处理 developing countries 中的 breastfeeding promotion 之类的问题了。

但正如标题所说，事情并不是这样运作的。我的 research 始终处于 self-directed exploration-mode，不断朝各个方向再探究一点，看看哪些方向最有希望让我对 breastfeeding interventions 如何起作用形成 conclusive view。由于这种 reasoning 极其依赖 analysis 的细节，我必须同时在脑中记住有关各项 breastfeeding studies 的海量信息，并且非常频繁地将其 aggregate 成 micro-decisions；一旦我作出了错误的决定，就可能让我花上几个小时钻进死胡同。

阅读和作决定所占的比例远高于实际行动，这让这类 research 相当耗神。我现在更能理解，为什么 GiveWell 声称他们 bottlenecked on people，尽管一般的“research” skills 看起来应该供应充足：我很容易想象，在把自己的 research 引向恰当方向，以及对我在那里工作时必须吸收的大量信息产出良好的 synthesis 这两方面，人们的能力可能相差 orders of magnitude。

## 原文

For about four weeks over winter break I interned at [GiveWell](http://www.givewell.org/), a nonprofit that evaluates charities to figure out which ones do the most effective work. I learned a lot from working there, including a lot about how GiveWell works that seems not to be widely known. So I thought I’d share my findings.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/gw/#fn:1)

## Be more skeptical!

### a.

In the developing world, water quality is a huge concern. Many natural water sources contain bacteria that cause diarrhea, a major killer. If you filter or chlorinate the water, you can get rid of those pathogens.

So if you give people filters or chlorine dispensers, that’ll reduce how much diarrhea they get, right? Not so fast—it’s a really plausible theory, but we need to check that it actually works in practice. Fortunately, we know how to do this: a randomized controlled trial!

So we did a bunch of studies. We gave some villages dispensers and others not, and periodically surveyed them for diarrhea prevalence. Sure enough, diarrhea went down in the intervention villages compared to the controls. Great! Let’s scale it up and save some lives!

Except we missed a thing: the trials weren’t blinded. The intervention group _knew_ they were getting cleaner water. And what’s worse, the outcome (diarrhea incidence) was self-reported. And when we did more studies, and blinded them this time, _the entire effect went away_. Turns out that self-reported diarrhea incidence isn’t a reliable enough measure of diarrhea.

Except we missed another thing! The blinded studies all had methodologocal problems. Except so did the unblinded ones! So it’s not really clear which way the evidence points. You can find out more at [GiveWell’s intervention report](http://www.givewell.org/international/technical/programs/water-quality); to quote from their summary:

> Overall, we are ambivalent about the effect of water quality interventions on diarrhea. We find plausible theories grounded in the available evidence for both believing that water quality interventions reduce diarrhea and for the more pessimistic conclusion that these interventions do not have an effect. We also do not see any analysis that may lead to a more definitive answer without a significant, additional investment of time. We therefore summarize our work so far, provide feedback we’ve received from scholars and leave questions for further investigation.

Just to repeat that: _stopping people from ingesting diarrhea-causing bacteria every day with their water may not decrease diarrhea prevalence._ Why? Well, I don’t have much of an idea. One speculative guess is that diarrhea is a [simple contagion](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_contagion#Examples_of_simple_contagion) and people who were getting it from their wells were also getting it from other places. But, as GiveWell notes, there are plausible theories in both directions.

### b.

My assignment at GiveWell was to do research around interventions that promote exclusive breastfeeding. The Global Burden of Disease study used a [meta-analysis](http://www.biomedcentral.com/1471-2458/11/S3/S15/) by Lamberti et al. of many observational studies to conclude that proper breastfeeding practices could save up to 800,000 deaths anually, making it the third-biggest risk factor for death in the developing world ([source](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140673612617668)). This meta-analysis found that in the first six months, infants who were partially breastfed were over _four times_ more likely to die than infants who were exclusively breastfed, and infants who weren’t breastfed at all were a staggering _fourteen times more likely to die_. And that didn’t change even when you controlled for all of the known confounders—maternal income, maternal health, job outside the home, etc. What an effect! And it was used by the GBD program, widely cited in cost-effectiveness studies, and seemed to be well-endorsed by the public health community.

Now, unlike for water quality, there aren’t any randomized trials of breastfeeding directly, because it would be unethical to make participants not breastfeed their children—breastfeeding has known health benefits. But it _is_ ethical to take two random groups and give one of them _support_ for breastfeeding—peer counselling, education about its benefits, etc. And this raises breastfeeding rates substantially, often almost doubling them.

Most of these studies don’t measure mortality directly, presumably because they don’t have enough statistical power to detect a reasonably-sized difference. But many of them _did_ record deaths, because when one of your subjects dies you have to drop them from your study, and it’s good practice to record the amount and reason for attrition. And when I pooled all the studies that recorded mortality this way, it was _totally inconsistent_ with the results obtained by Lamberti et al. Instead the drop in mortality that you might expect to find, mortality was almost significantly _higher_ in the intervention groups. Ouch![2](https://www.benkuhn.net/gw/#fn:2)

So what gives? Again, I don’t know. But my suspicion, after reading about 30 papers on breastfeeding and crunching a bunch of numbers, is that there’s simply too much intra-country variation in who breastfeeds, and in what mediates its benefits, to say anything very general about its effects. In some countries, the problem is that children are weaned too early; this might deprive them of nutrition or antibodies in their mother’s milk. In others, mothers breastfeed for long enough, but they introduce complementary foods (which may be contaminated with pathogens) too early, causing diarrhea, respiratory infections, and allergies. In yet other countries, mothers tend to introduce solid foods too late, which can cause stunting or underweight since breastmilk eventually stops having enough nutrition for a growing baby. There seems to be some sort of bonding effect as well, although this isn’t usually discussed in developing-world studies. And breastfeeding might or might not end up having a largish effect on IQ down the road, maybe through the same nutritional or health effects as before. So that’s at least four different mechanisms and six effects. Yikes.

The meta-analysis of Lamberti et al. drew from many studies, but their results on all-cause mortality were based on only three. So it’s possible that that simply wasn’t a representative enough sample of countries. In effect, the data was highly clustered, and their confidence intervals should have been way wider. Or maybe there were unknown confounders that would have changed the analysis when controlled for. Who knows!

## Public health is _really hard_

This probably doesn’t come as a surprise after what I just wrote, but I hadn’t previously grasped just how difficult it is to know anything about public health. For instance, after reading maybe ten different randomized controlled trials of breastfeeding promotion, I realized just how hard it is for study designers to get all the details right.

In fact, there were over 50 decent randomized controlled trials of breastfeeding support, according to a Cochrane review on the subject. Of those, only 11 measured any secondary outcomes—some kind of health outcome like reports of infections or hospitalizations that would add to the evidence base on the actual effects of breastfeeding. For the ones that did, there was no standardized set of metrics to test, so it was impossible to formally meta-analyze the secondary outcomes; I ended up just making a table of all the effects and staring at it until I came up with some sort of intuition about what was going on. Many of the studies had problems with the mechanics of the trial, like allocation (it turns out that assigning to treatment group by alternating based on the baby’s birth week is, um, not very random) and concealment (no, your data collectors aren’t supposed to know which group their interviewees are in). The lack of standardization and the difficulty with protocols made it much harder to understand the big picture on breastfeeding promotion.

## GiveWell takes a broader view than I thought

GiveWell are often accused of treating charity too narrowly. The focus on global health can come off as a kind of measurability bias: focusing on the the best-understood, most-researched charitable cause and ignoring anything that’s too hard to analyze. Within global health they’ve also been accused of treating symptoms rather than underlying issues, again because something like distributing bednets is easier to understand than e.g. changing laws or improving education. Sometimes it’s also described as “risk aversion”—GiveWell doesn’t want to risk making a bad recommendation.

But in fact, their focus on symptom treatments in global health doesn’t arise from risk aversion or measurability bias so much as [regressing everything a ton](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/08/18/why-we-cant-take-expected-value-estimates-literally-even-when-theyre-unbiased/). GiveWell has several times had the experience of making a “bad recommendation” in the sense of their top charity turning out to be not quite as good as they’d hoped (for instance, VillageReach’s data issues and AMF’s room for more funding). But I don’t think this has made them any more cautious (in the sense of sacrificing mean for variance), just changed their views on certain types of arguments.

In retrospect, it’s somewhat surprising to me that the impression still persists that GiveWell is conservative and focused on strength of evidence rather than strength of effect. After all, [GiveWell Labs](http://www.givewell.org/givewell-labs) is now maybe 50% of GiveWell’s resources, and their [list of shallow cause overviews](http://www.givewell.org/shallow) is pretty heavy on non-mainstream-EA causes like advocacy, existential risk, open borders, animal welfare, etc. I guess the myth that GiveWell is stodgy or risk averse will dissipate more as Labs becomes more active, but right now it still exists.

## Things depend ridiculously on the details

Another thing GiveWell gets accused of is being too thorough. And they truly are ridiculously thorough. One coworker recounted doing some due diligence on GiveDirectly by checking that they had correctly audited the work of their field officers who checked villages to mark down which houses had thatched roofs—one of the criteria GiveDirectly uses to determine who received cash transfers. This involved looking at an aerial photo of each village in question and comparing it to the audit spreadsheet to make sure that GiveDirectly had noticed all the discrepancies in their field workers’ work. When she mentioned this, I couldn’t _imagine_ how it could have a worthwhile return on time. _(Update: I came in late at the meeting where my coworker described this process and apparently made the wrong inference. The auditing process involved comparing GiveDirectly’s spreadsheets to make sure that households where GiveDirectly found a discrepancy, e.g. in roof materials, were marked for additional auditing.)_

But in the past, many cases for top charities have hinged on surprisingly picayune details. Errors in the DCP2 [radically changed](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/09/29/errors-in-dcp2-cost-effectiveness-estimate-for-deworming/) the cost-effectiveness estimates for deworming. A chart important for the evaluation of VillageReach [turned out](http://www.givewell.org/international/top-charities/villagereach/pilot-project-re-analysis) to overstate the case significantly because it didn’t mention missing data. Some strong-looking pieces of evidence for SCI [were much weaker than they appeared](http://blog.givewell.org/2013/11/29/rethinking-scis-evidence-of-impact-2/) once some unclear wording was interpreted correctly.

In fact, even the audit example turned out to be less extreme than it sounded. While looking at the spreadsheets, my coworker noticed that in one village every house was marked as needing an audit. It turned out to be just an error in copying over the data, but it might have been something as severe as the wording issues which essentially destroyed the evidential value of those studies. So GiveWell’s thoroughness is understandable; it’s been won through hard experience.

## Research is harder than I thought

Before working there, I thought that GiveWell’s research process was relatively straightforward. I’d come up with (or be given) a question, read a bunch of papers on it, figure out what the answer was, shore up any holes in the reasoning, and then we’d know what to do about (e.g.) breastfeeding promotion in developing countries.

As the headline indicates, though, that’s not how it worked. My research was constantly in self-directed exploration-mode, probing a little bit further in various directions to see which ones would be the most fruitful in finding a conclusive view of how breastfeeding interventions work. Because of how extremely this kind of reasoning depends on the details of the analysis, this required me to hold a huge amount of information about the various breastfeeding studies in my head at once, and very frequently aggregate it into micro-decisions that could cause me to spend hours down a rabbit hole if I made them incorrectly.

Something about the high ratio of reading things and making decisions to actually taking actions made this kind of research pretty draining. I now understand much better why GiveWell claims that they’re bottlenecked on people despite the fact that general “research” skills seem like they should be in good supply: it’s easy for me to imagine people varying by orders of magnitude in their ability to direct their research in appropriate directions and produce a good synthesis of the amount of information I had to take in during my work there.
