# Telomeres: everything you always wanted to know

---

## 正文

早在 2020 年撰写 [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity/) 时，我就写了一节关于 telomeres 的内容，并粗略考察了它们对 aging 可能有多重要。如今我了解得更多了，是时候重新审视这个问题，也补充一些历史背景，以及我写完那篇 FAQ 后发表的一些 papers。

写这篇文章的直接原因是，每当我说自己研究 aging（在 [Retro](https://retro.bio/) Biosciences，我们正在招聘！）时，常常有人问我是不是“telomeres 那回事”。不知为什么，几年前人们开始普遍把 telomeres 与 aging 联系起来。我想弄清楚原因，同时也想了解它们实际上有多相关，因为我怀疑自己和其他人都矫枉过正，从“telomeres 是 aging 的关键”过度转向了“它们没那么重要”。时不时就能看到一些看似信誉良好的机构，比如 [The Nobel Prize](https://twitter.com/NobelPrize/status/1358685249543299074) 的推特账号，声称“\[我们为什么会 age 的\]秘密就在我们的 telomeres 中”，而这个领域的人则会嘲笑它们。

首先，telomeres 是 chromosomes 末端的 regions，因此我们所有的 cells 都有 telomeres。FAQ 对这些 regions 的具体形态有稍为详细的介绍；对本文而言，重要的是：当一个 cell 进行 divides 时，它必须 copy 自己的 DNA。发生这一过程时，telomeres 会 shrink。当 telomeres 变得 too short 时，cells 就会停止 replicating，并变得 'senescent'。有一种 enzyme 叫作 telomerase。Telomerase 并不是单一的 protein，而是一种 complex，由（在 humans 中）proteins TERT（telomerase reverse transcriptase）、TERC（telomerase RNA）和 DKC1（dyskerin）组成。

有一点很重要，却有时会在讨论中被忽略：当有人谈论 telomeres 的 shortening 或 lengthening 时，通常指的是 T-cells 等 white blood cells 的 telomeres，因为 blood samples 最容易获取。这些 cells 的 telomeres 发生 Changes，并不 imply 其他地方的 telomeres 也发生 changes。尤其是 T-cells 能够 divide，而不同种类的 white blood cells 的 populations 会随 external factors 而 change，因此也可能出现这种情况：通常 measured 的 telomeres 之所以 lengthen，仅仅是因为具有 shorter telomeres 的 cells 发生 die，于是 average telomere size 随之 increases。

例如，一个人如果更多地 exposed to infections 和 carcinogens，可能就会让自己的 T-cells 更加卖力地工作，这意味着更多的 divisions 和 telomere shortening。这个人可能会更早 die，而在一项 cross-sectional study 中，我们可能看到 shorter telomeres 与 earlier mortality 存在 correlate with 的关系，但在这种情况下，这仅仅是 exposure to disease 的 downstream biomarker，而不是 direct causal driver。

哪些 cells 可以 divide（因此，哪些 cells 的 telomeres 有可能 shorten？）fibroblasts、endothelial cells、smooth muscle cells、glial cells，或 astrocytes。相比之下，neurons 或 myocytes 不会 divide，因此我们不应预期 telomere shortening 与这些 cells 的 aging 有多大关系。

通常赋予 telomeres 的 function 是充当一种 anti-cancer mechanism：如果一个 cell 开始过多地 dividing，那么它的 telomeres 就会 progressively shorten，而它将停止 dividing（或 die）。为了克服这一点，cancers 最终会通过 reactivating telomerase 来维持其 telomere length。没有 telomerase 时，它们也可以采用 [ALT](https://cellandbioscience.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13578-020-00391-6)（Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres），因此在 all else equal 的条件下，我们会预期 telomere elongation 将 lead to 更高的 cancer likelihood，但这种看法过于简单，因为 longer telomeres 也可能意味着 better functioning immune system（能够更多地 divide 的 T-cells）和更少的 inflammation（更少的 [senescent](https://nintil.com/longevity/#chromosomes-and-telomeres:%7E:text=facts%20about%20it.-,Cellular%20senescence,-I%27ve%20mentioned%20senescence) cells）。

关于 telomeres 及其相关性的历史，至少可以追溯到 Leonard [Hayflick's](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0014482761901926?via%3Dihub) 1961 年和 [1965](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=937a6abe443dfa2bc0829da18c2ff236d321deab) 年关于他所谓 senescence 的 papers：在 culture 中，cells 最终会停止 dividing；在那篇 paper 中，他指出了 cells 的 intrinsic factors，但当时并没有将其与 telomeres 联系起来。从 older donors 身上 extracted 的 Cells 会更早停止 dividing，这表明一个 cell 能够经历的 divisions 总量存在 limit，而且这个时钟从我们出生时就开始走动，并非只在 in vitro 才开始。

直到 [1970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9415101/) 年，[那时](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/293811225_Principle_of_marginotomy_in_the_synthesis_of_polynucleotides_at_a_template)一位苏联科学家 Alexey Olovnikov 突然有所领悟：

> The Theory of Marginotomy \[JR: Telomere shortening\] 是我在莫斯科那个地铁站里想到的。我听到一列驶近的列车发出低沉的轰鸣，从隧道进入车站。我把 DNA polymerase 想象成沿着隧道行驶的列车，而把隧道想象成 DNA molecule。**我想，这种 polymerase 无法从最开头开始 copy，因为 polymerase molecule 的 front end 与其 catalytic center 之间存在一个 dead zone。这类似于一节停在地铁站台起点的车厢：其前端与第一节车厢最近的入口门之间有一段盲区。**这场偶然发生在地下的头脑风暴是在 1966 年秋天。之后，我给 Hayflick 写信，询问了一些有关他的 discovery 的问题，他寄给我更多尚未发表的 data。随后我花了数年思考这个想法，才将其发表在我们科学院的中央期刊 Doklady Academii Nauk SSSR 上。

因此，他是第一个提出以下观点的人：正是 imperfect DNA replication 导致的 telomere shortening，在 causing Hayflick 所 observed 的现象。

与此同时，在美国，后来会因 telomeres 研究而获得诺贝尔奖的 Elizabeth Blackburn，于 [1978](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0022283678902942?via%3Dihub) 年发表了一种 protozoan——_Tetrahymena thermophila_——的 chromosomes 中这些 regions 的 sequence。结果发现，humans 中的 Telomeres 是 TTAGGG（repeated 约 3000 次），而 tetrahymena 中的则是 TTGGGG。其他 species 有其他 [repeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telomere)。此后的几年里，她将与 Jack [Szostak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_W._Szostak) 合作研究 telomeres。

后来，在 1984 年，Blackburn 的一位学生 Carol Greider [discovers](https://jscholarship.library.jhu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/9f71eb7d-0a40-4286-8d41-da0ab88ea77c/content) 了 enzyme telomerase，最初它被称为 telomere terminal transferase。

Blackburn 和 Greider 的研究还指出，telomerase 被 knocked out 的 Tetrahymena 会生病，并很快 died（[1990](https://sci-hub.wf/10.1002/bies.950120803)），因此 telomeres 和 telomerase 看来是维持 cells 存活的关键。Hayflick 的研究使用的是来自 humans 的 cells，与之不同，Tetrahymena 是完整的 unicellular organisms，因此她们的研究提供了更 robust 的 evidence，表明 telomeres 可能在 organismal scale 上 driving senescence。她们还指出，这一 system 可能是为 prevent cancer 而 evolved 的，从而使 senescence 与 cancer 相互对立。但原则上，人们可以设想：_如果_有办法 prevent cancer，那么在 mice 等更 complex organisms 中，telomere restoration 能否在 organismal level 上 prevent aging？

受到这些 findings 的吸引，[Geron Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geron_Corporation) 于 1990 年成立，邀请 Greider 和 Hayflick 担任顾问，希望 develop 针对 aging 的 telomere-based therapies。据 [Greider](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sageke.2003.42.nf19) 所说，似乎正是 Geron 及其公关部门在普通大众中掀起了“telomeres drive aging”的说法。

在 90 年代，一[本书](https://www.amazon.com/Reversing-Human-Aging-Michael-Fossel/dp/0688143245)问世，名为 _Reversing Human Aging_，也支持将 telomere shortening 视为 aging 的关键 explanation。在那本书中，我们可以看到 telomeres 被称为“aging 的时钟”，以及这样的说法：“Aging 是由 aging cells caused 的，而 aging cells 又是由 aging telomeres caused 的。”

1998 年，在 Geron 科学家的努力下，采用 regular cells 并 turn on telomerase、从而 permanently preserving telomeres，第一次[成为可能](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9454332/)。这使 cells 能够 escape senescence。这一 process 被称为 'immortalization'。在[媒体](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1998/01/14/scientists-discover-way-to-prolong-life-of-cells/231de2f6-5179-4f72-bb8a-46d8f7a08c58/)中，我们看到这样的引语：“我们发现，biological aging 可以被暂停。”

到了这个阶段，顺理成章的下一步就是将其用于 mice，看看会发生什么。因此，在 [2001](https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.1093/emboj/20.11.2619) 年，Blasco lab 在 mice 的 skin 中某一类 cell 里 turned on telomerase。结果并不是 mice 更加 longer lived，而是这些 mice 出现了更多 skin cancer（以及更快的 wound healing），证实了 telomeres 的双刃剑性质。

一年[之后](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.112515399?url_ver=Z39.88-2003&rfr_id=ori%3Arid%3Acrossref.org&rfr_dat=cr_pub++0pubmed)，另一篇 paper 发表，研究从出生起就在整个 mouse 中 turning on telomerase 的 effect。其 results confirm 了 Blasco 的结果：他们同样观察到了 cancers 的 increase。

结合这两篇 papers 以及另外几篇来看，2004 年构成了 aging 的 telomere hypothesis 的一个转折点。Gerontologist João Pedro de Magalhaes 发表了 [Telomeres 与 Telomerase：现代的青春之泉](http://rejuvenomicslab.com//rr04_telomeres_telomerase_aging.pdf)？并在其中以非常怀疑的语气得出结论：

> 尽管 telomerase 可能用于 regenerative medicine 及 treat specific diseases，但它不太可能成为 anti-ageing therapies 的来源。

其中提出了一个关键点，Greider 在试图反驳围绕 telomere 的炒作时也提过：telomere length 与一个 species 的 longevity 之间不存在 correlation。Mice 的 telomeres 比我们的 longer，但它们的寿命却短得多。文中据此得出结论：

> 因此，telomere length and/or telomerase activity 并不能 explain 为什么 humans 比其他 primates 和 mice 更慢地 age。

但这并不完全正确。多年之后（在 [2019](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1902452116) 年），Blasco 等人会证明：虽然 telomere lenght 的确不会与 longevity 发生 correlate，但 shortening 的 rate（在不同 species 中有所不同）却_确实会 correlate fairly well_：

![图片-20231015142820347](https://nintil.com/images/2023-10-15-telomeres/image-20231015142820347.png)

这并不意味着它们决定了 aging 的 pace！其他因素，比如 [DNA](https://twitter.com/NathanielEDavid/status/1514725731711942656) mutations 的 accumulation 的 rate，也与 lifespan 很好地 correlate。一般来说，如果长寿必须依靠许多 mechanisms，那么 evolution 就会 jointly optimize 所有这些 mechanisms，以使 lifespans 逐步 increased。看来，无论是 fast shortening telomeres，还是 high mutational burdens，都无法与长寿并存。

在 de Magalhaes 的 critique 之后，Blasco 于 [2008](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674\(08\)01191-4?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS0092867408011914%3Fshowall%3Dtrue) 首次发表了一份通过 telomerase overexpression 实现 lifespan extension 的 report，尽管是在 cancer-resistant mice 中。好吧，有人可能会说，让 telomeres 保持 long 会 causes cancer，但如果我们通过 genetically engineering 使这些 mice 比 normal mice 所能达到的状态更 resilient，从而避免这一点，那么我们能让 mice 拥有 healthier、更长的 lives 吗？答案是能。与 baseline 的 non-telomerase'd cancer resistant mice 相比，那些具有 constant telomerase expression 的 mice：

-   随着 age 增长仍 Preserve skin thickness
-   Preserve GI tract 中的 [villi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_villus) structure（以及 associated gut barrier integrity->less leaky gut）
-   在 motor test 中表现更好
-   Show improved glucose metabolism（表现出改善的葡萄糖代谢）
-   Live 的时间 substantially 更长，而且更多 mice 达到 very old age（42% vs 8%）
-   正如人们可能预期的那样，telomeres 更 longer

很有希望！

事实证明，对 telomere biology 的 Understanding 足够重要，使 [Blackburn](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2009/summary/)、Greider 和 Szostak 在 2009 年获得了 Nobel Prize。（对 [Olovnikov](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/sponsored/rbth/6396300/No-Nobel-physiology-and-medicine-award-for-Russian-gerontologist-Aleksey-Olovnikov.html) 来说，那是难过的一天）

随后一年，随着 _[Telomerase reactivation reverses tissue degeneration in aged telomerase-deficient mice](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature09603)_（端粒酶再激活逆转衰老端粒酶缺陷小鼠的组织退化）于 2010 年发表，telomere hypothesis 继续获得 support。请注意，这些并非 regular mice，而是完全没有 telomerase 的 mice；它们的 telomeres 比通常情况短得多。

有了这篇 paper，当时便有了 evidence，表明 longer telomeres 可以在某种 context（cancer-resistant mice）中 extend lifespan，而 telomerase deficiency 可以 shorten 它，并且 adding back telomerase 可以 reverse 这一点。但这能在 regular mice 中起作用吗？

在 [2012](https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.1002/emmm.201200245) 年，Blasco lab 再次发表了一篇 paper，他们采用 regular mice，并使用一种 AAV gene therapy 来 cause widespread telomerase activation。result 是：无论该 therapy 是在 early in life（24% increase）还是 later in life（13% increase）delivered，都出现了 increased lifespan。这一 increase 还伴随着其他 health benefits。重要的是，它们的 cancer 更少，而不是更多。他们 especulate，由于 AAV 会在 fast dividing cells 中被 diluted，cancer cells 将无法从这些额外的 telomerase 中 benefit。

同年，一个由 aging researchers 组成的 consortium 发表了该领域可能被 cited 最多的 paper，_[Hallmarks of Aging](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674\(13\)00645-4?source=post_page---------------------------)_（衰老的标志），在其中，他们将 telomeres 的 importance 确立为一种 consensus view。

再后来，在 2015 年，我们看到了一个有趣的 self-experimentation case。[Elizabeth](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2016/jul/24/elizabeth-parrish-gene-therapy-ageing) Parrish 是一家名为 BioViva 的 biotech company 的 CEO，她为自己 injects 一种 gene therapy，以 lengthen 她的 telomeres。他们在 2021 年又在一些 [volunteers](https://maplespub.com/article/Safety-Study-of-AAV-hTert-and-Klotho-Gene-Transfer-Therapy-for-Dementia) 身上做了一次，进行得相当低调，我直到今天才听说这项 study！

同年，我们又看到[另一篇](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568163715300155#bib0065)针对 telomeres involvement 于 aging 的 critique。这篇 paper 开篇指出，在 mice 中 knocking out telomerase 并 breeding 这些 mice，会导致 newborns 的 telomere size 随着 generations 递减，但在 first generation 中并不会 decrease lifespan（不过之后会），这 implying，mice 中的 telomere length 并不是其 lifespan 的 limiting factor。那么，Blasco lab 在 2012 年使用 AAV 的那篇 paper 又该如何解释？author argues，其 sample size 很小，而且所观察到的 effects 背后，可能是 telomerase expression 本身（而不只是 telomere length）在起作用。他也对 telomeres 能够 involved 于 aging 表示怀疑，因为 mice 的 telomere length 在 inbred strains 与 true wild mice 之间 varies 很大，却并不 correlating 于 lifespan（我们认为这并不神秘：重要的是 shortening rate，但 Blasco 的那篇 paper 当时还没有发表）。

Blasco 后来在一篇令人印象深刻的 2019 年 paper 中，对这些 critiques 中的一项提供了回答：具有 [hyperlong](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12664-x) telomeres 的 mice live 得更久（12.8%），并且在多个方面更 healthier。这些 mice 经过 bred，在没有 overexpressing telomerase 的情况下拥有 longer telomeres，因此可以 rule out telomerase 的 potential action 造成 observed effects 的可能。它们的 cancer 也更少，这 suggesting，来自更好的 immune system 的 anti-cancer effects，或 longer telomeres 带来的 inflammation reduction，outweigh 了 telomerase 的 tumorigenic risks。

所以，在 2012 年的 AAV paper 之后，人们或许会认为该领域的许多人会对 telomeres 比较看好，但事实并非如此。Aubrey de Grey 在 2018 年被问及 telomere lengthening 的前景时说，他担心 cancer 的 risk，并指出了一些显示 cancer increase 的 studies。为了解释那些具有 telomerase overexpression 的 mice living 得更久的 results，他指出 observation periods 太短（AAV paper 绝对不是这种情况），或者这些 animals 特别 cancer resistant（较早的 papers 确实如此）。

最后，de Grey 还 cited 了一些 human genetics studies，它们使用 mendelian randomization，观察当 humans 中 telomere shortening 的 rate 被 altered 时会发生什么。result 是 cancer 有 [slight](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/acel.13017) increase，coronary artery disease 有 slight decrease，而对 lifespan 则是 null effect，这 implying，尤其对于 humans 而言，telomere length 不会 extend life。也许吧！但请记住，在 mice 的 general population 中，telomeres 的 length 并不 determine lifespan，尽管 hyper-long telomere mice 确实 show improved lifespan。拥有 hyper-long telomeres 的人会不会 live 得更久呢？考虑到 human telomeres shrink 得如此缓慢，我并不预期如此，但这种可能性确实存在。人们还可以指出其他据称表明 humans 中的 long telomeres 是 deleterious 的 papers；[在 POT1 中具有 mutations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37140166/) 的 gene 携带者拥有 longer telomeres，但有人可能会回应说，是 mutation 本身在 telomeres 之外产生了 deleterious effect。可以设想一种 one-off AAV therapy，它 lenghtens telomeres，而如果 cells turns cancerous 并开始 dividing，AAV 就会被 diluted，从而使对 cancer 的 effects 被 minimized（正如 Blasco et al. 在他们自己的 paper 中所 speculated 的那样）。

另一篇 critique 来自 [Ehninger](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01680-x) lab，发表于 2022 年。authors 正确地指出，在不同 mice strains 之间，telomere length 与 lifespan 没有 correlation，并由此 inferring，telomere shortening 并不 underlie murine aging。但考虑到 hyperlong telomeres 的那篇 paper，这似乎不对。他们还指出 telomere lengthening 会 increasing carcinogenesis，却把各种不同的 interventions 混在一起。Constitutive telomerase activation 并不等同于通过 virus delivered 的 transient activation（后者会更接近假设中的 clinical translation）。Ehninger 只是统计了声称 cancer 更多与更少的 studies 的数量。2 项 studies 显示更少（AAV study 和 hyperlong telomeres paper），而其他 studies 使用的是 constitutive expression。这显然不是对 evidence 的公正解读！

telomere 领域最近一篇重要且颇有争议的 paper，是 Liz Parrish 及其同事的[这一篇](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2121499119)（它为何[有争议](https://pubpeer.com/publications/9B76A71C2995A92B90D37AC1BE0693?utm_source=Chrome&utm_medium=BrowserExtension&utm_campaign=Chrome)，请参见 Pubpeer），但我不认为它的争议_足以_否定最主要的相关 result：它在某种意义上是对 Blasco 那篇显示 lifespan extension 的 AAV paper 的 replication，只不过改用了 CMV，显示 lifespan 有 41% increase。

那么，我们应当预期在 humans 中看到什么？一种 disease，[dyskeratosis congenita](https://www.childrenshospital.org/conditions/dyskeratosis-congenita#symptoms--causes)（DC），可能给我们一些线索。患有 DC 的 Patients 具有 short telomeres，因此他们会在那些经历更快 turnover 的 organs 和 tissues 中 present with defects：也就是我们预期 telomeres 会 shorten 得更快的地方（skin、nails、lungs、bone marrow）。所以，我们或许可以预期，如果我们活得足够久，而且我们的 telomeres shorten 得足够多，我们便会开始经历类似的情况。根据[这篇](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3291588/) paper，DC patients 的 telomere length（在 white blood cells 中）与可能超过 90 岁的人的 telomere length 相似，因此，如果人们在那个 age 左右开始自然地经历此类 symptoms，也就并非完全意外；而且，考虑到 bone marrow 是这些 tissues 之一，telomere-shortening 最终会在 advanced ages 成为 healthy lifespan extension 的 bottleneck。

最后，我想谈谈那些似乎发现 telomere length 会随着某种 lifestyle intervention 而 changes 的 studies。首先要说的是，telomere [measurement](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/44/5/1673/2594545) 是一门 [imperfect](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245582) 的技艺。其次，正如我在 introduction 中指出的，所有 cells 都有 telomeres，而人们大多指的是“white blood cells 中的 telomeres”，它们仅仅为了应对一次 infection 就可能 change（甚至看起来像是 elongate）。不过，你可能会说，好吧！也许 telomeres 更像是 damage 的 indicator，那么，通过 measuring 它们来考察 lifespan 如何？我不会费这个心。如果你想考察这类事情，DNAme clocks，或者干脆采用传统的 frailty index，对 mortality risk 都具有大得多的 predictive value。Telomere length 能告诉你的很少（除非它们是 extremely short，但那会是因为 rare genetic disease，而不是因为你做过什么）。这个视频对 telomere 作为 aging 的（[poor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sokdzTaHX0g)）biomarkers，给出了相当不错的解释。

所以，in conclusion，通过 gene therapy 进行 telomere lengthening，也许再结合 cancer-targeted therapy，作为 anti-aging intervention 仍然值得 investigating，尽管它可能会被 ex vivo reprogramming 或 iPSC-derived therapies 所 superseded，因为 reprogramming 会 lenghtens telomeres，因此人们在获得 epigenetic age reset 的同时，也能免费获得 telomere reset。

早期的炒作（“telomeres 是 aging 的关键”）与反炒作（“它们 lead to cancer，没有 evidence 表明 manipulating telomeres 对 aging 有帮助”）同样错误。

## 原文

Back in 2020 when I wrote the [Longevity FAQ](https://nintil.com/longevity/) I had a section on telomeres and some cursory examination of how much they might matter for aging. Now that I know more it's time to revisit it, including also some historical notes and some papers that came out after I wrote that FAQ.

The direct reason for writing this post is that often when I say that I work on aging (at [Retro](https://retro.bio/) Biosciences, we are hiring!) I am asked if "that's the telomeres thing". For some reason, telomeres became tied to aging broadly a few years ago and I wanted to understand why while at the same time I wanted to understand how relevant they actually are, as I suspect myself and others have overcorrected much away from "telomeres are the key to aging" in the direction of "they don't matter that much". From time to time one can see things from seemingly reputable outlets like [The Nobel Prize](https://twitter.com/NobelPrize/status/1358685249543299074) twitter account saying that "the secret \[to why we age\] lies in our telomeres" and people in the field making fun of them.

To start, telomeres are the regions at the end of chromosomes, so all of our cells have telomeres. The details of what these regions look like are covered in a bit more of length in the FAQ; for this post what matters is the fact that when a cell divides it has to copy its DNA. When this happen, telomeres shrink. When the telomeres are too short the cells stops replicating and becomes 'senescent'. There is an enzyme called telomerase. Telomerase is not a single protein, rather it's a complex made of (in humans) proteins TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase), TERC (telomerase RNA), and DKC1 (dyskerin).

It's important to note, as this gets lost in discussion sometimes, that when someone talks about telomeres shortening or lengthening it's usually the telomeres of white blood cells like T-cells, because accessing blood samples is the easiest. Changes in the telomeres of those cells does not imply changes in telomeres elsewhere. T-cells in particular can divide and the populations of different kinds of white blood cells can change with external factors, so it can also be the case that telomeres as usually measured lengthen simply because cells with shorter telomeres die, so average telomere size then increases.

For example, a person more exposed to infections and carcinogens may make their T-cells work harder, implying more divisions and telomere shortening. That person might die earlier and in a cross-sectional study we may see that shorter telomeres correlate with earlier mortality but this will just be a downstream biomarker of exposure to disease in this case, and not a direct causal driver.

What cells can divide (and so which cells have telomeres that can potentially shorten?) fibroblasts, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, glial cells, or astrocytes. In contrast, neurons or myocytes do not divide so we shouldn't expect telomere shortening to have much to do with the aging of those cells.

The usual function ascribed to telomeres is as an anti-cancer mechanism: if we cell begins dividing too much then its telomeres will progressively shorten and it will stop dividing (or die). To overcome this, cancers end up reactivating telomerase to keep their telomere length. Absent telomerase they can also engage in [ALT](https://cellandbioscience.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13578-020-00391-6) (Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres), so all else equal we would expect telomere elongation to lead to higher likelihood of cancer, but this is a simplistic view, as longer telomeres might also mean a better functioning immune system (T-cells that can divide more) and less inflammation (fewer [senescent](https://nintil.com/longevity/#chromosomes-and-telomeres:%7E:text=facts%20about%20it.-,Cellular%20senescence,-I%27ve%20mentioned%20senescence) cells).

The history of telomeres and their relevance goes back to at least Leonard [Hayflick's](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0014482761901926?via%3Dihub) 1961 and [1965](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=937a6abe443dfa2bc0829da18c2ff236d321deab) papers on what he called senescence: in culture, cells eventually stop dividing; in that paper he pointed to factors intrinsic to the cells but at the time he did not link that to telomeres. Cells extracted from older donors would stop dividing earlier, pointing to the existence of a limit to the total amount of divisions a cell could undergo, and that clock starting ticking since we are born, not just in vitro.

It wasn't until [1970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9415101/) [when](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/293811225_Principle_of_marginotomy_in_the_synthesis_of_polynucleotides_at_a_template) a Soviet scientist, Alexey Olovnikov had a sudden realization:

> The Theory of Marginotomy \[JR: Telomere shortening\] came to me in that Moscow subway station. I heard the deep roar of an approaching train coming out from the tunnel into the station itself. I imagined the DNA polymerase to be the train moving along the tunnel that I imagined to be the DNA molecule. **I thought that this polymerase cannot begin to copy from the very beginning because there is a dead zone between the front end of the polymerase molecule and its catalytic center. This is analogous to the dead zone between the front end of a subway car standing at the beginning of the subway platform and the nearest entrance door to the first car.** After this serendipitous underground brainstorm, which happened in the Fall of 1966, I wrote to Hayflick to ask some questions about his discovery and he sent additional unpublished data to me. I then spent several years thinking about this idea before publishing it in the central journal of our Academy of Sciences, Doklady Academii Nauk SSSR

So he was the first person to propose that it is telomere shortening due to imperfect DNA replication that is causing what Hayflick had observed.

Meanwhile in the US, Elizabeth Blackburn, who would later win the Nobel Prize for her work on telomeres publishes in [1978](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0022283678902942?via%3Dihub) the sequence of these regions in the chromosomes of _Tetrahymena thermophila_, a protozoan. Telomeres turns out to be TTAGGG (repeated 3000 times or so) in humans whereas in tetrahymena they turn out to be TTGGGG. Other species have other [repeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telomere). She would be working with Jack [Szostak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_W._Szostak) over the following years on understanding telomeres.

Later, in 1984 one of Blackburn's students, Carol Greider [discovers](https://jscholarship.library.jhu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/9f71eb7d-0a40-4286-8d41-da0ab88ea77c/content) the enzyme telomerase, initially called the telomere terminal transferase.

Blackburn and Greider's work also noted that Tetrahymena with knocked out telomerase became sick and died quickly ([1990](https://sci-hub.wf/10.1002/bies.950120803)), so telomeres and telomerase seemed key to keeping cells alive. Unlike Hayflick's work, which was in cells from humans, Tetrahymena are complete unicellular organisms, so their work provided more robust evidence that telomeres could be driving senescence at the organismal scale. They also noted that this system may have evolved to prevent cancer, putting senescence and cancer at odds. But in principle, one could think, _if_ there was a way to prevent cancer, would telomere restoration prevent aging at the organismal level in more complex organisms like mice?

Intrigued by these findings, [Geron Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geron_Corporation) launched in 1990 with Greider and Hayflick as advisors looking to develop telomere-based therapies for aging. It was seemingly Geron and its PR department that started the "telomeres drive aging" meme in the general population, according to [Greider](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sageke.2003.42.nf19).

In the 90s [a boo](https://www.amazon.com/Reversing-Human-Aging-Michael-Fossel/dp/0688143245)k titled _Reversing Human Aging_ came out, also supporting telomere shortening as key explanation for aging. In that book, we can find telomeres referred to as the "clocks of aging" and that "Aging is caused by aging cells and aging cells by aging telomeres."

In 1998 for the first time [it becomes possible](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9454332/), thanks to Geron scientists to take regular cells and turn on telomerase permanently preserving telomeres. This enables the cells to escape senescence. The process was dubbed 'immortalization'. In the [media](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1998/01/14/scientists-discover-way-to-prolong-life-of-cells/231de2f6-5179-4f72-bb8a-46d8f7a08c58/) we get quotes like "We found that biological aging can be put on hold"

Now at this point it would make sense to take this and put it in mice to see what happens. So in [2001](https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.1093/emboj/20.11.2619), the Blasco lab turned on telomerase in a certain type of cell in the skin of mice. The result wasn't longer lived mice, but rather they mice got more skin cancer (and faster wound healing), confirming the double-edged sword nature of telomeres.

A year [after](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.112515399?url_ver=Z39.88-2003&rfr_id=ori%3Arid%3Acrossref.org&rfr_dat=cr_pub++0pubmed) another paper comes out studying the effect of turning on telomerase in the entire mouse since birth. The results confirm Blasco's: they also saw an increase in cancers.

Looking at these two papers and a few others, 2004 supposes an inflection point for the telomere hypothesis of aging. Gerontologist João Pedro de Magalhaes publishes [Telomeres and Telomerase: A Modern Fountain of Youth](http://rejuvenomicslab.com//rr04_telomeres_telomerase_aging.pdf)? where he concludes in very skeptical terms that:

> though telomerase may be used in regenerative medicine and to treat specific diseases, it is unlikely to become a source of anti-ageing therapies.

One key point made there that Greider also made trying to counter the telomere hype is that there's no correlation between telomere length and the longevity of a species. Mice have longer telomeres than we do but they live much shorter lives. A conclusion is drawn there that

> Therefore, telomere length and/or telomerase activity do not explain why humans age slower than other primates and mice

But this is not quite right. Many years after (in [2019](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1902452116)) that Blasco and others would show that while it is correct that telomere lenght does not correlate with longevity, the rate of shortening (which is different in different species) _does correlate fairly well_:

![image-20231015142820347](https://nintil.com/images/2023-10-15-telomeres/image-20231015142820347.png)

This doesn't mean that they set the pace of aging! Other things like the rate of accumulation of [DNA](https://twitter.com/NathanielEDavid/status/1514725731711942656) mutations also correlate well with lifespan. In general if many mechanisms are necessary for long life, then evolution will jointly optimize them all for progressively increased lifespans. There's no living long with fast shortening telomeres OR high mutational burdens it seems.

After the de Magalhaes critique, Blasco publishes in [2008](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674\(08\)01191-4?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS0092867408011914%3Fshowall%3Dtrue) for the first time a report of lifespan extension using telomerase overexpression albeit in cancer-resistant mice. Fine, one could say, keeping telomeres long causes cancer, but what if we avoid that by genetically engineering the mice to be more resilient than normal mice will ever be, do we get healthier longer lives in mice? The answer is yes we do. Compared to the baseline non-telomerase'd cancer resistant mice, the ones that have constant telomerase expression:

-   Preserve skin thickness with age
-   Preserve [villi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_villus) structure in the GI tract (and associated gut barrier integrity->less leaky gut)
-   Do better in a motor test
-   Show improved glucose metabolism
-   Live substantially more, and more mice reach very old age (42% vs 8%)
-   As one might expect, telomeres are longer

Promising!

Understanding telomere biology turned out to be important enough to earn [Blackburn](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2009/summary/), Greider, and Szostak a Nobel Prize in 2009. (Sad day for [Olovnikov](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/sponsored/rbth/6396300/No-Nobel-physiology-and-medicine-award-for-Russian-gerontologist-Aleksey-Olovnikov.html))

Then one year after the telomere hypothesis continues to receive support with the publication in 2010 of _[Telomerase reactivation reverses tissue degeneration in aged telomerase-deficient mice](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature09603)_. Note that these are not regular mice, but mice that do not have telomerase at all; their telomeres are much shorter than usual.

With this paper, there was then evidence than longer telomeres could, in a context (cancer-resistant mice) extend lifespan as well as telomerase deficiency could shorten it and that adding back telomerase could reverse that. But could that work in regular mice?

In [2012](https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.1002/emmm.201200245), the Blasco lab once again published a paper where they take regular mice and using an AAV gene therapy cause widespread telomerase activation. The result: increased lifespan regardless of whether the therapy was delivered early in life (24% increase) or later in life (13% increase). This increase was accompanied by other health benefits. Importantly, they got less, not more cancer. They especulate that because the AAV would get diluted in fast dividing cells, cancer cells would not be able to benefit from this additional telomerase.

The same year, a consortium of aging researchers published what's probably the most cited paper in the field, _[Hallmarks of Aging](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674\(13\)00645-4?source=post_page---------------------------)_, where they establish the importance of telomeres as a consensus view.

Then later, in 2015 we get an interesting case of self-experimentation. [Elizabeth](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2016/jul/24/elizabeth-parrish-gene-therapy-ageing) Parrish, the CEO of a biotech company called BioViva injects herself with a gene therapy to lengthen her telomeres. They'd do it again in 2021 in some [volunteers](https://maplespub.com/article/Safety-Study-of-AAV-hTert-and-Klotho-Gene-Transfer-Therapy-for-Dementia) somewhat quietly, I hadn't heard of this study until today!

That same year we get [another](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568163715300155#bib0065) critique of the involvement of telomeres in aging. The paper starts by pointing out that knocking out telomerase in mice and breeding those mice, which leads to decreasing telomere size in the newborns over the generations does not decrease lifespan in the first generation (but it does later), implying that telomere length in mice is not a limiting factor for their lifespan. What then about the 2012 paper from the Blasco lab using AAV? The author argues the sample size was small and perhaps telomerase expression itself (and not just telomere length) is behind the effects seen. He also expresses doubt that telomeres can be involved in aging because mice telomere length varies a lot between inbred strains vs true wild mice without correlating to lifespan (Something we have thing is no mystery: what matters is the shortening rate, but that Blasco paper hadn't come out yet).

Blasco would provide an answer to one of these critiques in an impressive 2019 paper: mice with [hyperlong](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12664-x) telomeres live longer (12.8%) and are healthier in various ways. These mice were bred to have longer telomeres without overexpressing telomerase so one can rule out the potential action of telomerase in having the observed effects. They also get less cancer, suggesting that the anti-cancer effects from a better immune system or the inflammation reduction from longer telomeres outweigh the tumorigenic risks of telomerase.

So after the AAV paper from 2012, one'd think that many in the field would be relatively bullish on telomeres, but not quite. Aubrey de Grey, in 2018 was asked about the promise of telomere lengthening, said that he was worried about the risk of cancer, pointing to some of the studies showing an increase in cancer. To explain the results where mice with telomerase overexpression living longer, he points to the observation periods being too short (definately not the case for the AAV paper) or the animals being particularly cancer resistant (the case with the earlier papers).

Lastly, also cited by de Grey are some studies of human genetics using mendelian randomization to see what happens when the rate of telomere shortening is altered in humans. The result is a [slight](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/acel.13017) increase in cancer, a slight decrease in coronary artery disease, and a null effect on lifespan, implying that for humans in particular telomere length wouldn't extend life. Perhaps! But remember that in mice the length of telomeres doesn't determine lifespan in the general population despite the fact that hyper-long telomere mice do show improved lifespan. Could it be that people with hyper-long telomeres live longer? I don't expect so, given how slow human telomeres shrink, but the possibility exists. There are other papers one could point to that purportedly show that long telomeres in humans are deleterious; people [with mutations in the POT1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37140166/) gene have longer telomeres but one might reply that the mutation itself is having deleterious effect beyond telomeres. One could imagine a one-off AAV therapy that lenghtens telomeres and if cells turns cancerous and start dividing, the AAV will be diluted so the effects on cancer will be minimized (as Blasco et al. speculated in their own paper).

Another critique is from the [Ehninger](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01680-x) lab in 2022. The authors point out, correctly, that there's no correlation between telomere length and lifespan across mice strains, inferring from that that telomere shortening does not underlie murine aging. But given the hyperlong telomeres paper, this doesn't seem right. They also point to telomere lengthening increasing carcinogenesis, but they bunch together various kinds of interventions. Constitutive telomerase activation is not the same as transient, delivered via a virus (which would be closer to a hypothetical clinical translation). Ehninger just counts the number of studies saying there is more vs there is less cancer. 2 studies showing there's less (the AAV study and the hyperlong telomeres paper) vs others using constitutive expression. This is obviously not a fair reading of the evidence!

The latest big and somewhat controversial paper in telomere land is [this one](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2121499119) from Liz Parrish and colleagues (See Pubpeer for why it is [controversial](https://pubpeer.com/publications/9B76A71C2995A92B90D37AC1BE0693?utm_source=Chrome&utm_medium=BrowserExtension&utm_campaign=Chrome)) but I don't think it's controversial _enough_ to discredit the main relevant result: a replication, in a way, of the Blasco AAV paper showing lifespan extension, only that using CMV instead, showing a 41% increase in lifespan.

So what should we expect to see in humans? A disease, [dyskeratosis congenita](https://www.childrenshospital.org/conditions/dyskeratosis-congenita#symptoms--causes) (DC) might give us some clues. Patients with DC have short telomeres and as a result they present with defects in organs and tissues that experience faster turnover: where we would expect telomeres to shorten faster (skin, nails, lungs, bone marrow). So we mighte expect that if we lived long enough and our telomeres shorten sufficiently, we would begin to experience something like this. According to [this](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3291588/) paper, telomere length (in white blood cells) in DC patients is similar to the telomere length of someone that might be over 90 years old, so it wouldn't be totally surprising if around that age people would start experiencing such symptoms naturally and given that bone marrow is one of those tissues, telomere-shortening ends up being a bottleneck for healthy lifespan extension at advanced ages.

Now to finish, I want to address studies that seem to find that telomere length changes with some lifestyle intervention. The first point to make there is that telomere [measurement](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/44/5/1673/2594545) is an [imperfect](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245582) art. The second is that as I pointed out in the introduction all cells have telomeres and mostly people mean "telomeres in white blood cells" and those can change (seemingly elongate even) just to address an infection. But you could say well ok! Maybe telomeres are more an indicator of damage, so what about measuring them to look at lifespan? I wouldn't bother. If you want to look at things like that, DNAme clocks or just a good old frailty index have far more predictive value for mortality risk. Telomere length tells you very little (unless they are extremely short, but then it'd be because of a rare genetic disease, and not because of anything you have done). This video has a reasonably good explanation of telomere as ([poor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sokdzTaHX0g)) biomarkers of aging.

So in conclusion, telomere lengthening via gene therapy, and perhaps in combination with cancer-targeted therapy continues to be worth investigating as an anti-aging intervention, though it may be superseded by ex vivo reprogramming or iPSC-derived therapies, as reprogramming lenghtens telomeres so one gets telomere reset for free along with the epigenetic age reset.

The hype of the early days ("telomeres are the key to aging") is as wrong as the anti-hype ("they lead to cancer, there's no evidence that manipulating telomeres helps with aging").
