最近我注意到，在我看来，现有的大多数“为什么你应该 write 一个 blog”的 articles（[例如](https://sites.google.com/site/steveyegge2/you-should-write-blogs)）至少存在以下两个不足之一：

-   它们主要着眼于针对_不_创办 blog 的 _counterarguments_，而不是支持创办 blog 的 positive arguments——仿佛人们的自然状态就是创作出精彩的 blogs，阻碍他们的唯一因素只是一些愚蠢的 misconceptions。对于 Scott Alexander 这样的 extreme outliers，这也许是真的；但就我个人而言，我的自然状态是玩大量 video games，需要有人说服我才会转而做其他事情。

-   它们没有让 reader 做好准备，真正获得 blogging 的好处，因为它们在 mechanics 上给出的建议不够具体。我学到了很多关于 blogging 的东西，如果早点知道这些，我本可以 improve 得快得多（也因此会更有可能坚持 blogging，因为我 writing 的部分动力就来自自己擅长这件事）。

所以，下面是我对这类 post 的看法：给出一些 positive reasons，说明为什么你，没错，就是你，应该考虑在 Internet 上 writing，并尽我所能提供一份具体的 guide，说明怎样把这件事做得还不错（在我看来）。

## 为什么

### 更多美好的友谊

在我看来，对于任何一个普通人，创办 blog 最有力的理由是：**你会拥有更多美好的友谊**——一方面，你会因为自己的 blog 结识新的、很棒的人；另一方面，writing 会让你拥有更多有趣的 ideas，[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:1) 而这也会让你现有的友谊更加美好，因为你们会有更好的话题可聊。

生活中大多数其他重要的事情，比如工作机会和恋爱关系，都取决于你的朋友有多好，所以这相当棒。

来自我自己的 blog 的 Examples：

-   我和朋友聊天时，经常会试着向他们解释我正在 writing 或刚刚 published 的 post，这会引出一场有趣的对话，聊聊他们对这个 topic 的看法。例如，围绕 [Searching for Outliers](https://www.benkuhn.net/outliers/) 和[你不需要 work on hard problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/)，我有过很多很棒的对话。

-   我估计，在我的朋友中，大约 25%[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:2) 要么是通过我们某个人的 blogs 最初认识的，要么是通过 commenting 对方的 posts，显著加深了友谊。另外还有 25% 或更多，是我通过上述这些人间接认识的（他们格外有可能认识其他我非常喜欢的朋友）。

-   大约每个月都会有一次，我和某个以前认识的人重新联系上，因为他们在某处偶然看到了我的一篇 blog posts。

-   当 Wave 问新入职的 engineers 是什么让他们对 Wave 感兴趣时，几乎每个人都会提到我的 blog 是其中一个因素。（它通常不是唯一的因素，但作用很大，而且是被提及次数最多的单一因素，远远超过其他因素。）

-   有几个和我约会过的人提到，我的 writing 大大提升了他们对我的评价；即使没有它，我们也可能会约会，但它无疑起了帮助。在我的几段恋爱关系中，blog posts（包括我和伴侣各自的）一直是我们有趣对话最可靠的来源之一。

来自其他人的 blogs：

-   Milan Cvitkovic wrote 了一篇名为“[你可以做的事](https://milan.cvitkovic.net/writing/things_youre_allowed_to_do/)”的 post，小范围地 viral 了起来（它登上了 Hacker News 的榜首，因此大概获得了数万或数十万次 hits；不过，考虑到 HN 的一贯情况，其中大多数人可能只读了最初 30 秒左右的内容）。list 的第一项是：“你可以 hire 一位 research assistant，比如帮我 compile 这篇 post 的 Elizabeth van Nostrand。”仅仅因为这一篇 post，Elizabeth 就得以把自己的 hourly rate 提高到原来的三倍，如今主动找上门的意向已经多到让她想开始 hiring 自己的 research assistants 来 meet demand。严格说来，这并不是 Elizabeth 通过 blogging 改善自己生活的 example，但 Milan 是通过她的 blog 找到 Elizabeth 的，而且在 nearby possible worlds 中，Elizabeth 很可能也能 written 出一篇产生类似效果的 post。

    -   Elizabeth 也把其余工作的来源间接归功于自己的 blog，并补充说，即便她的 traffic 按 absolute terms 来看非常低——每天 hits 的 median 只有 50——这一点仍然成立。
-   [Jacob Falkovich](https://putanumonit.com/) 说，他通过自己的 blog 交到了许多朋友，也结识了几位伴侣。

-   一位朋友说，他工作团队中可能最出色的一位新员工，之所以决定加入，是受了他的一篇 blog post 的说服；那篇文章讲的是，他们为什么认为自己的工作有 positive impact。

-   我的朋友 Ben wrote 了[一篇关于他喜欢怎样工作的 post](https://tbenthompson.com/post/maintaining_momentum/)，为他带来了实际的工作机会，以及未来可能的工作机会。Ben 还因为自己的 blog 收到了很多主动联系，尽管（据他自己说）这个 blog 并不怎么活跃，内容也不算充实。

### 门槛比你想的更低

大多数人都严重低估了自己的 writing 对他人的 impact。

人们很容易觉得，必须拿出真正“interesting”的 writing，别人才会喜欢。[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:3) 我觉得，对于 going viral 来说确实如此，但如果只是想拥有一个朋友喜欢阅读、让熟人对你更有好感的 blog，就不是这样了。例如，就我自己而言，早在 2014 年，我的 blog 按照我现在的 standards 还远远算不上“good”时，我早期一些[“uninteresting”](https://www.benkuhn.net/downgrading-confidence-earning-give/) 的 [posts](https://www.benkuhn.net/self-vs-charity/)，[内容关于](https://www.benkuhn.net/etg-objections/) [effective](https://www.benkuhn.net/ea-outsiders/) [altruism](https://www.benkuhn.net/stress/)，就促使一些初中和高中朋友对 effective altruism 产生了兴趣，最终对他们的人生方向产生了很大影响。人们很容易低估这件事的重要性，因为大家通常不会给出这种 feedback。比如，那些朋友中大多数人的变化，我都是几年后才知道的！

想想你上一次读到对自己很重要的东西时——也许它促使你换一种方式做事，或者改变了你对某件事的看法。你有没有写信告诉作者？就我自己而言，据我记得，我_真的从来没这样做过_。（嗯，也许我应该试试……）同样，你也应该预料到，大多数喜欢你的 writing 的人都不会直接告诉你。

所以：对于什么值得写，降低你的门槛吧！我个人的 standard 是**任何我在交谈中说过不止一次的东西**。（实际上，我并没有写那么多 posts，所以我把这些当作 longlist，然后根据我觉得 topic 会有多 interesting 来进行 prioritize；但如果我想多写很多，也不会受限于 topics。）

我觉得一位真正把这件事做对了的 blogger 是 [Jeff Kaufman](https://www.jefftk.com/)——Jeff 有[很多](https://www.jefftk.com/p/equal-parenting-advice-for-dads)投入了 [high](https://www.jefftk.com/p/soundproofing-the-ceiling) [effort](https://www.jefftk.com/p/how-to-parent-more-predictably) 的 [posts](https://www.jefftk.com/p/scientific-charity-movement)，但也有很多这样的文章：

-   [五分钟做豆子](https://www.jefftk.com/p/five-minute-beans)
-   [放弃 T-Mobile](https://www.jefftk.com/p/giving-up-on-t-mobile)
-   [游乐场游戏：怪兽](https://www.jefftk.com/p/playground-game-monster)
-   [果酱已经过时了](https://www.jefftk.com/p/jam-is-obsolete)

而且人们很喜欢他的 blog——他有大量 subscribers 和 engagement（看看那篇果酱 post 下的 70 条 comments 就知道了）！[4](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:4)

## 怎么做

对于在 internet 上 writing，我最重要的两条建议是：

1.  要坚持 Publish consistently。

2.  留意并关注 feedback。

最容易让你对 writing 失去动力的做法，就是把自己的第一篇 post 与最喜欢的 writers 精心 polished 的 output 相比较，断定自己永远达不到那种水平，然后放弃。这是个错误。几乎没有人的第一篇 post 就很出色（比如可以看看[我的](https://www.benkuhn.net/www-prefix/)）；相反，大多数 writers 都是在 publish 得更多的过程中，逐步了解什么有效、什么无效。上面那两条戒律，就是为了尽可能快地经历这个 feedback cycle。下面所有具体建议，都是为了针对这一点进行 optimizing 而提出的。

事实上，我的建议是，所有与 writing 有关的选择都应该针对这一点进行 optimize。如果某件事原则上看起来是个好主意，但会让你更难做到 write consistently，或者更慢才能获得 feedback，那就不要做。

例如，我以前会花很多时间 polishing 单篇 posts，并在 publishing 之前获取 drafts 的 feedback。但有几次，我因为收到了一些觉得应该 incorporate 的 feedback，最终一直拖着没有 publishing 那篇 draft，因为这样做似乎需要花很多功夫（例如，有人建议 reorganize 文章的大部分内容）。每一次，我最终都没有 incorporating 那些 feedback 就 published 了，而人们还是很喜欢那篇 post。要是我一开始就明确认定，处理那些 feedback 太费事，直接忽略会更好。

### 建立一个 consistent writing habit

这几乎是每一份 writing advice 都会提到的共同 theme（[例如](https://twitter.com/patio11/status/1317627674810929152)）。这也符合我自己的经历；当我坚持每周 writing，并曾短暂地坚持[每天都写](https://www.benkuhn.net/tag/scraps/)时，我的 write 能力提升得最多。

除了 writing 的 quality 之外，consistency 对于获得 readers 也很重要。如果你的 blog 看起来 updates 得很不频繁，人们就不太可能 subscribe，也不太会记得来查看。

最后，blog posts 是 [searching for outliers](https://www.benkuhn.net/outliers/) 最典型的 examples 之一——最好的那些远远胜过 average。而提高获得 outlier 的机会的最佳方式之一，就是多多尝试。

显然，我目前并没有遵循自己在这里提出的建议，主要是因为我的本职工作长期以来都极其忙碌。不过，我觉得，如果没有那些年相对 consistent 的 writing，我今天的水平会差得多；而且，我仍然认为 writing 很有价值，因此正在积极尝试把其他工作交给别人，以便把一部分本职工作的时间用来为 Wave 的 company blog writing。

我建议从这样的 writing habit 开始：承诺自己做到 (1) 每天打开 text editor，至少 writing 一个 word，以及 (2) 每周 publishing 一些东西（并且 publicizing 自己已经 published 了）。（当然，什么 habits 对你有效是极其 individualized 的——你应该找出适合自己的 habit 形式！）

如果你觉得自己的东西似乎既不 original，也不 interesting（这在 new writers 中很常见），那么一开始 Publishing frequently 可能会让你不自在。我建议降低你的门槛。如果你抗拒降低门槛，可以看看 Alexey Guzey 的[《为什么你应该现在就开始写 Blog》§“可是我没有什么 original 的东西可说，只会重复 internet 上其他地方已经说过的话！”](https://guzey.com/personal/why-have-a-blog/#but-i-dont-have-anything-original-to-say-and-i-would-be-just-repeating-things-said-elsewhere-on-the-internet)

### 想出可以 write 的 ideas

如上所述，这里一个重要的起步动作，就是降低你对什么值得 write 的门槛。

在具体做法上，我建议保留一份打算 write 的 topics 清单；你可以结合 focused brainstorming，以及留意那些偶然冒出来的 ideas（聊天时、洗澡时……），不断往里添加内容。这既能帮助你养成留意 ideas 的习惯，也能让你想坐下来 write 时，不至于在决定 write 什么上卡住太久。

接下来，开始留意 topic ideas。它们可以来自许多不同的地方。下面这些来源对我都很有效（examples 既来自我的 blog，也来自别人的）：

-   你和朋友聊天时说过的、让对方感到意外的事情（[为 blub studies 辩护](https://www.benkuhn.net/blub/)，[你不必 work on hard problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/)）

    我不知道其他 writers 是不是也这样，但对我而言，这是最可靠的好 post ideas 来源！

-   Document 一个你最近做过的 project——哪些地方顺利，哪些地方不顺利（[你的房间可以和户外一样明亮](https://www.benkuhn.net/lux/)，[Comparing 在 Rust、Haskell、C++、Python、Scala 和 OCaml 中的同一个 Project](https://thume.ca/2019/04/29/comparing-compilers-in-rust-haskell-c-and-python/)）

-   Explain 你做的某件事该怎么做（这篇 post，[为什么以及如何 start 一个 serving emerging markets 的 startup](https://www.benkuhn.net/emco/)，[我的 weekly review 习惯](https://www.benkuhn.net/weekly/)，[如何让 video calls 几乎和 face-to-face 一样好](https://www.benkuhn.net/vc/)）

-   Explain 某件事如何 works（[为什么用 squared error？](https://www.benkuhn.net/squared/)，[什么是 major chord？](https://www.jefftk.com/p/what-is-a-major-chord)）

-   对一本书进行 Review（[_Why Nations Fail_ 与 global poverty 的 long-termist view](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/)，[Book review：与我们的截然不同的 Legal Systems](https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/11/13/book-review-legal-systems-very-different-from-ours/)）

-   围绕某个 topic 来 Curate 一组 links（[我最喜欢的 life advice essays](https://www.benkuhn.net/weeklyessays/)，[Gwern 最重要的 Writing（形式包括 essays、tweets、book reviews 及其他）](https://guzey.com/favorite/gwern/)）

-   来一番 rant（吐槽）（[Wireless 是个陷阱](https://www.benkuhn.net/wireless/)，[Matthew Walker 的“Why We Sleep”中充斥着 Scientific and Factual Errors](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/)）

-   internet commenters/public intellectuals 经常说、但在你看来不对的事情（[不要 claw back employee options](https://www.benkuhn.net/clawback/)，[Deliberate Practice 的 Problems](https://commoncog.com/blog/the-problems-with-deliberate-practice/)）

### 围绕这些 ideas 来 Write

这里最重要的是，write 你最迫不及待想 write 的东西（注意，不是你_觉得自己应该_最迫不及待想 write 的东西，也不是你最期待自己_已经写完_的东西），因为这样最有可能带来 consistent output。我建议试着按你有多想 write，给 topics 清单做一个大致的 ranked 排列。（只是大致排列，因为至少对我来说，precise ranking 会随着心情而频繁变化！）

至于 writing 本身的具体做法：大家在这上面总是很讲究，但我觉得它基本上不重要——怎么最方便你 publish，就怎么做。你可以随着时间慢慢 iterate 和 improve。我发现，先 writing 一份 outline，能帮助我把“认真琢磨 structure”和“埋头写出一大堆 words”这两部分分开，但还有很多人，包括过去的我，直接从头写到尾反而更顺利。

### 为你的 blog 进行 Set up

在你 written 四篇 blog posts 之前，不许在这个部分花超过 30 minutes！

-   默认建议：花 $10/mo 使用 [Ghost](https://ghost.org/)。设一个两个月后的提醒：如果你始终没有开始 writing blog posts，就 cancel 你的 subscription。

-   如果一想到要为 software 支付 $10/mo，你就犹豫不决或拖延，那就用 Substack。他们做出的愚蠢、烦人或 incentive-misaligned 的决定比 Ghost 更多（例如，你还没读 post，就先弹出 full-screen 的“subscribe” modal），但也够用了。

这两者最重要的地方在于：

-   它们都很容易 set up。blogging 最大的障碍，是忍不住去折腾 website，而不是 writing。别上这个当！

-   它们自带 mailing lists。这意味着，你可以轻松地把 posts 进行 push，送到人们面前，而不必指望他们记得来查看你的 site。[5](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:5)这对获得足够多的 readers、让 writing 有动力至关重要。

-   它们让你能够掌控自己的 audience。如果你在 Medium 这样的 platform 上 write，依靠 in-platform 的“follow” mechanism（或者更糟，algorithmic promotion）来触达 audience，那么哪天你想搬家，就麻烦了。

### 获得你的第一批 readers

\[说明：我是在八年前这么做的，当时的世界和现在很不一样。\]

你希望从第一批 readers 那里得到两样东西：

1.  由几位 draft readers 提供更深入的 feedback。

2.  一批人数更多的普通 subscribers，他们的 aggregate reaction 能帮助你逐渐找到会让更多人产生共鸣的 writing。

我直到几年前才开始请 draft readers 帮忙，但发现他们非常有用：相比 social media reactions 的变幻莫测，他们能提供更多 feedback。我还发现，把 draft posts 发给某些人，最后会促成我们之间更有趣的讨论，所以我也推荐把这当作增进友谊的一种 mechanism！

关于 draft readers，有几点提醒：

-   并非所有 improvement suggestions 都是好的；即便是好的 improvement suggestions，如果耗时太长，也不一定值得 implementing。别因为觉得“我真的应该 respond 所有那些 comments”，就迟迟无法 publishing。

-   反过来，许多重要的 improving 方式，draft readers 并不会明确告诉你，例如“你本来可以选一个更有趣的 topic”。所以，你仍然需要大量体会言外之意，并进行 experimentation。只 fixing 那些 draft readers 告诉你要 fix 的地方，无法让你变得出色。

-   由于上述原因，draft readers 的主要价值往往在于_了解他们的 overall reaction_，而不是那些细致的 improvement suggestions（虽然这些也可能有帮助），因为 overall reaction 能帮助我 calibrate，判断自己产出的东西总体上到底好不好。因此，在征求 draft feedback 时，我建议尽量引导 draft readers 关注 high-level comments。

-   我也知道有人会[聘请 professional editors](https://mtlynch.io/editor/)，以获得更多特定类型的 feedback，不过我自己从没试过。

我建议用以下 strategy 来获得 readers：

-   writing 完第一篇 post 后，把它通过 email 发给几位朋友，请他们对 draft 提些 comments。

    Email 的 copy

    > Subject line：对一篇 blog post 的 draft 有什么想法吗？
    > 
    > Body：嗨！希望你最近一切都好 :)
    >

## 附录：Writing quality 的 tips

我已经写下了我最重要的 tip，那就是 _别那么担心 quality！！_ 我建议，在你至少写完两篇 posts 之前，先不要试着 improve（通过 incorporating drafts 上的 feedback 来 improve 除外）。

话虽如此，我确实有一些经常提出的 writing suggestions；以下是我最看重的 5 条：

### 想出一个 good title

要让更多人阅读你的 post，这是你能做的事情中，单项 highest-return-on-time 的一件。一个 good title 会就 reader 能从阅读这篇 post 中获得什么作出 promise，而这个 promise 应当是 (1) exciting，以及 (2) accurate 的。

注意，这 _不_ 意味着你应该写 clickbait titles。你希望来阅读你的 blog 的那类人，很可能会把一个 clickbait title 理解为这样的 promise：他们读到的将是 content-free nonsense。

good titles 的 Examples：

-   [Matthew Walker 的“Why We Sleep”充斥着 Scientific and Factual Errors](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/)。没有什么比听到别人推荐 _Why We Sleep_ 时问一句“你读过《Matthew Walker 的‘Why We Sleep’充斥着 Scientific and Factual Errors》吗？”更令人满足了。（我或许会从 title 中去掉 Walker 的名字，不过这是我与 Alexey 之间的 style difference。）

-   [如果你有 Writer’s Block，或许你应该停止 Lying](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/if-you-have-writers-block-maybe-you)

bad titles 的 Examples：

-   我过去只是根据大致的 topic 来给 posts 起 title，例如 [Writing 来 clarify thoughts](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing-clarify-thoughts/) 或 [Second-order vanity（二阶虚荣）](https://www.benkuhn.net/second-order-vanity/)。这作出的 promise 是“点击这里，阅读一篇关于 writing 来 clarify thoughts 的 post”，却没有就 takeaways 作出任何 promise。

-   我建议，不要模仿著名 writers 使用“artsy”或“literary”titles 的倾向，除非你的目标就是 artsy 或 literary。（这是个不错的目标，但并不是本文真正讨论的事情。）[这就是水](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/TK) 是 David Foster Wallace 那篇 commencement speech 的合适 title，但它所 optimized 的目标，与我建议你为 blog posts 进行 optimizing 的目标不同。

### 找到合适的 framing

我所说的“framing”，指的是 post 中将全文收束为一个 overall takeaway，并说明它为什么重要、为什么值得阅读的部分。你的 readers 不会自动弄明白这些，而带着他们一步步理解，可能会让他们的反应从“嗯，我想还挺有意思的”变成“脑洞大开”。

例如，[想要 listen well，就 get curious](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/)，单独来看，似乎是个有些平淡的 point。那篇 post 中的 framing——“关于 listening 的 advice 大多看起来不怎么样，因为它教你 mimic good listening 的 surface features，而不是其 underlying generator”——准确指出了我的 advice 与“standard”listening advice 有何不同。

另一方面，[那些会 screw up 你的 causal inference 的事情](https://www.benkuhn.net/causreg/) 是我早期一篇没有 framing 的 post。我仍然认为，那篇 post 中的 info 相当重要（它 implies，你基本上不应该 trust observational studies），但由于缺乏 framing，它没有在 readers 中引起多少反响。我可能会在将来某个时候，用更好的 framing 来 rewrite 它。

### 使用大量 examples

没有 examples 的 Writing 往往是 dry 且 abstract 的。

examples 的一个有用之处在于，它们能帮助人们更加细致地理解你的 points 的 contours 和 boundaries。例如，看看我上面关于 framing 的 advice：

> 向 reader explain\[\] 他们的 overall takeaway 应该是什么，以及它为什么重要

如果把这条 advice 简单地 Applying 到 _想要 listen well，就 get curious_ 上，你最后可能会写出这样的内容：

> 总之，如果你想成为一个更好的 listener，就试着唤起你对对方的 curiosity。这很重要，也值得阅读，因为 listening 是一项重要的 skill，而大多数人并不十分擅长它。

这从字面上说确实遵循了那条 advice，但……并不怎么样。看到一个 reasonable framing 的 example，能让你避免许多可能的 misinterpretations，并让你直观地感受到，该把“good”的 overall bar 设在哪里。

我喜欢 examples 的另一个原因是，最好的 examples 包含许多有趣的小细节，它们独立于这些 examples 所 illustrating 的 post。对此，我的 gold standard 是 [愿意显得愚蠢](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/) 中的 examples，其中每一个单独拿出来，都可以成为一篇有趣的 blog post。

### 使用 microhumor

我是从 [Scott Alexander 关于 writing advice 的 post](https://slatestarcodex.com/2016/02/20/writing-advice/) 中学到这个 concept-handle 的：

> 你听说过 microaggressions。现在试试 microhumor。它们不是那种由喜剧演员讲出来、让人放声大笑的 joke，但当 reader 一路 skimming 过去时，它们仍然会让 reader 的脸上浮现出一丝极淡的微笑。

我非常赞同 Scott 的看法：microhumor 是 enjoyable writing 最重要的 differentiators 之一。正因如此，我觉得他把它埋在一个 10 项 list 的第 #5 项，有点好笑，不过也许他不像我这样，对自己的 lists 的 stack-ranking 那么神经质。

### 像说话一样 Write

这是关于如何 improve 你的 sentence-level prose quality 的 [80/20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle) compressed version。它来自 [Paul Graham 的一篇 essay](http://www.paulgraham.com/talk.html)：

> 大多数人一开始 writing，就像变了个人。他们 write 时用的 language，与和朋友说话时用的不同。sentence structure，甚至 words，都不一样。在 spoken English 中，没有人会把“pen”用作 verb。和朋友聊天时，如果你用“pen”而不是“write”，你会觉得自己像个傻瓜。

> 在 publish 一篇新的 essay 之前，我会把它 read it out loud，并 fix 所有听起来不像 conversation 的地方。我甚至会 fix 那些 phonetically awkward 的部分；我不知道这是否有必要，不过也花不了多少工夫。

## 附录：几个相关的 objections 和 rebuttals

### “这就是 signaling／胡扯”

拥有 blog 的一些 advantages 确实来自 signaling，但我不同意这就意味着它是胡扯。Signaling 极其有用，没有它，许多事情就无法运作。你应该尽量让自己的 signaling 是 _time-efficient_ 的，但你也应该把它看作一种 productive activity。Writing 是 signaling 最 efficient 的 forms 之一，因此值得为它花时间。

例如，作为一个想交朋友的人，我可以尝试与之交朋友的人，实际上有无限多个。我在朋友身上看重的一件事，是我们能不能有有趣的交谈。如果一个人有一个有趣的 blog，这就是相当好的 evidence，表明当面与他们相处也会很有趣。因此，与有趣的 bloggers 交朋友，远比不断尝试与 randomly-selected 的人交朋友、直到发现其中一个有趣的人，要 time-efficient 得多（差了 orders of magnitude！）。

### “但你的 blog 很好，我的却不会”

至少就“好”的一种含义（popularity）而言，这并不成立。我的 blog 并不是特别 popular——截至我开始 drafting 这篇 post 时，我有大约 1500 个 email subscribers，而上面许多 examples 发生时，它的 popularity 还远不及这个水平。（1500 看起来可能很多，但这是在写了超过 200 篇 posts 之后。如果你起初有 10 个 initial subscribers，那么只要每篇 post 都让 subscriber base 增长 2%，就能达到这个数量，而这完全可以做到。）

（另见：[当 followers/readers 超过 1000 时，Twitter/blogging 就是最好的 dating apps](https://twitter.com/yashkaf/status/1435700265785647108)、[1000 个 true fans](https://kk.org/thetechnium/1000-true-fans/)；早在我拥有 1000 个 readers 之前，我的 blog 就已经很有用了。）

你可能会反驳说，尽管我的 blog 不 popular，但它的 ideas 或 writing 的 quality 仍然高于你能产出的东西。即便现在确实如此，还是那句话：这也是我写了 200 篇 blog posts 之后的结果。如果你读读我 2014 年的 posts（[例如 1](https://www.benkuhn.net/why-im-taking-operating-systems/)、[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing-clarify-thoughts/)、[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/things-i-learned-toby-ord/)），你会发现，那些 posts 虽然还过得去，却远没有那么有趣，但我的 blog 当时仍然很有用。

### “writing 要花很多时间”

我 draft 的速度大约是每分钟 10 words，据我所知，这算不错，但并不出众。[6](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:6) 对我来说，一篇典型的 blog post 可能有大约 1500 words，也就是要花大约 2.5 小时来 write。再为 outlining 和 editing 把时间乘以二，我每天花大约 45 分钟 writing，就能每周发一篇 post。

如果你想把 _某一篇特定的 post 写得非常好_，Writing 花的时间就会比这长得多，但我不认为这是正确的 strategy：相反，如上所述，你应该追求 consistent volume，这样你就能多尝试，并从哪些内容更能或更不能引起 audience 共鸣中学习。

### “一想到要把文字写到纸上，我就充满一种无名的恐惧”

（或者，更一般地说，觉得 writing 的过程痛苦的种种原因）

我个人并不觉得 writing 有什么难受的，所以没法根据亲身经历来谈。但有这个问题的其他朋友提出过各种属于“假装你是在跟朋友说话，而不是在 writing”这一类的办法——例如，把内容写成发给朋友的 email（甚至是一连串 chats！），使用 dictation software（听说它可能比你上次试用时更好用了），或者 recording 你自己口头解释的过程，再把它 transcribed 成文字。（无论哪一种，你之后都需要 edit 那些 text，但 edit 总比从头 write 更容易！）

### “如果我 write 了以后会后悔的东西呢？”

这可能指几种不同但相关的事情：

1.  惹怒 journalist、Internet outrage mob，或者两者都惹怒。我认为，大多数人既高估了这种事情发生的 risk，也高估了它发生后的 downsides，因为这种事情往往以备受关注的方式发生（例如，[New York Times 对 Scott Alexander 进行 doxxing](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/22/nyt-is-threatening-my-safety-by-revealing-my-real-name-so-i-am-deleting-the-blog/)）。Scott 不仅 _非常_ 受关注，而且写的 topics 也特别容易激起争议（politics 和 social justice），他常用的 style 也不太经得起 taken out of context。

2.  收到 hostile comments。如果你的 posts 获得了大量关注（例如在 Hacker News 或某个 subreddit 上），这种事情很可能会发生。我个人觉得这有点烦，但当我注意到，基本上每篇 HN post 都会遇到这种事，不管 quality 如何，我就不再那么在意了。

    我还注意到，我那些写得不那么 formally、较少使用 hedging 或 caveating 的 posts，收到的 hostile comments 最多，所以我怀疑，注意 present evidence，并以恰当的 levels of confidence 来 make your claims，能够帮助 reduce 收到 hostile comments 的 risk。

3.  提出一些自己后来不再认同的 claims。这不算特别糟，原因有二。首先，readers 通常会预期，自己读的如果是旧 posts，author 可能已经不再 endorsed 其中的内容；其次，_几乎没有人会读旧 posts_（除非你 write 的东西真的能经久流传——而如果那么多人喜欢它，它就不太可能完全错得离谱）。

Recently I noticed that most existing “why you should write a blog” articles ([e.g.](https://sites.google.com/site/steveyegge2/you-should-write-blogs)) have at least one of two shortcomings, according to me:

-   They mostly focus on _counterarguments_ to _not_ starting a blog, rather than positive arguments in favor of starting one—as if people’s natural state is to produce amazing blogs and the only thing holding them back is silly misconceptions. This might be true for extreme outliers like Scott Alexander, but personally, my natural state is to play lots of video games and I need to be convinced to do other stuff instead.

-   They don’t set the reader up to actually get the benefits of blogging, because they don’t give concrete enough advice on the mechanics. I’ve learned a lot of things about blogging which, if I’d known them earlier, would have helped me improve much faster (and therefore would have made me much more likely to keep blogging, since part of my motivation for writing is being good at it).

So here’s my own take on this genre of post, with positive reasons why you, yes you, should consider writing things on the Internet, and a guide that’s as concrete as I can manage for how to do a decent (imo) job at it.

## Why

### More awesome friendships

In my opinion, the strongest reason for any random person to start a blog is that **you will have more awesome friendships**—both in the sense that you will meet new awesome people as a result of your blog, but also in the sense that writing will cause you to have more interesting ideas,[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:1) which will make your existing friendships more awesome because you’ll have better stuff to talk about.

Most other important things in life, like job opportunities and romantic relationships, are downstream of the quality of your friends, so this is pretty great.

Examples from my own blog:

-   Often when I’m talking to friends I’ll try to explain to them the post that I’m in the process of writing, or just published, and this leads to an interesting conversation about their take on the topic. I’ve had a lot of great conversations about e.g. [Searching for Outliers](https://www.benkuhn.net/outliers/) and [You don’t need to work on hard problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/).

-   I’d guess that for around 25% of my friends,[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:2) I either first met them through one of our blogs or our friendship was significantly reinforced by commenting on each other’s posts. Another 25% or more I met indirectly through one of those people (who are disproportionately likely to have other friends that I like a lot).

-   Around once a month I end up reconnecting with someone I used to know because they ran across one of my blog posts somewhere.

-   When Wave asks new-hire engineers what got them interested in Wave, almost everyone mentions my blog as a factor. (It’s usually not the only factor but a strong contributor and by far the single most often mentioned factor.)

-   A few people I’ve dated have mentioned that my writing substantially increased their opinion of me; we might still have dated without it but it certainly helped. During multiple relationships, blog posts (both mine and my partners’) have been one of our most reliable sources of interesting conversations.

From other people’s blogs:

-   Milan Cvitkovic wrote a post called “[Things You’re Allowed to Do](https://milan.cvitkovic.net/writing/things_youre_allowed_to_do/)” that went mildly viral (it made the top of Hacker News, so probably got tens or hundreds of thousands of hits, although, HN being what it is, most of those hits probably only read the first 30 seconds or so). The first item in the list was “You can hire a research assistant, like Elizabeth van Nostrand, who helped me compile this post.” As a result of this one post, Elizabeth was able to triple her hourly rate and now has enough inbound interest to want to start hiring her own research assistants to meet demand. Technically this isn’t an example of Elizabeth improving her own life by blogging, but Milan found Elizabeth through her blog and there are nearby possible worlds where Elizabeth probably could have written a post that had a similar effect.

    -   Elizabeth also attributes the rest of her work to her blog indirectly, and adds that this is true despite getting a very low level of traffic in absolute terms—a median of 50 hits per day.
-   [Jacob Falkovich](https://putanumonit.com/) says he’s made many friends and a few partners through his blog.

-   A friend says that probably the best new hire on his team at work was convinced to join the team by that friend’s blog post about why they thought their work had a positive impact.

-   My friend Ben wrote [a post about how he likes to work](https://tbenthompson.com/post/maintaining_momentum/) that has led to both actual, and potential future, jobs. Ben has also gotten a lot of outreach due to his blog despite it being (according to him) not very active or substantial.

### The bar is lower than you think

Most people dramatically underestimate the impact that their writing has on others.

It’s easy to think that you have to put out really “interesting” writing in order for other people to like it.[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:3) I think that’s true for going viral, but not true for having a blog that your friends enjoy reading, makes acquaintances feel more positively towards you, etc. In my case, for example, even in 2014, way before my blog was “good” by my current standards, some of my early [“uninteresting”](https://www.benkuhn.net/downgrading-confidence-earning-give/) [posts](https://www.benkuhn.net/self-vs-charity/) [about](https://www.benkuhn.net/etg-objections/) [effective](https://www.benkuhn.net/ea-outsiders/) [altruism](https://www.benkuhn.net/stress/) contributed to some of my middle-school and high-school friends getting interested in effective altruism, which ultimately had a big impact on their life direction. It’s easy to underrate how important this is because people don’t usually give this feedback. For instance, I didn’t learn about most of those friends’ updates until years later!

Think about the last time you read something important to you—maybe it motivated you to do something differently, or changed the way you thought about something. Did you write to the author and let them know? Personally, I have _literally never done this_, as far as I can remember. (Huh, maybe I should…) Similarly, you should expect that most people who love your writing aren’t going to tell you that directly.

So: lower your bar for what’s worth writing about! My personal standard is **anything that I’ve said more than once in a conversation**. (In practice I don’t write that many posts, so I use that as my longlist and then prioritize by how interesting I think the topic is going to be, but if I wanted to write a lot more, I wouldn’t be constrained on topics.)

A blogger who I think gets this really right is [Jeff Kaufman](https://www.jefftk.com/)—Jeff has a [lot](https://www.jefftk.com/p/equal-parenting-advice-for-dads) of [high](https://www.jefftk.com/p/soundproofing-the-ceiling) [effort](https://www.jefftk.com/p/how-to-parent-more-predictably) [posts](https://www.jefftk.com/p/scientific-charity-movement), but also a lot like:

-   [Five minute beans](https://www.jefftk.com/p/five-minute-beans)
-   [Giving up on T-Mobile](https://www.jefftk.com/p/giving-up-on-t-mobile)
-   [Playground Game: Monster](https://www.jefftk.com/p/playground-game-monster)
-   [Jam is Obsolete](https://www.jefftk.com/p/jam-is-obsolete)

And people love his blog—he has tons of subscribers and engagement (just look at the 70 comments on the jam post)![4](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:4)

## How

Here are my top two pieces of advice for writing things on the internet:

1.  Publish consistently.

2.  Notice and pay attention to feedback.

The easiest way to get demotivated about writing is to compare your first post to the polished output of your favorite writers, conclude you’ll never get that good, and give up. This is a mistake. Almost nobody’s first post is amazing (see e.g. [mine](https://www.benkuhn.net/www-prefix/)); instead, most writers learn incrementally about what works and what doesn’t as they publish more. The two commandments above are about going through that feedback cycle as fast as possible. All the specific advice below is given with the intention of optimizing for this.

In fact, my suggestion is to make all your writing-related choices to optimize for this. If something seems like a good idea in principle, but would make it harder for you to write consistently or slower to get feedback, don’t do it.

For example, I used to spend a lot of time polishing individual posts and getting feedback on drafts before publishing. But a few times I ended up procrastinating on publishing a draft because I got feedback that I felt like I should incorporate, but seemed like it would take a lot of work to do that (e.g. a suggestion to reorganize large parts of the post). In each case I eventually published without incorporating the feedback and people really liked the post anyway. I would have been better off just explicitly deciding that the feedback was too much work and ignoring it.

### Build a consistent writing habit

This is a common theme of nearly every piece of writing advice ([e.g.](https://twitter.com/patio11/status/1317627674810929152)). It also matches my own experience; my ability to write improved the most when I committed to writing every week and, briefly, [every day](https://www.benkuhn.net/tag/scraps/).

Separately from the quality of your writing, consistency is also important for getting readers. People are less likely to subscribe to your blog, or remember to check it, if it looks like it updates very infrequently.

Finally, blog posts are one of the ultimate examples of [searching for outliers](https://www.benkuhn.net/outliers/)—the best ones are massively better than the average. And one of the best ways to improve your chances of getting an outlier is just to take lots of shots on goal.

Obviously, I don’t currently follow my own advice here, mostly because my day job has been extremely busy for a long time. However, I think that without my years of relatively consistent writing I’d be much worse at it today, and I still think writing is valuable enough that I’m actively trying to delegate other work so that I can spend some of my day-job-time writing for Wave’s company blog.

My suggested starting point for a writing habit would be to commit to (1) opening your text editor and writing at least one word every day, and (2) publishing (and publicizing that you’ve published) something every week. (Of course, what habits will work for you is super individualized—you should figure out the right shape of the habit for you!)

Publishing frequently might be uncomfortable at first if you think that nothing you have seems original or interesting (which is common for new writers). I suggest lowering your bar. If you feel resistant to lowering your bar, see Alexey Guzey’s [Why You Should Start a Blog Right Now § “But I don’t have anything original to say and I would be just repeating things said elsewhere on the internet!”](https://guzey.com/personal/why-have-a-blog/#but-i-dont-have-anything-original-to-say-and-i-would-be-just-repeating-things-said-elsewhere-on-the-internet)

### Come up with ideas to write about

As mentioned above, an important first step here is to lower your bar for what’s worth writing about.

Mechanically, I suggest keeping a list of topics to write about, which you can populate with some mix of focused brainstorming plus noticing particular ideas as they randomly strike you (in a conversation, in the shower…). This will both help you build the habit of noticing ideas, and make it so that when you want to sit down and write, you don’t get too blocked on deciding what to write about.

Next, start paying attention to topic ideas. These can come from lots of different places. Here are some that have worked for me (with examples from both my blog and others’):

-   Something you said in a conversation with a friend that surprised them ([In defense of blub studies](https://www.benkuhn.net/blub/), [You don’t need to work on hard problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/))

    I’m not sure if this is true of other writers, but for me, this one is my most reliable source of good post ideas!

-   Document a project you did recently—what went well, what went poorly ([Your room can be as bright as the outdoors](https://www.benkuhn.net/lux/), [Comparing the Same Project in Rust, Haskell, C++, Python, Scala and OCaml](https://thume.ca/2019/04/29/comparing-compilers-in-rust-haskell-c-and-python/))

-   Explain how to do something you do (this post, [Why and how to start a startup serving emerging markets](https://www.benkuhn.net/emco/), [My weekly review habit](https://www.benkuhn.net/weekly/), [How to make video calls almost as good as face-to-face](https://www.benkuhn.net/vc/))

-   Explain how something works ([Why squared error?](https://www.benkuhn.net/squared/), [What is a major chord?](https://www.jefftk.com/p/what-is-a-major-chord))

-   Review a book ([_Why Nations Fail_ and the long-termist view of global poverty](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/), [Book review: Legal Systems Very Different From Ours](https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/11/13/book-review-legal-systems-very-different-from-ours/))

-   Curate a set of links about a topic ([My favorite essays of life advice](https://www.benkuhn.net/weeklyessays/), [Gwern’s Most Important Writing (in essays, tweets, book reviews, and other forms)](https://guzey.com/favorite/gwern/))

-   A rant ([Wireless is a trap](https://www.benkuhn.net/wireless/), [Matthew Walker’s “Why We Sleep” Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/))

-   Something internet commenters/public intellectuals frequently say that seems wrong ([Don’t claw back employee options](https://www.benkuhn.net/clawback/), [The Problems with Deliberate Practice](https://commoncog.com/blog/the-problems-with-deliberate-practice/))

### Write about the ideas

The most important thing here is to write about whatever you’re most excited to write about (n.b. not what you _think you should be_ most excited to write about, or what you’re most excited to _have written_ about), since this is the most likely to generate consistent output. I recommend trying to keep your list of topics roughly ranked by how excited you are. (Only roughly because at least for me, the precise ranking changes pretty often depending on what mood I’m in!)

On the mechanics of writing itself: people get all fussy about this, but I think it’s basically not important—just do whatever makes it easiest for you to publish. You can iterate and improve on it on over time. I’ve found that writing an outline first helps me separate the “think really hard about structure” part from the “grind out a bunch of words” part, but lots of other people, including my past self, have more success with just writing things straight through.

### Set up your blog

You are not allowed to spend more than 30 minutes on this part until you have written four blog posts!

-   Default suggestion: pay $10/mo for [Ghost](https://ghost.org/). Set a reminder in two months to cancel your subscription if you never get around to writing blog posts.

-   If the prospect of paying $10/mo for software makes you think twice or procrastinate, use Substack. They make more dumb/annoying/incentive-misaligned decisions than Ghost (e.g. the full-screen “subscribe” modal popping up before you’ve read the post), but they’re good enough.

The most important thing about either of these is that:

-   They’re simple to set up. The greatest obstacle to blogging is the temptation to futz with your website instead of writing. Don’t succumb!

-   They come with mailing lists. This means you can easily push posts to people without relying on them remembering to check your site.[5](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:5) This is key for getting enough readers for writing to be motivating.

-   They leave you in control over your audience. If you write on a platform like Medium that relies on an in-platform “follow” mechanism (or worse, algorithmic promotion) to reach your audience, then you’ll be screwed if you ever want to move.

### Get your initial set of readers

\[Disclaimer: I did this eight years ago when the world looked very different.\]

You want two things from your initial set of readers:

1.  More-involved feedback from a few draft readers.

2.  A larger set of normal subscribers whose aggregate reaction can help you navigate towards writing that resonates with a larger group of people.

I didn’t start using draft readers until a few years ago, but have found them super useful for getting more feedback than you can extract from the vagaries of social media reactions. For some people, I’ve also found that sending them draft posts ended up sparking more interesting discussions between us, so I recommend this as a mechanism for improving your friendships as well!

A couple notes on draft readers:

-   Not all improvement suggestions are good, and not all good improvement suggestions are worth implementing if they would take too long. Don’t let yourself get bogged down from publishing by the feeling of “I should really respond to all those comments.”

-   Conversely, there are lots of important ways of improving that draft readers won’t explicitly tell you, e.g. “you could have picked a more interesting topic,” so you’ll still need to do a lot of reading between the lines and experimentation. You can’t get good by just fixing what draft readers tell you to fix.

-   Because of the above, the main value of draft readers is often _getting their overall reaction_ rather than those detailed improvement suggestions (though those can be helpful), because the overall reaction helps me calibrate whether I’m producing stuff that’s overall good or not. So when asking for draft feedback, I’d suggest trying to focus your draft readers on high-level comments.

-   I also know of people who have [hired professional editors](https://mtlynch.io/editor/) to get more feedback of certain types, although I’ve never tried it myself.

My suggested strategy for getting readers:

-   After writing your first post, email it to a few friends asking for comments on the draft.

    Email copy

    > Subject line: Thoughts on a draft blog post?
    > 
    > Body: Hi! Hope things are going well :)
    > 
    > I decided to start a blog, inspired by some of the reasons in [this post from some guy on the internet](https://www.benkuhn.net/blogging/). I just drafted my first post—would you be up for giving me your thoughts on it? I’m especially curious for your high-level take on how you felt about the post overall.
    > 
    > (I’m planning to publish on \[some time a few days from now\], but let me know if you’re interested but need more time than that!)

-   When you publish, email some large-ish group of friends in bcc:

    Email copy

    > Subject line: Blog!
    > 
    > Body: Hi! Hope things are going well :)
    > 
    > I decided to start a blog, inspired by some of the reasons in [this post from some guy on the internet](https://www.benkuhn.net/blogging/). I just published my first post :)
    > 
    > Mind if I put you on the email list for future ones? I’d love to hear your thoughts on any of the writing!

-   When you publish, post your posts on social media as well (my best source currently is Twitter, in the past it was Facebook, but probably depends on your local culture). Don’t overthink! My preferred copy is:

    Post copy

    > New post! “Searching for outliers:” benkuhn.net/outliers/
    > 
    > It’s about \[a short summary of the topic\].

    Followed by either a quote from the post or a screenshot, depending on the platform.

## Appendix: Writing quality tips

I already wrote down my top tip, which is _don’t worry about quality so much!!_ I suggest not trying to improve (other than by incorporating feedback on drafts) until you’ve written at least two posts.

That said, I do have some writing suggestions I make frequently; here are my top 5:

### Come up with a good title

This is the single highest-return-on-time thing you can do to get more people to read your post. A good title makes a promise about what the reader will get from reading the post that is (1) exciting, and (2) accurate.

Note that this does _not_ mean that you should write clickbait titles. The kind of people you want to read your blog will probably interpret a clickbait title as a promise that what they’ll get is content-free nonsense.

Examples of good titles:

-   [Matthew Walker’s “Why We Sleep” Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/). Nothing more satisfying than hearing someone recommend _Why We Sleep_ and asking, “Have you read ‘Matthew Walker’s “Why We Sleep” is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors’”? (I would perhaps have removed Walker’s name from the title but that’s a style difference between me and Alexey.)

-   [If You Have Writer’s Block, Maybe You Should Stop Lying](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/if-you-have-writers-block-maybe-you)

Examples of bad titles:

-   I used to just title posts based on the general topic, e.g. [Writing to clarify thoughts](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing-clarify-thoughts/) or [Second-order vanity](https://www.benkuhn.net/second-order-vanity/). This promises “click on this to read a post about writing to clarify thoughts” but doesn’t promise anything about takeaways.

-   I’d suggest not copying famous writers’ tendency to use “artsy” or “literary” titles, unless your goal is to be artsy or literary. (That’s a fine goal to have but not really what this piece is about.) [This is Water](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/TK) is the right title for David Foster Wallace’s commencement speech, but is optimized for something different than I’d suggest optimizing your blog posts for.

### Find the right framing

By “framing” I mean the part of the post that ties it up into an overall takeaway and makes it clear why it’s important and worth reading. Your readers won’t automatically figure this out, and hand-holding them through it can make the difference between a reaction of “huh that was interesting I guess” and “mind = blown.”

For example, [To listen well, get curious](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/), taken on its own, might seem like a somewhat banal point. The framing in the post—“most advice about listening seems bad because it’s teaching you to mimic the surface features of good listening rather than the underlying generator”—pinpoints how my advice diverges from the “standard” listening advice.

On the other hand, [Things that screw up your causal inference](https://www.benkuhn.net/causreg/) is an early post of mine that has no framing. I still think the info in that post is quite important (it implies that you basically shouldn’t trust observational studies), but because of the lack of framing, it didn’t get very much traction with readers. I’ll probably rewrite it with better framing at some point.

### Use lots of examples

Writing without examples tends to be dry and abstract.

One helpful thing about examples is that they help people understand the contours and boundaries of your points in much more detail. For example, take my framing advice above:

> explain\[\] to the reader what their overall takeaway should be and why it’s important

Applying this naively to _To listen well, get curious_, you might end up writing something like:

> In conclusion, if you want to be a better listener, try summoning your curiosity about the other person. This is important and worth reading because listening is an important skill that most people aren’t very good at.

This technically follows the advice but is… not great. Seeing an example of reasonable framing inoculates you against many possible misinterpretations, and gives you an intuitive sense of where to set your overall bar for “good.”

Another thing I love about examples is that the best ones have lots of interesting tidbits that are independent of the post they’re illustrating. My gold standard for this is the examples in [Willingness to look stupid](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/), each of which could be an interesting blog post on its own.

### Use microhumor

I learned this concept-handle from [Scott Alexander’s writing advice post](https://slatestarcodex.com/2016/02/20/writing-advice/):

> You’ve heard of microaggressions. Now try microhumor. It’s things that aren’t a joke in the laugh-out-loud told-by-a-comedian sense, but still put the tiniest ghost of a smile on your reader’s face while they’re skimming through them.

I strongly agree with Scott that microhumor is one of the biggest differentiators of enjoyable writing. Given that, I think it’s kind of funny that he buried it as #5 on a 10-item list, but maybe he’s not as neurotic about stack-ranking his lists as I am.

### Write like you talk

This is the [80/20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle) compressed version of how to improve your sentence-level prose quality. It comes from [an essay by Paul Graham](http://www.paulgraham.com/talk.html):

> Something comes over most people when they start writing. They write in a different language than they’d use if they were talking to a friend. The sentence structure and even the words are different. No one uses “pen” as a verb in spoken English. You’d feel like an idiot using “pen” instead of “write” in a conversation with a friend.

> Before I publish a new essay, I read it out loud and fix everything that doesn’t sound like conversation. I even fix bits that are phonetically awkward; I don’t know if that’s necessary, but it doesn’t cost much.

## Appendix: a few relevant objections and rebuttals

### “this is signaling/bullshit”

It’s true that some of the advantages of having a blog come from signaling, but I disagree that that means it’s bullshit. Signaling is extremely useful and without it many things wouldn’t work. You should try to be _time-efficient_ with your signaling, but you should also think of it as a productive activity. Writing is one of the most efficient forms of signaling, which makes it a good use of time.

For example, as a person looking for friends, there’s an effectively infinite number of people I could try to be friends with. One thing I care about in friends is whether we’ll have interesting conversations. If someone has an interesting blog this is pretty good evidence that they’ll be interesting in person. So it’s much more time-efficient (orders of magnitude so!) to make friends with interesting bloggers than to try to make friends with randomly-selected people until one of them turns out to be interesting.

### “but your blog is good and mine won’t be”

For at least one sense of “good” (popularity) this is not true. My blog is not particularly popular—as of the time I started drafting this post, I have around 1500 email subscribers, and when many of the examples above happened, it was much less popular than that. (1500 might seem like a lot, but this is after over 200 posts. If you start out with 10 initial subscribers, you can get there by growing your subscriber base with 2% by each post, which is extremely achievable.)

(See also: [at over 1000 followers/readers, Twitter/blogging are the best dating apps](https://twitter.com/yashkaf/status/1435700265785647108), [1000 true fans](https://kk.org/thetechnium/1000-true-fans/); my blog was useful well before I had 1000 readers.)

You might object that although my blog isn’t popular, it still has higher quality ideas or writing than what you would produce. Even if this is true now, again, that’s after I’ve written 200 blog posts. If you read my posts from 2014 ([e.g. 1](https://www.benkuhn.net/why-im-taking-operating-systems/), [2](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing-clarify-thoughts/), [3](https://www.benkuhn.net/things-i-learned-toby-ord/)), you’ll find that the posts, while still ok, are much less interesting, and yet my blog was still useful.

### “writing takes a lot of time”

I draft at about 10 words per minute, which as far as I can tell is good but not exceptional.[6](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fn:6) A typical blog post for me might be around 1500 words, or about 2.5 hours to write. Multiply by two for outlining and editing, and I could do post one post a week with about 45 minutes a day of writing.

Writing takes a lot longer than that if you’re trying to make _one particular post very good_, but I don’t think that’s the right strategy: instead, as mentioned above, you should be going for consistent volume so that you can take lots of shots on goal and learn from what resonates worse or better with your audience.

### “The prospect of committing words to paper fills me with a nameless dread”

(Or more general reasons for finding the process of writing painful)

I personally don’t mind writing, so can’t speak from direct experience. But other friends who have this problem have suggested various things in the category of “pretend you’re talking to a friend instead of writing”—for example, writing it in the form of an email (or even a series of chats!) to a friend, using dictation software (which I hear is probably better than the last time you tried it), or recording yourself explaining it verbally and getting that transcribed. (In each case you’d want to edit the text afterwards, but it’s always easier to edit than to write something from scratch!)

### “what if I write something that I regret?”

This can mean a few different but related things:

1.  Triggering an angry journalist, Internet outrage mob, or both. I think most people overestimate both the risk of this happening, and the downsides if it does, because it often happens in high-profile ways (e.g. [the New York Times doxxing Scott Alexander](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/22/nyt-is-threatening-my-safety-by-revealing-my-real-name-so-i-am-deleting-the-blog/)). Scott was both _very_ high-profile and wrote about particularly charged topics (politics and social justice), often in a style that wasn’t very robust to being taken out of context.

2.  Getting hostile comments. This is probably going to happen if you have posts that get a lot of traction (e.g. on Hacker News or a subreddit). Personally, I’ve found this to be mildly annoying, but once I noticed that it was happened to basically every HN post regardless of quality, I stopped caring very much.

    I’ve also noticed that I get the most hostile comments on my posts that are written less formally, with less hedging or caveating, so I suspect that being careful to present evidence and make your claims with appropriate levels of confidence can help reduce the risk of hostile comments.

3.  Making claims that you later come to disagree with. This isn’t particularly bad, for two reasons. First, readers generally have an expectation that if they’re reading old posts, they might no longer be endorsed by the author; second, _almost nobody will read old posts_ (unless you write something that really has legs—and if that many people like it, it’s unlikely to be completely wrongheaded).

    Note that here I’m using “good but not exceptional” relative to readers of this post, not relative to the general population, and making the perhaps-unfounded assumption that if you’re spending your spare time reading articles about blogging on random websites, you could probably get a fairly high SAT Writing score. [↩︎](https://www.benkuhn.net/writing/#fnref:6)
