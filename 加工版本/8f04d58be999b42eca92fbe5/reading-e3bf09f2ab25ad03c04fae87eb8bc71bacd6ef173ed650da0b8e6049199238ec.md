# Mini Blog Post 26: Having an accurate self-image — Neel Nanda

---

## 正文

### 引言

我非常_在意_ [having true beliefs](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-13-beliefs-are-for-true-things)。这是 making good decisions、从而 achieve 我的 goals 所_必需_的一项 sub-skill。而需要对其拥有 true beliefs 的一个极其重要的对象，就是_我自己_——我是我所做的每一个 decision 的 input！这些 beliefs 可以有许多形式：

-   Understanding 我 good at 什么——我应该主动承担什么？哪些 tasks 即使只留出很少时间，我也仍能 get done？生活中的哪些部分，我可以完全不必担心？

-   Understanding 我 _bad_ at 什么——我应该在什么时候 seek advice or help？什么时候应该 give up 一项 task？对于哪些 tasks，我应该寻找 creative work-arounds？

-   Understanding 我如何 come across socially——我与哪些人 get on well？我喜欢谁？我会留下 good first impressions 吗？我是否需要在 structured environment 中认识新朋友？

-   Understanding 我的 quirks——什么会让我 happy？哪些奇怪的事情会让我感到 anxious？什么会让我感到真正 relaxed？

-   Understanding 我的 biases——我是 overconfident 吗？我会变得 defensive 吗？我是否过多地 adopt 身边人的 beliefs？

我认为这一点极其重要，值得写成一篇文章，因为拥有 accurate self-image 是_困难的_。我们的 minds _充满了_ biases：insecurities、overconfidence、Imposter Syndrome、defensiveness、blind spots、planning fallacy，以及想要 fit 我们_已有的_ self-image 的欲望。然而，关于我的某些 facts 存在[于 _world_ 中，而不是我的 mind 中](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory)——如果我开始一个 project，我要么 finish it on schedule，要么不能。所以，在这里拥有 true beliefs 真的很有用！

这件事之所以困难，一个重要的深层原因是：acknowledge 自己的某些情况会令人_不快_——如果我正在 procrastinating，要 confront 这一点就_真的_很难，因为假装自己没有 procrastinating、然后继续下去，要愉快得多。因为我内心的某个部分，只有在我 acknowledge 一个 flaw 时，才认为它是 real。我发现，提醒自己这一点很有帮助：“feeling real” 是我的 mind 的一种 property，而 flaws 在 world 中有 real consequences——understanding 一个 flaw 是令人兴奋的，因为它早已 exists。Understanding 只会让事情变得更好，因为现在我可以为此_做_些什么！如果这促使我去 [fix 它](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-7-problems-are-for-fixing)，那太棒了！但即使这个 flaw 是我自身 immutable 的一部分，我让自己置身其中的 _circumstances_ 也不是 immutable。例如，知道[我有 low willpower](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower) 对我很有价值，即使我对此无能为力，因为这意味着我可以 set up systems，让自己仍然 take 我想要的 actions。

**world 的 default state 是：你对自己拥有 incorrect beliefs**。

### 保持 Grounded

与所有涉及 bias 和 perception 的问题一样，**find truth 的方法是保持 grounded**。world 的 default state 是，我的 opinions 来自我的 intuitions。而涉及我的 self-image 时，我的 intuitions 往往很糟糕。它们_并不_完全糟糕——[intuitions 是 significant evidence，不应该被 ignored](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-20-emotions-are-bayesian-evidence)。但重要的是，将它们与其他形式的 evidence 结合起来。

也要保持 humble！记住，**world 的 default state 是：你拥有 incorrect beliefs**。意识到自己在某件事上错了，并没有什么可羞耻的——那个 false belief 一直在阻碍你，而 discarding 它会让你变得更强。如果你的 intuitions 与 evidence 不一致，而 evidence 还在不断 accumulating，**那么你的 intuitions 现在就是阻碍你的东西**。

说到这里，重要的是记住 evidence 的真正含义：**一个 observation，若 belief 是 true，它发生的可能性比 belief 不是 true 时更大**。一切都是 uncertain 的，一切都很 complicated。支持某个 belief 的 Evidence _不应_意味着你要不加质疑地接受它，但你_也_不应该 ignore 这些 evidence！

你应该始终 check evidence 的 strength！如果我不认为自己会变得 defensive，而一个泛泛之交指责我如此，那是 weak evidence。如果一位亲近且值得信赖的朋友温和地指出我正在表现得 defensive，那就是 _strong_ evidence。

一个极好的 evidence 来源是 **historical data**。过去事情进展如何？我过去的 actions 是否与我的 beliefs consistent？留意那些发生了_奇怪_事情的时刻——surprise 和 confusion 的感觉是一个强有力的 signal，表明其中有值得 learned 的东西。

但 science 的一个关键教训是，observational data 是 insufficient 的——往往有许多 hypotheses 都能 fit 这些 data。所以，你需要 [run **experiments**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/the-value-of-experiments)。去 Make predictions，再走进 world 中 test 它们！如果你认为自己擅长 learning 新事物，就随便选一门 online course，花一个下午学习，看看最后是否觉得自己 understand 了它。此外，要提前做出 **concrete 的 predictions**——你很容易 explain 自己看到的任何 data，却没有意识到自己正在设法逃避原先的 beliefs。为自己设定一个 concrete goal，比如能够用 5 minutes 向朋友 coherently explaining 这门课的 content！

另一个极好的 grounding 来源是：**其他人的 opinions**。你的 mind 充满 blind spots 和 biases。其他人的也一样！但他们拥有非常不同的 perspective，因此他们的 opinions 能够提供 significant evidence。

我发现，这对于 resolving insecurities 极其有用——我的 mind 在这方面存在 systematic bias，很难从内部 overcome。所以，比如说，如果我 give a talk 后对效果感到 anxious，我会发现从他人那里 get feedback 极其有价值。因为我_知道_自己很不擅长从内部 analysing 这件事，而我在意的是那个实际的问题——如果我反复 give bad talks，也许我就应该停止 giving talks！而且，因为我的 intuitions 是 biased 的，其他人的 opinions 就是_强得多_的 evidence——我基本上应该用 aggregate data 来 replace 我的 intuitions。这同时也是一个值得了解的有用 quirk。Anxiety 可以通过 seeking feedback 得到 efficiently resolved，因此我显然应该这么做，即使代价是稍微打扰别人！

在我 gathered evidence 之后，帮助我今后保持 grounded 的一个有用工具是 **policies**。很多时候，我并不是以 random 的方式出错，而是以 _systematic_ 的方式出错。我不擅长 time estimates，而且我_总是_ underestimate。这意味着我的 intuitions 其实是很好的 evidence，只是不应该照单全收——将我的 time estimates doubling，会使它们 systematically more accurate。这是一个我可以遵循的 simple rule，用来 address systematic biases，而不必在下一次为了变得 properly grounded 再付出全部努力！

我发现，我们往往已经_知道_自己的许多 systematic biases，只是没有对此作出反应。所以，如果你觉得这个想法有趣，我建议现在设一个 5 minute 的计时器，列出那些你可能会觉得值得遵循的 policies！

### 如何 Navigating Social Norms

形成 accurate self-image 很难，还有一个原因是许多 social norms 与之相悖——尤其是围绕 modesty、arrogance、self-esteem 和 weirdness 的 norms。承认自己其实很擅长某件事，或者自己很不擅长、应该停止尝试，会让人感觉不好。但如果这些是 true，知道它们就_真的_很有用！这些 norms 有其宝贵的作用，但不应阻碍你拥有 accurate beliefs。

对此，我的主要解决办法是 **be grounded**。提醒自己，我不在乎 arrogance 或 modesty——我想拥有 true beliefs。例如，我知道自己擅长 maths！我也知道自己擅长 giving talks！这些 beliefs 有大量 empirical data 支持，因此我对它们有 confidence，而它们也是我未来 actions 的重要 inputs（也就是说，我会让自己置身于需要 do maths 和 give talks 的情境中）。我也知道自己有不擅长的事情！我很不擅长 motivating myself 去做无聊的事情，也不擅长 rote memorisation。在 first impressions 中，我往往显得相当 blunt 和 intense。这些也都是知道了很有用的事情！

我认为这些 norms 有价值——arrogance 往往表现为偏向自己的 cognitive bias，以及拒绝承认与之相反的 evidence，而这种表现受到 socially punished 是合理的。虽然我提倡拥有这些 beliefs，但我_并不_提倡在大多数 social situations 中谈论它们（讨论 arrogance 和 truth 的博客文章是我特许的例外！）。这样做可能让别人感到 insecure，却不会给你带来什么好处；我认为，这在很大程度上就是这些 norms 的用意。Tact 也是一种宝贵的 skill（而了解自己是否不擅长 tact，也是一种有用的 belief！）

但我发现，如果不留神，这往往会渗入我_自己_的 self-image，影响我作出 right decisions 的能力。这个世界的 default state 是，你并没有 true beliefs。如果我在持续对抗 entropy 的过程中稍有松懈，就会不再拥有 accurate self-image。而_这_不是我愿意付出的代价。

### 关于 Feedback

我发现，从别人那里获取 feedback，是形成 better beliefs 的一种_极其_有价值的方式，本文余下的部分将着重讨论这一点。在开设这个博客之前，我比较公开的一种 weirdness，就是设有几份关于自己的 [public anonymous feedback forms](https://bit.ly/NeelFeedback)，我非常重视这件事！这真的非常、非常有用，因为它是发现 blind spots 的好方法——它能让我感到_意外_，而单靠 introspecting 很少能做到这一点。它对于 calibrating myself、了解我在社交中给人的印象，也有不可估量的价值——在这里，别人的 perceptions _就是_ grounding。

如何 Receiving feedback

妥善 receiving feedback 需要相当的 skill。主要有两种 failure modes：

-   变得 defensive，并 rejecting 这些 feedback

    -   这显然很糟糕，它使 feedback 的_全部意义_落空。

    -   我认为，妥善 receiving criticism 并从中 learning 是一种宝贵的 skill；我喜欢寻求 feedback，部分原因就是能借此练习这项 skill！

-   变得 insecure，并 assuming 这些 feedback 是 true，进而为之感到 anxious

    -   这_同样_很糟糕！Feedback 是 evidence，但往往是 weak evidence！这些年来，我收集了很多 feedback，它们_并不_ consistent。

我的主要工具，是先 _assume_ 这些 feedback 是 true，再尝试 _explain_ 为什么那样的世界说得通。尽我所能，详细充实这个 hypothetical world。这很有价值，因为它使 feedback 是 true 的 possibility 变得更加 concrete，也更难被直接 reject。但这也是一种 defence——如果我充实了这个 scenario，却觉得它古怪、不对劲，我就能安心地 rejecting 它。

然后，如果这些 feedback 确实显得 compelling，我会询问_其他_亲近的朋友，看看他们是否觉得这些 feedback 说得通，以此保持 grounded。注意——要问“这是否 plausible”，而不是“你是否 agree with 它”，并明确表示，你想要的是 honesty，而不是 emotional support！

feedback 的主要价值，在于额外的 data 和 ideas；你要用自己寻找 truth 的其他方式来 check 它们，而不是盲目 believe！看看自己能从中 learn 什么，认真对待它，但只把它当作一个**关于世界的 possible hypothesis**。在认真对待它和能够 discard 它之间取得 balancing，是一种困难的 skill，但也是可以学会的。

此外，feedback 要经过他人 point of view 这层透镜的 filtered，这是一种 **significant 且 systematic 的 bias**。例如，我知道自己的 work ethic 顶多算 mediocre，只能借助 [creativity](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower) 把事情做完。有时，人们会 assume 我_一定_有良好的 work ethic，但在这个问题上，我绝对更相信自己的 data，而不是别人的 perception！你需要 correct for 这种 bias，因为最终的 goal 是拥有 _true_ beliefs，而不是与 feedback _agree_。

还要记住，feedback 关注的是弄清什么是 _true_，这与你应该采取什么 _actions_ 是很不一样的。True beliefs 是一种 input，你的 values 和 goals 则是另一种。所有值得采取的 actions 都有一些 drawbacks！知道某个 drawback 很有用，因为你可以意识到它，并尝试 minimise 它。但这不应该让你完全放弃采取那个 action！例如，我知道自己在初次见面时可能显得 intense。我认为这是一个 downside、一个 drawback，也是我尝试 manage 和 minimise 的地方。但如果它能帮助我 learn from conversations 并建立 meaningful friendships，那么它就_极其_值得。

最后，请记住，giving feedback 往往是相当大的 emotional labour！给出好的 feedback 需要时间和精力，而 criticise 别人往往让人不自在。我认为，即便不为别的，单是为了感谢这份付出，也值得学会如何妥善且 constructively 地接受 feedback。

而且，表现出你能妥善接受 feedback，也会鼓励别人继续这样做！如果你希望朋友能告诉你你的 mistakes 和 blind spots，就很有必要 [reward 那种 behaviour](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/reward-the-ways-youd-like-to-be-treated)。这包括能够在心中 constructively 地接纳，也包括_表现出_你在倾听。我努力始终心怀感激，并认真对待他们。如果我从中获得了 concrete lessons，并作出了 behaviour changes，我也会告诉对方！[当感谢伴随着 specific details、concrete examples 和 actions 时，它会有意义得多](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities)。

当面 Receiving feedback 尤其有用（尽管在情绪上会更 intense），因为你可以确保自己正确理解了它。我总会尝试 [paraphrase back 那些 feedback](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations)，以确保自己充分理解——我第一次听时很少能做到！这同时也能表明，你确实在认真对待他们。

好的 feedback 是什么样的

给出好的 feedback，同样需要相当的 skill！Feedback 涉及将一种 opinion converting 为 language，脱离_他们_全部的 context 和 nuance，再让它在你的心中被 converted 为 concepts，带上_你_全部的 context 和 nuance。**Misunderstandings 是这个世界的 default state**。[learning from conversations](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) 的所有通用 skills，在这里都很有用！注意，misunderstandings 并不会使 feedback 变得_毫无用处_ \- 它只是 weaker evidence，你需要 collect 更多。

主要要点：

-   要 specific——避免 vague language，focus 于 specific things。这会使 feedback 更 believable、更 sincere，_也_更有用

-   要 concrete——给出 examples！这是另一种 channel of communication，并能_大幅_ reduces understandings

-   要 actionable——当 feedback 包含 next steps 时，根据它来 update 就容易得多，而这些步骤对 recipient 来说更难自行想清楚。

    -   Focus 于 actions，而不是 enduring personality traits；关注那些_能够_被 changed 的事情。或者至少是可以 worked around 的事情，例如“你不太擅长 public speaking，应该尝试把那些 roles 交给 \_\_\_”

-   Demonstrate [你们是在同一支队伍里](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/25-friendship)——要 empathetic，表明这些 feedback 是为了帮助 recipient 变得_更强_，而不是打击他们。我总会尽量在给出 feedback 的同时，附上一句真诚的赞美

只要可能，就当面给出 feedback，或者让对方有办法向你寻求 clarification，并表明你欢迎他们这样做！Misunderstandings 是这个世界的 default state，但它们在 monologue 中比在 dialogue 中_常见得多_。

我认为，理解好的 feedback 是什么样的，本身就是一种重要的 skill，这是[我能为别人提供的 value 的一个主要来源](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/25-friendship)；此外，它也能帮助我为自己征求到真正有用的 feedback！

如何获取 feedback

思考 feedback 时，最后也是最重要的部分，是弄清如何_获取_它！我很努力地向朋友表明，我欢迎 feedback 和 honesty，但很少收到。主要原因在于，没有什么_理由_要给出 feedback。你可能会想到针对某个 specific action 或 event 给出 feedback，或者在某个 flaw/virtue 被提及时这样做，但**这个世界的 default state 是，它永远不会被提及**。没有什么 _trigger_ 能让人停下来 reflect，并给出有意义的 feedback。所以，除非你为此_做_些什么，否则不会得到多少 feedback。

我的主要工具，是设定一个明确的时间点，专门请求 feedback——例如[在生日当天发布一份关于自己的 feedback form，并请求大家把 feedback 作为生日礼物](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSfB5Ky0vhwwn4Vi8wqzXhCSMX2GZ0MeV9VJGs48CJpt1aG5CA/viewform)，或者[在 self-improvement workshop 之前请求 feedback](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeeW5YUHbSL-JtuHKfsRP7GH983o_t63BWrZThLUjVUKYF6UQ/viewform)。我发现这_极其_有效——两次分别收到了 35 和 20 条 feedback。给朋友发消息，明确请求 feedback，也很有效。

以前，我喜欢用 _anonymous_ feedback forms 来做这件事，但现在已经没那么热衷了。Anonymity 很有价值，因为它鼓励 unfiltered honesty，而我非常重视这一点；但我相信，我的大多数朋友本来就能做到——他们知道我想要这个。而 feedback 要想_有用_，就需要是 specific、concrete 且 actionable 的。我往往可以通过提出恰当的问题，让 feedback _变成_这样，但如果它是 anonymous，我就做不到。更糟的是，为了保留 anonymity，feedback 往往会被_故意_说得 vague，使其几乎毫无用处。

如果你这样做，我强烈建议你明确请求自己想要的那种 feedback！始终请人们做到 specific，给出 concrete examples，提供 context，并让内容 actionable。我发现，这种形式的 feedback 非常侧重社交方面——我给人的印象、人们如何 perceive 我、我给出的 first impressions、别人如何 perceive 我，等等。我认为这些都是很有价值的 information！但如果你想要其他 information，就一定要说清楚——给出 feedback 的人想帮助你，所以请帮助他们帮助你。

feedback 的价值

说到这里，很容易觉得获取 feedback 不值得费这个劲——它很麻烦，开口请求有点奇怪，而且似乎并非_必要_。这种想法也有几分道理！Giving feedback _确实是_ emotional labour，你应该认真对待请求 feedback 这件事。但如果你能培养起妥善且 constructively 地 receiving feedback 的 skill，我认为，这是值得向朋友请求的一份帮助。

而如果寻求 feedback 感觉是_不必要的_，就检查一下，你是否真的对其他人如何 perceive 你感到 confident。人很容易把“从未真正想过这件事”与“不需要 feedback”混淆——如果你对自身某个方面的想法是 confusing 和 vague 的，从内部看，这往往会_感觉_像是对它有 confidence。你了解得不够多，无法注意到那些 open questions，所以感觉好像已经没有任何问题需要回答了！我认为，如果你对其他人如何 perceive 你之类的事情_确实_感到 uncertain，那么 feedback 就是_极其_有用的——你已经知道的越少，feedback 就可能越有价值！

就我个人而言，我发现自己使用 feedback forms 的 experiments 非常有价值——它 strongly updated 了我对自己给人什么印象、自己如何随着时间改变的看法，并 identified 了几个 blind spots。最值得一提的是，因为一条评论，我开始花更多心思考虑自己的穿着和仪容。出乎意料的是，这成了一个非常积极的变化！我对自己的个人风格有了好得多的感觉，也对自己的外表满意多了。这_完全不是_我预期会发生的事，但寻求 feedback 的全部意义，就是 expand 你的 hypothesis space，并 identify blind spots！如果某个变化是预期之中的，你大概一开始就不需要 feedback。

### Conclusion（结语）

我认为，拥有 accurate self-image 是_极其_有价值的，也是我做出 life decisions 时相当重要的一部分。我希望我的 mindset 和 tools 能有些用处！它永远不可能是_完全_ accurate 的，而随着我不断改变、discover 新的 evidence，我也一直在 updating 它。但我发现，我这个糟糕的 partial model 仍然_极其_有用，而且随着时间推移，已经变得有用多了。拥有 true beliefs 是做出 right decisions 的_必要条件_。

所以，如果这里的想法引起了你的共鸣，我强烈建议你明确向别人索取 feedback！仔细想想你_想要_什么 feedback、好的 feedback 是什么样的，然后向朋友们索取。公开发布一份 public feedback form 是我会谨慎赞同的做法，不过这需要勇气。一个同样有用、但感觉安全得多的做法，就是写一条请求 feedback 的消息，发给你所有的亲密朋友！如果你觉得这没有必要，就问问自己，你是否觉得目前得到的 feedback 已经_足够_。这条消息的意义不在于他们不_愿意_给你 feedback，而在于 default action 是从不停下来给出 feedback。所以，想想你能做些什么来**改变这个 default**，并**create 一个 trigger**。

最后说几句：我个人觉得 feedback 非常 motivating。而且，每当我开始做一件新事情，比如 teaching 时，我都发现 feedback 极其有用。它帮助我 identify 自己的 flaws 并 fix 它们，让自己 ground，并 substantially improved 了我的 self-confidence。而我最近开始做的新事情就是写博客。这是我承诺每天写博客的最后一篇文章（但愿不是永远的最后一篇！），所以我放宽了自己“不要 perfectionism，也不要 editing”的规则。因此，如果你一直在读这个博客，听到你对它的看法，以及你从阅读中得到了什么，会让我非常开心！我尤其感谢遵循好的 feedback 规则的想法！你最喜欢哪些文章或想法的 specific examples，你改变了哪些 beliefs 的 concrete examples，以及有哪些 actionable ways 可以让内容对你来说更清楚或更有用。

### Introduction

I care a _lot_ about [having true beliefs](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-13-beliefs-are-for-true-things). This is an _essential_ sub-skill to making good decisions that achieve my goals. And one extremely important thing to have true beliefs about is _myself_ - I’m an input to every single decision that I made! And these beliefs can take many forms:

-   Understanding what I’m good at - what should I volunteer to do? What tasks can I leave little time for, and still get done? Which parts of my life can I just not worry about?

-   Understanding what I’m _bad_ at - when should I seek advice or help? When should I give up on a task? Which tasks should I search for creative work-arounds to?

-   Understanding how I come across socially - which people do I get on well with? Who do I like? Do I give good first impressions? Do I need to meet new people in a structured environment?

-   Understanding my quirks - what makes me happy? What weird things make me feel anxious? What makes me feel truly relaxed?

-   Understanding my biases - am I overconfident? Do I get defensive? Do I adopt the beliefs of those around me too much?

I think this is an extremely important point, and worthy of a post, because having an accurate self-image is _hard_. Our minds are _full_ of biases: insecurities, overconfidence, Imposter Syndrome, defensiveness, blind spots, planning fallacy, a desire to fit our _existing_ self-image. Yet there are facts about me that exist [in the _world_, not my mind](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-12-the-map-and-the-territory) - if I start a project, I’ll either finish it on schedule or not. So having true beliefs here is really useful!

A big underlying reason why this is hard is that it’s _unpleasant_ to acknowledge some things about yourself - if I’m procrastinating, it’s _really_ hard to confront that, because it’s far more pleasant to pretend that I’m not and continue. Because some part of me only considers a flaw real when I acknowledge it. I find it helpful to remind myself that “feeling real” is a property of my mind, while flaws have real consequences in the world - understanding a flaw is exciting, because it already exists. Understanding can only make things better, because now I can _do_ something about it! If this prompts me [to fix it](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-7-problems-are-for-fixing), that’s amazing! But even if the flaw is an immutable part of myself, the _circumstances_ I put myself in are not. Eg, it is valuable to me to know that [I have low willpower](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower), even if I can’t do anything about this, because this means I can set up systems so that I take the actions I want to anyway.

**The default state of the world is that you have incorrect beliefs about yourself**.

### Be Grounded

As with all problems of bias and perception, **the way to find truth is to be grounded**. The default state of the world is that my opinions come from my intuitions. And when it comes to my self-image, my intuitions often suck. They do _not_ completely suck - [intuitions are significant evidence and should not be ignored](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-20-emotions-are-bayesian-evidence). But it is important to couple them with other forms of evidence.

And be humble! Remember, **the default state of the world is that you have incorrect beliefs**. There is no shame to realising you were wrong about something - the false belief was holding you back, and you become stronger for discarding it. If your intuitions disagree with the evidence, and the evidence keeps accumulating, **your intuitions are now the thing holding you back**.

At this point, it’s important to remember what evidence really means: **an observation that is more likely to have happened if the belief were true, than if the belief were not true**. Everything is uncertain, everything is complicated. Evidence for a belief should _not_ mean that you take it unquestioningly, but you should _also_ not ignore the evidence!

You should always check the strength of the evidence! If I don’t think I get defensive, and a causal acquaintance accuses me of this, that’s weak evidence. If a close and trusted friend gently suggests I’m being defensive, that’s _strong_ evidence.

An excellent source of evidence is **historical data**. How have things gone in the past? Are my past actions consistent with my beliefs? Notice when something _weird_ happens - the feeling of surprise and confusion is a powerful signal that there’s something to be learned.

But one of the key lessons from science is that observational data is insufficient - often many hypotheses can be fit to the data. So you need to [run **experiments**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/the-value-of-experiments). Make predictions, and go out into the world and test them! If you think you’re good at learning new things, pick a random online course, spend an afternoon on it, and see whether you feel like you understand it at the end. Further, make **concrete, advance predictions** - it’s easy to explain any data you see, without realising you’re weaseling your way out of your original beliefs. Set yourself a concrete goal, like being able to spend 5 minutes explaining the course content coherently to a friend!

Another excellent source of grounding: **other people’s opinions**. Your mind is full of blind spots and biases. And so are other people’s! But they have a very different perspective, so their opinions provide significant evidence.

I find this extremely useful for resolving insecurities - my mind has a systematic bias there, that it’s very hard to overcome from the inside. So if, say, I give a talk and am anxious about how it went, I find it extremely valuable to get feedback from people. Because I _know_ I suck at analysing this from the inside, and I care about the actual question - if I repeatedly give bad talks, I should probably stop giving talks! And because my intuitions are biased, other people’s opinions are _much_ stronger evidence - I should basically replace my intuitions with the aggregate data. This also doubles as a useful quirk to be aware of. Anxiety can be efficiently resolved by seeking feedback, and so I should obviously do this, even at the cost of mildly bothering other people!

Once I’ve gathered evidence, a useful tool to remain grounded in future are **policies**. Often, I’m not wrong in random ways, I’m wrong in _systematic_ ways. I suck at time estimates, and I _always_ underestimate. This means that my intuitions are actually great evidence, just shouldn’t be taken at face value - doubling my time estimates makes them systematically more accurate. This is a simple rule I can follow to address systematic biases, without needing all of the effort of becoming properly grounded next time!

I find that we often already _know_ a lot of our systematic biases, we just don’t react to them. So if you find this idea interesting, I recommend setting a 5 minute timer now, and listing policies you might find it useful to follow!

### Navigating Social Norms

A further reason that forming an accurate self-image is hard is that a lot of social norms go against this - especially norms focused around modesty, arrogance, self-esteem and weirdness. It feels bad to acknowledge that I’m actually awesome at something, or that I suck at it and should stop trying. But if these are true, it’s _really_ useful to know! These norms serve a valuable purpose, but should not hold you back from having accurate beliefs.

The main solution I have to this is to **be grounded**. Remind myself that I don’t care about arrogance or modesty - I want to have true beliefs. For example, I know that I’m good at maths! And I know that I’m good at giving talks! I have a large amount of empirical data for these beliefs, so I have confidence in them, and these are important inputs to my future actions (ie, I put myself in situations where I do maths and give talks). And I know that there are things I suck at! I’m pretty bad at motivating myself to do boring things, and rote memorisation. I tend to come across as fairly blunt and intense on first impressions. These are also useful things to know!

I think these norms have value - often arrogance manifests as a cognitive bias in favour of yourself, and a refusal to acknowledge evidence against this, and it is right that that is socially punished. And while I advocate for having these beliefs, I do _not_ advocate for talking about them in most social situations (I make a special case for blog posts about arrogance and truth!). This can make other people feel insecure, without adding anything to you, and I think that’s a lot of the purpose of these norms. Tact is another valuable skill (and understanding if you are bad at tact is a useful belief to have!)

But I find that, if I’m not paying attention, this often bleeds into my _own_ self-image, and affects my ability to make the right decisions. The default state of the world is that you do not have true beliefs. If I slip up in the constant fight against entropy, I will cease to have an accurate self-image. And _that_ is not a cost I am willing to pay.

### Feedback

I’ve found that an _incredibly_ valuable way to form better beliefs is getting feedback from other people, and that’s what I’ll focus on for the rest of this post. One of my more public forms of weirdness (pre this blog) was having several [public anonymous feedback forms](https://bit.ly/NeelFeedback) about myself, this is something I value a lot! This is really, really useful, because it’s a great way to spot blind spots - this can _surprise_ me, in a way that just introspecting rarely does. And it’s invaluable for calibrating myself about how I come across socially - other people’s perceptions _are_ the grounding here.

Receiving feedback

There is a significant skill to receiving feedback well. There are two main failure modes:

-   Becoming defensive and rejecting the feedback

    -   This is obviously terrible, this defeats the _entire point_ of feedback.

    -   I think the skill of receiving criticism well and learning from it is a valuable one, and in part I like seeking feedback as a way of practicing it!

-   Becoming insecure, and assuming the feedback is true, and feeling anxious about it

    -   This is _also_ terrible! Feedback is evidence, but often weak evidence! I’ve collected a lot of feedback over time, it is _not_ consistent.

My main tool is to _assume_ the feedback is true, and try to _explain_ why that world makes sense. Fleshing out the hypothetical world in as much detail as I can. This is valuable, because it makes the possibility of the feedback being true a lot more concrete, and harder to reject out of hand. But it’s also a defence - if I flesh out the scenario and it feels weird and off, I feel comfortable rejecting it.

And then, if the feedback does feel compelling, I remain grounded, by asking _other_ close friends whether they think the feedback makes sense. Note - ask “is this plausible” not “do you agree with it”, and be clear that you want honesty rather than emotional support!

The main value of feedback is additional data and ideas, which you check against your other ways of finding truth, not as something to blindly believe! See what you can learn from it, take it seriously, but only as a **possible hypothesis about the world**. And balancing between taking it seriously and being able to discard it is a difficult skill, but a learnable one.

Further, feedback is filtered through lens of being from somebody else’s point of view, which is a **significant and systematic bias**. For example, I know that I have a mediocre work ethic at best, and can only get stuff done with [creativity](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower). And sometimes people assume I _must_ have a good work ethic, but this is a question on which I definitely trust my own data over other people’s perception! You need to correct for this bias, because the ultimate goal is to have _true_ beliefs, not to _agree_ with the feedback.

And remember that feedback is about figuring out what is _true_, which is very different from what your _actions_ should be. True beliefs are one input, your values and goals are another. And all actions worth taking have some drawbacks! Knowing about a drawback is useful, because you can be aware of it and try to minimise it. But it shouldn’t stop you from taking the action at all! For example, I know that I can come across as intense on first meeting. And I consider this a downside, and a drawback, and one that I try to manage and minimise. But it is _overwhelmingly_ worth it, if it can help me learn from conversations and form meaningful friendships.

Finally, bear in mind that giving feedback is often significant emotional labour! It takes time and effort to give good feedback, and it often feels uncomfortable to criticise people. I think that it’s good to learn how to take feedback well and constructively, in thanks for this, if nothing else.

And demonstrating that you take feedback well encourages it! If you want your friends to be able to tell you about your mistakes and blind spots, it’s important to [reward that behaviour](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/reward-the-ways-youd-like-to-be-treated). This involves being able to take in constructively in your head, and _showing_ that you’re listening. I try to always be grateful, and to take them seriously. And if I take concrete lessons and behaviour changes from it, to tell the other person about it! [Thanks is a lot more meaningful when accompanied by specific details, concrete examples and actions](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-10-seek-positive-externalities).

Receiving feedback in person is especially useful (though more intense emotionally) because you can ensure you’ve understood it correctly. I always try to [paraphrase back the feedback](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations), to ensure I’ve understood it well - I rarely did the first time! This doubles as a way of demonstrating that you’re genuinely taking them seriously.

What good feedback looks like

There is also a significant skill to giving good feedback! Feedback involves converting an opinion into language, divorced from all of _their_ context and nuance, and having it be converted into concepts in your mind, taking on all of _your_ context and nuance. **Misunderstandings are the default state of the world**. All of the general skills of [learning from conversations](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) are useful here! Note that misunderstandings don’t make the feedback _useless_ \- it’s just weaker evidence, and you need to collect more of it.

The main points:

-   Be specific - avoid vague language, focus on specific things. This makes the feedback more believable and sincere, _and_ more useful

-   Be concrete - give examples! This is another channel of communication, and _majorly_ reduces understandings

-   Be actionable - it’s much easier to update on feedback when it includes next steps, and these are much harder to figure out for the recipient.

    -   Focus on actions rather than enduring personality traits, things that _can_ be changed. Or at least worked around, eg “you aren’t great at public speaking, and should try to give those roles to \_\_\_”

-   Demonstrate that [you’re on the same team](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/25-friendship) - be empathetic, show the feedback is about helping the recipient become _stronger_, not about tearing them down. I try to always couple feedback with a sincere compliment

If at all possible, give feedback in person, or give them a way to ask you for clarification, and show that you welcome this! Misunderstandings are the default state of the world, but are _much_ more common in a monologue than a dialogue.

I think understanding what good feedback looks like is important as a skill in its own sake, this is [a major source of value I can give to others](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/25-friendship), but also because this can help solicit genuinely useful feedback for myself!

How to get feedback

The final, and most important part of thinking about feedback, is figuring out how to _get_ it! I try quite hard to show friends that I welcome feedback and honesty, but rarely get it. And the main reason for this is that there’s no _reason_ to give feedback. You might think to give feedback on a specific action, or event, or if a flaw/virtue comes up, but **the default state of the world is that it never comes up**. There’s no _trigger_ to stop and reflect and give meaningful feedback. So you won’t get much feedback unless you _do_ something about it.

My main tool is to create a clear point in time where I explicitly ask for feedback - eg [posting a feedback form about myself on my birthday and requesting feedback as a birthday present](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSfB5Ky0vhwwn4Vi8wqzXhCSMX2GZ0MeV9VJGs48CJpt1aG5CA/viewform), or [requesting it in advance of a self-improvement workshop](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeeW5YUHbSL-JtuHKfsRP7GH983o_t63BWrZThLUjVUKYF6UQ/viewform). I find this works _incredibly_ well - getting 35 and 20 bits of feedback respectively. Sending messages to friends explicitly requesting it also works well.

I used to be a fan of _anonymous_ feedback forms for this, but I feel less enthused about that nowadays. Anonymity is valuable because it encourages unfiltered honesty, which I care about a lot, but I trust most of my friends to be capable of that anyway - they know I want that. And for feedback to be _useful_, it needs to be specific, concrete and actionable. I can often _make_ feedback into this by asking the right questions, but I can’t if it’s anonymous. Worse, feedback is often made _deliberately_ vague to preserve anonymity, making it approximately useless.

If you do this, I highly recommend explicitly asking for the kind of feedback you want! Always ask people to be specific, to give concrete examples, to give context, and to make things actionable. I find feedback of this form is very socially focused - how I come across, how people perceive me, first impressions I give, how others perceive me, etc. I think this is pretty valuable information! But if you want other information, it’s important to be clear about that - people giving feedback want to help you, so help them help you.

The value of feedback

At this point, it’s easy to dismiss the idea of getting feedback as not worth the effort - it’s a bother, it’s a bit weird to ask for, it doesn’t seem _necessary_. And there’s some merit to this! Giving feedback _is_ emotional labour, and you should take asking for feedback seriously. But if you can cultivate the skill of receiving feedback well and constructively, I think this is a favour worth asking from your friends.

And if seeking feedback feels _unnecessary_, check whether you truly feel confident in how other people perceive you. It’s easy to confuse “never having really thought about it” with “not needing feedback” - if your thoughts on an aspect of yourself are confusing and vague, this can often _feel_ like confidence in it from the inside. You don’t know enough to notice the open questions, so it doesn’t feel like there are any questions left to answer! I think that if you _are_ uncertain about things like how others perceive you, feedback is _incredibly_ useful - the less you already know, the more valuable feedback can be!

Personally I’ve found my experiments with feedback forms highly valuable - it’s strongly updated my impression of how I come across, and how I’ve changed over time, and identified a few blind spots. Most notably, as a result of one comment, I’ve started putting a lot more effort into thinking about how I dress and groom myself. Which, unexpectedly, has been a really positive development! I’ve developed a much better sense of my personal style, and feel much happier with my appearance. This was _completely not_ what I expected to happen, but the entire point of seeking feedback is to expand your hypothesis space, and identify blind spots! If a development is expected, you probably didn’t need feedback in the first place.

### Conclusion

I think that having an accurate self-image is _incredibly_ valuable, and is a pretty important part of how I make life decisions. I hope my mindset and tools are somewhat useful! This is never something that can be _perfectly_ accurate, and I’m constantly updating it as I change over time, and discover new evidence. But I’ve found my crappy partial model is still _extremely_ useful, and has gotten much more useful over time. Having true beliefs is _necessary_ for making the right decisions.

So, if the ideas in here have resonated with you, I strongly recommend explicitly asking people for feedback! Think through what feedback you _want_, what good feedback looks like, and ask your friends for it. Posting a public feedback form is something I’d tentatively endorse, but bold. One thing that is similarly useful, but feels much safer is just sending a message requesting feedback and sending it to all of your close friends! And if this doesn’t feel necessary, ask whether you feel like you currently get _enough_ feedback. The point of the message is not because they aren’t _willing_ to give you feedback, but because the default action is to never stop and give feedback. So think on what you can do to **change that default**, and **create a trigger**.

A closing note: I personally find feedback very motivating. And I find feedback has been incredibly useful whenever I’ve started something new, like teaching. It’s helped me to identify my flaws and fix them, ground myself, and substantially improved my self-confidence. And the most recent new thing I’ve started is blogging. This is the last post of my daily blogging commitment (though hopefully not the last post ever!) and so I’m relaxing my “no perfectionism or editing” rule. So, if you’ve been reading this blog, it would make me incredibly happy to hear what you’ve thought of it, and what you’ve gotten out of reading it! And I especially appreciate thoughts following the rules of good feedback! Specific examples of posts or ideas you’ve most enjoyed, concrete examples of beliefs you’ve changed, actionable ways things could have been clearer or more useful to you.
