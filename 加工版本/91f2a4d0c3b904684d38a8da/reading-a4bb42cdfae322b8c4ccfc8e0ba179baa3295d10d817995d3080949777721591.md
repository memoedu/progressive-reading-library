# *Why Nations Fail* and the long-termist view of global poverty

---

## 正文

在 effective altruism community 中，人们经常谈论“long-termist”与“short-termist”worldviews。两者的正式 distinction 是，short-termists 根据问题如何 affect 今天活着的人来 prioritize problems，而 long-termists 根据问题可能如何 affect humanity 的整个 future trajectory 来 prioritize problems。在实践中，人们通常把这视为与以下选择 synonymous：要么 prioritizing [existential risk reduction](https://concepts.effectivealtruism.org/concepts/existential-risks/)（如果是 long-termist），要么 scaling up proven global health interventions（如果是 short-termist）。

不过，每种 worldview 居然都恰好有一个最偏爱的 cause area，这有点令人意外。难道不能有针对 existential risk 的 short-termist work，或者针对 global poverty 的 long-termist work 吗？实际上，这些据称 discrete 的 worldviews 看起来更像是各种不同 beliefs 所形成的 correlated clusters：

| “Short-termist” | “Long-termist” |
| --- | --- |
| High [time discount rate](https://concepts.effectivealtruism.org/concepts/discounting-the-future/) | Low or no discount rate |
| Prefers highly robust “[outside view](https://wiki.lesswrong.com/wiki/Outside_view)” type arguments | More open to “inside view” that this case might be different |
| Extrapolates existing effects or trends | Reasons about the future from first principles |
| Skeptical of _prima facie_ bizarre claims | Takes weird-sounding ideas more seriously |
| Focuses on fixing known, concrete problems | Focuses on preventing hypothetical, nebulous risks |
| Fast [feedback loops](https://www.jefftk.com/p/why-global-poverty) are critical to making progress | Fast feedback is helpful, but not the most important thing |

其中一些为什么是 correlated，这是可以理解的，但肯定有很多人落在这些 clusters 之间的空隙里。如果你认同 short-termists 对 weird claims 和 hypothetical risks 的 skepticism，但又愿意专注于 first-principles reasoning，并在 long time scale 上开展工作，那会怎样？

你仍然会想专注于今天已经是 problem 的事情，所以你大概会想致力于 global poverty。但你会 dismiss [GiveWell’s top charities](https://www.givewell.org/)，认为它们是在 treating a symptom，而不是 cause。这些 countries 为什么一开始就需要 charity？South Korea 曾经和 Africa 的任何地方一样 poor，但如今它已经极其 prosperous，而 sub-Saharan Africa 取得的 progress 却少得多。如果我们能让 lowest-growth countries 从当前的 trajectory 转到 South Korea 的 trajectory 上，我们所做的就会远远超过任何一场 malaria-eradication campaign 所能做到的。

如果那就是你的 worldview，你一定会很喜欢 [Why Nations Fail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Nations_Fail)。在我见过的尝试中，它是最出色的尝试之一，力求以 first-principles understanding 来理解什么会 affects 各国的 long-term economic growth。

* * *

首先，economic growth _究竟是_什么？它就是人们付出相同的 effort，却 produce 更多（或 more valuable）的东西。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/#fn:1) _Why Nations Fail_ 的第一个、也是 least controversial 的 point 是：一个 nation 要不断做到 doing more with less，其 individual citizens 就需要受到 incentivized，从而变得 more productive。尤其是，state _不应_建立这样的 systems：每当某个人变得 more productive，其他人就过来 take away 他所 produced 的额外东西。这些 systems 就是作者所说的 _extractive economic institutions,_，包括 slavery、serfdom、indentured servitude、roving bandits、guilds、collectivized agriculture、nationalization of private assets、officials requiring bribes、kangaroo courts、banana republics，以及其他 \[动物或蔬菜\] \[civic institution\]。

你可能会认为，在 extractive institutions 下，即便人们无法保留 surplus，你也可以通过 forcing 人们变得 more productive 来 grow 你的 economy。这在 short term 确实常常有效（而 short term 可以持续长得令人意外）——例如，Soviet Russia 在 1930-1970 年间每年 grew 约 5%，[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/#fn:2) 尽管它拥有 extremely extractive institutions。但这之所以有效，只是因为 growth 始于 low base。Soviet Russia 的 economic institutions 虽然糟糕，但仍可以说比之前的 serfdom 更 less extractive。另外，这种 growth 主要来自“easy wins”，比如人们从 horrifically inefficient farming 转向 horrifically inefficient industry。

事实上，extractive institutions 下的 growth _只能_来自 easy wins——它们必须足够 easy，以至于任何负责 forcing 他人变得 more productive 的人都能一眼看出来。（没有人会 generate 自己的 productivity-boosting ideas，因为他们没有这样做的 incentive。）任何一个还算过得去的 Soviet central planner 都能告诉你，industry 会比 farming 更 productive，所以他们能够 force peasants 做出这种转变。但没有人的 central planning 水平足以 fix 他们的 industry 中所有 inefficient 的地方——更不用说像 US 那样迅速提出新的 inventions 或 technologies 了。因此，一旦所有 low-hanging fruit 都被摘完，大约在 1980 年，Soviet Union 就撞上了 productivity wall，并 collapsed。

而且，即便某项 innovation 对 extractive elite 来说_确实是_显而易见的，他们也可能主动试图 crush 它，因为它 threatens 他们对 power 的掌控。例如，在 19th-century Europe，factories 和 railroads 显然可能带来巨大的 productivity improvement，但 peripheral Europe 的 extractive rulers 却一样也不想要。Austria 的情况是这样的：

> 当一项 build northern railway 的 plan 被呈交给 \[Emperor\] Francis I 时，他回答：“不，不，我绝不参与，免得 revolution 来到这个 country。”

Russian Finance Minister (1823-44) Count Igor Kankrin 则这样说：

> $$R$$ailways 并不总是源于 natural necessity，反而更多是 artificial need 或 luxury 的对象。它们 encourage unnecessary travel，让人们从一个地方跑到另一个地方，这完全是我们这个时代的典型特征。

一个 ruler 因害怕 TEH REVOLUTIONZ 而 ban technology，可能显得很荒诞——直到你想起，今天有多少 extractive states，比如 Ethiopia 和 China，出于类似原因 resisted the Internet。

* * *

_Why Nations Fail_ 的下一部分问道：如果 economic institutions 能 explain long-term growth，那么什么能 explains long-term economic institutions？答案也许很显然，就是 politics。

当 social power 是 concentrated 于一个 small elite 手中时（“extractive political institutions”），Extractive economic institutions 往往更能 persist，因为这些人能够利用这种 power 把 economic surplus 据为己有——或者，如果它看起来可怕且 potentially destabilizing，就干脆 destroy 它。当 social power 是 distributed widely 于 population 中时（“inclusive political institutions”），它们就较难 persist，因为在这种情况下，被 extracted 的人往往拥有足够的 political power，能够反击 extraction。

例如，在 early 1900s 的 United States，各种 industries（steel、oil、banking、chemicals、farming tools 等）最终落入 extractive monopolists 的 control 之下；他们 charged 太多、paid 太少，把 difference 装进自己口袋，再把这笔 difference 花在[镀金马桶](https://www.washingtonpost.com/lifestyle/travel/escapes-evergreen-mansion-baltimores-hidden-gilded-age-gem/2012/03/05/gIQAjGLEES_story.html?utm_term=.56d51f35ebba)上。但即便是全世界最奢华的马桶，也无法击败起来 oppose monopolies 的 mass populist movement（因为 US 足够 pluralistic，所以 mass populist movements 非常 powerful），因此这些 monopolies 最终被 forcibly broken up 并 regulated。

在 politics 较不 pluralistic 的 states 中，elites 可以进行这种 extraction 而不受惩罚。例如，British 在 colonized Sierra Leone 时，设立了 monopoly cocoa and coffee purchasing boards，以低价从 farmers 手里 bought，再在 international markets 上 sold high，并把 margin 装进自己口袋。independence 之后，Sierra Leone 的 presidents 没有 dismantling 这些 boards，反而将其变成 slush fund，把 margin 推高到 90%。

Sierra Leone 还 illustrates 了 extractive economic institutions 的另一个令人难过的 fact：它们跨越 nominally different political regimes 而 persist 的程度，出人意料地高。Sierra Leone 发生的事，在 sub-Saharan Africa 几乎到处都发生过：colonists 被一位 revolutionary leader ousted，后者很快意识到，colonists 那台巨大的印钞机仍然原封不动，于是决定，也许不必把_整个_ colonial apparatus 都 smashed。当然，为了继续掌控 economic surplus，新的“democratic”government 很快就 resorted to repression and violence。（_Why Nations Fail_ 说得很清楚，重要的是 _de facto_ democracy，而不是 _de jure_ democracy，confusing 这两者会导致非常错误的 conclusions。）

因此，在 long timescales 上，inclusive 和 extractive institutions 的混合似乎是 unstable 的。如果 institutions 足够 inclusive，那么任何 deviates 并试图 institute extractive norms 的 group，都会遭到 society 其余部分的制止。如果 institutions _不够_ inclusive，其中某个 group 最终就会成功，随后受到 incentivized，进一步 consolidate 自己的 power，直到 society 完全变成 extractive。

* * *

这条 rule 最显著的 exception——许多 reviewers 都指出过——就是 Deng Xiaoping 以来的 China：在过去 40 年里，它拥有 relatively inclusive economic institutions，但 politics 却 extremely authoritarian。Acemoglu 和 Robinson acknowledge 这一点，但他们的基本 claim 是，China 并不处于 stable equilibrium：

> 即便如此，除非 extractive political institutions 为 inclusive institutions 让路，否则 \[Chinese\] growth 终将失去动力。只要 political institutions 仍是 extractive，growth 就会 inherently limited，正如其他所有类似 cases 中的情况一样。

按他们写出的样子，这算是一个有些薄弱的 explanation，因为他们甚至没有提到它将通过什么 _mechanism_ 而受到 limited。不过，我认为，如果善意理解，还是可以构建一个不错的 argument。具体而言，_Why Nations Fail_ model 似乎 predicts：如果没有 inclusive political institutions 支撑，China 当前的 inclusive economic institutions 就不是 sustainable 的。在当前 regime 下，Chinese political elite 的 local incentives 是尽可能 extract economy 的 surplus，因为他们做得到；仅仅指望他们一直足够 altruistic 而不这样做，并不是 sustainable 的。到某个时候，除非 China 的 political institutions 将真正的 power 交给广大的 majority of people，否则 elites 就会屈服于诱惑，开始把全部 economic surplus 据为己有。

（顺便注意，China 最近的 economic slowdown _并不能_ confirm 这个 model 的 predictions，除非你认为 slowdown 是由 political elite 变得更加 economically extractive 所 caused 的，而情况似乎并非如此。）

如果你担心 powerful authoritarian nations 一直存在，那么拥有这样一个 model 会令人安心。不过，China 已经“out of equilibrium”将近半个世纪，而 _Why Nations Fail_ 并没有提供任何 framework，来思考这些 disequilibria 能持续多久。

* * *

_Why Nations Fail_ 中最强的 claim 是：在足够长的 time horizons 上，institutions 是 shaping economic growth 的 _the dominant factor_；令人恼火的是，这个 claim 大多没有被明说。许多 reviewers 对此提出了质疑。

公平地说，其中很多 reviewers 似乎把这一 claim 简化成了“institutions _总是_ dominant factor”，然后抱怨这本书没有 explaining China（尽管书中花了半章 explaining China 是 out of equilibrium 的），或者没有充分 explaining 过去 30 年里的 Bolivia 与 Vietnam（如果 China 可以 out of equilibrium 达 40 年，其他 countries 也可以），等等。不过，即便考察 long periods of time，追问 institutions 是否真是所有可能因素中最重要的，也是合理的。

最显而易见的 alternative candidate 是 geography（包括 natural resources）。_Why Nations Fail_ 尝试 argue，geography 并不具有足够的 explanatory 力，援引的例子是：(a) North 和 South Korea 之间的 divergence，以及 (b) 在 European colonization of the Americas 之后，latitude 与 productivity 之间的 correlation 从 negative（tropics 更 productive）变成了 positive（temperate regions 更 productive）：

> 正如我们在上一章看到的，在 Columbus 对 Americas 的 conquest 发生时，\[tropical\] areas……拥有伟大的 Aztec 和 Inca civilizations……。与之形成鲜明对比的是……\[modern\] United States、Canada、Argentina 和 Chile，当时的居民大多属于缺乏这些 technologies 的 Stone Age civilizations。因此，Americas 的 tropics 比 temperate zones 富裕得多，这表明，tropical poverty 这一“显而易见的事实”既不显而易见，也不是事实。相反，United States 和 Canada 更为丰厚的财富，代表着相对于 Europeans 抵达时当地状况的一次鲜明的 reversal of fortune。

他们认为，之所以发生 reversal of fortune，是因为 colonists 更有可能在 Americas 的 densely populated（wealthiest）areas 中 install extractive institutions，因为在那里，他们可以依靠 forced indigenous labor；而在 sparsely populated areas 中则 install inclusive institutions，因为那里没有 forced labor supply，需要让 settlers 有动力成为 productive。这种 institutions 上的 difference 最终导致 tropics 的 stagnation，以及 temperate regions 中更快的 growth。

不过，这似乎并不是一个非常 definitive 的 refutation。Koreas 的 example 只表明，_当 geography 和 culture 被 held constant 时_，institutions 才 dominate（而且，这个 example 经过 cherry-picked，达到了 maximally extreme 的程度）。Americas 的 example 更有力，但我能想象出 alternative models。例如，也许在 industrial revolution 之前，处于 tropical 地带是一种 advantage，因为这意味着 weather 没那么致命；但在那之后却成了 disadvantage，因为这意味着你的 comparative advantage 是 farming，而不是 industry，而 farming-specialized economies 的 grow 速度较慢，因为[它们不会 develop compounding technical knowledge](https://www.benkuhn.net/globalization-paradox)。

Americas 的 reversal of fortune 这一 example 也没有 address 更 complex 的 geographical factors，例如 iron、coal 或 navigable rivers 是 industrial growth 的必要条件。在 medium term，这种形式的 geography 显然似乎非常重要；England 同时富有这三者，并非巧合。在 long term，它们似乎可能变得不那么重要，因为重要的 resources 的 types 会改变。（如今，如果你是一个没有 iron 或 coal deposits 的 developing country，你可以与其他 countries 进行 trade 来获取它们；如果你没有 navigable rivers，你可以 build railroads 或 highways。）但这两种办法都不是 perfect substitute，因此，geography 很重要这一观点似乎仍有充分理由。

另一方面，如果你试图 _encourage_ development，而不只是理解它，那么支持 geography 的 argument 究竟有多 actionable，就不清楚了。即便 geography 很重要，改变它也并不是特别 tractable！我唯一能想到的 implication，也许是 liberalizing immigration 变得更重要，好让人们更容易从 resource-poor areas 迁往 resource-rich areas。

* * *

如果属实，_Why Nations Fail_ 中的 claims 对（global-poverty-focused）effective altruism 有着巨大的 implications。Global poverty EA 目前 focuses on scaling up 那些具有强有力的 randomized controlled trials evidence base 的 development aid interventions。但如果你接受 _Why Nations Fail_ 的 argument，那么，只要这个 problem 是 tractable，你大概就应该更倾向于让具有 extractive institutions 的 countries 朝 inclusivity 迈进。

在我看来，这确实似乎是 tractable。例如，在我居住的 Senegal 最近的 election 中，incumbent president 拥有巨大的 spending advantage，广泛流传的说法是，这是因为他将 public funds 转用于自己的 campaign。（并非巧合的是，许多知名 opposition politicians 目前因 misuse of public funds 而身陷囹圄——这是同一位 incumbent president 发起 investigation 的结果。）目前 Senegalese media 不愿 investigate，因为 capital 的 media 大多由 government 高层人士拥有。资助 Senegal 的 independent investigative media 的发展，似乎是朝着 improving Senegalese political institutions 迈出的一个（相对）直接的步骤。

当然，这一看法仅仅基于我和 Senegalese 同事几个小时的交谈，所以它肯定不是 optimal。也许它甚至会让情况变得更糟！但希望它能说明一个 general point：我们知道 inclusive institutions 是什么样子，因此，努力朝那个方向迈进，主要是一个面向已知 goal 的 strategy 和 execution 问题，而不是要解开什么谜团。

* * *

_Why Nations Fail_ 提供了一个令人信服的 theory，具有清晰的 implications，并得到许多 examples 的支持，其中几个（如 Koreas 或 Americas 的 examples）甚至具有某种“control group”。但随着这本书不断展开，它在多大程度上依赖 anecdotes 而非 studies，变得越来越显眼。遗憾的是，这可能是因为 empirical 方面的状况实在是_糟糕透顶_。

Acemoglu 和 Robinson 可能不同意这个 claim，因为早在撰写 _Why Nations Fail_ 之前，他们就已经长期在 economics journals 上发表关于 institutions 的 empirical studies。但他们也选择不在书中介绍其中大部分 empirical research，而我能理解原因：我找不到任何一项看起来真正 defensible 的 study。

我看到的一些 problems 很愚蠢，而且可以避免，例如[将 Likert scales 当作 continuous 而不是 leveled-categorical 来 treating](https://growthecon.com/blog/the-skeptics-guide-to-institutions-part-1/)，或[明显 violating instrumental variables 的 assumptions](https://growthecon.com/blog/the-skeptics-guide-to-institutions-part-2/)。但据我所能判断，更大的 issues 是_任何_试图 empirically study growth 的 causes 的尝试都绕不开的核心问题：

-   sample size 很小。总共只有 200 个 countries，其中许多太小，或太特殊，无法提供有用信息。（对于某些 countries，我们确实有数百年的 growth data，但同一个 country 不同年份的 growth 并不是 independent data points。）“effective” sample size 甚至更低，因为“error term”往往是 spatially correlated，而[大多数 studies 并未对此 properly account for，从而 causing widespread p-value inflation](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2019/06/beware-of-persistence-studies.html)。

-   当你在 analyzing countries 的 growth 时，一切都是 [endogenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogeneity_%28econometrics%29#Dynamic_models)。你在 year 1 的 institutions 会 affect 你在 year 2 的 growth，后者会 affects 在 year 3 谁获得更多 power，而这又会 affects 你的 institutions 在 year 4 如何 change。如果你 measure 出 growth 与 institutions 之间的 correlation，也完全可能是 fast growth causes better institutions，而不是反过来。

-   Institutions 和 growth 有着非常 complicated 的 causal structure——两者都有大量 causes 和 effects。在这种情况下，_尤其_是 sample size 为 200 时，[correlational studies 从来都不起作用](https://www.benkuhn.net/causreg)。即便你试图 control for 所有 confounders，也仍然有太多微妙的地方可能出错。

-   通常，你可能会尝试使用更花哨的 causal inference technique 来绕过这个问题，例如寻找 [instrumental variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_variables_estimation)——即会 affect 一个 state 的 institutions、但又不与它们 correlated 的 random events。例如，一篇著名 paper 曾尝试利用 colonized countries 中的 European mortality rates 来做到这一点（其 theory 是，在有更多 Europeans immigrated 的 countries 中，colonists 较不可能 install extractive institutions）。

    但 instrumental variables 的一个关键 assumption 是，instrument 只会通过它对你已 measured 的其他事物（institutions 或其他 covariates）的 effect，来 affects outcome（growth）。换句话说，要获得 valid estimate，你就需要 measure settler mortality 可能 affect growth rates 的_每一种可能方式_。既然 measuring 一切是不可能的，把它们全部放进一个只有 200 个 data points 的 model 也不可能，那么 instrumental variables 就成了双重的不可能。（这并未阻止各位 economists 去尝试——显然做得很糟。）

这些 problems 让我几乎不给关于 institutions 和 growth 的 empirical literature 任何 weight，甚至_在_读到具体 papers 的具体 problems _之前_就是如此。但它们同样适用于任何试图寻找 economic growth 的 causes 的尝试。

* * *

那么，这让我们处于什么境地？

一方面，我们面临一个很大的 methodological gap。上述 problems 使我们相对更难真正理解什么对 development 重要。但这并不意味着，尝试在这个问题上取得 intellectual progress 是毫无希望的。

事实上，我能想象出一些 analyses，它们_会_让我更有信心认为 institutional hypothesis 是正确的——例如，一份包含许多不同 growth episodes 的 case studies 的 systematic review，或者涵盖许多不同 institutional changes、而你预期这些 changes 会 affect growth 的 systematic review。我不确定，为什么 Acemoglu 和 Robinson 的 empirical work 反而 focused on 那些看起来 badly-validated 的 instrumental-variables models——也许因为它们更容易做（或更容易在 top journals 上 publish）？但考虑到 noise 的数量以及有限的 sample size，case studies 似乎比那些 assumptions 存疑的花哨 models 更有可能提供有用信息。

即便没有无懈可击的 studies 支撑，许多 experts 仍然就某件事达成一致，这也并非没有先例。一个现实中的榜样可以是 laws of supply and demand——大多数 economists 都相信它们，尽管在许多 real-world cases 中，要 verify 它们其实很棘手，例如 [minimum wage laws](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/) 或 [housing](https://48hills.org/2016/09/palo-alto-and-the-tech-shop-of-horrors/)。甚至还可以是 [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis)，许多 effective altruists 似乎都 endorse 它，而这种认同远远超出了我们 empirically test 它的能力。

但在 poverty reduction 的问题上，effective altruism community 似乎并没有真正寻找 randomized controlled trials 以外的任何 methodology。事实上，这是对 global-poverty-focused effective altruism 的一项主要 criticism（而且不常得到回应），Acemoglu 本人也在[他于 _Boston Review_ 发表的对 effective altruism 的 critique](http://bostonreview.net/forum/logic-effective-altruism/daron-acemoglu-response-effective-altruism) 中提出了这一点：

> 对一个 donated dollar 的 social value 进行 $$P$$recise measurement，可能是不可能的。……如果如一些 economists 和 political scientists 所言，political and economic institutions 的 changes 对 long-run economic growth 至关重要，那么 Amnesty 这样的 watchdog organizations 对 transforming dysfunctional regimes 可能是不可或缺的。Effective altruists（还？）没有看到这些更具 political 性质的 organizations 的重要性。如果这种 narrow focus 持续下去，它可能会使 public 和 media 的 priorities 偏离那些 underpinning economic development 的 political factors。

[Angus Deaton](http://bostonreview.net/forum/logic-effective-altruism/angus-deaton-response-effective-altruism) 也抱有同样的担忧：

> 更广泛地说，关于 development effectiveness、关于“什么起作用”的 evidence，主要来自最近这一波 randomized experiments。……这些 experiments 怎么会错呢？因为它们只考虑 interventions 的 immediate effects，而不考虑这些 interventions 所处的 contexts。最重要的是，它们也无法说明那些 wide-ranging unintended consequences。
> 
> 无论这看起来多么 counterintuitive，……$$d$$evelopment 既不是 financial problem，也不是 technical problem，而是 political problem，而 aid industry 往往会让 politics 变得更糟。

Peter Singer 的回应（正如我[以前论证过的](https://www.benkuhn.net/slippery)，这个回应没有切中要点）：

> 如果 large-scale reform 能提供一些 reducing poverty 的前景，那么 effective altruists 就会尝试 assess 它 doing good 的 chance；如果这种 action 的 expected value 高于更 limited 的 interventions 的 expected value，他们就会 advocate 为这些 large-scale reforms 而 working。

Peter Singer 写下这段话已经四年了，而我并没有看到多少迹象表明这个 prediction 正在成真。处于领先地位的 EA global poverty charity evaluator GiveWell [推辞了](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/criteria#Why_the_focus_on_direct_aid_rather_than_addressing_root_causes)，因为 institution-focused interventions 不会足够 transparent：

> 在我们看来，Root-causes-based approaches 属于 speculative 且 long-term 的 undertakings，最适合 highly engaged donors（如上文所述）。

但 EA community 似乎并没有产生多少这样的“highly engaged donors”，[Aceso Under Glass 向 Tostan 捐赠](https://acesounderglass.com/2016/12/01/5167/)或许是个例外。（GiveWell 自身可能正在[成为其中之一](https://blog.givewell.org/2019/02/07/how-givewells-research-is-evolving/)。我希望这能改变现状！）相反，大多数 global-poverty-focused effective altruists 似乎都很乐意遵循 GiveWell 的 recommended charities。也许这是因为所有 long-termists 都在致力于 mitigating existential risks。但对于那些仍然认同将 global poverty 作为 cause area 的人来说，_Why Nations Fail_ 提供了一个有意思的 alternate paradigm 的 example。

## 原文

Within the effective altruism community, people often talk about “long-termist” vs “short-termist” worldviews. The official distinction between the two is that short-termists prioritize problems by how they affect people alive today, while long-termists prioritize problems by how they could affect humanity’s entire future trajectory. In practice, people usually treat this as synonymous with prioritizing either [existential risk reduction](https://concepts.effectivealtruism.org/concepts/existential-risks/) (if long-termist), or scaling up proven global health interventions (if short-termist).

It’s a bit surprising that each worldview should have exactly one favorite cause area, though. Couldn’t you have short-termist work on existential risk, or long-termist work on global poverty? In reality, these supposedly discrete worldviews seem more like correlated clusters of various different beliefs:

| “Short-termist” | “Long-termist” |
| --- | --- |
| High [time discount rate](https://concepts.effectivealtruism.org/concepts/discounting-the-future/) | Low or no discount rate |
| Prefers highly robust “[outside view](https://wiki.lesswrong.com/wiki/Outside_view)” type arguments | More open to “inside view” that this case might be different |
| Extrapolates existing effects or trends | Reasons about the future from first principles |
| Skeptical of _prima facie_ bizarre claims | Takes weird-sounding ideas more seriously |
| Focuses on fixing known, concrete problems | Focuses on preventing hypothetical, nebulous risks |
| Fast [feedback loops](https://www.jefftk.com/p/why-global-poverty) are critical to making progress | Fast feedback is helpful, but not the most important thing |

It’s understandable why some of these are correlated, but there must be a lot of people who fall through the cracks between the clusters. What if you share short-termists’ skepticism of weird claims and hypothetical risks, but you’re willing to focus on first-principles reasoning and work on a long time scale?

You’d still want to focus on something that’s a problem today, so you’d probably want to work on global poverty. But you’d dismiss [GiveWell’s top charities](https://www.givewell.org/) as treating a symptom and not a cause. Why do these countries need charity in the first place? South Korea used to be just as poor as anywhere in Africa, but today it’s incredibly prosperous, while sub-Saharan Africa has made way less progress. If we could move the lowest-growth countries from their current trajectory onto South Korea’s, we’d have done much more than any single malaria-eradication campaign could.

If that were your worldview, you’d really enjoy [Why Nations Fail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Nations_Fail), one of the best attempts I’ve seen at getting a first-principles understanding of what affects countries’ long-term economic growth.

* * *

First of all, what _is_ economic growth? It’s when people produce more (or more valuable) stuff with the same effort.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/#fn:1) The first and least controversial point in _Why Nations Fail_ is that for a nation to keep on doing more with less, its individual citizens need to be incentivized to become more productive. In particular, the state should _not_ set up systems where, whenever someone gets more productive, other people come and take away the extra stuff they produced. Those systems are what the authors call _extractive economic institutions,_ and they include things like slavery, serfdom, indentured servitude, roving bandits, guilds, collectivized agriculture, nationalization of private assets, officials requiring bribes, kangaroo courts, banana republics, and other \[animal or vegetable\] \[civic institution\].

You might think you could grow your economy under extractive institutions by forcing people to become more productive even if they won’t get to keep the surplus. This does often work in the short term (and the short term can last a surprisingly long time)—for instance, Soviet Russia grew at about 5% annually from 1930-1970,[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/wnf/#fn:2) despite having extremely extractive institutions. But that only worked because the growth started from a low base. Soviet Russia’s economic institutions, while horrible, were arguably still less extractive than the serfdom that preceded them. Plus, the growth came mainly from “easy wins” like people switching from horrifically inefficient farming to horrifically inefficient industry.

In fact, growth under extractive institutions can _only_ come from easy wins—easy enough that they’re obvious to whoever has the job of forcing other people to be more productive. (Nobody is going to generate their own productivity-boosting ideas, because they have no incentive to.) Any half-decent Soviet central planner could have told you that industry would be more productive than farming, so they were able to force peasants to make the switch. But nobody was good enough at central planning to fix all the ways their industry was inefficient—let alone come up with new inventions or technologies as fast as the US was. So once all the low-hanging fruit was plucked, circa 1980, the Soviet Union ran into a productivity wall and collapsed.

What’s more, even if an innovation _is_ obvious to the extractive elite, they might actively try to crush it because it threatens their hold on power. For instance, in 19th-century Europe, it was obvious that factories and railroads could be a huge productivity improvement, but the extractive rulers of peripheral Europe wanted none of them. Here’s Austria:

> When a plan to build a northern railway was put before \[Emperor\] Francis I, he replied, “No, no, I will have nothing to do with it, lest the revolution might come into the country.”

And here’s Russian Finance Minister (1823-44) Count Igor Kankrin:

> \[R\]ailways do not always result from natural necessity, but are more an object of artificial need or luxury. They encourage unnecessary travel from place to place, which is entirely typical of our time.

It might seem cartoonish for a ruler to ban technology out of fear of TEH REVOLUTIONZ—until you remember how many extractive states today, like Ethiopia and China, have resisted the Internet for similar reasons.

* * *

The next part of _Why Nations Fail_ asks: if economic institutions explain long-term growth, what explains long-term economic institutions? The answer, maybe obviously, is politics.

Extractive economic institutions tend to persist more when social power is concentrated in a small elite (“extractive political institutions”) that can use this power to take the economic surplus for themselves—or just destroy it if it looks scary and potentially destabilizing. They persist less when social power is distributed widely in the population (“inclusive political institutions”) because in that case the people being extracted will often have enough political power to fight back against the extraction.

For instance, in the early 1900s in the United States, various industries (steel, oil, banking, chemicals, farming tools, etc.) ended up under the control of extractive monopolists who charged too much, paid too little, pocketed the difference, and spent the difference on [gold-plated toilets](https://www.washingtonpost.com/lifestyle/travel/escapes-evergreen-mansion-baltimores-hidden-gilded-age-gem/2012/03/05/gIQAjGLEES_story.html?utm_term=.56d51f35ebba). But even the world’s fanciest toilets couldn’t defeat the mass populist movement that rose up to oppose the monopolies (because the US was sufficiently pluralistic that mass populist movements were very powerful), and so they were eventually forcibly broken up and regulated.

In states with less pluralistic politics, the elites can get away with this kind of extraction. For instance, when the British colonized Sierra Leone, they set up monopoly cocoa and coffee purchasing boards that bought low from the farmers, sold high on international markets, and pocketed the margin. After independence, instead of dismantling the boards, Sierra Leone’s presidents turned them into a slush fund, driving the margin up to 90%.

Sierra Leone illustrates another sad fact about extractive economic institutions, which is that they persist surprisingly much across nominally different political regimes. What happened in Sierra Leone happened nearly everywhere in sub-Saharan Africa: the colonists were ousted by a revolutionary leader, who quickly realized that the colonists’ giant money-printing machine was still in place, and decided that maybe the _entire_ colonial apparatus didn’t need to be smashed. And of course, in order to keep their hold on the economic surplus, the new “democratic” government quickly resorted to repression and violence. (_Why Nations Fail_ is very clear that what matters is _de facto_, not _de jure_, democracy, and that confusing these two will lead to very wrong conclusions.)

Over long timescales, then, it seems like a mix of inclusive and extractive institutions is unstable. If institutions are inclusive enough, then any group that deviates and tries to institute extractive norms will get shut down by the rest of society. If institutions _aren’t_ inclusive enough, one of those groups will eventually succeed, and then be incentivized to consolidate their power even further until the society is totally extractive.

* * *

The most glaring exception to this rule—which many reviewers have pointed out—is China since Deng Xiaoping, which has relatively inclusive economic institutions, but extremely authoritarian politics, for the last 40 years. Acemoglu and Robinson acknowledge this, but claim, basically, that China is not in a stable equilibrium:

> All the same, \[Chinese\] growth will run out of steam unless extractive political institutions make way for inclusive institutions. As long as political institutions remain extractive, growth will be inherently limited, as it has been in all other similar cases.

This is a kind of weak explanation as written because they don’t even mention the _mechanism_ by which it will be limited, but with some charity I think you can construct a decent argument. Concretely, it seems like the _Why Nations Fail_ model predicts that China’s current, inclusive economic institutions are not sustainable without inclusive political institutions to support them. In the current regime, the Chinese political elite’s local incentives are to extract as much of the economy’s surplus as possible, because they can; it’s not sustainable to just hope they keep being altruistic enough to not do that. At some point, unless China’s political institutions give real power to a broad majority of people, the elites will give in to temptation and start taking all the economic surplus for themselves.

(Note by the way that China’s recent economic slowdown does _not_ confirm this model’s predictions, unless you think the slowdown was caused by the political elite becoming more economically extractive, which doesn’t seem like the case.)

If you’re worried about having powerful authoritarian nations hanging around, this is a comforting model to have. On the other hand, China has been “out of equilibrium” for almost half a century, and _Why Nations Fail_ doesn’t offer any framework for thinking about how long these disequilibria can last.

* * *

The strongest claim in _Why Nations Fail_, which is mostly left annoyingly implicit, is that institutions are _the dominant factor_ in shaping economic growth, over long enough time horizons. Many of their reviewers disputed this.

To be fair, a lot of those reviewers seemed to have rounded off this claim to “institutions are _always_ the dominant factor,” and then complained about the book not explaining China (despite the half-chapter explaining that China is out of equilibrium), or not sufficiently explaining Bolivia vs Vietnam over the last 30 years (if China can be out of equilibrium for 40 years, so can other countries), or similar. But even over long periods of time, it’s reasonable to ask whether institutions were really the most important possible thing.

The most obvious alternative candidate is geography (including natural resources). _Why Nations Fail_ makes some attempt to argue that geography isn’t sufficiently explanatory, citing (a) the divergence between North and South Korea, and (b) that after European colonization of the Americas, the correlation between latitude and productivity went from negative (tropics more productive) to positive (temperate regions more productive):

> As we saw in the last chapter, at the time of the conquest of the Americas by Columbus, the \[tropical\] areas… held the great Aztec and Inca civilizations…. In sharp contrast… the \[modern\] United States, Canada, Argentina, and Chile, were mostly inhabited by Stone Age civilizations lacking these technologies. The tropics in the Americas were thus much richer than the temperate zones, suggesting that the “obvious fact” of tropical poverty is neither obvious nor a fact. Instead, the greater riches in the United States and Canada represent a stark reversal of fortune relative to what was there when the Europeans arrived.

The reversal of fortune happened, they argue, because colonists were more likely to install extractive institutions in the densely populated (wealthiest) areas of the Americas, where they could rely on forced indigenous labor; and inclusive institutions in the sparsely populated areas where there was no forced labor supply and settlers needed to be incentivized to be productive. This difference in institutions eventually led to stagnation in the tropics and faster growth in the temperate regions.

This doesn’t seem like a very definitive refutation, though. The Koreas example only shows that institutions dominate _when geography and culture are held constant_ (plus, it’s cherry-picked to be maximally extreme). The example of the Americas is stronger, but I can imagine alternative models. For instance, maybe being tropical was an advantage before the industrial revolution because it meant less lethal weather; but a disadvantage afterwards because it meant that your comparative advantage was farming, rather than industry, and farming-specialized economies grow less quickly because [they don’t develop compounding technical knowledge](https://www.benkuhn.net/globalization-paradox).

The example of the Americas’ reversal of fortune also doesn’t address more complex geographical factors, like that iron, coal or navigable rivers are necessary for industrial growth. In the medium term, geography of this form seems obviously very important; it’s no coincidence that England was rich in all three. In the long term, it seems like they might become less important, because the types of resources that are important will change. (Today if you’re a developing country with no iron or coal deposits, you can trade with other countries to get them; if you don’t have navigable rivers, you can build railroads or highways.) But neither of those is a perfect substitute, so it seems like there’s still a strong case for geography mattering.

On the other hand, if you’re trying to _encourage_ development, not just understand it, it’s not clear how actionable the pro-geography argument is. Even if geography is important, it’s not particularly tractable to change! The only implication I can think of is maybe that liberalizing immigration becomes more important, so that it’s easier for people to move from resource-poor to resource-rich areas.

* * *

If true, the claims in _Why Nations Fail_ have huge implications for (global-poverty-focused) effective altruism. Global poverty EA currently focuses on scaling up development aid interventions that have a strong evidence base of randomized controlled trials. But if you buy _Why Nations Fail_’s argument, you should probably prefer to take countries with extractive institutions and move them towards inclusivity, if that problem is tractable.

To me, it does seem tractable. For instance, in the recent election in Senegal, where I live, the incumbent president had a huge spending advantage, which was widely rumored to be because he diverted public funds to his campaign. (Not coincidentally, many prominent opposition politicians are currently in jail for misuse of public funds—the result of an investigation initiated by the same incumbent president.) The current Senegalese media is reluctant to investigate because the media in the capital are largely owned by people high up in government. Sponsoring the development of independent investigative media in Senegal seems like a (relatively) straightforward step towards improving Senegalese political institutions.

Of course, this take is only based on a couple hours of conversation with my Senegalese coworkers, so it’s definitely not optimal. Maybe it would even make things worse! But hopefully it illustrates the general point, which is that we know what inclusive institutions look like, so trying to move things in that direction is mostly a question of strategy and execution towards a known goal, not of solving some sort of mystery.

* * *

_Why Nations Fail_ provides a compelling theory, with clear implications, supported by many examples, a few of which (like the Koreas or Americas examples) even had some kind of “control group.” But as the book goes on, it becomes more and more glaring how much it relies on anecdotes, not studies. Unfortunately, that’s probably because the empirical situation is _abysmal_.

Acemoglu and Robinson might not agree with that claim, since they were publishing empirical studies of institutions in economics journals for a long time before they wrote _Why Nations Fail_. But they also chose not to cover most of that empirical research in the book, and I can see why: I couldn’t find a single study that seemed really defensible.

Some of the problems I saw were silly and avoidable, like [treating Likert scales as continuous instead of leveled-categorical](https://growthecon.com/blog/the-skeptics-guide-to-institutions-part-1/) or [obviously violating the assumptions of instrumental variables](https://growthecon.com/blog/the-skeptics-guide-to-institutions-part-2/). But as far as I could tell, the bigger issues were core to _any_ attempt to empirically study the causes of growth:

-   The sample size is tiny. There are only 200 countries, many of which are too small or too weird to be informative. (We do have hundreds of years of growth data for some countries, but different years of growth for the same country aren’t independent data points.) The “effective” sample size is even lower because the “error term” is often spatially correlated, which [most studies do not properly account for, causing widespread p-value inflation](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2019/06/beware-of-persistence-studies.html).

-   When you’re analyzing countries’ growth, everything is [endogenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogeneity_%28econometrics%29#Dynamic_models). Your institutions in year 1 affect your growth in year 2, which affects who gets more power in year 3, which affects how your institutions change in year 4. If you measure a correlation between growth and institutions, it could just as easily be that fast growth causes better institutions, not the other way around.

-   Institutions and growth have a really complicated causal structure—both of them have a huge number of causes and effects. In situations like that, and _especially_ with a sample size of 200, [correlational studies never work](https://www.benkuhn.net/causreg). Even if you try to control for all the confounders, there are just too many subtle things that can go wrong.

-   Ordinarily you might try to get around that by using a fancier causal inference technique, like finding [instrumental variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_variables_estimation)—random events that affect a state’s institutions, but aren’t correlated with them. For example, one famous paper tried to do this with European mortality rates in colonized countries (the theory being that colonists were less likely to install extractive institutions in countries where more Europeans had immigrated).

    But a key assumption of instrumental variables is that the instrument only affects the outcome (growth) via its effect on other things you’ve measured (institutions or other covariates). In other words, to get a valid estimate, you’d need to measure _every possible way_ that settler mortality could affect growth rates. Since measuring everything is impossible, and throwing it all into a model with only 200 data points is also impossible, that makes instrumental variables double-impossible. (Which didn’t prevent various economists from trying—apparently badly.)

These problems make me put almost zero weight on the empirical literature for institutions and growth, even _before_ reading about the specific problems with specific papers. But they apply equally well to any attempt to find the causes of economic growth.

* * *

So where does that leave us?

Partly, it leaves us with a big methodological gap. The problems above make it relatively harder to get to a true understanding of what matters for development. But it doesn’t mean it’s hopeless to try and make intellectual progress on the question.

Indeed, I can imagine analyses that _would_ make me more confident that the institutional hypothesis is correct—for instance, a systematic review with case studies of many different growth episodes, or many different institutional changes that you’d expect to affect growth. I’m not sure why Acemoglu and Robinson’s empirical work focused instead on what looks like badly-validated instrumental-variables models—maybe because they were easier to do (or easier to publish in top journals)? But given the amount of noise and the limited sample size, case studies seem much more likely to be informative than fancy models with questionable assumptions.

Nor is it unprecedented for lots of experts to agree on something even without airtight studies backing it up. A real-world role model could be something like the laws of supply and demand—which most economists believe in, even though they turn out to be tricky to verify in many real-world cases, like [minimum wage laws](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/) or [housing](https://48hills.org/2016/09/palo-alto-and-the-tech-shop-of-horrors/). Or even like the [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis), which many effective altruists seem to endorse, far beyond our ability to empirically test it.

But on questions of poverty reduction, the effective altruism community doesn’t seem to be really looking for any methodology other than randomized controlled trials. In fact, this is a major (and not often addressed) criticism of global-poverty-focused effective altruism, including from Acemoglu himself in [his _Boston Review_ critique of effective altruism](http://bostonreview.net/forum/logic-effective-altruism/daron-acemoglu-response-effective-altruism):

> \[P\]recise measurement of the social value of a donated dollar may be impossible. … If, as some economists and political scientists suggest, changes in political and economic institutions are critical for long-run economic growth, then watchdog organizations such as Amnesty may be essential for transforming dysfunctional regimes. Effective altruists don’t (yet?) see the importance of these more political organizations. If this narrow focus continues, it may divert public and media priorities from political factors underpinning economic development.

His concern was shared by [Angus Deaton](http://bostonreview.net/forum/logic-effective-altruism/angus-deaton-response-effective-altruism):

> More broadly, the evidence for development effectiveness, for “what works,” mostly comes from the recent wave of randomized experiments. … How can those experiments be wrong? Because they consider only the immediate effects of the interventions, not the contexts in which they are set. Nor, most importantly, can they say anything about the wide-ranging unintended consequences.
> 
> However counterintuitive it may seem, … \[d\]evelopment is neither a financial nor a technical problem but a political problem, and the aid industry often makes the politics worse.

Peter Singer’s response (which, as I’ve [argued before](https://www.benkuhn.net/slippery), misses the point):

> \[I\]f large-scale reform offers some prospect of reducing poverty, then effective altruists will try to assess its chance of doing good, and if the expected value of such action is higher than the expected value of more limited interventions, they will advocate working for the large-scale reforms.

It’s been four years since Peter Singer wrote that, and I don’t see much sign of the prediction coming true. The leading EA global poverty charity evaluator, GiveWell, [begged off](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/criteria#Why_the_focus_on_direct_aid_rather_than_addressing_root_causes) because institution-focused interventions wouldn’t be transparent enough:

> Root-causes-based approaches are, in our view, the kind of speculative and long-term undertakings that are best suited to highly engaged donors (as discussed above).

But the EA community doesn’t seem to have produced many such “highly engaged donors,” with the possible exception of [Aceso Under Glass giving to Tostan](https://acesounderglass.com/2016/12/01/5167/). (GiveWell itself may be in the process of [becoming one](https://blog.givewell.org/2019/02/07/how-givewells-research-is-evolving/). I hope that changes this!) Instead, most global-poverty-focused effective altruists seem happy to follow GiveWell’s recommended charities. Maybe that’s because all the long-termists are working on mitigating existential risks. But for any who are still sympathetic to global poverty as a cause area, _Why Nations Fail_ provides an interesting example of an alternate paradigm.
