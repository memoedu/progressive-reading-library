# Hip tech, useful tech

---

## 正文

Fast Company 上有一篇有趣的文章，是 Hugh Whalan 的 [“面向 Developing World 的 Hip Gadgets 不会 Solve Global Poverty：别再 Making 它们了”](http://www.fastcoexist.com/3026537/hip-gadgets-arent-going-to-solve-global-poverty-stop-making-them)：

> 每次看到一个 gimmicky product 被誉为能够缓解 world poverty 的“下一件大事”，我都恨不得抓狂。market 上已经有足够多的 products，可以 change 地球上每一个 poor consumer 的 lives。Poor consumers 不需要又一个在 U.S. developed 的 slick product，他们只是需要一个能 reaches 他们所在村庄的 product。让我解释一下。
> 
> 许多 entrepreneurs（incorrectly）认为，最大的 challenge 其实是 making 这个 product。他们如此得意，因为自己 invented 了一个 widget，它比 market 上其他 widgets 更 efficient、具有更 lower cost，或者更花哨。既然有 1 bazillion 人的 life 会因为 getting access to 他们的 widget 而得到 immeasurably improved，而且他们的 widget 又是最好的，那么 success 就是板上钉钉的事了。
> 
> …
> 
> 一个 extreme example 是 [Playpump](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundabout_PlayPump)（如今已停止运营），那是一种价格为 \$14,000 to \$20,000 的 merry-go-round，孩子们在上面玩耍时，它就会 pumped water。2010 年，Laura Bush 和 Bill Clinton 宣布了一笔 \$16.4 million 的 grant，用于在 Africa build 数百个 Playpumps，但这个 organization [很难接近最初 hype 所描绘的情景](http://www.pbs.org/frontlineworld/stories/southernafrica904/video_index.html)，因为正如[一份 Mozambique government report 所指出的](http://www-tc.pbs.org/frontlineworld/stories/southernafrica904/flash/pdf/mozambique_report.pdf)：孩子们并没有如预期那样在 merry-go-rounds 上玩耍，而成年人觉得它很难 operate；pumps 每次 out of commission 的时间长达 17 months；maintenance 更是一场真正的灾难。
> 
> 我们对 products 的热爱还有一个较近的 example，即 [Soccket](http://www.unchartedplay.com/)，这家 company made 了一种 soccer ball，能够 harnesses kinetic energy 来 generate light。很酷，对吧？这家 company 获得了大量媒体报道（包括这里的 [Co.Exist](http://www.fastcoexist.com/1681514/this-soccer-ball-generates-energy-while-you-play-and-you-can-buy-it-now)），并且已经[从 crowdfunding sites raised 了近 $600,000](http://www.fundable.com/unchartedplay?utm_source=newsletter)。就连 Obama 和 Bill Clinton 都很喜欢它。它无疑是一件精巧的 technology。
> 
> 问题呢？它的 cost 为 \$60 per Soccket, it is the most expensive six-watt light on the market. D.Light, for example, produces [a high-quality study light with a two-year warranty and similar functionality that retails for $10](http://www.fastcoexist.com/1678042/dlight-launches-an-ultra-cheap-solar-lantern-for-studying)。Soccket 也肯定是 customer 这辈子能见到的最 expensive 的 soccer ball，而这些 customer 想必是没有 access to electricity 的孩子。（对于 Soccket 的 approach 所引发的 concerns，更 in-depth 的 analysis [可以在这里找到](http://stayingfortea.org/2012/06/23/deflating-the-soccket-ball/?relatedposts_exclude=714)。）

有趣的是，Soccket 其实出自 Harvard 的一门课，[ES 20：如何 Create Things 并让它们发挥作用](http://thelaboratory.harvard.edu/courses/es20/)，这门课简直是一座生产类似 glamorous gadgets 的[名副其实的工厂](http://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/04/science-in-service/)：能夹进特制 running shoes、以获取 kinetic energy 的 electric chargers；在 exposed to hazards 时会 changes color 的 paint；经过 genetically modified、呈 bottle-shaped 的 coconuts。我怀疑，其中大多数都会在 translational phase 遇到类似的问题。对于一门如此深入 startup scene 的课来说，他们竟如此彻底地忽视 Paul Graham 的告诫，实在令人惊讶：[“make 人们想要的东西。”](http://paulgraham.com/good.html)

不过话说回来，我想他们确实 make 了 Obama 和 Bill Clinton 想要的东西。也许到了某个时候，那些 celebrity endorsers 会发现，这类东西实际上并不 work；到那时，世上像 Soccket 和 PlayPump 这样的 creators 就无法再沿着如此诱人、但最终 horrendously misaligned 的 incentive gradient 向上攀爬了。又或许，我们得依靠更多像 Whalan 这样 effectiveness-minded 的人不断揭穿他们，直到他们弄明白真正的问题是什么。

无论如何，对 Soccket 的 skepticism 是 well-justified 的，将其与 PlayPumps 的惨败作 comparison 也很 apt。但我认为，这一 comparison 也凸显了 Whalan 的 analysis 没有说到点子上的一个地方。即使 PlayPumps 的 maintenance worries 奇迹般地消失，孩子们仍然不会玩它们，家长们也仍然会觉得它们 operate 起来 uselessly cumbersome。我们尽可以随意 distribute Socckets，但它们的 cost 仍然会是 $60。其 value proposition 根本就不存在。问题不在于 logistics；而在于这些都是 _bad ideas_。

Whalan 得出的 conclusion 是，真正重要的东西根本不是 technological 的：

> 值得庆幸的是，越来越多的 companies 开始专注于一项更大、却远没有那么光鲜的任务：向 poor consumers delivering 平凡却有用的东西。例如，Honey Care Africa 正在 East Africa building 一条 integrated honey production value chain。它无所不包，从 educating smallholder beekeepers，到充当他们购买 inputs 的 buying agent，再到为 honey providing financing、distribution 和 sales support。如今，Honey Care 的 network 中有 15,000 名 beekeepers，他们的 household income 正在 increase，幅度高达 50%。

但我认为，仅仅因为一群 Americans 宁愿 engineer 奇形怪状的 coconuts，也不愿让 global poor 得到购买它们的钱，就全盘忽视 tech 的 potential impact，是一个错误。早在 1960s，tech innovation 就已经[让数百万人免于 food shortages](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution)。近些年来，Africa 强大的 cell phone infrastructure 又[使 economies 发生了 radically changed](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-pesa)。我满怀希望，认为今天推动 educational technology 和更广泛的 Internet access 的努力，也会产生同样 transformative 的 effects。

Norman Borlaug 和 M-PESA 与 light-up soccer balls 的区别，或许在于前两者有一个共同点：他们拥有真正从事 development 的 experience。Borlaug 在 Mexico 工作了 10 years；那些 strains of wheat 之所以被 imported 到 India，是因为 Indian government——而不是某些随便什么 American college students——认为它们会有帮助。M-PESA 的 idea 源自一个观察：citizens 正在 exchanging cell phone airtime，而不是 currency；它并不是靠叫一些 design students 去 remake Kenya 的 commerce 而产生的。Technical solutions 可以有所帮助——糟糕的是，许多人抱着 looking for a problem 的 attitude。

在最近的一次 [Developers For Development](http://harvardd4d.wix.com/home) 聚会上，我们正在观看的一场演讲的 speaker 告诉了我们 international development 的 rule #1：_没有捷径_。这话没错，而人们试图强行塞进 developing countries 的许多 tech，都透着对这一 tenet 的误解。大多数 social entrepreneurs 都专注于 glamorous-gadget 这一边。但只要多一点智慧，多一些处理实际问题的 experience，tech 也能着眼长远。

## 原文

An interesting article up at Fast Company, Hugh Whalan’s [“Hip Gadgets For The Developing World Won’t Solve Global Poverty: Stop Making Them”](http://www.fastcoexist.com/3026537/hip-gadgets-arent-going-to-solve-global-poverty-stop-making-them):

> Every time I see a gimmicky product hailed as the “next big thing” to make a dent in world poverty I want to pull my hair out. There are already enough products in the market to change the lives of every single poor consumer on the planet. Poor consumers don’t need another slick product developed in the U.S., they simply need a product that reaches them in their village. Let me explain.
> 
> Many entrepreneurs (incorrectly) think the biggest challenge is actually making the product. They are so pleased they have invented a widget that is more efficient or lower cost or fancier than the other widgets on the market. Since there are 1 bazillion people whose life would be immeasurably improved by getting access to their widget, and their widget is the best, success is a sure thing.
> 
> …
> 
> An extreme example is [Playpump](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundabout_PlayPump) (now defunct) which was a $14,000 to $20,000 merry-go-round that pumped water when children played on it. In 2010, Laura Bush and Bill Clinton announced a $16.4 million grant to build hundreds of Playpumps in Africa, but the organization [struggled to come close to the initial hype](http://www.pbs.org/frontlineworld/stories/southernafrica904/video_index.html) because, as [a Mozambique government report noted](http://www-tc.pbs.org/frontlineworld/stories/southernafrica904/flash/pdf/mozambique_report.pdf): children are not playing as expected on the merry-go-rounds and adults find it difficult to operate; pumps are out of commission for up to 17 months at a time; and maintenance is a real disaster.
> 
> A more recent example of our love of products is [Soccket](http://www.unchartedplay.com/), a company that has made a soccer ball that harnesses kinetic energy to generate light. Cool right? This company has received loads of press (including here at [Co.Exist](http://www.fastcoexist.com/1681514/this-soccer-ball-generates-energy-while-you-play-and-you-can-buy-it-now)), and has [raised nearly $600,000 from crowdfunding sites](http://www.fundable.com/unchartedplay?utm_source=newsletter). Even Obama and Bill Clinton love it. It is undoubtedly a nifty piece of technology.
> 
> The problem? At a cost of $60 per Soccket, it is the most expensive six-watt light on the market. D.Light, for example, produces [a high-quality study light with a two-year warranty and similar functionality that retails for $10](http://www.fastcoexist.com/1678042/dlight-launches-an-ultra-cheap-solar-lantern-for-studying). The Soccket is also certainly the most expensive soccer ball the customer, presumably kids with no access to electricity, is ever going to see. (A more in-depth analysis of the concerns with Soccket’s approach [can be found here](http://stayingfortea.org/2012/06/23/deflating-the-soccket-ball/?relatedposts_exclude=714).)

Funnily enough, the Soccket actually came out of a Harvard class, [ES 20: How to Create Things and Have Them Matter](http://thelaboratory.harvard.edu/courses/es20/), which is a [veritable factory](http://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/04/science-in-service/) of similarly glamorous gadgets: electric chargers that clip into specially-made running shoes to get kinetic energy, paint that changes color when exposed to hazards, genetically modified bottle-shaped coconuts. I suspect that most of them will run into similar problems in the translational phase. For a class that’s so deeply embedded in the startup scene, it’s surprising how badly they ignore Paul Graham’s edict: [“make something people want.”](http://paulgraham.com/good.html)

Then again, I guess they did make something that Obama and Bill Clinton wanted. Maybe at some point the celebrity endorsers will figure out that this kind of thing doesn’t actually work, and then the Soccket and PlayPump creators of the world won’t be able to climb such a tempting but ultimately horrendously misaligned incentive gradient. Or maybe we’ll have to rely on more effectiveness-minded people like Whalan calling them out until they figure out what the real problems are.

At any rate, the skepticism in Soccket is well-justified and the comparison to the PlayPumps debacle is apt. But I think that comparison also highlights an area where Whalan’s analysis misses the mark. Even if PlayPumps’ maintenance worries magically disappeared, kids still wouldn’t have played with them and parents still would have found them uselessly cumbersome to operate. We can distribute Socckets all we want, but they’ll still cost $60. The value proposition is fundamentally not there. The problem isn’t logistics; it’s that these are _bad ideas_.

Whalan draws the conclusion that the really important things aren’t technological at all:

> Thankfully, there are an increasing number of companies focused on the bigger, yet far less glamorous task, of delivering mundane but useful stuff to poor consumers. Honey Care Africa, for example, is building an integrated honey production value chain in East Africa. It does everything from educating smallholder beekeepers, to acting as their buying agent for inputs, to providing financing, distribution, and sales support for the honey. There are now 15,000 beekeepers in Honey Care’s network who are experiencing an increase in household income of up to 50%.

But I think it’s a mistake to ignore the potential impact of tech wholesale just because a bunch of Americans would rather engineer funny-shaped coconuts than get the global poor the money to buy them. As far back as the 1960s, tech innovation [saved millions from food shortages](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution). More recently, Africa’s strong cell phone infrastructure has [radically changed economies](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-pesa). I have high hopes that today’s pushes for educational technology and broader Internet access will have similarly transformative effects.

The common thread that separates Norman Borlaug and M-PESA from light-up soccer balls may be that the former had real experience working in development. Borlaug worked in Mexico for 10 years, and the strains of wheat were imported to India because the Indian government—not some random American college students—thought it would be helpful. The idea for M-PESA was sparked by noticing citizens exchanging cell phone airtime instead of currency, not by telling some design students to go remake commerce in Kenya. Technical solutions can be helpful—the bad part is the attitude that many people have of looking for a problem.

At a recent [Developers For Development](http://harvardd4d.wix.com/home) meeting, the speaker for the talk we were watching informed us of rule #1 for international development: _there are no shortcuts_. That’s true, and much of the tech that people try and force into developing countries reeks of misunderstanding this tenet. Most social entrepreneurs are focused on the glamorous-gadget side. But with a bit more wisdom, and experience with the actual problems, tech can play the long game too.
