# Ortega hypothesis（奥尔特加假说）

---

## 正文

在之前的文章中，我曾[说过](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih)，"Fund people" 能在多大程度上 works，将取决于 scientific talent 的 distribution。想想以下情形：设想在任何时候，都只有屈指可数的 scientists 能够——如果有时间和条件——lead revolutions，足以媲美 Darwin、Einstein 或 Galileo 的 work（诚然，这是一个 extreme case，因为大多数 science 并不是这样的；大多数 science 更加 incremental，也不那么令人难忘）。我最近发现，两位作者持有类似的看法，

Braben 的[书](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom) _Scientific Freedom_ 我曾评论过。他说，尽管大多数 scientists 只要尝试，都可能成为下一个 Einstein，但只有少数人甚至会去尝试：

> 我的主张可能会被视为 elitist，而且只有**那极少数可能被视为 twenty - first century Planck Club 的 putative members 的 scientists**才会感兴趣。这样的理解是不对的。这里的一个主题是，几乎每一位 serious researcher，在 career 的某个时候，都能够迈出那些关键的步伐，而这些步伐可能带来 great discovery，或创造 penetrating new insight。如果他们要取得 progress，届时可能需要拿出巨大的勇气和决心，或许还需要一点运气。当然，在任何一个时候，**准备好抓住这个可能一生只有一次的机会的 researchers，其 proportion 都会非常 small**，因此，如果他们受到阻止，无法这样做，他们所能施加的 democratic pressure 就是 insignifican't。$$...$$ 一个 properly constituted TR initiative 应当只吸引少数心怀 radical thoughts 的 scientists。难处在于 recognize 他们，因为 scientists 有数百万之多，人们甚至不知道针藏在哪一堆干草中。$$...$$ **让我们 assume，在 twentieth century，约有 300 位 transformative researchers——也就是 Planck Club 的 extended membership。**

在 physics 中，[Lee Smolin](https://www.amazon.com/Trouble-Physics-String-Theory-Science/dp/061891868X) 更偏向 elitist 一边，他的 revolutionaries' club 中的 scientists 甚至更少：

> “但说到 theoretical physics，我们谈论的根本不是多少钱。假设某个 agency 或 foundation 决定 fully support **所有 visionaries**，他们 ignore mainstream，follow 自己的 ambitious programs，以 solve quantum gravity 和 quantum theory 的 problems。**我们谈论的或许就是** **two dozen theorists**。要充分 Supporting 他们，只需要任何一个大国的 physics budget 中 tiny fraction 的资金。”

我认为这种看法有一定道理，而且与我在上一篇博文中所说的并不 incompatible（即 peer review 能够 select best work）。在 physics 中，自 70s 以来一直是 [no progress](https://nintil.com/is-useful-physics-over/)，就这个 [fundamentals](https://www.amazon.com/Trouble-Physics-String-Theory-Science/dp/061891868X) 所属的 [field](https://www.amazon.com/Lost-Math-Beauty-Physics-Astray/dp/0465094252/ref=tmm_hrd_swatch_0?_encoding=UTF8&qid=1610576984&sr=8-2) 而言，因此可以主张，或许有些 key assumptions 需要被 thrown away，而且非常有必要 funding 那些在许多 physicists 看来 obviously wrong 的东西。在这种情况下，peer review 可能正在 stifling progress：physics 或许能从更少的 clever math 和更多的 philosophical thought 中受益，像 Smolin 所建议的那样，回到 Einstein 等人的 mode of thinking。Braben 并不一般性地 oppose peer review；他主张，peer review 会 systematically underrate 那些提出 paradigm-breaking ideas 的人，而大多数 science 并不是这样的。

\[1\]. biology 中的 geniuses 是谁？Darwin？Biology 可能不像 physics 那样是一个 Kuhnian world，因为 biology 内含的 strong assumptions 较少，而这些 assumptions 必须被 overthrown，才会算作一场 revolution

在 biology 中，与 physics 相比，无论目前的 framework 是什么（如果有的话）[1](https://nintil.com/newton-hypothesis/#sidenote-1)，它仍然是 extremely fruitful 的。如果以 [CRISPR](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2020/10/advanced-chemistryprize2020.pdf) 为例——它可以说是一个 "good science" 的案例——可以将其 break it down 为：

1.  Noticing 某些 bacteria 中存在 suspicious repetitive sequences

2.  Figuring out 它们是做什么用的

3.  将它们 Leveraging 为用于 gene editing 的 tool

4.  对 CRISPR 进行 Further improving（例如 Prime Editing）

这些 good science 的 examples（或者，如果算上最后 2 项，可能是 engineering）都不是 paradigm shifts，但毫无疑问，CRISPR 已经是 highly useful 和 influential 的，无论是在 academia，还是不久后的 clinic 中。

在 [wikipedia](https://d1wqtxts1xzle7.cloudfront.net/30686866/Ineq_Wikipedia.pdf?1361917900=&response-content-disposition=inline%3B+filename%3DOn_the_inequality_of_contributions_to_Wi.pdf&Expires=1610411522&Signature=P7P2cCDnu6yNhrt1L64caVUnkBRb40qvLrCZnROjgKvsSB9IP2MavdPs86gdf6AQYNf0FPLRXnH1K-dza1dQ4mBrvhWyoAUAYMlx%7EMXivPNvU9HK%7En5l59Etl8afosE63Odc9gz5aMsWVEAZTkGfgV3peaiEbk7I%7EQfQGhLsAyvz5JXI5YBJHK8u1EWrDN9xnkgKpXavLnv0-L046CM-BJntNX2sMR6j60Lt1nnZ%7ELGYQtWsn0ixPMxhDoNzNcPRCz7y%7Ef5vUQG7KGqRt-xAG%7EOZAyM78RpSF2mp871PHkbf9w1bnojA-ibc8QlJRGtb00qDEEGLV8qi7Y0bQqimWQ__&Key-Pair-Id=APKAJLOHF5GGSLRBV4ZA) 中，只有 small fraction（10%）的 active users 完成了大多数（90%）edits。science 也是这样，还是更加 extreme？[Ioannidis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0101698) 等人（2014）指出，在 1996-2011 期间，有 15M 位 scientists published 过东西，但只有 1% 在这一期间的每一年都 published。这个规模较小、拥有 150k 人的 group，占了所有 papers 的 40%，以及所有 citations >1000 的 papers 的 87%。

Nobel Prize winner William Shockley published（[1957](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/4056505)）了一篇 paper，measuring 已经是 high-performing 的 environment 中 productivity 的 distribution：Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory 以及 Brookhaven National Laboratory。Shockley 发现，publications 和 patents 呈 log-normally distribution：大多数 scientists publish 得非常少，而人数 progressively smaller 的那些人则 publish 得 increasingly more。

还有关于 Lotka's law 的 observation（是的，就是 Lotka-Volterra 中的那个 Lotka）：拥有 given number 的 publications 的 authors，其 relative frequency follows a power law，这个 power law 随 articles published 的 number 的 square 而 scales；例如，在至少 publishes 1 篇 article 的 100 位 scientists 中，只有 1 位会 publish 10 篇 articles。precise exponent 可能因 discipline 而 vary（Pao，[1985](https://asistdl.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/\(SICI\)1097-4571$198601$37:1%3C26::AID-ASI4%3E3.0.CO;2-Z)），但每一个似乎确实都 follow 同样的 pattern：屈指可数的人 publish 了全部 papers 中的 overwhelming fraction。

我们可以说：“我们只记得屈指可数的那些 advanced modern physics 的 scientists，所以这必定 imply，你只需要那屈指可数的几个人。”回到 early 20th century，有多少 physicists 做了 "useful" work，最终带来了如今 accepted 且 useful 的 knowledge？当然，我们记得 Einstein、Planck、Hilbert、Lorentz、Mach、Poincaré 和 Minkowski。但当时 physicists 的 total 究竟有多少？Lotka 的 paper 从 Auerbach 的 _[Geschichtstafeln der Physik](https://archive.org/details/geschichtstafel00auergoog)_（1910）中 samples，该书汇集了作者认为足够 important 的 physicists。这本书 published 于 Einstein 的 Annus Mirabilis 之后 5 年，但其中还没有 cited 他，Auerbach 只写到 1900 年。事实证明，physicists 并不只有那些知名人物（或许我们 biased towards 那些在某个特定 area 工作、而那个 area 最终 changing 了 physics 的 foundations 的人）。例如，有一位 [Heinrich Kayser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Kayser)，他在 "adsorption" 方面做了 pioneering work，并 coined 了这个 expression。我敢打赌，你以前没听说过他。仅仅 count _Geschichtstafeln_ 中所有的名字，再试图得出 physicists 的 total count，是不够的；即便这本书，也可能 undercounting 了那些仍然 contributed 的 physicists（例如，那些被书中提及的 authors 所 cited 的人）。幸运的是，如今我们有包含 journals 和 citations 的 indices，因此我们确实可以选取一个更接近 scientists 的 entire population 的对象，并更详细地 explore _Ortega hypothesis_。

## Ortega hypothesis（奥尔特加假说）

> 这个 Ortega hypothesis predicts，highly-cited papers 和 medium-cited（或 lowly-cited）papers 会 equally refer to 具有 medium impact 的 papers。如果 top-level research 相比 medium-level research cites highly-cited work 而言，更 frequently cites 先前的 highly-cited work，那么 Newton hypothesis 就会得到 supported。\[出自稍后 cited 的 Bornmann paper\]

或者更直接地说：_大多数 science 并不 matter_，是当今一个 small circle 的 elites feeds into 未来一个 small circle 的 elites。

Cole & Cole（[1972](https://science.sciencemag.org/content/178/4059/368.abstract)）是对这个 question 的 seminal examination，由此产生了围绕 Ortega hypothesis 是否为 true 的一整套 literature。他们选取了 84 位 physicists 各自 most cited 的 paper，并考察了他们 cited 的 work。其中 60% 指向“top nine” departments 的 researchers（这些 departments 包含 physicists 整个 population 的 21%）；43% 的 citations 指向本身就被 highly (>60) cited 的 researchers \[在某一段时间内\]，尽管那些只占全部 papers 的 8%。70% 的 citations 指向拥有一项或多项 honorific awards 的 researchers，尽管他们只占全部 physicists 的 26%。

他们还在一个 larger sample 中 replicated 了这一做法，选取 _Physical Review_ 中 most cited 的 top 10 papers，考察这些 papers 所 cited 的 work；也就是说，拿其中一篇 paper，找出所有 cited authors，compute 他们的 total citations，再看看这些 authors 是否比 average author 被 disproportionately more cited；结果确实如此。对于并非那么 highly cited 的 papers，情况也一样，这使 authors 得出了如下 conclusion：T_这些 data 为这个 hypothesis 提供了 further support：即使是 limited impact 的 research 的 producers，也 predominantly depend on 一个 relatively small elite 所 produced 的 work。_。

> 相反，似乎是 relatively small number 的 physicists produce 了成为 physics 未来 discoveries 的 base 的 work。我们发现，即使是 relatively minor significance 的 papers，也曾以 disproportionate degree 使用 eminent scientists 的 work。

不出所料，Nobel Prize winners 即使在获奖之前，也已经被 extremely well cited，

> 在 1961 SCI 中，Nobel laureates（于 1955 至 1965 年间获得 physics 奖项）的 work 所获 citations 的 average number 是 58，相比之下，其他 scientists 的 average 是 5.5 citations。出现在 1961 SCI 中的二十五万 scientists 里，只有 1.08 percent 获得了 58 或更多 citations。我们曾认为，获奖可能使一位 scientist 更 visible，并带来超出其 work 的 quality 所应得数量的 postprize citations。因此，我们把 laureates divided into 两个 groups：在 1961 年前五年以内获奖的人，以及在那一年之后获奖的人。1957-1961 laureates 在 1961 SCI 中被 cited 的 average 是 42 次；未来的 prize winners（于 1961 至 1965 年间获奖的人）的 average 是 62 次。由于 prospective laureates 比 actual laureates 更经常被 cited，我们 concluded，较大的 citations 数量 reflects 的是 work 的 high quality，而非获奖所带来的 visibility。

这显然引出了一个人们很自然会想到的、[炽热到发白的](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence)激进看法：正如有人提议[将 healthcare funding slash in half](https://www.cato-unbound.org/2007/09/10/robin-hanson/cut-medicine-half)，而不会造成重大后果，如果 highly cited science 是由 small elite 完成的，并且假定 citations correlate with importance，那么 R&D budgets 是否也可以同样被大幅削减，而没有任何重大 downside（就 science 的 advancement 而言）？

> 如果未来对其他 science 的 fieldsof 的 research corroborates 我们的 results，我们就可以追问：这对于 scientists 的 number 与 science 的 rate of advance 之间的 relationship 意味着什么，以及是否可能 reduced scientists 的 number，而不 affecting rate of advance。这些 data 似乎 suggest，大多数 research 很少被 physics community 的大多数人 cited，更少被那些 produce 最 significant discoveries 的最 eminent scientists cited。即使是在 leading journals 中 published 的大多数 articles，也只获得很少的 citations。在一项关于 Physical Review 中 published 的 articles 所获 citations 的 study 中，我们发现，1963 年在 Review 中 published 的全部 articles 中，80 percent 被 cited 四次或更少；在 1966 SCI 中，47 percent 只被 cited 一次或从未被 cited。显然，即便在如此 outstanding journal 中，大多数 published work 对 science 的 development 也只有很小的 impact。**因此，basic question 就出现了：如果 active research physicists 的 number 被 sharply reduced，physics 是否还能 maintained 同样的 rate of advance。**

这些 authors 考虑了对此的一些可能 counterarguments：首先，important papers 所 cited 的 work 中，仍有 15-20% 是由 non-elite scientists produced 的；如果这些 work 与那篇 important paper 是 relevant 的，那么 system 中可能也需要这些 scientists。他们对此的回应是，这些 scientists 可能是 fungible 的；比如，假设有 3 位 top researchers 和 100 位其他 scientists，每位 top researchers 都 published 一篇 paper，各自 cites 其余 scientists 的 10 篇 papers（total 为 30 篇 papers），同时那 100 人中有 70 人的 work 是 uncited 的，那么从 theory 上说，你仍然可以去掉其中 70 人，并得到同样的 results。

> 尽管所有 scientists 都是 replaceable 的，其含义是其他 scientists 最终会 duplicate 他们的 discoveries，但有些 scientists 比其他人有多得多的 functional equivalents。例如，要 replace 一位 Murray GellMann 的 work 会 relatively difficult，但要 replace 一位只在 Gell-Mann 某篇 papers 中被 cited 一次的 scientist 的 work，就没有那么 difficult。如果 less distinguished scientists 有许多 functional equivalents，那么那些常常在 scientific discoveries 的产生过程中 perform vital tasks 的众多 laboratory technicians 和 staff workers 也是如此。我们并不是说这些 tasks 是不必要的，而是说有许多人能够 perform 它们。

他们考虑的另一个 critique 是，其余 scientists 是否 perform key non-research functions；他们以这样一个 observation 回应：top scientists 往往是由其他 top scientists trained 的

> 从关于 Nobel laureates、National Academy members 和其他 eminent scientists 的 qualitative sources 与 statistical studies 中，我们知道，最终进入 elite strata 的绝大多数 scientists，都是由其他 elite members trained 的 (22)。事实上，目前 Academy 的 members 中有 69 percent，American Nobelists 中有 80 percent，仅从九所 universities 获得了他们的 doctora'tes。可以开玩笑地断言，赢得 Ndbel prize 的最佳办法，就是师从一位过去的 laureate。对那些 work 被 heavily cited 的 physicists 所就读的 graduate schools 的 Analysis indicates，后来表现为 productive 的 scientists 中，绝大多数是在 top 20 graduate departments 获得 doctorates 的。

这里顺带一提，[Clarivate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarivate_Citation_Laureates) 曾根据 work 被 cited 的程度 predicted 多位 Nobel award winners，这为使用 citations 来 predict 真正 good research 的 approach 提供了一些 validity。即便其选择与 Nobel committee 的实际选择远非 100% match，他们所选出但未获得 Nobel 的人，似乎仍然称得上 great scientists。

最后，他们考虑了 prediction question。如果我们无法 identify 谁会是 good 的，那么 increasing top scientists 的 absolute count 的唯一希望，就是为更多人腾出空间。我曾在这里指出，许多如今被视为 important 的 research [pieces](https://nintil.com/discoveries-ignored)，都曾以某种形式遭到 rejected。但此前我也展示过，scientists 能在一定程度上，从已经 excellent 的 work 中 [pick](https://nintil.com/grant-peer-review) 出将会成为 best 的那些。不过，如果我们关心的是 best work，那么他们说，我们主要只需要 top departments，而且仍然可以留有一些 margin of error；在 physics 中，这将意味着把每年 granted 的 PhDs 的 number 减半。他们认为，这有利于留在 system 中的那些人的生活和 research environment，

> 我们所 reported 的 data 导向了这样一个 tentative conclusion：**reducing scientists 的 number，可能不会 slow down scientific progress 的 rate**。仍有一个 crucial question 有待回答：如果新的 Ph.D. candidates 的 number 被 sharply reduced，truly outstanding applicants 的 number 是否也会 reduction，还是 applicants 的 reduction 会来自那些我们如今会视为 borderline cases 的人。这不是 social selection 的问题，因为我们相信，academic departments 有可能 distinguish 具有 high potential 的 applicants。这是 self-selection 的问题。science 的 size 的 reduction，可能会促使一些非常聪明的未来 scientists 转向其他职业。**一种 occupation 能否 attract high-level recruits，在很大程度上 depends on 该 occupation 的 prestige、working conditions，以及人们所感知的该 occupation 中的 opportunities**。当然，我们并非有意建议采取任何会 reduce science 的 prestige 或 scientists 可用 resources 的 policy。我们的意思是，减少 new recruits 的 number，同时 increase 最终较少的 scientists 所能获得的 resources，science 大概不会因此受损。**也许 science 如今在 recruiting 方面面临的最严重问题，是人们所感知到的现实：可供新 Ph.D.'s 从事的 jobs 很少。Reducing science 的 size，使 supply 与 demand 达到更好的 balance，最终可能 increase science 作为一种 career 的 attractiveness。**

这里，想想如今在 Facebook、Google 和各种 quant hedge funds 工作的所有 talent。假设你能够 increase science 中的 salaries，使其与那些机构相当。[据一则轶事](https://www.amazon.com/Gerald-Geison-Princeton-1995-05-02-Hardcover/dp/B002CA6OII/ref=sr_1_3?dchild=1&keywords=private+science+pasteur&qid=1610480709&sr=8-3)，Pasteur 在 60 岁时获授一笔 annuity，价值相当于百货商店员工 salary 的 10x，放在如今的 US 会是 $[440k](https://www.glassdoor.com/Salaries/store-worker-salary-SRCH_KO0,12.htm) for Pasteur, a salary that seems competitive with that paid by high status, high pay jobs. Rounding that to 500k, with an annual expenditure of 5bn$ 人们可以 fund 一个由 top 10k scientists 组成的 permanent college，让他们能够按照自己认为合适的方式自由 research。

应当指出，这些 authors 并没有以 high certainty 建议立即 cut science 的 funding；在我稍后会谈到的一篇 paper 中，其中一人实际上还 disavows 了这样的 conclusion。

两位 Cole 得到两位 MacRoberts 的回应（[1986](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02016665)），倒也恰如其分；后者认为 bibliographies 是 incomplete 的。在较早的 work（MacRoberts & MacRoberts，[1986](https://www.jstor.org/stable/285292?seq=1)）中，他们把这一 critique 表述得更 precise：假设有一篇 paper，它 builds on 另外 10 篇 papers 的 knowledge。现在，第二篇 papers cites 第一篇 paper，却把一些 assumptions 视为理所当然，而且只 cites 前面的 papers，而不是前面的 paper 加上作为其基础的那 10 篇 papers。这难道不会导致这一篇 paper 被大量 cited，而作为其基础的 10 篇 papers 得到较少的 cited 吗？同样，众所周知，Einstein 的早期 work 并没有 formally cite 任何人，尽管大家都知道，他受到了 Mach 或 Planck 等人的 work 的启发。这些 authors 提到，这种 informally citing、却不 formally 出现在 bibliography 中的做法，在 science 中很常见。我不同意（而且十多年来，我读过数千篇 papers，横跨 economic history、longevity research 和 psychology 等不同 disciplines）。science _过去_确实如此，但任何熟悉现代 citation practices 的人，大概都会发现，总体而言，这只占 paper 实际 citations 的很小一个 %。两位 Cole 回应了（[1987](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02016678)）两位 MacRoberts。

当然，有些 acknowledgements 没有达到 citation 的层级，但这很公平，acknowledgement 意味着比 full citation 更小的 contribution。此外，如果我们把这些 uncited papers 加进 bibliography，我预计 distributions 会更加 skewed。例如，想一想，如果每当我们在一篇 paper 中看到“PCR”时，都 cited Mullis et al. 那篇已经 highly cited 的 PCR paper（[1986](http://symposium.cshlp.org/content/51/263.extract)），会发生什么。我预计，unacknowledged work 的 distribution 甚至比 acknowledged work 的 distribution 更加 skewed；但这是一个需要 tested 的 assumption。归根结底，两位 MacRoberts 是对的：如果 a) unacknowledged influences 是 highly important 的，而且 b) 这些 influences 比 observed distribution of citations 更 widespread，而且 c) 那些特定的 scientists 是 relatively hard to replace 的（也就是说，那篇 publication 不会由另一个人完成，而这个人在 counterfactual 中本会更加 productive）。那么 Cole and Cole 的 conclusion 就不会 hold：大量相对不知名的 scientists 将被证明是 highly cited papers 的 key contributors。

Turner & Chubin (1976) [consider](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/053901847601500407?journalCode=ssic) 了一种 alternative explanation：_raw talent 的 differences 是 genuine 的，但也是 marginal 的_（考虑到关于 intelligence 和 creativity 的 [research](https://www.amazon.com/Genius-Creativity-Problems-Behavioural-Sciences/dp/0521485088)，我认为这不太可信）。但如果这是真的，而且运气最终让一位 randomly selected scientist 变得更加 prestigious（即 Matthew effect），那么我们就可能 observe 到类似的 distribution。我将在下一节 review 关于 Matthew effect 的 direct evidence。我之前提到、稍后还会讨论的 Bornmann study 考察了一个相当大的 dataset，似乎不赞同这些 authors 的 critique，但他们 \[Bornmann et al.\] 没有给出很好的回应：他们所做的是在更大的 sample 中 replication Cole & Cole，因此也同样面临这一 critique；我们必须直接 examine Matthew effect。

我尝试寻找声称能够 validate 或 disproof Cole&Cole paper 的 natural experiments。我在 Nintil 这里提到过一个例子：在 WWII 期间，scientists 的 total number、scientists 占 population 的 %，以及 total R&D funding 都 sharply increased。尽管 scientists 比以往任何时候都更多（即使按占 population 的 % 来看也是如此），这也[没有明显改变 growth](https://nintil.com/was-world-war-ii-good-for-growth/)，这里指的是 US economy 的 growth。事实上，大多数评论者如果要指出什么，反而会指出它的 slowdown。如果所有 scientists 都 contribute equally，在 all else equal 的情况下，我会预计，更多 R&D 和更多 scientists 几乎会 linearly 带来更多 output，但实际似乎并非如此。遗憾的是，这远不是一个 clean experiment，因为除了 scientists doing science 之外，TFP 和 GDP growth 还有各种 determinants。

## Matthew effect（马太效应）

Highly cited scientists 会 cite 其他 highly cited scientists。这是因为 prestige bias，还是因为他们在 acknowledging 真正的 good work？对此的肯定回答被称为 Matthew effect（“富者愈富”），最早由 RK Merton 提出[2](https://nintil.com/newton-hypothesis/#sidenote-2)

\[2\]. 就是这个人 coined 了 'unintended consequences'、'role model' 和 'self-fulfilling prophecy' 这些 terms，他也是 Black-Scholes-Merton model 中那位 Nobel-winning Merton 的父亲

[Azoulay](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1755)（又是他！）等人 (2013) 最近有一篇关于这个问题的 paper；具体 examined 的 question 是，与 synthetic sample 相比，成为 HHMI Investigator（又来了！）是否会 affects _先前的_ work 的 citation patterns（如果你读过我介绍另一篇 [Azoulay](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih) paper 的文章，就知道这是怎么回事）。当然，如果他们 publish 的新 work 获得了更多 cited，就很难把 Matthew effect 与真正的 good work disentangle：他们既获得 award 的 prestige，也获得随之而来的资源，两者都可能 contribute to 获得更多 cited 的 work。但对于_过去的 work_，获得 award 实际上应该不会让 citation count raise 太多；当然，也许有些 scientist 此前错过了这项 work，而 award 如今让它获得了额外的 visibility。他们确实 find 了一个小的 effect，相对于 controls，这个 effect 往往随时间逐渐消退，也许在 10 年后消失。这个 effect 是平均多出一次 citation。当他们按照 papers 的 published 时间（相对于获得 HHMI award 的时间）对 published papers 进行 split 时，他们 find，获奖前 2 年及更早 published 的 articles 保持 unchanged，大部分 effect 是由获奖紧前一年 published 的 articles driven 的；这些 articles 平均多出 3 次 citations，而这个 effect 随时间 diminishes，以至于 appointment 后 10 年，这就变成了一次 citation；authors 将此 attribute to award 通过表明 winner 做出了经 panel of experts examined 的 good science，从而 reducing 较新 work 的 uncertainty；较早的 work 可能被认为更 solid，而且已经被 cited，而新 work 将来可能被发现有 faulty 之处。与这个解释 Consistent，较年轻 scientists 的 work 更可能从这个 effect 中受益，因为 peers 可以根据较年长 scientists 更长的 track records，更容易地对他们进行 assessed。所以，也许 Matthew effect 并不大！如果我们对许多 scientists 取 average，并着眼于他们 publication careers 的 long term，上一节的 citation analysis 就仍然 hold。

Li 和 Agha（[又是他们](https://nintil.com/grant-peer-review)！）在同一项 work 中，也尝试看看 NIH study sections 给出的 higher scores 与后来 higher citation performance 之间的 association，是否可能由 prestige mediated。如果 Matthew effect 存在，它可能以两种方式 manifest：

1.  更 prestigious 的 scientists 获得 higher scores
2.  在 grant being funded 这一 Conditional on 下，对于 given score，prestigious scientists 会更频繁地被 cited。

![因果关系](https://docs.google.com/drawings/d/e/2PACX-1vRWntHKTe6sQ499MYOvqjTNpe8X5ebRaVH3WrVJkJI4zAxCCkI21bmJRyusy5tOg0GKIPrtQqFxMVgq/pub?w=641&h=232)

这篇 paper 尝试通过使用表示是否拥有 PhD、MD 或两者兼有，以及 PI 在 academia 已有多少年的 dummy variables，来 control for prestige。这些作为 prestige 的 proxies 是极不完善的，但无论如何，controlling for 这些并不 change scores 与 citations 之间的 association。他们也 control for 一组听起来更接近“prestige”的事物，包括过去的 funding 和 institution of origin，find 这并不会大幅 alter citation performance。

> 具体而言，我们 include 了 controlling for PI 过去是否 received NIH funding 的 variables，其中包括四个 indicators，分别表示此前 received 过一个 R01 grant、两个或更多 R01 grants、一个 R01 以外的 NIH grant，以及两个或更多其他 NIH grants。如果 reviewers 可能是在回应 applicant 在 proposal writing 方面的 experience 和 skill，那么我们会 expect，纳入这些 reflecting 先前 NIH funding 的 variables，会 attenuate 我们对 value-added 的 estimates。然而，我们 find，纳入这些 variables 并不会 substantively affect 我们的 findings。最后，在 Model 6 中，我们还 control for institutional quality、gender 和 ethnicity，以 capture prestige、connectedness 或 access to resources 中其他可能 unobserved 的方面，这些方面可能 influence review scores 和随后的 research productivity。我们的 estimates 再次 remain stable：comparing backgrounds 为 statistically identical 的 applicants，score 差 1-SD 的 grant 被 predicted 会有 7.3% 更少的未来 publications，以及 14.8% 更少的未来 citations（两者均为 P < 0.001）。

authors 只能以 black box 的方式说，总体而言 prestige 对 science 并不重要，因为他们没有一个能 disentangle work 的 quality 和 prestige 如何影响 score 的 model；但既然总体上 prestige 并不 change correlation，那么要么 a) 1) 和 2) 都不重要，要么 b) 它们的 effects 方向相反（Prestige reduces 获得 high score 的 odds，而 prestige increases 获得 higher citation performance 的 odds，或者反过来，从而相互 cancel out）。我愿意接受第一种 interpretation，即从 aggregate 来看，prestige 没有那么重要。

在前面那篇 Azoulay [HHMI](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15466/w15466.pdf) paper 中，他们确实有一个 model，尝试 predict 谁会成为 HHMI researcher（Table 4），但这个 model 没有那么强的 predictive power，也无法让我们在 selection level 将 prestige 与 quality disentangle。

考察 prestige 及其 impact 的另一种方式，是 examining 一位 scientist 获得 Nobel Prize 时会发生什么；这大概是一位 scientist 所能获得的最强 status boost。[Li et al. (2020)](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsif.2020.0135) examine 了 citations 总体上（获得 Nobel 之后）以及获奖之前会发生什么。与人们可能的预期 Contrary，laureates 获得的 average citations per paper 在获奖后会 _goes down_ 约 11%，而这个 dip 最终会 recovers！与此同时，winners 在不同 fields 中 author 了更多 papers（也许他们觉得，既然已经最大限度地证明了自己，现在就可以 explore 新 topics 了）。Li 等人并不 claim 这些是 causally related。这项 work 也为 citations 能够 predictive of success 提供了一些 indirect evidence，因为 laureates 在 early careers 期间，_就 publishing hit papers 的 rate 而言，相比 comparison group 有 more than sixfold increase，其中 hit papers 被 defined 为同一年、同一 field 中 rescaled 10 year citations 位于 top 1% 的 papers_。

我找到一篇 paper，显示在另一种 context 中存在 Matthew effect。我认为，这并不是这种 effect _一般而言_存在的 good evidence。Bol 等人 ([2018](https://www.pnas.org/content/115/19/4887)) report，在荷兰，年轻 scientists 有一个主要的 funding 来源，即 Innovation Research Incentives Scheme。这项 study compares 了那些位于 threshold 之上（因而获得 grant）的人，与那些根本没有获得 grant、但差一点就获得的人后来的境遇。以获奖后 8 年进行 measured，获得这项 grant 的人作为一个整体，获得的 funding 是未获得者的 3x；而且他们获得 full professorship 的可能性是后者的两倍。这部分是由 nonwinners 未来甚至更不可能尝试获取更多 funding caused 的（占 effect 的 10-26%）。请注意，这里存在与我在[这里](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih)对 NIH Director's award 的评论中指出的 comparison group 相同的问题：尽管这篇 paper 比较的是略低于和略高于 cutoff threshold 的 scientists，这两个 groups 的 scientists 在 citations、number of publications 或 h-index 上却相差近一个 standard deviation。深入查看 supplemental data，考察 citations（因为 impact 最终是由 citations 而不是他们获得了多少钱 measured 的），差一点获得 grant 的 group 与以微小差距未能获得 grant 的 group 之间的 difference 就 reduced 了；authors 实际上说，根本不存在 difference！rank 与 citations correlates 这一事实，似乎也意味着 peer review panels 能够 identify top talent。因此，这与上面的 analysis 并不 incoherent。

## 结论

就 citations 而言，Newton hypothesis 似乎是 true 的：science 是由一个 small elite 来 advanced 的。这不仅仅是指 "Einstein-level" breakthroughs，这个 small elite 可能并不是 practicing scientists 总数的 0.01%，而是 1-5%。即使是 10%，也仍然与 scientific elitism 的理念相符。至少初步看来，无论是从日常观察（Highly cited classic papers），还是从 peers 的 assessment（Nobel prize panels；Nobel-winning papers 是 highly cited 的，而且会 cite highly cited research）来看，Citations 确实似乎与 "good science" 存在 correlate 关系。人们总可以提出这样的 critique：citations 并没有 objectively measuring 一项 work 的 worth。但什么能做到呢？是的，citations 的出现有各种原因（包括讨好 reviewers 或其他 labs，因为如果它们也被 cited，就可能更愿意 cite 这篇 paper），但主要原因仍然是，被 cited 的 work 确实 influence 了这篇 paper。人们可以走向不同程度的 epistemic nihilism，最终走到“我们永远无法知道谁或什么会 successful”的地步，因此主张我们应该 fund everyone equally，maximally equalizing funding。我不同意这一点，并将在本系列的下一部分讨论 scientific egalitarianism 和 lotteries。我认为，这种思路可以在 science 如何被 organized 这件事上发挥作用，但不能是唯一的作用。

在本系列的第一篇文章中，我说过，funding 少数经过挑选的 scientists 并给予他们额外 resources（就像 HHMI/Pioneer award 所做的那样）这一想法，取决于若干 assumptions：a) 我们能够在一定程度上 pick the winners，以及 b) 确实存在 winners（或者说，存在 contribute disproportionately 的 scientists）

到目前为止，evidence supports b)，也在较小程度上 supports a)。如果 discriminating between good and best 是 costless 的，那么任何 better-than-random selection process 都应该优先于 randomization。但当前者是 costly 的时候（peer review 需要 peers 花时间做 review），system 的 costs 就可能 outweigh 其 benefits。归根结底，如何以 ideal setup 来 allocate funding 和 select proposals，将取决于这项 cost/benefit calculation。这项 calculation 很可能是 field dependent 的。对于哪些类型的 work 会得到 funded，也是如此；biology 很可能会从大量的 "fund projects" 中 benefit，而 physics 则相对会从 "fund people" 中 benefit 更多。如果 Smolin 和 Braben 是对的，而且值得获得这种特殊待遇的人数很少，那么只用 total funding 中极小的一部分，就可能 achieve outsized returns。

Cole & Cole 的 paper 试探性地提出，cutting science funding（以及/或者 concentrating 它）可能是 beneficial 的。这种可能性确实存在，值得 exploring，甚至值得在有限规模上 experimenting。这当然会引起极大争议：unequally 增加 funding，比 unequally 减少 funding 更容易。

在下一篇文章中，我将考察支持另一种 approach 的 evidence：using lotteries。这种 approach 在其 extreme form 下，是 elitist 的 "fund a small elite very well" 的 alternative；而在较弱的形式下，则是 complementary 的。

## 原文

In previous posts I [said](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih) that the extent to which "Fund people" works will depend on the distribution of scientific talent. Think about the following situation: Imagine that only a handful of scientists at every point in time are able to –if given the time and means–lead revolutions on par with the work of Darwin, Einstein, or Galileo (This is an extreme case admittedly because most of science does not look like this; most of science is more incremental and less memorable). I recently found two authors that believe something like this,

Braben, whose [book](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom) _Scientific Freedom_ I reviewed says that even though most scientists could be the next Einstein if they tried, only a few will even try:

At any one time, of course, **the proportion of researchers ready to seize that possibly once - in - a - lifetime opportunity will be very small**, so if they are prevented from doing so the democratic pressure they can exert is insignifican't. \[...\] a properly constituted TR initiative should appeal to only a small number of scientists with radical thoughts on their minds. It wouldn't be enough to just count all the names in the _Geschichtstafeln_ and then try to come up with a total count of physicists; even that book can be undercounting physicists that still contributed (For example, that were cited by the authors mentioned in the book). Fortunately now we have indices with journals and citations so we can truly take something closer to the entire population of scientists and explore in more detail the _Ortega hypothesis_.

## The Ortega hypothesis

> The Ortega hypothesis predicts that highly-cited papers and medium-cited (or lowly-cited) papers would equally refer to papers with a medium impact. The Newton hypothesis would be supported if the top-level research more frequently cites previously highly-cited work than that medium-level research cites highly-cited work. \[From the Bornmann paper cited later\]

Or to state it in a more direct way: _Most science does not matter_, it is a small circle of elites of today that feeds into a small circle of elites in the future.

Cole & Cole ([1972](https://science.sciencemag.org/content/178/4059/368.abstract)) is the seminal examination of this question, which led to an entire literature around whether or not the Ortega hypothesis is true or not. They took the most cited paper from each of 84 physicists and looked at the work they cited. 60% of it was to researchers at "top nine" departments (Which comprise 21% of the entire population of physicists), 43% of citations were to researchers that themselves were highly (>60) cited \[in a given period of time\], despite those being only 8% of all papers. 70% of citations were to researchers that have one or more honorific awards, despite them being 26% of all physicists.

They also replicated this in a larger sample, taking the top 10 papers most cited in _Physical Review_, they looked at the work cited by those; so take one of those papers, get all the cited authors, compute their total citations and see if this authors are disproportionately more cited than the average author; and they are. The same is true for not so highly cited papers, leading the authors to conclude that T_hese data offer further support for the hypothesis that even the producers of research of limited impact depend predominantly on the work produced by a relatively small elite._.

> It seems, rather, that a relatively small number of physicists produce work that becomes the base for future discoveries in physics. We have found that even papers of relatively minor significance have used to a disproportionate degree the work of the eminent scientists.

Nobel Prize winners, unsurprisingly are extremely well cited even before they get the award,

> The average number of citations in the 1961 SCI to the work of Nobel laureates (who won the prize in physics between 1955 and 1965) was 58, as compared with an average of 5.5 citations for other scientists. Only 1.08 percent of the quarter of a million scientists who appear in the 1961 SCI received 58 or more citations. We thought it possible that winning the prize might make a scientist more visible and lead to a greater number of postprize citations than the quality of his work warranted. We therefore divided the laureates into two groups: those who won the prize five or fewer years before 1961 and those who won it after that year. The 1957-1961 laureates were cited an average of 42 times in the 1961 SCI; the future prize winners (those winning the prize between 1961 and 1965), an average of 62 times. Since the prospective laureates were more often cited than the actual laureates, we concluded that the larger number of citations reflects the high quality of work rather than the visibility gained by winning the prize.

This obviously leads to the natural [white-glowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence) hot take one may consider: In the same way that it has been proposed to [slash healthcare funding in half](https://www.cato-unbound.org/2007/09/10/robin-hanson/cut-medicine-half), for no major consequence, if highly cited science is done by a small elite and assuming citations correlate with importance, could R&D budgets be likewise be axed without any major downside (In terms of the advancement of science)?

> If future research on other fieldsof science corroborates our results, we may inquire what it implies about the relationship between the number of scientists and the rate of advance in science, and whether it is possible that the number of scientists could be reduced without affecting the rate of advance. The data would seem to suggest that most research is rarely cited by the bulk of the physics community, and even more sparingly cited by the most eminent scientists who produce the most significant discoveries. Most articles published in even the leading journals receive few citations. In a study of citations to articles published in Physical Review, we found that 80 percent of all the articles published in the Review in 1963 were cited four times or less; 47 percent, once or never in the 1966 SCI. Clearly most of the published work in even such an outstanding journal makes little impact on the development of science. **Thus the basic question emerges, whether the same rate of advance in physics could be maintained if the number of active research physicists were to be sharply reduced.**

The authors consider some possible counterarguments to this: First, that still 15-20% of the work cited in important papers is produced by non-elite scientists and if this is relevant to the important paper then those scientists might be needed in the system as well. Their reply to this is that these scientists may be fungible; say suppose there are 3 top researchers and 100 other scientists and each of the top researchers published one paper that cites 10 papers from the rest (30 papers total) while at the same time 70 of those 100 go uncited, you could still could drop 70 of them and still get the same results in theory.

> Although all scientists are replaceable in the sense that other scientists would eventually duplicate their discoveries, some scientists have many more functional equivalents than others. For example, it would be relatively difficult to replace the work of a Murray GellMann, but not so difficult to replace that of a scientist who is cited once in one of Gell-Mann's papers. If the less distinguished scientists have many functional equivalents, so do the many laboratory technicians and staff workers who often perform vital tasks in the making of scientific discoveries. We are not saying that the tasks are unnecessary, but that there are many people who could perform them.

Another critique they consider is whether the rest of the scientists perform key non-research functions, they counter this with the observation that top scientists tend to be trained by other top scientists

> We know from qualitative sources and statistical studies of Nobel laureates, National Academy members, and other eminent scientists that the great majority of scientists who end up in the elite strata are trained 'by other members of the elite (22). In fact, 69 percent of current members of the Academy and 80 percent of American Nobelists received their doctora'tes from only nine universities. It might be facetiously asserted that the best way to win a Ndbel prize is to study with a past laureate. Analysis of the graduate schools attended by physicists whose work is heavily cited indicates that a large majority of scientists who turn out to be productive get their doctorates at the top 20 graduate departments.

As an aside here, [Clarivate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarivate_Citation_Laureates) has predicted a number of Nobel award winners from how cited their work is, which lends some validity to the approach of using citations to predict actually good research. Even when the match is far from 100% with the actual choices of the Nobel committee, their picks that didn't get the Nobel still seem to count as great scientists.

Lastly, they consider the prediction question. If we can't identify who'll be good then our only hope of increasing the absolute count of top scientists is by making room for more. Here I noted that many [pieces](https://nintil.com/discoveries-ignored) of research now seen as important were once rejected in some form or another. But previously I showed that scientists can to some extent [pick](https://nintil.com/grant-peer-review) among already excellent work what'll be the best. But if we're worrying about the best work, then they say that we mostly just need the top departments, and we can still have some margin of error; in physics this would involve halving the number of PhDs granted per year. They find this good for the lives and research environment of those who would remain in the system,

> The data we have reported lead to the tentative conclusion that **reducing the number of scientists might not slow down the rate of scientific progress**. One crucial question remains to be answered: whether, if the number of new Ph.D. candidates is sharply reduced, will there be a reduction in the number of truly outstanding applicants or will the reduction in applicants come from those whom we would now consider borderline cases. This is not a matter of social selection, for we believe it possible for academic departments to distinguish applicants with high potential. It is a matter of self-selection. A reduction in the size of science might motivate some very bright future scientists to turn to other careers. **The ability of an occupation to attract high-level recruits depends to a great extent on the prestige of the occupation, working conditions, and perceived opportunities in the occupation**. We, of course, do not intend to suggest the advisability of any policy that would either reduce the prestige of science or the resources available to scientists. What we are suggesting is that science would probably not suffer from a reduction in the number of new recruits and an increase in the resources available to the resulting smaller number of scientists. **Perhaps the most serious problem that science faces today in recruiting is the perceived reality that there are few jobs available to new Ph.D.'s. Reducing the size of science so that supply would be in better balance with demand might ultimately increase the attractiveness of science as a career.**

Here, think of all the talent that is currently working at Facebook, Google, and various quant hedge funds. Suppose you could increase salaries in science to match those. [Anecdotally](https://www.amazon.com/Gerald-Geison-Princeton-1995-05-02-Hardcover/dp/B002CA6OII/ref=sr_1_3?dchild=1&keywords=private+science+pasteur&qid=1610480709&sr=8-3), Pasteur was awarded, at the age of 60, an annuity worth 10x the salary of a department store worker which today in the US would be $[440k](https://www.glassdoor.com/Salaries/store-worker-salary-SRCH_KO0,12.htm) for Pasteur, a salary that seems competitive with that paid by high status, high pay jobs. Rounding that to 500k, with an annual expenditure of 5bn$ one could fund a permanent college of the top 10k scientists that would be free to research as they see fit.

The authors do not recommend with high certainty that funding for science should be cut straightaway, it should be noted, in a paper I'll touch on in a moment one of them actually disavows such conclusion.

It is fitting that a pair of Coles gets a reply by a pair of MacRoberts ([1986](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02016665)) who argue that bibliographies are incomplete. In earlier work (MacRoberts & MacRoberts, [1986](https://www.jstor.org/stable/285292?seq=1)) they make the critique more precise: Suppose there's a paper that builds on the knowledge of 10 other papers. Now a second papers cites the first paper but takes some assumptions for granted, and cites only the former papers instead of the former paper plus the 10 papers that underlie it. Wouldn't this lead to this one paper to be cited a lot, whereas the 10 papers that underlie it get cited less? Likewise Einstein's early work famously didn't formally cite anyone, despite it being broadly known that he was inspired by the work of others like Mach or Planck. The authors mention that this practice, informally citing without formally appearing in the bibliography is common in science. I disagree (And I've read thousands of papers over a decade across disciplines as diverse as economic history, longevity research, or psychology). It _used to_ be the case that science was like that, but anyone familiar with modern citation practices will probably have seen that by and large this is a very small % of the actual citations of the paper. The Coles replied ([1987](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF02016678)) to the MacRoberts.

Sure there are acknowledgements that do not make it to the citation level, but this is fair, an acknowledgement implies a lesser contribution than a full citation. Furthermore, if we added these uncited papers to the bibliography, I expect the distributions would be even more skewed. Consider for example what would happen if the already highly cited PCR paper by Mullis et al. ([1986](http://symposium.cshlp.org/content/51/263.extract)) were cited every single time we see "PCR" in a paper. I expect the distribution of unacknowledged work is more skewed even than that of acknowledged work; but this is an assumption that would have to be tested. Ultimately the MacRoberts are right: if a) unacknowledged influences are highly important and b) Those influences are more widespread than the observed distribution of citations and c) Those specific scientists are relatively hard to replace (That is, that publication wouldn't have been made by someone else, who would have been more productive in the counterfactual). Then the Cole and Cole conclusion wouldn't hold: Masses of relatively unknown scientists would turn out to be key contributors to highly cited papers.

Turner & Chubin (1976) [consider](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/053901847601500407?journalCode=ssic) an alternative explanation: _that the differences in raw talent are genuine, but marginal_ (Something I consider implausible given [research](https://www.amazon.com/Genius-Creativity-Problems-Behavioural-Sciences/dp/0521485088) on intelligence and creativity). But to the extent that this is true and that luck ends up leading to a randomly selected scientist to become more prestigious (a Matthew effect) then we could observe a similar distribution. I'll review direct evidence for the Matthew effect in the next section. The Bornmann study I mentioned previously and that I will discuss in a moment, considering a fairly large dataset does not seem to agree with the author's critique, but they \[Bornmann et al.\] don't offer a good reply, theirs is a replication of Cole & Cole in a larger sample, and as such is open to the same critique, we have to examine the Matthew effect directly.

I tried to look for natural experiments that claim to validate or disproof the Cole&Cole paper. One example I noted here at Nintil was that the total number of scientists as well as the % of scientists within the population and total R&D funding sharply increased during WWII. Despite the fact that there are more scientists than ever (even as % of the population) that [hasn't obviously changed the growth](https://nintil.com/was-world-war-ii-good-for-growth/) of the US economy. In fact, most commentators, if anything, will point to its slowdown. If all scientists contribute equally, all else equal, I would expect that more R&D and more scientists almost linearly leads to more output, but this doesn't seem to be true. Unfortunately this is far from a clean experiment because TFP and GDP growth have all sort of determinants other than scientists doing science.

Another quasi experiment was the increase and later decrease in the hiring of physicists (Assistant professors) between 1963 and 1975. What does good work=f(scientists) look like? Cole & Meyer ([1985](https://akjournals.com/view/journals/11192/7/3-6/article-p443.xml)) look at this:

![image-20210112152015798](https://nintil.com/images/2021-01-11-newton-hypothesis/image-20210112152015798.png)

And examine the productivity of each cohort, as measured by publications in _Physical Review_ and _Physical Review Letters_ (The reason for picking those two journals is that they are generally agreed to be the top physics journal and they have existed since 1963; if more journals were included then citations would increase just because in the period 1963-1975 more journals were founded which would lead to more citations being possible over time)

> Three hundred and seventy-six, or **45%,** of new assistant professors hired between 1963 and 1965 **did not receive a single citation throughout the 11-year period**. Only 212, or 25%, received five or more citations to work published in one or more of the four periods. In general, those physicists who begin their careers by publishing work which is cited are far more likely than those whose early work is not cited to continue to produce high impact work.

Despite that, we don't really see a change in the proportion of physicists that ended up being cited:

![image-20210112154758124](https://nintil.com/images/2021-01-11-newton-hypothesis/image-20210112154758124.png)

This is the author's interpretation:

> The data presented in this paper offer empirical support for the implicit assumption in the work of both Merton and Ben-David that scientific advance is a function of the number of people entering science. The data does not support the assumption of Price that the number of "good" scientists grows at a slower rate than the total number of scientists **nor does it support the assumptions of the 1972 Ortega hypothesis paper that we could reduce the size of science without reducing the rate of scientific advance.**

(This is quite nice and honest given that the Cole of the same paper is the same Cole as in the original paper). This is not straightforward though: The paper can be taken to show that during the period studied we were not "scraping the bottom of the barrel", that we can still add physicists capable of making contributions. Or that for hiring we can't distinguish between good and best. But it still can be true that most of the ones that were added did not make a high impact contribution. If (And this is a sizable if) we can identify them ahead of time, then we could just not hire those? The paper also explores another question of _why there has been a decline in the number of talented young scientists_ and here they point to a tighter job market for science.

More recently Bornmann et al. ([2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0013327)) studied the same question:

Looking at a large dataset comprising all the papers published in 2003 by over 6k journals in various fields (i.e. 249k papers in the life sciences alone), the authors take all the papers those papers cited and examine what the distribution of citations look like,

> The Ortega hypothesis predicts that highly-cited papers and medium-cited (or lowly-cited) papers would _equally_ make references to papers with a medium impact (papers in the 50th or 75th percentile). The Newton hypothesis would be supported if the top-level research is more frequently based on previously highly-cited work (papers in the 99th percentile) than that medium-level research cites highly-cited work. If scientific advancement is a result of chance processes (the Ecclesiastes hypothesis), no systematic association between the impact of cited and citing papers is expected.

They find that this is indeed the case, which they further add supports the case for concentrating the funding in science. They repeat the analysis for various fields (which is weaker for the social sciences; and they try to explain why), here's the result for the life sciences:

![image-20210112094455330](https://nintil.com/images/2021-01-11-newton-hypothesis/image-20210112094455330.png)

An example of how to read this: If you take all the cited papers that papers that were published in 2003 that ended up in the 99th percentile of citations (By 2010) ended up citing then over 50% of those were to top (1%) papers and less than 10% of lower 90% of the distribution were cited. If one accepts that top papers are "the best" then this is evidence that "the best" only relies on "the best" research done in the past: Giants build on top of other giants, not on top of the masses, to the extent that citations are a proxy for good work. A caveat here is that the authors did not exclude reviews; and reviews plausibly are very cited and cite the most cited work by design. But reviews are a small fraction of all published papers, so this bias is there but probably not enough to change the results. (In the top 100 of all papers ever in Web of Science, [none](https://www.nature.com/news/the-top-100-papers-1.16224) is a review).

## The Matthew effect

Highly cited scientists cite other highly cited scientists. Is this because of prestige bias, or because they are acknowledging truly good work? The affirmative answer to this is known as the Matthew effect ("the rich get richer") and was first proposed by RK Merton[2](https://nintil.com/newton-hypothesis/#sidenote-2)

\[2\]. This is the guy that coined the terms 'unintended consequences', 'role model', and 'self-fulfilling prophecy' and the father of the Nobel-winning Merton in the Black-Scholes-Merton model

[Azoulay](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1755) (again!) et al. (2013) has a recent paper on this; specifically the question examined is whether becoming an HHMI Investigator (again!) affects the citation patterns of _previous_ work, compared to a synthetic sample (If you have read my post on a different [Azoulay](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih) paper you know how this goes). Of course it would be hard to disentangle the Matthew effect from genuine good work if the new work they publish is more cited: They get both the prestige of the award and the means that come with it and both could contribute to more cited work. But for _past work_ , getting the award should not really raise the citation count by much; sure maybe some scientist missed this work and the award now gives it extra visibility. They do find a small effect that tends to wash out relative to controls over time, perhaps 10 years later. The effect is one extra citation on average. When they split the papers published by when they were published (relative to getting the HHMI award), they find that articles published 2 years and before prior to the award stay unchanged, most of the effect is driven by articles published immediately the year before getting the award; those get 3 more citations on average, and this effect diminishes over time so that 10 years after the appointment this becomes one citation; the authors attribute this to the award reducing the uncertainty of newer work by revealing that the winner has done good science as examined by a panel of experts; older work may be perceived as more solid and already cited whereas new work may be found faulty in the future. Consistent with this story, work of younger scientists is more likely to benefit from this effect, as older scientists can be more readily assessed by their peers from their longer track records. So maybe there is not much of a Matthew effect! In we average over many scientists, and look at the long term over their publication careers, the citation analysis in the previous section would continue to hold.

Li and Agha ([again](https://nintil.com/grant-peer-review)!), as part of the same work also try to see whether the association between higher scores given by the NIH study sections and higher citation performance later could be mediated by prestige. If the Matthew effect is there, it could manifest itself in two ways:

1.  More prestigious scientists get higher scores
2.  Conditional on a grant being funded and for a given score, prestigious scientists are cited more often.

A sample causal diagram is here:

![causal](https://docs.google.com/drawings/d/e/2PACX-1vRWntHKTe6sQ499MYOvqjTNpe8X5ebRaVH3WrVJkJI4zAxCCkI21bmJRyusy5tOg0GKIPrtQqFxMVgq/pub?w=641&h=232)

The paper tries to control for prestige by using dummy variables for having a PhD, an MD, or both, and how many years a PI has been in academia. These are highly imperfect as proxies of prestige, but in any case controlling for this does not change the association between scores and citations. They also control for a set of things that sound closer to "prestige", including past funding and institution of origin, finding that this does not alter citation performance much.

> Specifically, we include variables controlling for whether the PI received NIH funding in the past, including four indicators for having previously received one R01 grant, two or more R01 grants, one NIH grant other than an R01, and two or more other NIH grants. To the extent that reviewers may be responding to an applicant’s experience and skill with proposal writing, we would expect the inclusion of these variables reflecting previous NIH funding to attenuate our estimates of value-added. We find, however, that including these variables does not substantively affect our findings. Finally, in Model 6, we also control for institutional quality, gender, and ethnicity, to capture other potentially unobserved aspects of prestige, connectedness, or access to resources that may influence review scores and subsequent research productivity. Our estimates again remain stable: comparing applicants with statistically identical backgrounds, the grant with a 1-SD worse score is predicted to have 7.3% fewer future publications and 14.8% fewer future citations (both P < 0.001).

The authors can only say that prestige doesn't matter for science in general in a black box way as they don't have a model that could disentangle how quality of work and prestige play into the score, but given that overall prestige doesn't change the correlation, either a) neither of 1) and 2) is important or b) they have effects in opposite directions (Prestige reduces the odds of getting a high score and prestige increases the odds of higher citation performance, or viceversa such that they cancel out). I'm willing to accept the first interpretation, that prestige doesn't matter that much on the aggregate.

In the previous Azoulay [HHMI](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15466/w15466.pdf) paper they do have a model to try to predict who becomes a HHMI researcher (Table 4) but the model doesn't have that much predictive power nor can allow us to disentangle prestige from quality at the selection level.

Another way of looking at prestige and its impact is examining what happens when a scientist gets a Nobel Prize which is, presumably, the most potent status boost one can get as a scientist. [Li et al. (2020)](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsif.2020.0135) examine what happens to citations both in general (after the Nobel) as well as prior to getting it. Contrary to what one may expect, the average citations per paper laureates get _goes down_ after the prize by around 11%, a dip that eventually recovers! In parallel with that winners author more papers in different fields (perhaps they feel like now they can explore new topics, given that they have proven themselves to the utmost extent). Li et al. do not claim these are causally related. This work also gives some indirect evidence for citations being predictive of success, given that laureates during their early careers _had a more than sixfold increase over the comparison group in terms of the rate of publishing hit papers, defined as papers in the top 1% of rescaled 10 year citations in the same year and field_.

There is one paper I found that shows a Matthew effect in a different context. I think this is not good evidence for the existence of such an effect _in general_. Bol et al. ([2018](https://www.pnas.org/content/115/19/4887)) report that in the Netherlands, there is a main source of funding for young scientists, the Innovation Research Incentives Scheme. The study compares the fate of those that were above the threshold (and got the grant) and those that did not get the grant at all but were close to getting it. Those that get that grant taken as a whole get 3x the funding of those that do not, measured 8 years after the award; and they are twice as likely to earn a full professorship. This is partly (10-26% of the effect) caused by the nonwinners being less likely to even try to get more funding in the future. Note that there is the same problem I noted for the comparison group in the NIH Director's award review [here](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih), despite this paper comparing scientists that were slightly below and slightly above the cutoff threshold, the scientists in those two groups are almost a standard deviation apart in citations, number of publications, or h-index. Digging into the supplemental data, looking at citations (because ultimately impact is measured by citations and not by how much money they got), the difference between the group that almost got the grant and the one that failed to get it by a slim margin is reduced; the authors actually say there is no difference at all! The fact that the rank correlates with citations also seems to suppose the fact that peer review panels can identify top talent. So this is not incoherent with the analysis above.

## Conclusion

The Newton hypothesis seems true, as far as citations are concerned: science is advanced by a small elite. This is not just "Einstein-level" breakthroughs, the small elite may not be 0.01% but 1-5% of the total number of practicing scientists. Even 10% would still cohere with the idea of scientific elitism. Citations at least on a first pass do seem to correlate with "good science" both casually (Highly cited classic papers) and by assessment of peers (Nobel prize panels; Nobel-winning papers are highly cited, and cite highly cited research). One can always make the critique that citations are not objectively measuring the worth of a piece of work. But what would? Yes, citations happen for all sorts of reasons (Including to garner favor with reviewers or other labs that might be more inclined to cite the paper if they are cited as well) but chiefly they happen because the work cited did influence the paper. There are various levels of epistemic nihilism one can go, culminating into the "We can't ever know who or what will be successful" so we should fund everyone equally, maximally equalizing funding. I don't agree with this, and will discuss scientific egalitarianism and lotteries in the next part of this series. I think this thinking can play a role in how science is organized, but not the only role.

In the first post of the series I said that the idea of funding a select few scientists and giving them extra resources (As the HHMI/Pioneer award do) depends on various assumptions: a) That we can pick the winners to some extent and b) That in fact there are winners (Or scientists that contribute disproportionately)

So far the evidence supports b) and to a lesser extent a). If discriminating between good and best were costless, any better-than-random selection process should be preferred to randomization. But when the former is costly (peer review requires peers to spend time doing the review), then it can be the case that the costs of the system outweigh the benefits. Ultimately the question of what's the ideal setup to allocate funding and select proposals will depend on this cost/benefit calculation. This calculation is most likely field dependent. The same is true for the kind of work that gets funded; biology will probably benefit from a lot of "fund projects" whereas physics will relatively benefit more from "fund people". If Smolin and Braben are right and the number of people that deserve this special treatment is small then potentially with a tiny sliver of total funding it would be possible to achieve outsized returns.

The Cole & Cole paper toys with the idea that cutting science funding (and/or concentrating it) could be beneficial. This possibility is real and worth exploring, even experimenting with on a limited scale. This of course will be highly controversial: It is easier to add funding unequally than subtract it unequally.

In the next post I will consider evidence for an alternative (on its extreme form) or complementary (in a weaker form) approach to the elitist "fund a small elite very well": using lotteries.
