# To listen well, get curious

---

## 正文

[![](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/shawarma_hu8d3539d292afb8dcad4d284a93a9ab60_28658_828x278_resize_q75_box.jpg)](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/shawarma.jpg "full size")

[来源](https://twitter.com/thecassiecao/status/1276506378388017155/photo/1)

一条常见的 interacting-with-people 建议是：“人们 complain 时，往往并不想要 help，只想让你 listen！”

例如，_Nonviolent Communication_ 中写道：✻✻ _Nonviolent Communication_，第 7 章。

> 对于需要 empathy 的人来说，我们擅自认为他们想要 reassurance 或“fix-it” advice，往往会让他们感到 frustrating。

_Active Listening_ 中写道：†† [Active Listening](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/active-listening.pdf)，第 2 页

> 同样，advice 和 information 几乎总会被视为试图 change 一个人，因而成为其 self-expression 和 creative relationship 的 development 的 barriers。

在大多数关于 relationships、people management 等主题的书中，你都能找到类似的建议。

过去，我一直觉得这很傻。如果我向伴侣 complain，而她“只是 listens”，那我除了可能让她 empathetically sad 之外，什么也没做到。我向别人 complain 时，想要的是 _results_，而不是对着虚空发牢骚！‡‡ Empirically，我确实注意到，相比 giving advice，我通常通过 listening 得到了 better results。所以我 inferred，这条建议对别人是 true 的，但对我不是，因为别人其实并不想 fix 他们的 problems。（显然，我当时错了。）

“只要 listen”这条建议经常附带 tactical tips，比如“把别人说的话 reflect 回去，以证明你在 listening”。例如，看看 _Nonviolent Communication_ 中的这些 example dialogues：§§ _Nonviolent Communication_，第 7 章，Exercise 5.5、5.6 及 solutions。

> 甲：你怎么能对我说这种话？
> 
> 乙：你 feeling hurt，是因为你本来希望我答应做你 requested 的事吗？

或者：

> 甲：我对丈夫感到 furious。每当我需要他时，他都不在。
> 
> 乙：所以你 feeling furious，是因为你希望他陪在你身边的时间比现在更多吗？

我说这话时，对 _Nonviolent Communication_ 怀着极大的尊重，但这些话听起来像一个 [1970s-era chatbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA)。如果我是这两段对话中的甲，我接下来就会说：“对，你这个笨蛋——能不能把 nonviolence 调低几档？”知道有人正在对我逐项套用他们的 NVC checklist，我会感到 alienated。

* * *

最近，我明白了为什么人们总在给出这种看似古怪的建议。Good listeners _确实_经常把 words reflect 回去——但并不是因为他们在某本书里读到过这种做法。相反，这是一种 [cargo cult advice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_cult_science)：它教你 imitate good listening 的 surface appearance，却遗漏了真正重要的东西，也就是那个正在 _generating_ 这种 surface appearance 的东西。

这个 generator 就是 curiosity。

我对别人 listened 得最 effectively 的时候，是因为我有着强烈的 curious 心态——我在尝试 build 一种 _detailed, precise_ 的 understanding，弄清他们脑中正在发生什么。当一位朋友说“我对丈夫感到 furious。每当我需要他时，他都不在”，这一句话背后藏着大量的内容。她多久需要他一次？她需要他做什么？他为什么不在？他们谈过这件事吗？如果谈过，他说了什么？如果没谈过，为什么没有？

事实证明，[reality 中的 detail 多得令人惊讶](http://johnsalvatier.org/blog/2017/reality-has-a-surprising-amount-of-detail)，而这些 details 对于弄清 root problem 或 best solution 是什么，可能至关重要。所以，如果我想 help，就不能把这些 details 当作 black box：我需要[打开它，看看里面的齿轮](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B7P97C27rvHPz3s9B/gears-in-understanding)。否则，我提出的任何建议都会是错的——即使是对的，我和朋友之间也不会有足够的“shared language”，让建议被正确理解。

最近记忆中的几个故事：

-   我们开始在 Wave 实行 pair programming rotation 时，我建议，为了让 scheduling 更容易，我们指定一个进行 pairing sessions 的 default time。一位同事反对，说这似乎是 authoritarian 的。我非常困惑，但对方先前提过自己是 anarchist，所以我很想直接把这归结为 political disagreement，不再深究。不过，我还是试着让自己 curious，更深入地 explore 那些 generating 这一 disagreement 的“political” models。在花了很多工夫探究对方认为什么是 authoritarian、什么不是，以及为什么之后，我们才发现，之所以有 disagreement，是因为对方漏听了“default”这个词，以为我是在建议为 pair programming 规定一个唯一且 _mandatory_ 的时间。

-   我的伴侣 Eve 写了一篇关于 Polish 人对 sex 的 attitudes 的文章，其中一些细节让她的父母（也是 Polish 人）感到不快。父母告诉她这件事时，她起初因为必须与他们谈一谈、安抚他们而感到非常 stressed。我觉得她不必担心，谈话会顺利的，但当然，只是这么告诉她并没有多大帮助。于是，我唤起自己的 curiosity，问了许多问题，涉及她与父母的 relationship、父母彼此之间的 relationship，以及他们各自与 Catholicism 的 relationships 等等。谈话结束时，在想清楚其中涉及的所有 baggage 之后，Eve 同意了我的看法；她对即将到来的谈话的 attitude，也从大难临头转变成了 compassionate curiosity，想了解父母为什么会这么想。

-   我因为工作而 stressed，向 Eve complained 了一些让我感到 frustrated 和 stuck 的事。她没有 suggesting solutions，而是不停地追问更多 details，直到她差不多掌握了我的 mental state 的完整 snapshot。那时，她 observed，每次我提到 feeling sad，听起来都对自己充满 contemptuous 和 exasperated 的情绪。她 hypothesized，我没有给自己 permission to be sad。最后，我的 problem 的“solution”是她给了我一个大大的拥抱，让我靠在她肩上哭了一会儿，之后我立刻觉得没那么 stressed 了。

在每个例子中，“helper”都尽可能详细地尝试了解“complainer”的 reality——不只是 problem，还包括这个人整体的情况，以及眼前 issue 背后的其他一切。正是这一点，让他们能够真正 help。

我常常觉得，即使不知道所有这些 details，我的 understanding 也已经足以提供帮助。但每当我这样想时，通常都错了：我最后会基于 bad assumptions 给出 bad advice，而与我交谈的人不得不花上很多工夫与我争辩，correct 我的 bad assumptions。这让 conversation 显得 disfluent、adversarial，而不是 collaborative。

事实证明，这是 helping-conversations 中非常常见的一种 failure mode。我认为，正是它 generates 了本文开头那句老话：“有时人们不想要 help，只想有人 listened to 自己。”

我认为，这种 framing 实际上对 helper 太宽容，对 complainer 却是 uncharitable 的（因为它 assumes，对方竟然不在乎 solving 自己的 problem）。实际情况很可能是，你的 advice 很 bad，因为你没有真正 listen，而你没有真正 listen，是因为你还不够 curious。

* * *

当我对别人正在说的事情感到 curious 时，我确实经常用自己的话向他们 repeat 所说的内容。但这是因为我 genuinely curious，而不是因为我在自己的“做个 good listener” checklist 中勾选“reflect words”这一项。这意味着，我会用自己平时自然的说话方式来做这件事，而不是像 chatbot 一样 mimicking 对方。

以这种方式进行时，reflective listening 让人感到 validating，而不是 alienating。这是一种表明我非常在乎对方所说内容的方式。用自己的话表述他们的 idea，会让他们知道我已经 fully digested 它，也帮助我们 establish 一种可以用来谈论它的 shared language。这样一来，conversation 就会变得 fluent、collaborative，而不再是在 bad assumptions 与 corrections 之间来回曲折。

所以，正确的建议不是“更努力地 listen，再把一切 repeat 回去”——如果你只是在 imitating good listener 的 surface appearance，你就不会是 genuine 的。相反，要 humble，并让自己 curious！提醒自己，无论你听到了什么，它的背后都有大量 detail，并尽可能尝试 internalize 这些内容。做到这些之后，你的 advice 就更可能切中要害，你也能把它 communicate 得很 clearly。

## 原文

[![](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/shawarma_hu8d3539d292afb8dcad4d284a93a9ab60_28658_828x278_resize_q75_box.jpg)](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/shawarma.jpg "full size")

[source](https://twitter.com/thecassiecao/status/1276506378388017155/photo/1)

A common piece of interacting-with-people advice goes: “often when people complain, they don’t want help, they just want you to listen!”

For instance, _Nonviolent Communication_:✻✻ _Nonviolent Communication_, ch. 7.

> It is often frustrating for someone needing empathy to have us assume that they want reassurance or “fix-it” advice.

_Active Listening_:†† [Active Listening](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/active-listening.pdf), p. 2

> Similarly, advice and information are almost always seen as efforts to change a person and thus serve as barriers to his self-expression and the development of a creative relationship.

You can find similar advice in most books on relationships, people management, etc.

This always used to seem silly to me. If I complain at my partner and she “just listens,” I’ve accomplished nothing except maybe made her empathetically sad. When I complain at people, I want _results_, not to grouse into the void!‡‡ Empirically, I did notice that I usually got better results from listening than from giving advice. So I inferred that this advice was true for other people, but not me, because other people didn’t actually want to fix their problems. (That was wrong, obviously.)

Frequently the “just listen” advice comes with tactical tips, like “reflect what people said back to you to prove that you’re listening.” For instance, consider these example dialogues from _Nonviolent Communication_:§§ _Nonviolent Communication_, Chapter 7, Exercise 5.5, 5.6 and solutions.

> Person A: How could you say a thing like that to me?
> 
> Person B: Are you feeling hurt because you would have liked me to agree to do what you requested?

Or:

> Person A: I’m furious with my husband. He’s never around when I need him.
> 
> Person B: So you’re feeling furious because you would like him to be around more than he is?

I say this with great respect for _Nonviolent Communication_, but these sound like a [1970s-era chatbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA). If I were Person A in either of these dialogues my next line would be “yes, you dingbat—can you turn the nonviolence down a couple notches?” I’d feel alienated knowing that someone is going through their NVC checklist on me.

* * *

Recently, I realized why people keep giving this weird-seeming advice. Good listeners _do_ often reflect words back—but not because they read it in a book somewhere. Rather, it’s [cargo cult advice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_cult_science): it teaches you to imitate the surface appearance of good listening, but misses what’s actually important, the thing that’s _generating_ that surface appearance.

The generator is curiosity.

When I’ve listened the most effectively to people, it’s because I was intensely curious—I was trying to build a _detailed, precise_ understanding of what was going on in their head. When a friend says, “I’m furious with my husband. He’s never around when I need him,” that one sentence has a huge amount underneath. How often does she need him? What does she need him for? Why isn’t he around? Have they talked about it? If so, what did he say? If not, why not?

It turns out that [reality has a surprising amount of detail](http://johnsalvatier.org/blog/2017/reality-has-a-surprising-amount-of-detail), and those details can matter a lot to figuring out what the root problem or best solution is. So if I want to help, I can’t treat those details as a black box: I need to [open it up and see the gears inside](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B7P97C27rvHPz3s9B/gears-in-understanding). Otherwise, anything I suggest will be wrong—or even if it’s right, I won’t have enough “shared language” with my friend for it to land correctly.

Some stories from recent memory:

-   When we started doing a pair programming rotation at Wave, I suggested that, to make scheduling easier, we designate a default time when pairing sessions would happen. A coworker objected that this seemed authoritarian. I was extremely puzzled, but they’d previously mentioned being an anarchist, so I was tempted to just chalk it up to a political disagreement and move on. But instead I tried to get curious and explore more deeply whatever “political” models were generating that disagreement. After a lot of digging into what was or wasn’t authoritarian for them and why, it turned out the disagreement was because they’d missed the word “default” and thought I was suggesting a single _mandatory_ time for pair programming.

-   My partner, Eve, wrote a post about Polish attitudes about sex, with some details that upset her (Polish) parents. When her parents told her that, she initially got very stressed about having to have a conversation to calm them down. I thought she shouldn’t be worried and the conversation would be fine, but of course just telling her that wasn’t very helpful. Instead, I summoned up my curiosity and asked lots of questions about her relationship with her parents, her parents’ relationship with each other, each of their relationships with Catholicism, etc. By the end of the conversation, after thinking through all the baggage involved, Eve agreed with me, and her attitude about the upcoming conversation shifted from impending doom to compassionate curiosity about where her parents were coming from.

-   I was stressed by work and complained to Eve about some things that I felt frustrated and stuck about. Instead of suggesting solutions, she kept asking for more details until she had more or less a complete snapshot of my mental state. At that point, she observed that every time I mentioned feeling sad, I sounded contemptuous and exasperated with myself. She hypothesized that I wasn’t giving myself permission to be sad. The “solution” to my problem ended up being to give me a big hug and let me cry on her shoulder for a bit, after which I immediately felt much less stressed.

In each case, the “helper” tried to learn about the “complainer’s” reality in as much detail as possible—not just the problem, but the whole person and whatever else was behind the immediate issue. And that’s what made it possible for them to actually help.

It often feels like I understand enough to be helpful without knowing all those details. But when I think that, I’m usually wrong: I end up giving bad advice, based on bad assumptions, and the person I’m talking to ends up having to do a bunch of work to argue with me and correct my bad assumptions. That makes the conversation feel disfluent and adversarial instead of collaborative.

It turns out this is a really common failure mode of helping-conversations, which is what I think generates the old saw at the beginning of this post, that “sometimes people don’t want help, just to be listened to.”

I think that framing is actually too nice to the helper, and uncharitable to the complainer (in that it assumes they weirdly don’t care about solving their problem). What’s really going on is probably that your advice is bad, because you didn’t really listen, because you weren’t curious enough.

* * *

When I’m curious about what someone’s saying, I often do repeat things back to them in my own words. But it’s because I’m genuinely curious, not because I’m checking off the “reflect words” box in my “be a good listener” checklist. That means I do it in a way that sounds like my natural speech, instead of mimicking them like a chatbot.

When done this way, reflective listening feels validating rather than alienating. It’s a way of demonstrating that I care a lot about what someone has to say. Putting their idea into my own words shows them that I’ve fully digested it, and helps us establish a shared language in which to talk about it. That, in turn, makes the conversation fluent and collaborative, rather than a zigzag of bad assumptions and corrections.

So the right advice isn’t “listen harder and repeat everything back”—you won’t be genuine if you’re just imitating the surface appearance of a good listener. Instead, be humble and get curious! Remind yourself that there’s a ton of detail behind whatever you’re hearing, and try to internalize all of it that you can. Once you’ve done that, your advice will be more likely hit the mark, and you’ll be able to communicate it clearly.
