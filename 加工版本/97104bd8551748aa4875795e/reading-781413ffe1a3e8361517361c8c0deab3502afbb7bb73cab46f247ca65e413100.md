# Huemer contra Caplan: Why Bryan Caplan should abandon desert

---

## 正文

Bryan caplan 在最近的一系列 [文章](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/08/scott_on_victim.html)中为 desert 这一 concept 进行 defends。他说

> 然而，当人们因 committing \_major\_offenses 而 seriously suffer 时，我把这称为 [just deserts](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2006/04/defending_deser.html#)。

在这里，我将 argue，Caplan 的 philosophical views 与他关于 desert 的 views 相 contradict。具体来说，你不能既接受 Michael Huemer 的整体 ideas，又 accept desert。

按照 Huemerian view，也就是 moral realism，moral facts 是 true 或 false 的。于是我们可以问自己，人们为什么会 do wrong things。在 Huemerian 的 intuitionism view 中，moral beliefs 可以是 intrinsically motivating 的（与 Hume 相反，见 Ethical Intuitionism 的 sec. 7）。也就是说，知道“murder 是 wrong 的”，会给你一些不去 murder 的 reason。但在某个 situation 中，即使你知道这一点，你也可能无法 properly weight moral reasons against prudential reasons，因此可能由于这种 erroneous reasons calculation 而最终 doing wrong。所以，按照 Huemerian view，如果某人 does wrong，那就是因为 ignorance。我提出两个 thought experiments，以便从这里走向 Caplanian contradiction：

第一个 case 如下：假设世界将在一分钟后终结，而你有能力对一个大多数人会视为 evil 的人 inflicting 某种 appropiate punishment（也就是你认为 appropiate 的任何 punishment）；这个人就是 ISIS 的 leader Abu Bakr Al-Baghdadi。你会对 Al-Baghdadi inflict punishment 吗？Caplan 大概会说会。他会 deserve 被 punished，punish 他会是 right 的，他做过 wrong things。或者，假设有人正要 punish Al-Baghdadi，而你可以按一个按钮阻止他。我 argue，punish 他是 wrong 的，而且在第二个 case 中，你应该阻止那个 punisher。在这个 hypothetical 中，世界将在一分钟后终结，所以 punishing 他不会给其他人起到 example 的作用（因此不会有未来的 extremism reduction）。这唯一会 cause 的，就是让一个不知道该做什么才是 right 的人，在这个世界中承受 increased suffering。Punishing 那些 ignorant 的人或许能够 decrease 未来的 ignorance，但这个 thought experiment 中没有未来。

第二个、也更有力的 case 是这样：假设一个孩子不知道 bleach 是 toxic 的，因此看到朋友口渴，就从 bleach bottle 里倒出 liquid 给他，以为那只是某种气味古怪的 water。他的朋友喝了下去，结果死了。你会因为这个孩子 killing 了他的朋友而 blame 他吗？如果不会，现在假设这个孩子发现自己做了什么，正在哭泣。这个孩子 deserve punishment 吗？这个孩子现在知道自己的 mistake 是什么，这一 fact 会 make a difference 吗？对于上述问题，我的回答依次是不会、不会、不会。这里，孩子所 ignorant 的不是 moral facts，而是 natural facts。这种 reduced understanding，正是在大多数 legal systems 中，针对 children 的 punishments 通常会被 reduced，而且也存在 [negligent homicid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negligent_homicide)e 这样的事物的 cause。

或者，假设一个 North Korean guard 一生都被 NK propaganda 所 indoctrinated，认为在 concentration camps 中 punishing dissidents 没有问题。后来，当他得知 truth 时，他 repents：他承认了自己的 ignorance。我们为什么应该对那些 ignorant 于自身 ignorance 的人区别对待（除非是为了 desincentivize ignorance）？

上述 examples 并不是在 argue 他们所做的事情并非 wrong（那个孩子 murdering 他的朋友、Abu Bakr leading ISIS、那个 guard torturing）。那些事情确实是 wrong 的。我所 argue 的是，desert 无法与我们对 particular cases 的更 deep intuitions 相协调。一个 ignorant person 为什么会因为自己的 ignorance 而 deserve punishment？因为他没有留意让自己 informed 吗？但他之所以没有这样做，又是因为他 ignorant 于该做的 right thing 就是 decrease 自己的 ignorance。

Moral realism 或 desert，你不能两者兼得。

你可以在[这里](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2006/04/defending_deser.html#)更清楚地看到 Caplan 的 view，它 rests on 一个 confused intuition。请看：

> Olsaretti 在很大程度上 relies on 这样一个 Rawlsian premise：没有人 deserves 从 inborn talent 中 profit。当然，如果这是 right 的，free market 就显得很糟糕，恰恰因为它允许、甚至鼓励 talented people 出人头地。但这个 Rawlsian premise 确实很奇怪。例如，它 implies，smart students 不 deserve better grades，great athletes 不 deserve win，而 inventors 不 deserve 凭借自己的 ideas get rich。而[显然，他们是 deserve 的](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2005/08/the_economics_a_1.html)。

还有 [deserving and undeserving poor](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2012/02/how_deserving_a.html) 这一 idea，但这种“desert”可以从纯粹 consequentialist 的 point of view 来理解：帮助那些“deserving”的人，会比其他做法 creates better incentives。

好吧，Rawls 在这一点上基本是 right 的（而在其他几乎所有事情上都是 wrong 的）。如果把 Desert 理解为 merit 的 consequence，它就是衡量 achievement 相对于 initial conditions 的 efficiency 的 measure，而 achievement 的很大一部分取决于 initial conditions：genetics 和 family qualities（Caplan 也知道这一点）。不过，说 smart students 不 deserve better grades，确实有些奇怪。

顺便说一句，Rejecting desert 以及一个由 desert structured 的 society，也是 Nozick 所做的事情。

让我们探究一下这种古怪之处。首先，我想我们都会同意，better student 应该获得 better grades。但这是因为 academic institutions 的 rules 如此规定：如果你 do better，你就 entitled to better grades。事情未必一定要这样。假设一些 students 玩一个 game，他们必须 feign ignorance 才能 achieve low marks。good student 是 deserve higher marks（从而 lose the game），还是应该按照这个 game 的 rules，根据他 feigns ignorance 的表现来被 awarded marks？

我认为，关键 difference 是 entitlement（对某物 having a right，即使在这个 case 中有些 fuzzy）与 desert（某种 moral property，凭借它，一个 agent 的某个 feature 使这个 agent attain 某物成为 good 的事情）之间的 difference。某人不 deserve 某物，并不意味着他不 entitled to 它。我并不 deserve 我的 knowledge，Caplan 也不 deserve 他的。我们生来 smart，后来选择读书、学习。如果我们生来 less intelligent，还会这样做吗？大概不会。但由此 infer，knowledge 就 ought 被 socialiced，如同 Rawls 所希望的那样，也是完全 implausible 的（尽管他本人没有 draw 那个 inference，但可参见 Lomasky 的 Persons, Rights and the Moral community，p. 135-141）

我认为，理解 desert/entitlement difference，并理解关于 desert 的 consequentialist view，就是 Caplan 在不 modifying 自己整体 philosophical framework 的情况下 abandon 当前 views 所需要的。因此，你不必 embrace utilitarianism（我就不 embrace 它），也能 reject desert。Common sense morality 经过 reflection 后，会 rejects desert。

最后，我想请 Caplan 清楚说明，为什么即使无法起到 deterrent 的作用，criminals 也 ought 被 punished；或者，为什么 victims 对自己的 agressors 在 restitution 之外进一步[take revenge](https://twitter.com/bryan_caplan/status/261152390928027648) 会是 just 的。

## 原文

Bryan caplan defends the concept of desert in a recent series [of posts](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/08/scott_on_victim.html). He says

> When people seriously suffer as a result of committing \_major\_offenses, however, I call that [just deserts](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2006/04/defending_deser.html#).

Here, I will argue that Caplan's philosophical views contradict his views on desert. Concretely, you can't follow Michael Huemer's overall ideas  and also accept desert.

On the Huemerian view, which is moral realism, moral facts are true or false. We then may ask ourselves why do people do wrong things. In the Huemerian view of intuitionism, moral beliefs can be intrinsically motivating (against Hume, sec. 7 of Ethical Intuitionism). That is, knowing "murder is wrong" gives you some reason not to murder. But in a given situation, even if you know that, you may not properly weight moral reasons against prudential reasons, so you may end up doing wrong because of this erroneous reasons calculation. Therefore, on the Huemerian view, if someone does wrong it is because of ignorance. I offer two thought experiments to go from here to the Caplanian contradiction:

The first case is as follows: Suppose the world will end in one minute, and you have the ability of inflicting some sort of appropiate punishment (whatever punishment you consider appropiate, that is) on someone most people would regard as evil, namely Abu Bakr Al-Baghdadi, the leader of ISIS. Would you inflict punishment on Al-Baghdadi? Caplan would probably say yes. He would deserve to be punished, it would be right to punish him, he did wrong things. Alternatively, suppose that someone is about to punish Al-Baghdadi, and you can, with a button, stop him. I argue that it is wrong to punish him, and that you should stop the punisher in the second case. In this hypothetical, the world will end in a minute, so punishing him won't serve as an example to others (so no future extremism reduction). The only thing this will cause is increased suffering in the world to a man who did not know what was the right thing to do. Punishing the ignorant may decrease future ignorance, but there is no future in this thought experiment.

The second, and stronger case is this: Suppose a kid doesn't know bleach is toxic, and so, seeing his friend is thirsty, serves him liquid from a bleach bottle, thinking it is some kind of funny smelling water. His friend drinks, and as a consequence, he dies. Would you blame the child for killing his friend? If not, suppose now the child is now crying because he discovers what he did. Does the child deserve punishment? Does the fact of the child now knowing what his mistake was make a difference? I argue that no, no, and no to the above questions. The child was ignorant not of moral facts here, but of natural facts. This reduced understanding is the cause that in most legal systems, punishments are usually reduced for children, and that such a thing as [negligent homicid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negligent_homicide)e is also present.

Or suppose a North Korean guard, indoctrinated all of his life with NK propaganda, who sees no problem in punishing dissidents in concentration camps. Afterwards, when he learns the truth, he repents: He admits his ignorance. Why should we treat those ignorant of their ignorance different (Except to desincentivize ignorance)?

The above examples do not argue for the idea that they were not wrong in what they did (The kid murdering his friend, Abu Bakr leading ISIS, the guard torturing). They were. I argue for the idea that desert cannot be squared with more deep intuitions about particular cases. Why does an ignorant person deserve punishment for his ignorance? Because he didn't take care of being informed? But then, he didn't do so because he was ignorant that the right thing to do was to decrease his ignorance.

Moral realism or desert, you can't have both.

Caplan's view, which you may ore clearly see [here](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2006/04/defending_deser.html#) rests on a confused intuition. See this:

> Olsaretti relies heavily on the Rawlsian premise that no one deserves to profit from inborn talent. If this is right, of course, the free market looks awful, precisely because it allows and indeed encourages talented people to get ahead. But this Rawlsian premise is truly bizarre. It implies, for example, that smart students don't deserve better grades, that great athletes don't deserve to win, and that inventors don't deserve to get rich from their ideas. And [obviously they do](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2005/08/the_economics_a_1.html).

There's also the idea of [deserving and undeserving poor](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2012/02/how_deserving_a.html), but such 'desert' can be understood from a purely consequentialist point of view: helping the 'deserving' creates better incentives than otherwise.

Well, Rawls is basically right on that (And wrong on mostly everything else). Desert understood as a consequence of merit is a measure of efficiency of achievement over initial conditions, and large parts of achievement are down to initial conditions: genetics and family qualities (as Caplan is aware of). However, there is certainly something weird about saying that the smart students don't deserve better grades.

Rejecting desert, and a desert structured society is also something Nozick does, by the way.

Let's explore this weirdness. First, I think we will all agree that the better student should get better grades. But this is because the rules of academic institutions say so: If you do better, you are entitled to better grades. This doesn't necessarily need to be so. Suppose some students play a game in which they have to feign ignorance to achieve low marks. Does the good student deserve higher marks (And thus, lose the game), or should he be awarded marks according to the rules of the game, taking into account how well he feigns ignorance?

I think the key difference is the difference between entitlement (having a right to something, even if in this case is somewhat fuzzy), and desert (Some sort of moral property by virtue of which a feature of an agent makes it good for the agent to attain something). That someone does not deserve something does not mean someone is not entitled to that. I don't deserve my knowledge, nor does Caplan. We were born smart, and then we chose to read books and learn things. Would we have done the same had we been born less intelligent? Probably not. But it is also totally implausible to infer from there that knowledge ought then to be socialiced, as Rawls would want (even if he didn't draw that inference himself, but see Lomasky's Persons, Rights and the Moral community, p. 135-141)

I think understanding the desert/entitlement difference, and understanding a consequentialist view of desert is what Caplan needs to abandon his current views, without modifying his overall philosophical framework. Thus you don't need to embrace utilitarianism (I don't) to reject desert. Common sense morality, after reflection, rejects desert.

I would, concluding, want to ask Caplan to state clearly why, even if it won't serve as a deterrent, criminals ought to be punished, or why it is just for victims to[take revenge](https://twitter.com/bryan_caplan/status/261152390928027648) above and beyond restitution on their agressors.
