# Moral arguments

## 正文

_再次感谢 Gautam Mohan 带来的灵感与修改！_

前段时间，我[写过](http://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought)不同的 moral systems 为什么往往是 logically irreconcilable，以及为什么我们在 morals 上的分歧远比在 facts 上多：

> 不过，当我们思考的是 morals 而不是 facts 时，情况就有些不同了。我们仍然可以保留自己的 rules of inference：我们并不否认，一个人的 moral system 应当是“logical”的。但 logic 的 rules of inference 无法把一个“is”变成一个“ought”。Moral reasoning 需要一套 independent 的“ought”axioms，而麻烦正是从这里开始的。Factual reasoning 之所以 evolved，是为了帮助我们理解某种在其最 basic level 上不会改变的东西——那些 governing 世界如何 works 的 laws。但从 evolutionarily 的角度来看，moral reasoning 的存在主要是为了帮助 individual organisms 以一种对整个 species 而言 beneficial 的方式，在 complex social landscape 中 navigate。这个 landscape 在不断 shifting，也要难理解得多得多。因此，我们并没有像在 descriptive reasoning 上那样，converged 到一个单一的 evolutionarily favored moral system（如果这样的 optimal system 真的存在的话）。

> 这就是为什么我们会看到如此繁多的 moral systems，也解释了为什么像 [consequentialism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism) 与 [deontology](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontological_ethics) 之间这样的 normative debates，往往显得 fundamentally irreconcilable，而 factual debates 却并非如此。在 factual debates 中，我们的 species 对 rules 大体上是有共识的，但在 moral debates 中，我们就没有这样的好运了。

一个悲观的人可能会从这种 logical irreconcilability 得出结论，认为 debate ethics 根本毫无意义。如果一个人一开始的 moral axioms 就与你不同，你又怎么可能改变他的想法？你们根本没有可供讨论的 common ground！

不知是幸还是不幸，human brains 的一个重要 feature 是，它们实际上并不是 pure abstract entities of reason。一般人会接受许多并非 strictly logical 的 argument，例如 Argument from Aesthetics（“有人写了一本好书，其中有 X，therefore X”）、Argument from Warm Fuzzies（“X 让我觉得自己的 hindbrain 很不错，therefore X”），以及 Argument from General Awesomeness（“如果 X，岂不是很酷？Therefore，X！”）。

比如，我高中时有个朋友，和我一起上过一门 Russian literature 课。到学期结束时，他已经成了 Tolstoy 的 anarchist-pacifist philosophy 的坚定信奉者。这并不是因为 Tolstoy 对自己 beliefs 的 logical defense 格外 superior；而是因为他的 aesthetic treatment 精彩绝伦，极其 appealing。他能成为 Russian literature 巨匠并非没有缘由；如果赞同 Tolstoy 能让我的生活像他的书一样美好，我也会赞同他。

事实上，我认为，即使不是全部，绝大多数 successful moral arguments 都是由这些 non-logical techniques 构成的。我的第一反应是对此感到担忧。毕竟，在 descriptive reasoning 中，这样的 appeals 往往被视为一种“dark art”，用来 encouraging bias，并 promoting sophistry 而非 truth-seeking。

不过，仔细想想，我并不认为在 normative debates 中使用所谓的“dark arts”有什么不好。在那里，extra-logical appeals 确实就是我们拥有的全部。“truth”是更 fuzzier 的（如果它真的存在的话），而什么算是“bias”也更不明确。沿用[之前](http://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought)的 math analogy，这就像说服一个人换用另一套 set of axioms。在一个 system of reasoning 内，如果你 abandon 自己的 rules of inference，就会开始 contradicting 自己。但在 systems 之间 translating 时，rules of inference 甚至都没有任何 meaning，因此试图使用它们也就没有意义。

所以，如果你希望人们听取你的 moral statements，即使你平时重视 intellectual rigor，也避免 rhetoric，只要他们所处的是另一套 set of axioms，就不要用 logic 对他们强加说教。相反，只需让你的 ethics 显得更 viscerally appealing。这才是真正让人愿意倾听的方式。

## 原文

_Thanks again to Gautam Mohan for inspiration and edits!_

A while ago I [wrote](http://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought) about how different moral systems are often logically irreconcilable, and why we disagree so much more about morals than facts:

> When we’re thinking about morals and not facts, though, things change a bit. We’re still able to keep our rules of inference: we don’t deny that one’s moral system should be “logical”. But logic’s rules of inference can’t turn an “is” into an “ought”. Moral reasoning requires an independent set of “ought” axioms, and it’s here that the trouble begins. Factual reasoning evolved to help us understand something that, at its most basic level, doesn’t change—the laws governing how the world works. But evolutionarily, moral reasoning exists largely to help individual organisms navigate a complex social landscape in the a way beneficial to the species as a whole. This landscape is constantly shifting and much, much harder to understand. As a result, we haven’t converged on a single evolutionarily favored moral system (if such an optimal system even exists) the same way we have for descriptive reasoning.

> This is why we see such a massive profusion of moral systems, and why normative debates like [consequentialism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism) vs. [deontology](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontological_ethics) often seem fundamentally irreconcilable in a way that factual debates don’t. In factual debates, our species largely agrees on the rules, but in moral debates we have no such luck.

A pessimist might conclude from this logical irreconcilability that it’s pointless ever to debate ethics. If someone starts off with different moral axioms from you, how can you ever change their mind? You have no common ground to work with!

Fortunately or unfortunately, one important feature of human brains is that they’re not actually pure abstract entities of reason. Your typical person will accept many types of argument that aren’t strictly logical, such as the Argument from Aesthetics (“someone wrote a good book in which X, therefore X”), Argument from Warm Fuzzies (“X makes my hindbrain feel good about itself, therefore X”), and Argument from General Awesomeness (“Wouldn’t it be cool if X? Therefore, X!”).

For instance, I had a friend in high school who took a class on Russian literature with me. By the end of the semester, he was a firm believer in Tolstoy’s anarchist-pacifist philosophy. This wasn’t because Tolstoy’s logical defense of his beliefs was particularly superior; it was because his aesthetic treatment was brilliant and incredibly appealing. Not for nothing is he a giant of Russian literature; I would agree with Tolstoy too if it would make my life as beautiful as his books.

In fact, I think these non-logical techniques comprise most, if not all, successful moral arguments. My immediate reflex is to be worried by this. After all, in descriptive reasoning, such appeals are often regarded as a “dark art” for encouraging bias and promoting sophistry over truth-seeking.

On reflection, though, I don’t think it’s bad to use so-called “dark arts” for normative debates. There, extra-logical appeals are really all we have. The “truth” is fuzzier (if it exists at all), and what’s a “bias” is less clear. To continue with the math analogy from [before](http://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought), it’s like convincing someone to switch to a different set of axioms. Within the system of reasoning, if you abandon your rules of inference, you’ll start contradicting yourself. But when translating between systems, the rules of inference don’t even have any meaning, so there’s no point in trying to use them.

So if you want people to listen to your moral statements, even if you normally value intellectual rigor and avoid rhetoric, don’t browbeat them with logic if they live in a different set of axioms. Instead, just make your ethics seem more viscerally appealing. That’s how you actually get people to listen.
