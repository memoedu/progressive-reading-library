# Spending on yourself vs. charity

---

## 正文

当人们第一次听说 effective altruism 这一理念时，尤其是从 Peter Singer 这样的人那里听说时，往往会提出一个特定的 objection。大致是这样的：

> Peter Singer 提出，如果我看到一个孩子在池塘里溺水，即使我穿着 \$500 non-waterproof boots. He concludes that because of this, I should donate \$500 捐给 charity，这笔钱在那里可以挽救一个孩子的生命。但如果把这一点推到它的 logical conclusion，会怎么样呢？我会把所有的钱都花在 charity 上，住进纸箱里！而你不能指望我这样做！我想，我还是不听 Peter Singer 的了。

（我知道这是一个常见的 objection，因为我曾用它来 dismiss Peter Singer，长达两年，之后才意识到，他自己花钱买西装、房子之类的东西，可能是有 _理由_ 的。所以，如果你在和别人谈 effective altruism，要留意这一点！）

这个 objection 的问题在于，它对 optimizing 你的 donation 的 size 采取了非常 short-term 的看法，因为 earn money 是需要钱的。如果你今年把全部 income（以及 assets）都 donate 给 Against Malaria Foundation，然后住进纸箱里，那么你明年的 income 就会小得多，因为没有人愿意 hire 住在纸箱里的人。

事实上，几乎每一笔本来可以用于解决你自己生活中某个问题的 donation，都有一定的 opportunity cost，即使它所解决的问题并不直接花费你很多时间或金钱。这是因为，即使是相对较小的问题，如果你不解决它们，它们也会在你为之担忧时占用你的 attention。而 Attention 本来可以用于做 valuable 的事情，这些事情又可以 converted into money。

事实上，这个 rate 可能相当 high。例如，我最近买了第二台 monitor。如果我背着它，看到一个溺水的孩子，我会跳进池塘，救起孩子，弄坏这台 monitor。但之后，我会再去买一台 monitor，因为我意识到，如果我一直在 programming、documentation 和其他 windows 之间 switching back and forth，那些处于 background 的 windows 就会不断占用零星的 attention，让我在做 programming 这样的 high-value tasks 时表现差得多。我 calculate，考虑到我的 hourly wage，如果第二台 monitor 因为让我能 keep track of 更多事情，而使我的 programming 哪怕只改善 1%，它也会在不到一年内 pay for itself，所以这绝对是划算的。

当然，wealth 肯定存在某个 inflection point，到了那里，你就应该停止花钱解决自己的问题，因为那些问题实在太小，已经不值得这样做了。而且，我同意 Peter Singer 的看法：这个 point 比大多数人想象的要 lower（既然 day-to-day well-being 在 incomes 超过 \$75,000 后就 stops rising，[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/self-vs-charity/#fn:1) 我们或许可以 infer，人们花在自己身上的许多钱，对解决他们自己的问题毫无帮助，而且可以 redirected harmlessly）。但我认为，这个 inflection point 也比对 Singer 跳进池塘的 example 的 naive reading 所 imply 的要 higher 不少，而在介绍这个 argument 时，把这一点说清楚很重要。

## 原文

When people first hear about the idea of effective altruism, especially from someone like Peter Singer, there’s one particular objection that is often raised. It goes something like this:

> Peter Singer suggests that if I see a child drowning in a pond, I would probably jump in and save them even if I’m wearing $500 non-waterproof boots. He concludes that because of this, I should donate $500 to charity, where it can save a child’s life. But what happens if you take this to its logical conclusion? I would spend all my money on charity and live in a cardboard box! And you can’t expect me to do that! I guess I’m not going to listen to Peter Singer.

(I know this is a common objection because I used it to dismiss Peter Singer for two years before I realized that there might be a _reason_ that he himself spent money on things like suits and a house. So if you are talking to someone about effective altruism, be aware of this!)

The problem with this objection is that it takes a very short-term view of optimizing the size of your donation, because it takes money to earn money. If you donate your entire income (and assets) to the Against Malaria Foundation this year and go live in a cardboard box, then your income will be much smaller next year, because nobody wants to hire people who live in cardboard boxes.

In fact, pretty much every donation that would otherwise be spent on solving a problem in your own life has some opportunity cost, even if the problem it’s solving doesn’t directly cost you very much time or money. This is because even for relatively small problems, if you don’t solve them, they will take up your attention while you worry about them. Attention can otherwise be spent on doing valuable things, which can be converted into money.

In fact, the rate can be quite high. For instance, I recently bought a second monitor. If I were carrying it on my back and saw a drowning child, I would jump into the pond, save the child, and fry the monitor. But then I would go buy another monitor, because I realized that if I’m switching back and forth between programming, documentation, and other windows all the time, the ones in the background consume nagging bits of attention and I get a lot worse at doing high-value tasks like programming. I calculate that given my hourly wage, if my second monitor makes me even 1% better at programming because I can keep track of more things, it will pay for itself in less than a year, so it’s a definite win.

Now, there’s definitely some inflection point of wealth at which you should stop spending money to solve your own problems, because they’re so minor it’s just not worth it. And I agree with Peter Singer that this point is lower than most people think it is (since day-to-day well-being stops rising with incomes over $75,000,[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/self-vs-charity/#fn:1) we might infer that much of the money people spend on themselves doesn’t help at all with solving their own problems and could be redirected harmlessly). But I think this inflection point is also a fair amount higher than a naive reading of Singer’s diving-into-the-pond example would imply, and it’s important to make this clear when introducing this argument.
