# 对 Health 而言，Optimal 的 Exercise 量是多少？

---

## 正文

Physical exercise 是过上美好生活的一项 foundation。我们所有人都能从中 benefit。不论我们是否特别喜欢 running 或 lifting weights。

我们常常未能进行足够的 exercise。在现代生活中维持日常生活所需的 physical labor，低于我们达到 optimally healthy 所需的量。因此，除非我们确实喜欢某项 sport，否则往往会 move 得比应有的更少。

我们不仅 move 得比应有的更少，也比自己以为的更少。在[我最喜欢的 studies 之一](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797\(11\)00012-2/abstract)中，researchers 发现，当被问及是否达到了 physical activity 的 recommended guidelines（每周 150 minutes 的 moderate 或 75 minutes 的 vigorous physical activity）时，大多数 respondents 回答是。但当他们的 activity 通过 wrist-based accelerometers 进行 measured 时，实际达到的还不到百分之十。

鉴于此，现实是，对大多数人来说，最好的 exercise 量，就是他们实际能够 sustain 的量。每天 Running 很好，但如果做不到，walking 也不错，而且远比什么都不做好。

即使从实际角度来说，最好的 exercise routine 是你能轻松坚持的那一种，我仍觉得这种含糊其辞的说法不能令人满意。显然，exercise 很重要。但究竟有多重要？

Quantity 很重要。能让生活改善 0.1% 的事情，或许只是一个 rounding error——你可以为它提出理由，但它并不是什么大事。能让生活改善 50% 或 100% 的事情，则非常重要。忽视如此巨大的潜在改善，实在是惊人的浪费。

幸运的是，我们能做到的远不止含糊其辞。我们不仅可以 determine 多少 exercise 对 health 而言是 optimal，还可以根据你实际上有多喜欢 exercising，大致判断什么对你个人而言是 ideal。

## 对 Health 而言，Optimal 的 Exercise 量是多少？

[Arem 及其 colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25844730/) 将超过 600,000 人的 data pooled together，发现了下面这条 curve，展示了 exercise 量与 all-cause mortality 之间的 relationship：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise2.jpg)

关于这条 curve，有几点值得注意：

**首先，它 shows diminishing returns。**这意味着，从每周零 exercise 增加到一小时，比从每周一小时增加到两小时更为 advantageous。你进行 exercise 最少的时候，增加 exercise 最为重要。

**其次**，**benefits 似乎会 plateau。**在 standard guidelines 的大约五倍处，额外 exercise 的 benefits 似乎已经完全 flat。这意味着，如果你每周已有 750 minutes 的 moderate（或 375 minutes 的 vigorous）physical activity，再增加 exercise 并不 associated with 更好的 health outcomes。

这是很大的 exercise 量。如果我们把 vigorous exercise 视为 running，把 moderate exercise 视为 brisk walk（或者，如果你有这么大的 exercise 量，更可能是 jog），这就 equivalent 于每天 running 将近一小时，或每天 briskly walking 将近两小时。如果你有这么大的 exercise 量，很可能是出于 health 以外的原因。

**第三，benefits 究竟只是 flatten，还是在超过这一点后实际 become negative，仍存在一些 uncertainty。**从方向上看，这条 curve 似乎可能向后弯。换句话说，一旦达到 ultramarathoners 的 training volume，甚至超过它，你的 health 实际上可能变得更差。

然而，这有一点误导性。处于如此 extreme 水平的人太少，以至于 confidence intervals 变得 broad，我们对 measurement 的 certainty 也更低。进行那么多 exercising，可能对你而言是 unhealthy。但正如 study 的 authors 指出的，我们实际上不知道这是否属实。这一切想说的是，即便某些 exercise 量在普通人看来很 extreme，也没有明确证据表明它们对 health 比 optimum amount 更差，而且它们很可能仍比我们大多数人通常进行的量更 healthier。

因此，根据这些 data，对 human health 而言绝对最好的 exercise 量，很可能处于 standard guidelines 的 3-5x 范围内。

## 但对 Health 而言的 Optimal，真的对你最好吗？

这个 analysis 的问题在于，它忽视了 tradeoffs。除非你是 [Bryan Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Johnson)，否则除了尽可能长寿，你也看重其他事情。你如何 compare 多活一个月的 value，与比如更大的事业成就、更深厚的友谊，或者多看几次日落的 value？

我并不自认为能够 resolve 人生中 values 的 incommensurability。但如果换一个问题，我们就能让 analysis 变得 tractable：与 exercise 将 replace 的 time/activities 的 average value 相比，你有多看重花在 exercising 上的时间？

既然进行 exercise 的一个主要原因是 live longer，我们就可以把这看作一种 trade-off。你现在进行 exercise，放弃了原本会在那段时间里做的其他事情。但作为 consequence，你现在能 live longer（on average），因此在一生中重新获得其中一部分时间。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise4.jpg)

考虑到 trade-offs 的这一作用，更好的 analysis 应该考察，每增加一点 physical activity，你会 gain 多少“life”，然后从中 subtract：相较于你的时间的 next best use，exercise 对你的 worth 少了多少。

这有点棘手。Arem 使用的是 [hazard ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_ratio)，而不是“years of life gained”，因此，如果不知道所研究的 population 的 baseline survival rates，我们就无法轻易 estimate 它。幸运的是，[Moore 及其 colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23139642/) 的一篇较早的 paper 已经替我们完成了这个 calculation，尽管使用的 data set 更为 limited。

通过一点 math[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18466 "Moore et al. 提供了 hazard ratio 为 0.59 时 lifespan gained 为 4.5 years 的 estimate。为了将 hazard ratios convert 为 lifespan gained，我使用了 equation Y(V) = 4.5 * (-ln(HR(V))/-ln(HR(.59))，其中 Y 是 years of life added，V 是 exercise 的 volume，而 HR(V) 是 associated amount of exercise 所对应的 hazard ratio。")，我们可以以 Moore 的 paper 为 anchor，将 Arem 的 hazard ratios convert 为 years of extra life gained：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise3r.jpg)

我们需要把它 convert 为一张 graph，展示 additional exercise 所带来的 additional benefit：[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18466 "Arem 及其 colleagues 使用了 binned data，这让事情稍微复杂了一些。为了简化，我将每个 bin 的 midpoint 用作 fixed point，然后将从每个 level 到下一个 level 所预期的 lifespan 的 gain 视为 linear function。你可以在<a href=\"https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/marginal-lifespan-gain.xlsx\">这里</a>查看我的 calculations。")

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise5.jpg)

在这张 graph 上，我们可以 superimpose exercise 的“costs”。Evaluating 这些 costs 有些棘手，因为它取决于你 intrinsically 有多看重（或讨厌）exercise 的 experience。

你可以设想，exercise 是 purely instrumental 的——一种达到目的的手段。如果有人挥一下魔杖，让你获得 exercise 的 benefits，却使你在整个 experience 期间完全 unconscious，且不留下任何 memories，你也会觉得无所谓。

在这种 scenario 中，exercise 是纯粹的 experiential waste，我们只是为了 benefits 才做它。在这一 assumption 下，exercise 的 optimal point 在每周 225 至 375 minutes 之间。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise6.jpg)

你也可以根据自己有多讨厌或喜欢 exercise 来 scale 这一结果。在下方，我画了一些 graphs，考虑了这样一种可能性：你花在 exercising 上的时间，其 intrinsically valuable 的程度是 next-best alternative 的 80%，在这种情况下，optimal point 在每周 375 至 625 minutes 之间。

我也考虑了 opposite case：exercise 具有 negative intrinsic value，以至于你宁愿放弃人生中的一小时，也要避免三十分钟的 exercise。在这种情况下，optimal point 位于每周 75 至 225 minutes 之间（不过更接近后者）。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise7.jpg)

从这个 analysis 中可以看出，只有假定人们真的、_真的_讨厌 exercise，他们通常进行的 exercise 量才是“rational”的。比如，从零到一点点 exercise，所 associated with 的 lifespan 的 increase，大约是 exercise 本身 time cost 的六倍。这意味着，你必须宁愿每花三十分钟 exercising，就失去 total lifespan 中将近三个半小时，这才算是一个 rational choice！

我不知道你怎么想，但在我看来，这种厌恶程度相当强烈。即使是我讨厌的活动，比如去牙医那里进行 cavity filled，我也不确定自己会主动放弃远超其两倍时长的 equivalent life experiences。

另一个复杂之处在于，我假定你的 exercise 是 moderate intensity。对很多人来说，这 equivalent 于 brisk walking 或 slow jog。对于 vigorous activity，standard efficiency 大约是它的两倍，因此这一点 _a fortiori_ 成立。 [3](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-3-18466 "有一些<a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41057301/\">新出现的 evidence</a>表明，harder workouts 的重要程度，可能甚至超过上文所说的 2x。如果属实，那么 running、HIIT、sports 或其他使你进入 heart rate range 的 upper range 的 activities，其 efficiency 可能还会更高。确实，鉴于这一点，你得极其讨厌它们，才会连每周做一点都不愿意。")

## 如果你想慢慢生活、年纪轻轻就死去呢？

一种异议可能是，这个 analysis 将当下的时刻与遥远未来所经历的时刻 equally weights。我认为这是一个 justifiable assumption。

在考虑 financial decisions 时，我们经常使用 [discount rate](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/discountrate.asp) 来 reduce 遥远未来的 money 的 value。但我不确定在这里这样做是否 justified，原因有几个：

1.  **由于 opportunity cost，Money 在未来的 value 更少。**现在 invested 的 Money 会 compound，这意味着，同样的 income 在今天比在一年后 worth more。对于 lifespan，并不存在 comparable mechanism。
2.  **Exercise 的 benefits 不止于 longevity。**Exercise improves [mood](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/18/1203)、[cognition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12229068/)、[sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34163383/)、[stress](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4013452/)，甚至你的 [sex life](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38288234/)。其中许多 benefits 会在不久之后出现，因此，一个更完整的 analysis 需要 account for 这些不应被大幅 discounted 的 benefits。
3.  **从 Philosophically 的角度说，我们未来经历的时刻是否真的比当下的时刻 worth less，并不明确。**毕竟，当未来到来时，我们也会同样经历它。

当然，显而易见，exercising 的许多 benefits 在遥远的未来才 accrue，而 exercising 的 costs 却是 immediate，这一事实_确实_会 impact 人们的 decision making；其他 prudent behaviors 也是如此，例如为 retirement 而 saving、不 excessively drinking 或 smoking，等等。但这种 imprudence 通常是 irrational 的。[4](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-4-18466 "将 discount rate 纳入考虑的一个 legitimate reason 是 risk。当下放弃的时间是 certain。未来获得的时间只是一个 average，并非 guaranteed。不过，这篇文章已经够长了，所以我就把加入 discount rates 留给读者作为一道 exercise。")

## 如果 Exercise 如此重要，我们为什么还会为它苦苦挣扎？

Exercise 在这里是一个很好的例子，但绝非唯一的例子。生活中有许多 actions，与它们的 costs 相比，它们能够 dramatically increase 我们生活的 quality（或 quantity），但我们往往很难 adopting 或 sustaining 它们。

我们常常将未能抓住所有这些唾手可得的机会，归因于 information 的缺乏、willpower 的薄弱，或 personal preferences。

 但我倾向于认为，问题不止于此。从 Cognitively 的角度说，exercise 属于这样一类 problems：我们没有良好的 built-in mechanisms 来 solving 它们。因此，要年复一年地成功坚持 exercise routine 并收获 benefits，就需要 constructing systems、habits、beliefs 和 attitudes，以弥补 instincts 不能帮助我们的地方。

培养这些 systems 的方法有很多，但它们全都需要一种基本要素：一定程度的 committed focus，至少在一段时间内是如此。花一点时间 creating 这个 system，对它进行 refining 和 stabilizing，它就会在你的一生中持续带来 benefits。

这就是我为期一年的 [Foundations](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/foundations-project/) 项目的目标；在这个项目中，我每个月都会专注于十二项不同的生活 foundations 中的一项，其中包括 fitness。我的想法是，将精心选择的 habits 与 deep research 结合起来，我就能 build systems，从而获得更好的 fitness、relationships、productivity、reading 等方面的 benefits。

## 原文

Physical exercise is a foundation for living a good life. It’s something all of us benefit from doing. Whether or not we particularly like running or lifting weights.

We often fail to exercise enough. The physical labor needed to get by in modern life is below what we need to be optimally healthy. So, unless we actively enjoy a sport, the tendency is to move less than we should.

Not only do we move less than we should, we also move less than we think we do. In [one of my favorite studies](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797\(11\)00012-2/abstract), researchers found that when asked whether they met the recommended guidelines for physical activity (150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous physical activity per week), a majority of respondents said yes. But, when their activity was measured using wrist-based accelerometers, fewer than ten percent actually did.

Given this, the reality is that, for most people, the best amount of exercise to get is the amount they can practically sustain. Running every day is great, but if you can’t do that, walking is good too, and it’s a lot better than doing nothing.

Even if, practically speaking, the best exercise routine is the one you can comfortably stick with, I find this hand-waviness unsatisfying. Clearly, exercise is important. But how important?

Quantity matters. Something that improves your life by 0.1% is maybe a rounding error—you could make the case for it, but it isn’t a big deal. Something that improves your life by 50% or 100% is very important. To ignore such a big potential improvement is insanely wasteful.

Fortunately, we can do a lot better than hand-waving. We can determine not only how much exercise is optimal for health, but also give some sense of what would be ideal for you personally—given how much you actually enjoy exercising.

## What’s the Optimal Amount of Exercise for Health?

[Arem and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25844730/) pooled together data from over 600,000 people and found the following curve showing the relationship between amount of exercise and all-cause mortality:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise2.jpg)

There are a few things to note about this curve:

**First, it shows diminishing returns.** That means going from zero to one hour of exercise per week is much more advantageous than going from one hour to two hours of exercise per week. Getting more exercise matters most when you do it the least.

**Second**, **benefits appear to plateau.** At around five times the standard guidelines, the benefits of additional exercise appear to be completely flat. Meaning if you’re getting 750 minutes of moderate (or 375 minutes of vigorous) physical activity per week, adding more exercise isn’t associated with better health outcomes.

This is a lot of exercise. If we consider vigorous exercise to be running and moderate exercise to be a brisk walk (or, more likely if you’re getting this amount of exercise, a jog), this is the equivalent of running nearly an hour every day, or briskly walking for nearly two hours every day. Chances are, if you’re getting this much exercise, you’re doing it for reasons other than health.

**Third, there’s some uncertainty as to whether the benefits merely flatten or if they actually become negative beyond this point.** Directionally, it looks like the curve may bend backwards. In other words, once you reach the training volume of ultramarathoners and beyond, your health may actually get worse.

However, this is a bit misleading. There are so few people at such an extreme that the confidence intervals become broad, and we have less certainty in the measurement. It may be that exercising that much is unhealthy for you. But, as the study authors point out, we don’t actually know if this is true. All of this is to say is that even amounts of exercise that look extreme to a normal person aren’t clearly worse for health than the optimum amount, and it’s probably still healthier than the amount most of us typically get.

So, based on these data, the absolute best amount of exercise for human health is probably in the range of 3-5x the standard guidelines.

## But is the Optimal for Health Actually Best for You?

The problem with this analysis is that it ignores tradeoffs. Unless you’re [Bryan Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Johnson), you value things other than living as long as possible. How do you compare the value of an extra month of life to, say, greater professional success, deeper friendships or watching more sunsets?

I don’t pretend to be able to resolve the incommensurability of values in a human life. But we can make the analysis tractable if we ask a different question: how much do you value the time spent exercising, compared to the average value of the time/activities that exercise would replace?

Since a major reason to exercise is to live longer, we can consider this as a kind of trade-off. You exercise now, giving up something else you would have done in that time. But, as a consequence, you now live longer (on average), so you regain some of that time over the course of your life.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise4.jpg)

Considering this role of trade-offs, a better analysis would look at how much “life” you gain for each incremental bit of added physical activity, and then subtract from it however much less the exercise was worth to you than the next best use of your time.

This is a little tricky. Arem used [hazard ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_ratio), not “years of life gained”, so without knowing the baseline survival rates of the population studied, we can’t easily estimate it. Luckily, an earlier paper by [Moore and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23139642/) did this calculation for us, albeit using a more limited data set.

With a little math[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18466 "Moore et al. provides the estimate of 4.5 years of lifespan gained with a hazard ratio of 0.59. To convert hazard ratios into lifespan gained, I used the equation Y(V) = 4.5 * (-ln(HR(V))/-ln(HR(.59)) with Y being years of life added, V being the volume of exercise and, HR(V) being the hazard ratio with the associated amount of exercise."), we can convert Arem’s hazard ratios into years of extra life gained, using the Moore paper as an anchor:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise3r.jpg)

We need to convert this into a graph that shows the additional benefit for additional exercise:[2](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-2-18466 "This is complicated somewhat by the fact that Arem and colleagues used binned data. To simplify things, I used the midpoint of each bin as a fixed point, and then considered the gain in lifespan expected from going from the each level to the next level as a linear function. You can see my calculations <a href=\"https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/marginal-lifespan-gain.xlsx\">here</a>.")

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise5.jpg)

On this graph, we can superimpose the “costs” of exercise. Evaluating the costs is tricky, because it depends on how much you intrinsically value (or hate) the experience of exercise.  

You could imagine that exercise is purely instrumental—a means to an end. If someone waved a magic wand that let you get the benefits of exercise, but rendered you completely unconscious for the duration of the experience with no memories, you’d be indifferent to it.  

In this scenario, exercise is pure experiential waste that we only do for the benefits. Under this assumption, the optimal point of exercise is between 225 and 375 minutes per week.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise6.jpg)

You can also scale this by how much you hate or enjoy exercise. Below, I’ve drawn graphs which consider the possibility that your time spent exercising is 80% as intrinsically valuable as your next-best alternative, in which case the optimal point is between 375 and 625 minutes per week.

I’ve also considered the opposite case, where exercise has negative intrinsic value, such that you’d rather give up an hour of time in your life to avoid thirty minutes of exercise. In this case, the optimal point lies between 75 and 225 minutes per week (although closer to the latter).

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-much-exercise7.jpg)

What we can see from this analysis is that the amount of exercise people typically get is only “rational” if we imagine that they really, _really_ hate exercise. For instance, going from zero to a little bit of exercise is associated with roughly six times the increase in lifespan compared to the time cost of the exercise itself. That means you’d have to prefer losing nearly three and a half hours of your total lifespan for every thirty minutes spent exercising for this to be a rational choice!

I don’t know about you, but that seems pretty strong to me. Even for activities I hate, like getting a cavity filled at the dentist, I’m not sure I would actively give up much more than twice the amount of equivalent life experiences.

An additional wrinkle is that I’m assuming your exercise is moderate intensity. For a lot of people this is equivalent to brisk walking or a slow jog. For vigorous activity, the standard efficiency is roughly twice that, so this applies _a fortiori_. [3](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-3-18466 "There’s some <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41057301/\">emerging evidence</a> that harder workouts may matter even more than the 2x claim above. If this is true, then the efficiency for running, HIIT, sports or other activities that put you in the upper range of your heart rate range might be even more efficient. Indeed, given this, you’d have to have an extreme dislike of them to not want to do at least a little every week.")

## What if You Want to Live Slow and Die Young?

One quibble might be that this analysis weights present moments equally with ones experienced in the far future. I think it’s a justifiable assumption.

When we consider financial decisions, we often use a [discount rate](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/discountrate.asp) to reduce the value of money in the far future. But I’m not sure that’s justified here, for a few reasons:

1.  **Money has less value in the future due to opportunity cost.** Money invested now will compound, meaning the same income is worth more today than a year from now. There is no comparable mechanism with regards to lifespan.
2.  **Exercise has benefits beyond longevity.** Exercise improves [mood](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/18/1203), [cognition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12229068/), [sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34163383/), [stress](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4013452/) and even your [sex life](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38288234/). Many of those benefits occur in the near future, so a fuller analysis would need to account for these benefits that ought not to be discounted much.
3.  **Philosophically speaking, it’s not clear that future moments we experience are genuinely worth less than present ones.** After all, when the future comes, we will experience it just the same.

Of course, it’s obvious that the fact that many of the benefits of exercising accrue in the far future while the costs of exercising are immediate _does_ impact people’s decision making, as it does with other prudent behaviors like saving for retirement, not drinking or smoking excessively and so on. But that imprudence is usually irrational.[4](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/06/15/optimal-exercise/#easy-footnote-bottom-4-18466 "One legitimate reason for including a discount rate is risk. Time given up in the present is certain. Time gained in the future is only an average and is not guaranteed. Still, this essay is getting long enough already, so I’ll leave adding in discount rates as an exercise for the reader.")

## If Exercise Matters So Much, Why Do We Struggle With It?

Exercise is a good example here, but it’s hardly unique. Life is full of actions that can dramatically increase the quality (or quantity) of our lives compared to their costs, but which we often have a hard time adopting or sustaining.

We often blame lack of information, weakness of willpower, or personal preferences as reasons we fail to capture all this low-hanging fruit.

 But I tend to think the problem is more than that. Cognitively speaking, exercise belongs to a class of problems that we do not have good built-in mechanisms for solving. And thus, to successfully stick to an exercise routine year after year and reap the benefits requires constructing systems, habits, beliefs and attitudes that make up for where our instincts fail us.

There are many ways to cultivate such systems, but the basic ingredient in all of them is a certain degree of committed focus, at least for a time. Spend a little bit of time creating the system, refining and stabilizing it, and it will continue to deliver benefits throughout your entire life.

This was the goal of my year-long [Foundations](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/foundations-project/) project, where I spent each month focusing on one of twelve different foundations for life, including fitness. The idea was that, with a combination of carefully chosen habits and deep research, I could build systems that would yield the benefits of better fitness, relationships, productivity, reading and more.
