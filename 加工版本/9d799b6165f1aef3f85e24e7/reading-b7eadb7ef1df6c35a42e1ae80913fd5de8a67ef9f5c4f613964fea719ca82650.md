T. E. Lawrence 是一位英国军人、外交官和作家，他拥有一种被他的一位传记作者称为 capacity for enablement 的能力：他 enabled 别人运用他们一直拥有、却直到结识他才意识到的 abilities。人们与 Lawrence 接触，有时仅仅几分钟，便会 activated 自己原本不知道拥有的 talents，人生也随之改变，而且往往改变得十分剧烈。

我们大多数人都有过类似的经历。一位睿智的朋友或熟人会深入了解我们，看见某种 latent aspiration，甚至可能比我们自己看得更清楚。他们还会看出，我们 capable of taking action，能够 achieve 那个 aspiration，并如同举起一面镜子，让我们清清楚楚地看见自己的 capability。平日里的 self-doubts 不再作声，我们笃定地意识到：“是的，我能做到。”

这是 volitional philanthropy 的一个 instance：帮助 expand 人们能够 act on the world 的方式的 range。

那些将这种 volitional philanthropy 行为 scale up 的 institutions 令我着迷。

Y Combinator 以 startup incubator 闻名。朋友们开始参加早期的 batches 时，我注意到，他们回来后往往有所改变。即使他们的 company failed，他们也更像自己，更 confident，更 capable of acting on the world。这是这个 program 送给 participants 的礼物 [1](https://michaelnotebook.com/volitional/index.html#fn1)。因此，我将 Y Combinator 视为 volitional philanthropists。

我曾在 Recurse Center 担任过一年的 Research Fellow。它是一个为期三个月的“面向 programmers 的 writer's retreat”。它是 unstructured 的：没有人告诉 participants 应该做什么。相反，他们必须自行选择 projects，并为自己的道路安排 structure。这颇具挑战。但四处摸索以及选择道路时的困难，对于 growing 一个人的 sense of choice，以及对 choice 的 responsibility 而言至关重要。因此，创造这样的空间，同样是一种 volitional philanthropy。

有些 institutions 认为自己在从事 volitional philanthropy，实际上却并非如此。许多 educators 相信自己是。在 non-compulsory education 中，情况往往确实如此。但 compulsory education 是围绕对 volition 的 fundamental denials 建立的：student 被 denied choice，不能选择自己身处何地、正在做什么，以及与谁一起做。这些 choices 被 denied 后，compulsory education 就会 shrinks 并 constrains 一个 student 的 sense of volition，无论它在其他方面显得多么 progressive。

帮助一个人 develop 其 volition，这个 notion 有某种 paradoxical 之处。就其 nature 而言，volition 不是能够被 given 的东西；它必须被 taken。我也不认为“加油啊”式的鼓励有多大帮助，因为它丝毫不能 permanently expand 接受者的 sense of self。相反，我猜测，关键在于某种 listening-for-enablement，以此帮助人们发现自己内在或许尚未意识到的东西。然后，诚实而切合实际地解释自己看到了什么（同时明白自己可能判断有误）。值得探究的是，我们如何在自己身上 develop 这种 ability，又如何在能够将它 scale up 的 institutions 中 develop 它。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

_Originally [posted to Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=224735391342335&id=100014176268390) on April 11, 2017. Facebook shows it only to people who are logged in, so this is a copy, taken from the Internet Archive's [capture of October 19, 2020](https://web.archive.org/web/20201019120830/https://www.facebook.com/permalink.php?story_fbid=224735391342335&id=100014176268390)._

T. E. Lawrence, the English soldier, diplomat and writer, possessed what one of his biographers called a capacity for enablement: he enabled others to make use of abilities they had always possessed but, until their acquaintance with him, had failed to realize. People would come into contact with Lawrence, sometimes for just a few minutes, and their lives would change, often dramatically, as they activated talents they did not know they had.

Most of us have had similar experiences. A wise friend or acquaintance will look deeply into us, and see some latent aspiration, perhaps more clearly than we do ourselves. And they will see that we are capable of taking action to achieve that aspiration, and hold up a mirror showing us that capability in crystalline form. The usual self-doubts are silenced, and we realize with conviction: “yes, I can do this”.

This is an instance of volitional philanthropy: helping expand the range of ways people can act on the world.

I am fascinated by institutions which scale up this act of volitional philanthropy.

Y Combinator is known as a startup incubator. When friends began participating in early batches, I noticed they often came back changed. Even if their company failed, they were more themselves, more confident, more capable of acting on the world. This was a gift of the program to participants [1](https://michaelnotebook.com/volitional/index.html#fn1). And so I think of Y Combinator as volitional philanthropists.

For a year I worked as a Research Fellow at the Recurse Center. It's a three-month long “writer's retreat for programmers”. It's unstructured: participants are not told what to do. Rather, they must pick projects for themselves, and structure their own path. This is challenging. But the floundering around and difficulty in picking a path is essential for growing one's sense of choice, and of responsibility for choice. And so creating that space is, again, a form of volitional philanthropy.

There are institutions which think they're in the volitional philanthropy game, but which are not. Many educators believe they are. In non-compulsory education that's often true. But compulsory education is built around fundamental denials of volition: the student is denied choice about where they are, what they are doing, and who they are doing it with. With these choices denied, compulsory education shrinks and constrains a student's sense of volition, no matter how progressive it may appear in other ways.

There is something paradoxical in the notion of helping someone develop their volition. By its nature, volition is not something which can be given; it must be taken. Nor do I think “rah-rah” encouragement helps much, since it does nothing to permanently expand the recipient's sense of self. Rather, I suspect the key lies in a kind of listening-for-enablement, as a way of helping people discover what they perhaps do not already know is in themselves. And then explaining honestly and realistically (and with an understanding that one may be in error) what it is one sees. It is interesting to ask both how to develop that ability in ourselves, and in institutions which can scale it up.
