# Comments on Rules for a Flat World (Gillian Hadfield)

## 正文

我最近读完了[这本书](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0199916527/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0199916527&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21)，这里写一些读后感。

这本书的 summary 大致如下：Law 是 useful 的，因为它为 social interaction 提供了 stable framework。没有 Law，我们所知道的 civilisation 就不可能存在。但如今，Law 的 practice 已经变得 bloated、expensive 而且 slow。至少在 US 是如此。因此，这又算是 Scott Alexander 在最近一篇文章中指出的 cost disease 的一个 instance。Hadfield 将其归咎于某种可以比喻为 lawyerly coup d'état 的事情。

solution 是什么？Law 中的 Markets。没有 [Randy Barnett](http://www.bu.edu/rbarnett/TOC.htm) 那么远，但比 US 现有的情况更进一步。她提出的 solutions 并不 radical，因为它们在 UK 和 EU 已经在一定程度上得到实行。这些 proposals 主要是

-   通过 increasing arbitration 和 private courts 的 role，来 lessen State 的 court system 的 burden
-   允许 creation parallel bodies of law，尤其是 contract law，但不包括 criminal law，供 individuals 和 corporations 遵守。States 仍会为这些 codes of law 被允许的内容 set the boundaries。
-   允许 private regulators 的 existence，使 companies 可以选择自己想要的 regulator。一个 meta-regulator 将 oversee 这些 regulators 是否在 working adequately
-   真正 liberalise law services 的 market，允许许多不同的 entities 来 offer legal services，而不只是 lawyers
-   让 legal sector 更容易发生 innovation（显然，这在 US 相当困难，但其他国家并非如此）

她还强调，试图将 West 的 legal systems 原样照搬到 poorer countries，恐怕不会奏效，因为这些 systems 的 run 是 expensive 的，而且并不 adapted to 当地的 realities。

提出的这些 solutions 完全说得通，而且如我所说，它们已经在一定程度上得到实行。需要作出调整、以获得 XXI century 的 legal system 的，是 US。

例如，在 Europe，你可能见过那个著名的 [CE logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CE_marking)。它的意思是，该 product complies with European Comission 的 Directives。

怎样才能获得 CE logo？这些 Directives specify 了各种方式（取决于具体的 Directive）。有些 products 接受 self-certification：company checks product，如果他们认为它 meets Directive，就加上 CE logo 并出售。另一些情况下，获得 CE 需要 external party。但这些 entities（notified bodies）不必是、通常也不是 government 或 public entities。它们是收取费用、check 一个 product 是否 complies with regulation 的 agencies（例如 Spain 的 AENOR、UK 的 BSI）。这些 agencies 又由一个 State level regulator 来 audited。随后，像 Spain 的 ENAC 或 UK 的 UKAS 这样的 State level regulators 会 regulate 这些 notified bodies。这些 agencies 甚至不必是 public 的。UKAS 就是一家与 British government signed 了 memorandum of understanding 的 private non-profit。

这些 Directives 与一般的 regulation 也很不一样。它们不是告诉你该怎么做，而是告诉你要做什么。我的意思是，你可能会被告知，为了 design safe glass，你需要 follow 这种或那种 manufacturing procedure、use 这种或那种 material，等等。regulation 通常就是这样运作的。EU Directives 却会说：‘glass 在 typical use 期间不得 break’。第二种 approach 的 advantage 在于，它不会 stiffle innovation：只要你的 glass 不 break，你想怎么做都可以。它确实会给 regulator 带来额外工作，因此通常会随 Directive 一起 issued 一份 implementation guide，只要 followed，就能 guaranteed approval。否则，manufacturer 就需要针对所 followed 的 process 来 collect evidence，并将其 submit 给 regulator 以获得 approval，这需要更多时间。这被称为 New Approach。例如，如果你 manufacture lifts，那么 [Directive 2014/33/EU](http://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32014L0033&from=EN) 就 applies to 你。欣赏一下这份 Directive 的 vagueness：

## 原文

I recently finished reading [this book](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0199916527/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0199916527&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21) and here there are some thoughts about it.

A summary of the book is something along these lines: Law is useful because it provides a stable framework for social interaction. Without Law, no civilisation as we know is possible. But currently the practice of Law has become bloated, expensive, and slow. At least in the US. So this would count as one more instance of what Scott Alexander pointed out in a recent post: cost disease. Hadfield blames it on something that metaphorically could be described as a lawyerly coup d'état.

The solution? Markets in Law. Not as far as [Randy Barnett](http://www.bu.edu/rbarnett/TOC.htm), but a bit beyond what exists in the US. The solutions she proposes are not radical, as they are already in place to some extent in the UK and the EU. The proposals are essentially

-   To lessen the burden of the State's court system by increasing the role of arbitration and private courts
-   To allow the creation of parallel bodies of law, especially contract law, but not criminal law for individuals and corporations to abide by. States would still set the boundaries of what those codes of law would be allowed.
-   To allow the existence of private regulators, so that companies could choose which regulator they want. A meta-regulator would oversee that the regulators are working adequately
-   To really liberalise the market for law services, allowing many different entities to offer legal services, not just lawyers
-   Make it easier for innovation to happen in the legal sector (Apparently this is quite difficult in the US, but not so other countries)

She also emphasises that attempts to copypaste the legal systems of the West onto poorer countries won't probably work, as those systems are expensive to run, and not adapted to the local realities.

The solutions proposed make all the sense in the world, and as I said they are already in place to some extent. It is the US that has to adapt to get a XXI century legal system.

For example, in Europe, you might have seen the famous [CE logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CE_marking). What it means is that the product complies with the European Comission's Directives.

How do you get the CE logo? The Directives specify various way (It depends on the Directive). Some products accept self-certification: the company checks the product and if they think that it meets the Directive, they include the CE logo and sell it. Other times, an external party is required to get CE. But these entities (notified bodies) need not be and usually aren't government or public entities. They are agencies (Like AENOR in Spain, BSI in the UK) that check whether a product complies with the regulation, for a fee. In turn these agencies are audited by a State level regulator. State level regulators like ENAC in Spain  or UKAS in the UK then regulate the notified bodies. These agencies need not even be public. UKAS is a private non-profit that signed a memorandum of understanding with the British government.

These Directives are also quite different from your average regulation. Instead of telling you how to do things, they tell you what things to do. By this I mean that you could be told that in order to design safe glass, you need to follow this or that manufacturing procedure, use this or that material, etc. This is how regulation typically works. EU Directives instead say 'The glass shall not break during typical use'. The advantage of the second approach is that it doesn't stiffle innovation: As long as you glass doesn't break, you can do whatever you want. It does create extra work for the regulator, which is why typically along with a Directive, an implementation guide is issued such that if followed, approval is guaranteed. If not, the manufacturer needs to collect evidence about the process followed and submit it to the regulator for approval, which takes more time. This is known as the New Approach. For example, if you manufacture lifts, the [Directive 2014/33/EU](http://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32014L0033&from=EN) applies to you. And then, harmonised standards, [EN 13411-7:2006+A1](https://krok.biz/info/file_download/134/BS_EN_13411-7_2006.pdf) are far more complete.

A similar system is in place in the UK for the regulation of the legal profession. To provide legal services one need not go to law school. You only need to be accepted by one of many entities that act as regulators for the profession.

The lawyerly coup that I mention earlier is the reason why the US is not like this. Initially, anyone could provide legal services.

In a way it follows the story of other regulations that exist today in the US (Like the minimum wage or regulations of medical practise). They are today rationalised in one way (raising wages or ensuring quality), but they didn't come to exist to fix existing problems, but for more self-interested reasons.
