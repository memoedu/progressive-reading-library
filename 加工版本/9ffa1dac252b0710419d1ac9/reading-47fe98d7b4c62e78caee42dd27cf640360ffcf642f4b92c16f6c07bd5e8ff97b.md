# To stressed-out altruists

---

## 正文

最近，effective altruism community 中有几个人提到，一直想着 EA 会让人相当 stressful。如果 effective altruism 让你 anxious，我觉得你很可能对自己太苛刻了，应该试着更多地 compartmentalize。刻意对自己宽容一些，或许看起来并不 optimal；如果你是一个 perfect utilitarian robot，也许确实如此，但你是一个运行在 imperfect brain 上的 human mind，更重要的是别把自己弄坏了。如果你感到 stressed，下面这些考虑可以帮助你把对自己的要求稍微降低一些。

首先，“effective”并不是 binary-valued。有些人似乎因为达不到我喜欢称为“perfect utilitarian robot agent”的标准而责备自己：按照这个标准，你要努力表现得像一个 completely rational actor，人生唯一关心的事情就是 maximizing aggregate utility。通常，拿来作比较的那个想象中的人，也不必为 social contact 或 emotions 之类烦人的 mental 事情操心（这里只举几个例子）。实际上没有人达到这个标准，原因想必显而易见。因此，从这个意义上说，没有人是“perfect”的 effective altruist，也就没有理由责备自己。

比如，假设你花了一些时间和心仪的人相处，而没有把这些时间拿来榨出最后一点 donated income。当然，这也许是 [satisficing](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisficing)，而不是 optimizing；如果你读了很多 Less Wrong（或某些 EA 内容），你可能会觉得这是什么十恶不赦的罪过。但并不是所有 satisficing 的实例都一样。你放弃那几个小时所构成的逃避，与——嗯，仔细想想，与几乎所有其他事情相比——都是 epsilonically small。比如，有那么多人把钱 donate 给 Make-a-Wish 而不是 AMF，或者支持“为孤儿手绘个性化故事”（没开玩笑，这是一个 Harvard 学生团体）而不是 CEA。

所以，你责备自己的程度应当根据 difference 的 size 来 properly calibrated，也就是说，它也应该是 epsilonically small。如果你为小事过度责备自己，你的 degrees-of-fault scale 上就没有足够的空间来 distinguish 小事与糟糕的想法了：前者比如在 Internet 上浪费时间，后者比如完全放弃 effective altruism。而且，既然完全放弃能让你避开一大堆困难的事情，你就可能 burn out 并退出，那就糟了。所以，如果你要为某件事责备自己，一定要让责备的程度与它造成的 disutility 成 proportion（而且一定要对这个 disutility 有 accurate conception！）。

不过，我觉得，为小事责备自己根本就不是什么好主意。如果你努力做的事情让你 stresses you out，我觉得它从一开始就连“optimal”都算不上。或者，它对 perfect utilitarian robot agent 来说也许是 optimal，但对 human 来说肯定不是 optimal。你的 brain 对事情有一些你无法控制的 reactions，比如，当你试图做太多事情时会 stressed out，或者没有 social contact 时会 depressed。试图对抗这些 reactions，或者像 perfect utilitarian robot agent 那样 suppress 它们，其 effective 程度，大概就像试图用头撞出一条隧道来穿过一座山：如果你是一个完美的撞山机器人，那确实是最直的路线，但你（以及我们其他所有人）根本不是那样构造的。

在这种情况下，正确的做法是绕过那座山。同样，试图在 stress 下做成事情并不 sustainable，既会消耗日常的 productivity，也会带来 burning out 的 risk。我认识的许多最 productive、最 effective 的人，几乎完全不 stressed。

我认为，许多 anxious EAs 的 stress，罪魁祸首是缺少 [compartmentalization](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentalization_%28psychology%29)。当然，要真正理解 effective altruism 的思想，你需要_不要_过度 compartmentalize——例如，我有一个室友，他在 abstract 层面认同 altruism 的理念，却没有为此做任何事情，因为他把 brain 的“abstract morality” module 与“decide what to do” module separates 开了。因为这类事情，compartmentalization 经常名声不好，而且往往确实如此：它让人们 evade cognitive dissonance，却不必真正面对自己 conflicting ideas。但 compartmentalization 并不总是 maladaptive：如果你做得太少，那么无论你关心什么，它都会完全吞噬你，让你痛苦到 non-functional 的地步。

Effective altruism 所 requires 的 compartmentalization 比普通人现有的更少，因此，以普通人为基准进行 calibrated 的 standard effective altruism discourse，会试图 break down compartmentalization。但你很可能不是普通人。如果你因为 effective altruism 而 stressed out，_不要理会 standard EA discourse，多一些 compartmentalize_！例如，[Jeff](http://www.jefftk.com/index) 和 [Julia](http://www.givinggladly.com/)（以及许多其他 earners-to-give）刻意决定，对花在自己身上和 charity 上的钱进行 [compartmentalize their spending](http://www.jefftk.com/p/keeping-choices-donation-neutral)，这样他们就不必为每一个决定苦苦纠结。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/stress/#fn:1)

当然，这种想法也可以被用来 rationalize 过于 self-interested 和不够努力。我不知道有什么好规则，能够判断什么时候我们可以降低对自己的要求，什么时候应该咬牙坚持，去 maximize aggregate utility。但如果你觉得自己正在与自己交战，如果努力成为 effective altruist 在 psychologically 层面很困难，那你几乎肯定是在相反的方向上犯了错。如果你就是这样，不要总担心自己是在 satisficing 而不是 optimizing：有时候，satisficing _就是_ optimizing。

## 原文

Lately several people in the effective altruism community have mentioned that thinking about EA all the time can be pretty stressful. If effective altruism is making you anxious, I think you’re probably being far too hard on yourself, and you should try to compartmentalize more. It may not seem optimal to deliberately go easy on yourself, and it might not be if you’re a perfect utilitarian robot, but you’re a human mind running on an imperfect brain and it’s more important not to break anything. If you’re stressed, here are some considerations for holding yourself to a somewhat lower standard.

First of all, “effective” is not binary-valued. It seems like some people are berating themselves for not meeting what I like to call the “perfect utilitarian robot agent” standard, where you try and behave like a completely rational actor whose sole concern in life is maximizing aggregate utility. Typically the imaginary person of comparison doesn’t have to worry about pesky mental things like social contact or emotions, either (to name a few). Nobody actually meets this standard, for hopefully obvious reasons. So in that sense, nobody is a “perfect” effective altruist, and it doesn’t make sense to fault oneself.

Suppose, for instance, you spend some time hanging out with a romantic interest instead of taking those hours and squeezing out the last drop of donated income. Sure, maybe it’s [satisficing](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisficing) and not optimizing, and it you read a lot of Less Wrong (or a lot of certain EA stuff) you might get the impression that that’s some kind of cardinal sin. But not all instances of satisficing are created equal. The amount by which you are copping out by giving up those few hours is epsilonically small compared to—well, come to think of it, compared to almost everything ever. Like all the people who donate to Make-a-Wish instead of AMF, or “hand-illustrated personalized stories for orphans” (no joke, this is a Harvard student group) instead of CEA.

So the amount you berate yourself should be properly calibrated to the size of the difference, i.e., it should also be epsilonically small. If you fault yourself too much for the little things, you’ll run out of room on the degrees-of-fault scale to distinguish between small stuff, like wasting time on the Internet, and terrible ideas, like giving up on effective altruism entirely. And since giving up entirely would let you avoid a whole bunch of difficult things, you might burn out and quit, which would suck. So if you’re going to fault yourself for something, make sure you do it in proportion to the disutility it caused (and make sure you have an accurate conception of what that is!).

But I don’t think it’s even a good idea to fault yourself for small things at all. If what you’re trying to do stresses you out, I don’t think it’s even “optimal” to begin with. Or maybe it’s optimal for a perfect utilitarian robot agent, but it’s certainly not optimal for a human. Your brain has reactions to things that you just can’t help, like getting stressed out when you try to do too much, or depressed when you have no social contact. Trying to fight against these reactions, or suppress them like a perfect utilitarian robot agent would, is going to be about as effective as trying to get past a mountain headbutting a tunnel through it: if you were a perfect mountain-headbutting robot it would be the straightest route, but you (and the rest of us humans) just aren’t built like that.

The right answer in that case is to walk around the mountain instead. Similarly, trying to accomplish things under stress isn’t sustainable, and imposes both a drain on day-to-day productivity and a risk of burning out. Many of the most productive and effective people I know are barely stressed at all.

I think the culprit of stress for many anxious EAs is lack of [compartmentalization](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentalization_%28psychology%29). Now, to really understand the ideas of effective altruism, you need _not_ to compartmentalize too much—for example, I have a roommate who buys the idea of altruism in the abstract, but doesn’t do anything about it because he separates his brain’s “abstract morality” module and its “decide what to do” module. Because of things like this, compartmentalization has an often-deserved poor reputation for letting people evade cognitive dissonance without really coming to terms with their conflicting ideas. But compartmentalization isn’t always maladaptive: if you do it too little, then whatever you care about completely consumes you to the point of non-functional misery.

Effective altruism requires less compartmentalization than the average person has, so standard effective altruism discourse, which is calibrated against the average person, tries to break down compartmentalization. But you probably aren’t the average person. If you’re stressed out about effective altruism, _ignore the standard EA discourse and compartmentalize more_! For instance, [Jeff](http://www.jefftk.com/index) and [Julia](http://www.givinggladly.com/) (and many other earners-to-give) made the deliberate decision to [compartmentalize their spending](http://www.jefftk.com/p/keeping-choices-donation-neutral) on themselves and on charity, so that they weren’t agonizing over every decision.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/stress/#fn:1)

Of course, this kind of thinking can be used to rationalize being too self-interested and not working hard enough. I don’t know of a good rule for when it’s OK for us to lower our standards for ourselves and when we should suck it up and maximize aggregate utility. But if you feel like you’re at war with yourself, if trying to be an effective altruist is psychologically difficult, you’re almost certainly erring in the opposite direction. If that’s you, don’t worry all the time that you’re satisficing instead of optimizing: sometimes, satisficing _is_ optimizing.
