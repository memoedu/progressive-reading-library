# Newsletters 内部的同质性：Homogeneity Within Newsletters

---

## 正文

下面是一篇关于 Substack 的**未经编辑的客座文章，作者是 [Applied Divinity Studies](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/) 的作者**。虽然我未必赞同下文的所有说法，但我觉得这篇文章很有意思，而且作者请我把它作为客座文章发表在这里。

先说一些我自己的想法，作为开场：

回顾我在 RSS 中阅读的东西时，我首先注意到，我看的确实是_我的 RSS reader_（Feedly），而不是我的 mailbox。RSS 是一种文明的方式，让你 following 来自你喜欢的不同 sites 的 updates，把它们 aggregating 到一个与 mailbox 分开的 central place，在那里可以保存起来以后再读，必要时还可以按 source 来 organized。Newsletters——那些涌进你 inbox 的东西——在我看来很野蛮。我们已经解决了 diverse sources 的 consumption 的 centralization 问题，怎么后来大家又集体忘记了？？也许，对于没有 corporate blog 的 business，newsletter（里面有关于 sender 的消息）更说得通，但那些 spammy inbox periodicals 并不能很好地替代 blogs-via-RSS。支持 newsletters 的一个 argument 是，它让 senders 能够 track metrics，了解谁在读他们的作品、读了多少，等等。比如在 Nintil，虽然我有一些 Google Analytics metrics，但如果你通过 RSS 阅读而不 open the page，我就得不到任何信息；不过我并不在意，我甚至不能随口说出每个月有多少人读 Nintil；我使用 Analytics，是为了知道我的 blog 在哪里被 cited，这样就能跟进别处关于我所写内容的讨论。Nintil 不是为我精心 cultivate 的 audience 而写的。如果你喜欢 Nintil，尽管阅读就好（这里没有 paywalls）；如果不喜欢，也可以读别的。因此，我没有必须一直写 blogposts 的压力，也不会仅仅为了 push 当天的 post，就急着从日常偶然发生的事情里硬挤出一篇 post。

但并不是每一种 publication 都是 self-expression 的载体。Monetized newsletters 就是为了赚钱。这没什么不好。但 paid content 似乎总落在少数几类里：不是 politics（hooliganism 保证你会付钱），就是 businessy/financey pieces（因为阅读也许能以某种方式让你获得优势，或者至少你这样以为）。谁会为 Nintil 付钱，而且是定期付钱呢！如果你喜欢 Nintil，可以通过 Patreon 捐款。Nintil 也是 public goods 的 production（比如我的 Longevity FAQ；我在那里免费向所有人开放长达数月的 research），而这同样与 paid model 不相容。

我读 substacks 吗？我使用 [kill the newsletter](https://kill-the-newsletter.com/) 把 newsletters convert 成规规矩矩的 RSS feeds，翻看我存下的 feeds，可以看到 Nadia Eghbal 和 Anton Howes 的。考虑到我上面说的，这并不令人意外：我的兴趣不在于 operating businesses 或 following current events [1](https://nintil.com/substack-milquetoast/#sidenote-1)，而这些似乎正是 Substacks 的主要内容。当然，我也没有特别努力地翻找它们的内容，看看有没有我更喜欢的东西。

\[1\]. 你应该已经注意到了，Nintil 是 covidposting 荒漠中的一片绿洲 :)

所以，我无论如何都算不上合格的 Substack critic；首先，我不太了解各种 'stacks，其次，它们似乎 target 的 audience 并不包括我。这就像让我写 beer reviews（在我尝来，所有 beers 都同样难喝）；问题在我，而不在各种 beers。

话虽如此，写作获得报酬还是有其可取之处的，而且有 platforms 让感兴趣的人按照这种 model 来 publishing，也是件好事。我在这里并不是提出 [Sandelian](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sandel/markets-morals) critique，我非常支持 [markets in everything](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Markets-without-Limits-Jason-Brennan/dp/0415737354)，但我也希望这个 blog 完全 unpaywalled（毕竟，我不是在向你卖 product），而且希望其他 blogs（或 newsletters）也如此；此外，我不认为 paywalling content 会带来更好的 quality，_除非_作者在 full time 做这件事。对于 supererogatory open blogging，是否存在 moral case？大概有吧。

现在，请看客座文章。希望你读得愉快。

> 这种 style 往往会是 strange、incorrect、overburdened 而 loose 的，却又几乎总是 strong 而 bold 的。Writers 会更急于 work quickly，而不是 perfect details。Short works 会比 long books 更常见，wit 会比 erudition 更常见，imagination 会比 depth 更常见。thought 将有一种 rude 而 untutored 的 vigor，呈现出丰富的 variety 和非凡的 fecundity。Authors 会更努力地 astonish，而不是 please；更努力地 stir passions，而不是 charm taste。

_Alexis de Tocqueville，《Democracy in America》（《论美国的民主》，1835）_

> 自 patronage 发明以来，churn 的 mathematics 是 niche artistic interests 遇到的最好的事情。

_Byrne Hobart，[理解 Netflix：Understanding Netflix](https://medium.com/@byrnehobart/understanding-netflix-5ab8f528f5b4)（2017）_

De Tocqueville 对 American style 的 indictment，同时也有同等分量的 exoneration。如果情况一直很糟，我们就很难把 society 的 decline 归咎于 new media。

然而，每当[一个新的 platform 声称自己是 the exception to the rule](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qpG11vFqglA)，就有必要反复强调，多少遍都不嫌多：这并不是一种好局面。

这篇文章我已经重写了三次，一次比一次 dogmatic。所以，我决定放弃所有华丽的 rhetoric，只冷静地解释 Substack 那始终 milquetoast 的 taste 背后的 structural mechanics。

## Newsletters 内部的同质性：Homogeneity Within Newsletters

最受欢迎的 substack writers 都有明确的 focus。The Dispatch 写 politics，Matt Stoller 写 monopolies，Bill Bishop 报道 China。

这不正常吗？毕竟，不能指望任何 writer 涵盖每一个 topic。在 disaggregation 的时代，我们应该阅读 experts 的作品，而不是 [casual polymaths](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/2020/09/28/polymath/) 的作品。

然而，这种表面上的 normality 掩盖了它在历史上的 peculiarity。

看看 SlateStarCodex，它是 Substack 出现之前最受欢迎的 blogs 之一。它的 posts 从 [political science fiction](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/17/slightly-skew-systems-of-government/)，到 [pharmacology](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/15/the-vision-of-vilazodone-and-vortioxetine/)，到 [artificial intelligence](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/10/the-obligatory-gpt-3-post/)，再到 [media economics](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/09/11/update-on-my-situation/)，无所不包。这并不是刻意挑选的组合，而是他最近几篇 posts 按 chronological order 排列的结果。

（或者看看 Nintil，它涵盖了从 [longevity](https://nintil.com/longevity)，到 [meta-science](https://nintil.com/better-science)，再到 [education](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma) 和 [decision theory](https://nintil.com/pascals-mugging) 的各种内容。）

Heterogeneity 对 blogs 行得通，因为你通过偶尔一次 viral hit 实现 grow，再通过偶尔一篇恰好打动某位 reader 的 post 获得 loyal readership。正如 SlateStarCodex 的作者[描述自己成功的原因](https://www.reddit.com/r/slatestarcodex/comments/3ygr2w/why_is_scott_alexander_sorta_popular/)时所说：

> 在 Granting 这些 premises 的情况下，也许是因为我写的东西很多？SSC articles 大约有 500 篇，每隔几天就有一篇。即使我并不比其他人更好，人们偶然看到一篇自己喜欢的文章、留下深刻印象并开始 following 我的机会，也多得多。

问题不仅仅是 topic 的 homogeneity，还在于 substance 的 homogeneity。如果你每周都必须 publish 一份 newsletter，就没有余地或 incentive 去 take risks。

用 financial terms 来说，blog posts 有着 asymmetric returns，其 downside 是 capped 的，upside 却是 unlimited 的。如果你写了一篇糟糕的 post，它就不会被 shared，也不会有人看见。如果你写了一篇出色的 post，而且它 goes viral，那么 internet 上的所有人都会觉得你是个天才。由于 content 是 shared organically 的，你最好的作品获得的 exposure 远远超过最差的作品。在这些情况下，incentive 是提高 variance，尽你所能写出最有意思的作品。

这种 dynamic 对 readers 也有好处。如果一篇文章 bold 却 wrong，你可以直接 close the tab。如果它 bold 而且 right，你就挖到金矿了。由于 readers 可以在 authors 之间做到 massively diversified，他们可以受益于 underlying assets 的 increased variance，同时又 insulated from associated risk。在 Blogosphere、Twitterverse，甚至 Medium 上，readers 都可以享受 long tail upside，而不必承担 commensurate cost。

相比之下，newsletter 的每一期都会 delivered 给每一位 reader，而且由于许多内容是 paywalled 的，potential virality 很小。subscribers 身上的 one way valve 进一步加剧了这一点：一旦有人 churns out，他们就不太可能再给你第二次机会。因此，随之而来的 incentive 是不去 take 任何 bold risks，避免 alienating readers，并写出任何能 appeal to 你当前 audience 的东西。

这些话对其他 newsletters 来说也许成立，但你的不一样。毕竟，你是个有趣的人，也有有趣的想法。更重要的是，你的 readers 都是要求 insight 的聪明人！

问题在于，就像 pop-music、pop-science 以及其他所有 pop-culture 一样，pop-writing 只能 appeal to 那个 largest common denominator。即使 author 和 readers 各自都是有趣的人，aggregation 也会把这群 individuals 挤进一片 milquetoast 的泥沼。正如 David Foster Wallace [曾经描述的那样](https://books.google.com/books?id=qN9Z9UQWwsgC&pg=PP35&lpg=PP35&dq=%E2%80%9Cpeople+tend+to+be+extremely+similar+in+their+vulgar+and+prurient+and+dumb+interests+and+wildly+different+in+their+refined+and+aesthetic+and+noble+interests.%E2%80%9D&source=bl&ots=AjrMDxtfb7&sig=ACfU3U3-gMCmnE9nVX7fjG_K2cDvjw4sVw&hl=en&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwjls7yHrJvsAhWxMn0KHYCQAj0Q6AEwA3oECAEQAg#v=onepage&q=%E2%80%9Cpeople%20tend%20to%20be%20extremely%20similar%20in%20their%20vulgar%20and%20prurient%20and%20dumb%20interests%20and%20wildly%20different%20in%20their%20refined%20and%20aesthetic%20and%20noble%20interests.%E2%80%9D&f=false)：

> 当然，不可否认，television 是 Low Art 的一个例子，是那种必须 please 人们才能拿到他们的钱的 art……这是它需要 attract 和 please Audience 的一个 logical function。而且，我并不是说 television 是 vulgar 而 dumb 的，是因为组成 Audience 的人们是 vulgar 而 dumb 的。Television 之所以是现在这样，仅仅是因为人们在 vulgar、prurient 和 dumb 的 interests 上往往极其 similar，而在 refined、aesthetic 和 noble 的 interests 上却极其 different。这一切都关乎 syncretic diversity：无论 medium 还是 Audience，都不应为 quality 受到指责。

## Newsletters 之间的 Homogeneity

不过，newsletters 内部的 homogeneity 听起来或许还不至于是什么罪过。如果每个人都是自己 domain 中的 expert，reader 仍然可以选择 reading broadly，领略每一个 hermetic utopia。

然而，structural forces 也确保了 newsletters 之间的 homogeneity，让你永远读不到太过 original 的东西。

### 全局排名（Global Ranking）

Subcultures 让人得以摆脱作为 zero-sum game 的 status。如果你是[自己世界中的 king](https://daviddfriedman.blogspot.com/2006/10/economics-of-status.html)，就不会因为处在别人 social ladder 的底层而难过（[1](https://www.gwern.net/The-Melancholy-of-Subculture-Society#external-links)、[2](https://meaningness.com/geeks-mops-sociopaths)）。Bloggers 知道自己并非 universally popular，但只要你在 internet 上有自己的一隅，这就无关紧要。

相比之下，Substack 彻底 violates 了 [Social Fog of War](https://alexdanco.com/2020/01/23/social-capital-in-silicon-valley/)，其方式是设立一个 [global leader board](https://substack.com/discover?utm_source=substack#top-paid)。结果就是，如果你是一个崭露头角的 writer，就能清楚看到什么最 popular；而由于 [humans are apes](https://www.briantimar.com/notes/mimetic/mimetic/)，你就会倾向于 imitate 它。当然，那只是一个 number，你也没有什么真正的理由非得让它 go up，但正如 Byrne Hobart（#17 Paid）曾经说过的，“这个 global leaderboard 非常 addicting”。

如果 leaderboard 有趣而且 diverse，那或许还好；但在我写下这些文字时（9 月 6 日），排名前 3 的 posts 讲的是 [Donald Trump](https://taibbi.substack.com/p/the-trump-era-sucks-and-needs-to)、[Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/september-3-2020) 和 [Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/september-2-2020)。一个月后，当我做最后的 edits 时（10 月 4 日），排名前 3 的 posts 又是在讲 [Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/october-3-2020)、[Donald Trum](https://frenchpress.thedispatch.com/p/praying-for-a-reckless-president)p 和 [Donald Trump](https://messagebox.substack.com/p/now-is-the-time-for-politics)。

显而易见的 objection 是，Twitter 也有 public follower counts，却仍然能容纳生机勃勃的 subcultures。

区别在于，Twiter 上的 status 来自_谁_对你 follows、likes 和 retweets，而不只是这些人有多少。在一个 unpaid platform 上，没人在乎靠随便什么陌生人和 bots 来抬高自己的 follower count，但每个人都在乎能否得到自己钦佩之人的 retweeted。

### Style 的 Homogeneity

一个 Substack newsletter 可以有 About page，但也仅此而已。这里永远不会出现像 [Nadia 的 notes](https://nadiaeghbal.com/notes/) 那样定期 updated、尚未成熟的 stream of consciousness，也不会有 [Guzey 的 Tweets 列表](https://guzey.com/twitter/2019/)，甚至不会有 [Nintil 的 categories](https://nintil.com/categories)。它们当然也不会有 [Gwern 的 link preview on hover](https://www.gwern.net/)，或是 [sidenotes](https://www.gwern.net/Sidenotes)。

这些不只是好的 features，还是 self-expression 的 forms；更关键的是，它们是创造 message 的 medium 的 variations，而在极端情况下，它们还是用于 [thinking the unthinkable](http://worrydream.com/MediaForThinkingTheUnthinkable/) 的 media。

这听起来或许没什么大不了，谁在乎是不是只有一种 font 呢？

但正如 Nadia [曾经写过的](https://nadiaeghbal.com/notes/2019)（在她加入 Substack 之前）：

> 也许毁掉 Medium 上 writers 的并不是 likes（或“claps”），而是 WSIWYG editor。如果你们全都想得一样，写出来也就一样……换句话说，让我们的 words 变得 santitize 的压力，究竟来自 demand 的 homogeneity（为了得到别人的 approval 而 writing），还是 production 的 homogeneity（为了得到 text editor 的 approval 而 writing）？

### 有限采样（Limited Sampling）

作为 reader，你过去可以在一场无穷无尽的自助餐中 sample 各种 diverse 而古怪的 creations。你可能从一百个不同的 blogs 中各读一篇 post，从不逗留太久，以免陷入某个 author 的 pattern of thought。

如今，一旦你 subscribe 了某个 author，就得相伴一生，或者彻底 sever 这段 relationship。再也没有 intellectual fling，也没有长久的试探调情，这成了一个 binary choice。

Organic sharing、growth 和 virality 让 readers 接触到各式各样的 authors，呈现每个人 writing 中最好的部分。在 Substack 上，你得到的不是 100 位 authors 各自最好的 1% 的 posts，而是每个人的 100%。你拿到的不是精华，而是 low fat milk，大部分都是水。

## Time 上的 Homogeneity

对 authors 来说，持续思考、偶尔写作，比反过来更好。

但在 Substack 上，你按月获得报酬，这就带来了不断赶制 regular updates 的压力。由于不可能每周都有两次有趣而新颖的想法，这也意味着 writers 会严重倾向于 summarizing the news、不断抛出 quick takes，或是[围绕自己在 Twitter 上读到的任何东西 riffing](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/2020/09/17/markets-nasa/)。

这不利于 intellectual biodiversity，也确实不利于 quality。一位以 longform content 闻名的 blogger 看过这篇 post 的 draft 后写道：

> 如果你在 writing 一个 substack，你就不能去度 creative vacation！你不能花 3 个月 writing 一部宏篇巨制！你必须一周接着一周、再接着一周地 churn out content，最好每周还得来上好多次。

既然总有东西可读，increasing output 本身并不就是好事。重要的是 quality-density，我们可以粗略地将其 model 为 temporal leverage。过去，你可能花 10 hours 读一本耗时 [4 years 才 research 和 write 完成](https://nadiaeghbal.com/phd)的书，那可是 time 上 3500x 的 multiple！如今，一份在 M-F publishes、需要 30 minutes 才能读完的 newsletter，只提供了 67x 的 multiple。

### 不利于 Archives 的 Incentivization

Substack 最大的 risk 并不是 Gmail changes 了自己的 algorithm，也不是 readers 设定 automatic forwarding 并 share accounts。而是多年以后，每位 author 都积累了足够多的 content，以至于 reader 只需支付 1 month subscription，download 整个 archive，就不愁没有阅读材料了。

要 prevent text scraping 真的很难，所以不妨把这想象成“只需 $9，你就能 download Netflix 有史以来的每一部节目”，然后再想象一下这对 churn-rate 的 effect。

避免 backlog effect 的唯一可靠办法，是确保 posts 都是 time-sensitive；而所谓“time-sensitive”，不过是委婉地说，它们并不试图触及任何 fundamental truths 或 deep abstractions。

从更个人的角度来说，这与我在不同 domains 中最喜欢的一些 writers 的 theses 背道而驰，从 Gwern 的 Long-Content，到 Andy Matuschak 的 Evergreen Notes，再到 Byrne Hobart 的 Ideas that Don’t Mean-Revert，无不如此。

[Gwern 引用 The Clock of the Long Now](https://www.gwern.net/About#long-content)：

> 做过、想过、写过或说过的东西并不是 culture；culture 只是其中被记住的那一部分。

[Andy](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/About_these_notes?stackedNotes=z3SjnvsB5aR2ddsycyXofbYR7fCxo7RmKW2be&stackedNotes=z6cFzJWgj9vZpnrQsjrZ8yCNREzCTgyFeVZTb)：

> creative work 中涉及的许多日常 thinking，就像潮水中的沙堡一样，就这么消失了。Ephemerality 在 low-fidelity thought 中其实可能很有用，但在许多情况下，它只是一种 accidental property。我们应该以 Evergreen notes 的形式进行严肃的 thinking，让 thinking 能够 accumulates。

[Byrne：](https://medium.com/@byrnehobart/about-best-of-faq-25df97a74467)

> 世界充满了以 cycles 运行的 phenomena——countries rise and fall，economies speed up and slow down，ideas 时而流行、时而过时，人们在 culture wars 中挖出新的战壕。而有时，世界发生了改变，此后就再也不会一样了……

有很多 topics 是我避免写的。我的大致原则是，我想写那些五年后仍值得一读的东西，理想情况下，还应该是一年后变得更加 relevant 的东西。由于 news site algorithms 目前的运作方式，这恰恰与所有以写作为生的人所做的相反。

## 作为 Platform 的 Substack，作为 Aggregator 的 Substack

尽管我心存疑虑，但很明显，Substack 不会消失。internet 在过去 20 年里有机会为 blogs 找到 sustainable monetization 的办法，却大体上失败了。与大多数 digital 和 creative work 一样，blogging 的 cost of distribution 为 zero，这意味着 revenue 遵循 power law。除少数极受欢迎的 writers 外，你不可能靠 ads、grants 和 patronage 养活自己。

因此，我只能希望 Substack 会改进，而且我对他们会这样做抱有希望。

撇开 Moral 和 aesthetic arguments 不谈，我列出的这些问题也是一种 financial imperative。

我前面说过，Substack 最大的 risk 是 archives，但这只在关乎存亡的意义上成立。就未来的 growth 而言，最大的 risk 是 mainstream population 继续阅读 mainstream journals，而 Substack 始终无法 [crosses the chasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_Chasm)。

第二大的 risk 是，在某个时候，所有最知名的 authors 都会离开。它的 costs 或许为 $50k to pay a freelancer to duct tape Twilio SendGrid to Stripe Checkout, and Substack takes 10%, so at around $500k 的 revenue，authors 就会开始 migrating away（实际上会早得多，因为这是所有 future expected revenue 的 function）。由于 creative work 的 popularity 具有 power law，从顶部抽取的 revenue 就代表了全部可能 revenue 的大部分。这还没有发生，是因为 migrations 总是极其麻烦，但 $500k 仍然是合理的数量级。

用 Ben Thompson 的话来说，Substack 有两种选择。它可以成为一个 full fledged aggregator，build network effects 和 community、personalized content 等等，但要冒着损害其 moral purity 的风险——它原本是 internet 上最后几处 ad-free、algorithm-free 的角落之一；或者，它可以成为一个 platform，为大规模的 writers 提供有价值的 infrastructure 和 flexible pricing，作为面向 small writers 的廉价 service 来 operate，同时从巨大成功的 upside 中获益。

这两种都是很好的选择，问题在于它们是 mutually exclusive。

writers 参与一个 shared interlinked reality 的 aggregator 世界，就是 Twitter 的世界，因此也违背了 [Substack 的 founding principles](https://on.substack.com/p/a-better-future-for-news)。

但他们目前身处的 platform 世界，却由日复一日赶制出来的 milquetoast hot takes 构成。这不是好的 content。这里没有 editorial accountability。它不会 produce 下一部伟大的 American novel，不会 produce 任何严肃的 investigative reporting，甚至不会 produce 一篇令人难忘的 essay。

正如 Jonah Goldberg（The Dispatch，#1 Paid）[所描述的](https://gfile.thedispatch.com/p/exciting-politics-are-bad-politics)：我正带着某种情绪，而在这样一份即兴写成的“news”letter 中，情绪往往就是你唯一拥有的 muse。

## 尾声：那些 Dogmatic 的部分

好吧，下面是我最初写下的内容；如果我不是个胆小鬼，我本来会用它开篇。

Substack 或许正在大把赚钱，但它已经彻底没了主意。

他们这样讲述自己的 [founding mythology](https://on.substack.com/p/two-years-of-substack)：“Chris 离开 Kik 后正在休息，他在自己位于 Ontario 的 Kitchener 的公寓空房间里，把 Stripe、一个 enterprise email delivery tool 和一些匆忙搭建的 web publishing software 拼接起来，做出了一个 prototype。”

这本来会是一个典型的酷而又靠拼凑起步的 founding story，只不过 2 年后（现在是 3 年后），Substack 仍然仅此而已。

他们涉足了 fellowships 和 legal support，但读到“为了 accelerate 这个新 media economy 的到来，我们将 build 越来越 powerful 的 tools 来帮助 readers”这样的说法，很难不心生讥讽。已经一年了。[他们有 ~~44~~17 名 employees](https://www.linkedin.com/company/substack/)。\[Edit 29Nov 2020：我（Divinity Studies）被他们的 Linkedin 弄糊涂了\] 他们 raised 了 [$15,000,000](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/16/substack-series-a/)。请哪怕说出一个为 readers built 的“powerful tool”。

（候选项是 Import from Twitter，以及一个 designed 得很差的 share modal。）

或者，用 Packy（#15 Free）回应早先一版草稿时的话来说：“他们的 product velocity 就是一坨狗屎……对 discovery 什么都不做……它总是 crashes……他们 raised 了那么多钱，却只对 product 做了那么一点 improved，这实在让我难以置信。”

当然，Substack 并没有什么东西，会让文字一写进它的 editor 就染上毒性。与 TV 不同，这里没有 barrier to entry，只有成名的门槛。所以，如果你需要一种方便的方式来 publish 自己的 writing，那就尽管使用 Substack；但如果你寻找的是好的 writing，或者是好的 writing 的救星，以及能给优秀 writers 带来 financial sustainability 的人，那就继续找吧。

Substack 最令人无法辩护的地方，并不是这些 theoretical structural mechanics 中的任何一个，而是一种更容易、更 intuitive 的认识：这里不会写出任何伟大的作品。我们阅读并 publish 的每一篇作品，都是一小口 momentary stimulation。它遵循着双方之间一份不成文的约定——我不会太努力地写任何严肃的东西，你也不会太努力地理解我的 writing，而我们都会因此更快乐——这给人的感觉不像 patronage，倒更像对真正 craft 的廉价模仿：实现了 surface level goals，却到此为止。

这番话可能会滑向把 insight 比作 porn 的 extended metaphor（至少后者确实具有 infinite variety），也可能滑向更糟糕、更泛泛的怪人式抱怨，哀叹自己的 culture 已死，被可怕的 youth culture 取代。但有一个显而易见的事实，仍然值得说出来：让一个并非你配偶的人，每周 5-days-a-week 地向你灌输他们的 thoughts，而这些 thoughts 他们自己也只花了一天去琢磨，在 longer-time horizons 的 publication 中，这些 thoughts 很可能会继续 refined，或者根本不被表达出来——这对你的 brain 大概并不好。这不是“weekly update”，而是一种 parasocial relationship，是你从其他每一个 one-sided social media platform 那里得到的同一种毒药。

现在退后一步，想象你是一本写于 1920 年的书。你知道，有些书成为 classics，另一些则只取得昙花一现的成功。自然，你会想知道自己处在 spectrum 的什么位置。但请注意，这个意义上的 survival 更接近 power-law，而非 normal，只有极少数书能长存，否则 canon 就会无休止地膨胀。

所以，每过去一天，你不是在接近一个即将到来的 expiration date，而是在接近 longevity 的可能性。Conditional on being alive，你属于 immortal classics 这个幸运 cohort 的可能性会越来越大。Paradoxically，你活得越久，就越可以 expect 自己继续活得更久。这就是 [Lindy Effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindy_effect)，一种由 survivorship bias 促成、近乎魔法般的 aging-in-reverse。

[这是对 Doomsday argument 一种不同寻常、却 valid 的 application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomsday_argument)。在没有其他 knowledge 的情况下，每一个 observed instance 都被 assumed 为 sampled from the median，因此你遇到的任何 writing，都处于其 lifespan 的 halfway point 左右。

现在回到你自己的身体，你又是一名 reader 了。你最喜欢的 author 在 9:15am publishes 一篇 post，几分钟后它就到了你的 inbox。你在 1pm 吃午饭时 skim 了一遍，delete 了那封 email，然后就在同一天的 4:45pm，它咽下了最后一口气。

如果这一切听起来都过于 theoretical，与你的 lived experience 完全不相干，那也无妨。但你有多少次回头读过自己最喜欢的 newsletter 的旧 edition？既然明天又会有新的一期，何必费这个劲？最近读过的东西，有什么留在了你心里吗？如果有，它是像初吻那样留在你的心里，还是只黏在你的舌头上，不是差劲或恶臭，不是辛辣或甜美，而是 bland、boring、milquetoast。

## Homogeneity Within Newsletters

Top substack writers all have a clear focus. The Dispatch writes about politics, Matt Stoller writes about monopolies, Bill Bishop covers China.

Isn’t this normal? After all, no writer can be expected to cover every topic. In an age of disaggregation, we should read from experts instead of [casual polymaths](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/2020/09/28/polymath/).

And yet, the apparent normality obscures its historical peculiarity.

Consider SlateStarCodex, one of the most popular blogs in a pre-Substack era. It had posts ranging from [political science fiction](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/17/slightly-skew-systems-of-government/), to [pharmacology](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/15/the-vision-of-vilazodone-and-vortioxetine/), to [artificial intelligence](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/06/10/the-obligatory-gpt-3-post/), to [media economics](https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/09/11/update-on-my-situation/). That’s not a selective assortment, it’s his last few posts in chronological order.

(Or consider Nintil, which covers everything from [longevity](https://nintil.com/longevity), to [meta-science](https://nintil.com/better-science), to [education](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma) and [decision theory](https://nintil.com/pascals-mugging).)

Heterogeneity works for blogs since you grow through an occasional viral hit, and gain loyal readership through the occasional post that strikes a reader just right. As SlateStarCodex’s author [describes his success](https://www.reddit.com/r/slatestarcodex/comments/3ygr2w/why_is_scott_alexander_sorta_popular/):

> Granting the premises, maybe because I write a lot of stuff? There are about 500 SSC articles, one every couple of days. Even if I'm no better than anyone else, there are a lot more opportunities for people to stumble across the one that they like, be impressed, and start following me.

The problem is not merely homogeneity of topic, but homogeneity of substance. If you have to publish a newsletter every week, you don’t have the room or incentive to take risks.

In financial terms, blog posts have asymmetric returns with capped downside but unlimited upside. If you write a bad post it won’t get shared and no one will see it. If you write a great post and it goes viral, everyone on the internet thinks you’re a genius. Since content is shared organically, your best work gets way more exposure than your worst. The incentive in these situations is to ramp up variance and do the most interesting writing you can muster.

That dynamic is also good for readers. If a piece is bold but wrong, you can just close the tab. If it’s bold but right, you’ve hit a gold mine. Since readers can be massively diversified across authors, they can benefit from increased variance in the underlying assets while insulated from associated risk. In the Blogosphere, Twitterverse and even on Medium, readers could enjoy long tail upside without commensurate cost.

In contrast, every single edition of a newsletter is delivered to every single reader, and since a lot of it is paywalled, there’s little potential virality. This is aggravated by the one way valve on subscribers: one someone churns out, they’re unlikely to give you a second try. So the ensuing incentive is not to take any bold risks, avoid alienating readers, and write whatever will appeal to your current audience.

This might sound true for other newsletters, but not for yours. After all, you’re an interesting person, and you have interesting thoughts. More importantly, your readers are smart people who demand insight!

The problem is that like pop-music, pop-science and all other pop-culture, pop-writing can only appeal to the largest common denominator. Even if the author and readers are interesting people in isolation, aggregation forces this blob of individuals into a milquetoast morass. As David Foster Wallace [once described it](https://books.google.com/books?id=qN9Z9UQWwsgC&pg=PP35&lpg=PP35&dq=%E2%80%9Cpeople+tend+to+be+extremely+similar+in+their+vulgar+and+prurient+and+dumb+interests+and+wildly+different+in+their+refined+and+aesthetic+and+noble+interests.%E2%80%9D&source=bl&ots=AjrMDxtfb7&sig=ACfU3U3-gMCmnE9nVX7fjG_K2cDvjw4sVw&hl=en&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwjls7yHrJvsAhWxMn0KHYCQAj0Q6AEwA3oECAEQAg#v=onepage&q=%E2%80%9Cpeople%20tend%20to%20be%20extremely%20similar%20in%20their%20vulgar%20and%20prurient%20and%20dumb%20interests%20and%20wildly%20different%20in%20their%20refined%20and%20aesthetic%20and%20noble%20interests.%E2%80%9D&f=false):

> It is of course undeniable that television is an example of Low Art, the sort of art that has to please people in order to get their money ...this is a logical function of its need to attract and please Audience. And I'm not saying television is vulgar and dumb because the people who compose Audience are vulgar and dumb. Television is the way it is simply because people tend to be extremely similar in their vulgar and prurient and dumb interests, and wildly different in their refined and aesthetic and noble interests. It's all about syncretic diversity: neither medium nor Audience is faultable for quality.

## Homogeneity Across Newsletters

Still, homogeneity within newsletters might not sound like such a crime. If each person is an expert in their domain, a reader still has the option of reading broadly and getting a taste of each hermetic utopia.

Except that structural forces also ensure a homogeneity across newsletters, ensuring that you never read anything too original.

### Global Ranking

Subcultures allow for an escape from status as a zero-sum game. If you’re the [king of your own world](https://daviddfriedman.blogspot.com/2006/10/economics-of-status.html), you don’t feel bad for being at the bottom of someone else’s social ladder ([1](https://www.gwern.net/The-Melancholy-of-Subculture-Society#external-links), [2](https://meaningness.com/geeks-mops-sociopaths)). Bloggers know they aren’t universally popular, but it doesn’t matter as long as you have your corner of the internet.

In contrast, Substack totally violates [Social Fog of War](https://alexdanco.com/2020/01/23/social-capital-in-silicon-valley/) by instituting a [global leader board](https://substack.com/discover?utm_source=substack#top-paid). The result is, if you’re an up and coming writer, you can see exactly what’s popular, and since [humans are apes](https://www.briantimar.com/notes/mimetic/mimetic/), you’ll be inclined to imitate it. Sure, it’s just a number, and there’s no real reason you have to make it go up, but as Byrne Hobart (#17 Paid) once said, “the global leaderboard is very addicting”.

This might be fine if the leaderboard was interesting and diverse, but as I’m writing this (September 6) , the top 3 posts are about [Donald Trump](https://taibbi.substack.com/p/the-trump-era-sucks-and-needs-to), [Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/september-3-2020) and [Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/september-2-2020). As I’m doing final edits a month later (October 4), the top 3 posts are again about [Donald Trump](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/october-3-2020), [Donald Trum](https://frenchpress.thedispatch.com/p/praying-for-a-reckless-president)p and [Donald Trump](https://messagebox.substack.com/p/now-is-the-time-for-politics).

The obvious objection is that Twitter also has public follower counts but still manages to host vibrant subcultures.

The difference is that status on Twiter comes from _who_ follows, likes and retweets you, not just how many of them there are. On an unpaid platform, no one cares about pumping their follower count with random strangers and bots, but everyone cares about getting retweeted by the people they personally admire.

### Homogeneity of Style

A Substack newsletter can have an About page, but that’s it. There will never be anything like [Nadia’s notes](https://nadiaeghbal.com/notes/) which are a regularly updated half-baked stream of consciousness, or [Guzey’s list of Tweets](https://guzey.com/twitter/2019/) or even [Nintil’s categories](https://nintil.com/categories). And they certainly won’t have [Gwern’s link preview on hover](https://www.gwern.net/), or [sidenotes](https://www.gwern.net/Sidenotes).

These aren’t just good features, they’re forms of self-expression, and even more critically, variations on the medium that creates the message, and in the extreme case, media for [thinking the unthinkable](http://worrydream.com/MediaForThinkingTheUnthinkable/).

This might not sound like a big deal, who cares if there’s only one font?

But as Nadia [once wrote](https://nadiaeghbal.com/notes/2019) (before she joined Substack):

> Maybe it wasn’t likes (or “claps”), but the WSIWYG editor, that destroyed writers on Medium. If you all think the same, you write the same... In other words, did the pressure to santitize our words come from the homogeneity of demand (writing for others’ approval), or the homogeneity of production (writing for the text editor’s approval)?

### Limited Sampling

As a reader, you used to be able to sample an endless buffet of diverse and wacky creations. You might read a single post from a hundred different blogs, never stopping long enough to get bogged down in one author’s pattern of thought.

Today, once you subscribe to an author, you’re in for life, or until you sever the relationship entirely. There’s no more intellectual fling or extended flirting, it’s a binary choice.

Organic sharing, growth and virality exposes readers to a wide variety of authors, serving up the best of each one’s writing. On Substack, instead of getting the best 1% of posts from 100 authors, you get 100% from each one. Instead of getting the cream of the crop, you’re left with low fat milk, mostly water.

## Homogeneity Across Time

It’s better for authors to think persistently and write occasionally than the other way around.

But on Substack, you’re paid monthly, creating pressure to churn out regular updates. Since it’s impossible to have interesting novel thoughts twice a week every week, this also means writers skew heavily towards summarizing the news, pumping out quick takes, or [riffing on whatever they read on Twitter](https://applieddivinitystudies.com/2020/09/17/markets-nasa/).

This is bad for intellectual biodiversity, but it’s also just bad for quality. After seeing a draft of this post, a blogger known for their longform content wrote:

> If you're writing a substack, you can't go on a creative vacation! You can't spend 3 months writing something epic! you have to churn out content week after week after week preferably many times per week.

Since there’s always something to read, increasing output is not intrinsically good. What matters is quality-density, which we can naively model as temporal leverage. In the past, you might have spent 10 hours reading a book that took [4 years to research and write](https://nadiaeghbal.com/phd), a 3500x multiple on time! Today, a newsletter that publishes M-F and takes 30 minutes to read only provides a 67x multiple.

### Incentivization Against Archives

The biggest risk to Substack isn’t that Gmail changes its algorithm or that readers set up automatic forwarding and share accounts. It’s that years from now, each author will have built up so much content that a reader can pay a 1 month subscription, download the archive, and be set on reading material.

It’s really hard to prevent text scraping, so imagine this as “for $9, you can download every Netflix show ever”, and then imagine the effect on churn-rate.

The only reliable way to avoid the backlog effect is to ensure that posts are time-sensitive, where “time-sensitive” is a nice way of saying they don’t reach for any kind of fundamental truths or deep abstractions.

On a more personal note, this is antithetical to the theses of some of my favorite writers across domains, ranging from Gwern’s Long-Content to Andy Matuschak’s Evergreen Notes and Byrne Hobart’s Ideas that Don’t Mean-Revert.

[Gwern, quoting The Clock of the Long Now](https://www.gwern.net/About#long-content):

> What has been done, thought, written, or spoken is not culture; culture is only that fraction which is remembered.

[Andy](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/About_these_notes?stackedNotes=z3SjnvsB5aR2ddsycyXofbYR7fCxo7RmKW2be&stackedNotes=z6cFzJWgj9vZpnrQsjrZ8yCNREzCTgyFeVZTb):

> Much of the day-to-day thinking involved in creative work is simply lost, like sand castles in the tide. Ephemerality can actually be useful in low-fidelity thought, but it’s simply an accidental property in many cases. We should do our serious thinking in the form of Evergreen notes so that the thinking accumulates.

[Byrne:](https://medium.com/@byrnehobart/about-best-of-faq-25df97a74467)

> The world is full of phenomena that move in cycles — countries rise and fall, economies speed up and slow down, ideas come in and out of fashion, people dig new trenches in the culture wars. And then there are times that the world changes, and it’s never the same again….

There are a bunch of topics that I avoid writing about. My rough rule is that I’d like to write stuff that will still be worth reading in five years, and ideally stuff that will be more relevant a year from now. Because of the way news site algorithms currently work, that’s the opposite of what everybody who writes for a living does.

## Substack as Platform, Substack as Aggregator

Despite my misgivings, it’s pretty clear that Substack isn’t going away. The internet had the last 20 years to figure out sustainable monetization for blogs, and largely failed. Like most digital and creative work, blogging has zero cost of distribution, which means that revenue follows a power law. Outside of a few tremendously popular writers, it’s not possible to fund yourself through ads, grants and patronage.

Instead, I can only hope Substack will improve, and I’m hopeful they will.

Moral and aesthetic arguments aside, the problems I’ve laid out are a financial imperative.

I said earlier that the biggest risk to Substack is archives, but that’s only true in the existential sense. With regards to future growth, the biggest risk is that the mainstream population continues to read mainstream journals, and Substack never [crosses the chasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_Chasm).

The second biggest risk is that at some point, all the biggest authors will leave. It costs maybe $50k to pay a freelancer to duct tape Twilio SendGrid to Stripe Checkout, and Substack takes 10%, so at around $500k in revenue, authors will start migrating away (actually much earlier since it’s a function of all future expected revenue). Since popularity in creative work has a power law, revenue skimmed off the top represents the bulk of all possible revenue. This hasn’t happened yet because migrations are always a massive pain, but $500k is still the right ballpark.

In the language of Ben Thompson, Substack has two choices. It can become a full fledged aggregator, build network effects and community, personalized content and so forth, but risk the moral purity of being one of the last ad-free algorithm-free corners of the internet, or it can become a platform, provide valuable infrastructure and flexible pricing for writers at scale, and operate as a cheap service for small writers while benefiting from the upside of the huge successes.

These are both great options, the problem is that they’re mutually exclusive.

The aggregator world where writers participate in a shared interlinked reality is the world of Twitter, and thus a violation of [Substack’s founding principles](https://on.substack.com/p/a-better-future-for-news).

But the platform world they currently live in consists of milquetoast hot takes churned out day after day after day. It’s not good content. There’s no editorial accountability. It will not produce the next great American novel, or any serious investigative reporting, or even a memorable essay.

As Jonah Goldberg (The Dispatch, #1 Paid) [describes it](https://gfile.thedispatch.com/p/exciting-politics-are-bad-politics): I’m in a mood, and in a “news”letter such as this, written on the fly, mood is often the only muse you’ve got.

## Epilogue: The Dogmatic Bits

Okay, so here’s what I wrote initially, and what I would have led with if I wasn’t a coward.

Substack may be printing money, but it’s totally out of ideas.

They tell the [founding mythology](https://on.substack.com/p/two-years-of-substack) as: “Chris, who was taking time off after leaving Kik, had built a prototype in the spare room of his Kitchener, Ontario, apartment, by lashing together Stripe, an enterprise email delivery tool, and some quickly-built web publishing software.”

It would be a prototypically cool and scrappy founding story, except that 2 (now 3) years later, that’s still all Substack is.

They’ve ventured into fellowships and legal support, but it’s difficult to get through the claim “to accelerate the advent of this new media economy, we’ll build increasingly powerful tools to help readers” without feeling cynical. It’s been a year. [They have ~~44~~17 employees](https://www.linkedin.com/company/substack/). \[Edit 29Nov 2020: I(Divinity Studies) was confused by their Linkedin\] They raised [$15,000,000](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/16/substack-series-a/). Name literally one “powerful tool” built for readers.

(The candidates are Import from Twitter, and a poorly designed share modal.)

Or in the worlds of Packy (#15 Free) in response to an earlier draft, “their product velocity is dog shit… don’t do anything for discovery… it crashes all the time… It absolutely blows my mind that they’ve raised as much as they have and have improved the product as little as they have.”

Now obviously, there is nothing about Substack that poisons words as soon as they’re written in its editor. Unlike TV, there is not a barrier to entry, only a barrier to fame. So if you need a convenient way to publish your writing, then by all means use Substack, but if you’re looking for good writing, or for the savior of good writing and deliverer of financial sustainability for good writers, keep looking.

The most damning thing about Substack is not any of these theoretical structural mechanics, it’s the easier more intuitive understanding that nothing great will be written here. Each piece we read and publish is a bite sized dose of momentary stimulation. It follows an unwritten contract between each party–I will not try too hard to writing anything serious, you will not try too hard to understand my writing, and both of us will be happier for it–that feels less like patronage, and more like a cheap imitation of actual craft, something that fulfils its surface level goals but goes no further.

At the risk of veering into the extended metaphor of insight as porn (at least the latter does actually have infinite variety), or the worse and more general crank-dom lamenting the death of my culture and its replacement with the horrid youth culture, it is still worth speaking the obvious truth that having someone who is not your spouse feed their thoughts to you 5-days-a-week, thoughts that they themselves have only had a day to work on, thoughts which would likely go refined or unexpressed in a publication with longer-time horizons, is probably not good for your brain. That’s not a “weekly update”, it’s a parasocial relationship, it’s the same poison you get from every other one-sided social media platform.

Now take a step back, and imagine you’re a book written in 1920. You know that some books become classics while others have a mere flash-in-the-pan success. Naturally, you’d like to know where you fit on the spectrum. But note that survival in this sense is closer to power-law than normal, only a tiny minority of books can endure, least the canon bloat endlessly.

So with each passing day, rather approaching an impending expiration date, you approach the possibility of longevity. Conditional on being alive, it becomes increasingly likely that you are part of the privileged cohort of immortal classics. Paradoxically, the longer you live, the longer you can expect to continue living. This is the [Lindy Effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindy_effect), a kind of near-magical aging-in-reverse enabled by survivorship bias.

[This is an unusual, but valid, application of the Doomsday argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomsday_argument). Given no other knowledge, each observed instance is assumed to be sampled from the median, so any writing you come across is around the halfway point of its lifespan.

Back in your body now, you’re a reader once more. Your favorite author publishes a post at 9:15am that arrives in your inbox minutes later. You skim it over lunch at 1pm, delete the email, and then at 4:45pm that same day, it breathes its last breath.

If this all sounds overly theoretical and not at all applicable to your lived experience, then fine. But how often have you gone back to read an old edition of your favorite newsletter? Why bother when you’ll have a new one tomorrow? Has anything you’ve read recently stuck with you? And if so, has it stuck in your heart like your first kiss, or merely to your tongue, not poor or foul, spicy or sweet, but bland, boring, milquetoast.
