# Fighting conversational death spirals

---

## 正文

有时在 Internet conversations 中，怀着善意、目标相近的人会开始误解彼此，让对方感到受到攻击和伤害，即使实际上并没有那么多 disagreement。于是，对话里充斥着“<strawman of your position> 错得令人反感！”“不，<strawman of your position> 才错得令人反感！”之类的话，也就很难有什么成效。下面是一些似乎可能有所帮助的一般性建议：

-   如果可能，尽量当面谈。我注意到，在网上交谈时，有些话经常让我觉得被冒犯或攻击了，但只要对方把其中大概两个词换成别的说法，我就完全可以接受（例如，“我觉得你相信 <X> 是错的”，与“你相信 <X> 这件事令人不安”）。不知为什么，当面交谈时，这种情况似乎远没有那么常见，可能是因为它的 bandwidth 更高。

-   如果可能，尽量 one-on-one 或在 small groups 中谈。在 polarized topics 上，我有时会遇到这样的情况：说了一些 centrist 的话，随后因为忘了加上一条 anti-side-A caveat，而受到不喜欢 side A 的人的攻击；接着又因为忘了加上一条 anti-side-B caveat，而且那条 anti-side-A caveat 让不喜欢 side B 的人觉得我太像一个 side-B-person，而受到他们的攻击。

    即使 sides A and B 本质上都是讨论中_没有人_相信的 strawman positions，这种情况也会发生：问题不在于有人是 A 或 B 的 tribalist，而只是人们_担心遇到_ A- or B-tribalists，所以与你争论，以确认你不是其中之一。在足够小的 group 中，要记住哪些 caveats 必须加上、哪些可以让别人自行 infer，就容易得多。

-   要注意把人们的 arguments 与他们的 character 分开。正如我最近在 Facebook group 中写的那样（当时有人说“看到有人在这里谈论 <X>，真令人难过”，以及“你对 Y 的态度令人担忧”，我对此作出了回应）：

    > 也可以在_不_对别人进行 shaming 的情况下 correct 他们的 misconceptions——例如，注意暗示他们之所以 incorrect，仅仅是因为 misinformed，而不是出于某种 malice 或 character flaw（事实上，他们往往确实如此，尽管并非总是如此）。例如，不要说“你相信 <X>，真令人难过”——这会立刻让对方进入 defensive 状态，因为此时如果他们承认你是对的，就也得承认你对他们 character 的 judgment 是 correct 的——你可以说“我不认为 <X> 是 true。”不要说“<attitude Y> 令人担忧”——人们有时会将其理解为，你不仅觉得他们错了，还觉得他们因为相信它而成了坏人！——你可以说“<attitude Y> 存在 <general misconception Z>”，或者“<attitude Y> 的 express 实际上对 <reason W> 是 harmful 的。”
    > 
    > 我并不是在主张，相比第一种 statement，你应该更 _believe_ 第二种 statement；我只是说，纯粹从 empirical 的角度来看，它似乎能促成更有效、更深思熟虑的讨论。而且，我也不一定是在主张绝不应该 shame；我只是说，如果一个人特别在意不要对别人进行 shaming，那么，不进行 shaming 也可以 dissent。

## 原文

Sometimes in Internet conversations, well-intentioned people with similar goals start to misunderstand each other in ways that make the other person feel attacked and hurt even when there isn’t that much actual disagreement. Then the conversation becomes full of things like “<strawman of your position> is offensively wrong!” “No, <strawman of your position> is offensively wrong!” and it’s not very productive. Here are some general suggestions that seem like they might help:

-   Talk about things in person if possible. When talking online, I’ve noticed that I often feel offended/attacked by remarks where, if someone had changed like two words of the remark with something else, I would have been fine with it (e.g. “I think you’re wrong to believe <X>” vs. “it’s disturbing that you believe <X>”). For some reason this doesn’t seem to happen nearly as often in person, probably because it’s higher bandwidth.

-   Talk about things one-on-one or in small groups if possible. On polarized topics, I sometimes have the experience of saying something centrist, then being attacked by people who don’t like side A because I forgot to put in an anti-side-A caveat, and then being attacked by people who don’t like side B because I forgot to put in an anti-side-B caveat and the anti-side-A caveat made me look too much like a side-B-person to them.

    This can happen even if sides A and B are both essentially strawman positions that _nobody_ in the discussion believes: the problem isn’t that anyone is a tribalist for A or B, just that people are _worried about running into_ A- or B-tribalists and argue with you to make sure you’re not one. In a small enough group, it’s much easier to remember which caveats you have to insert and which ones you can let people infer.

-   Be careful to separate people’s arguments from people’s character. As I recently wrote in the Facebook group (in response to someone saying “it’s sad to see people talking about <X> here” and “your attitude about Y is troubling”):

    > One can also correct people’s misconceptions _without_ shaming them—e.g., by being careful to imply that they’re incorrect simply by being misinformed rather than out of some kind of malice or character flaw (which indeed they often are, though not always). For example, instead of saying “it’s sad that you believe <X>”—which immediately puts someone on the defensive, because now if they admit that you’re right then they also have to admit that your judgment of their character was correct—you can say “I don’t think <X> is true.” Instead of saying “<attitude Y> is troubling”—which people sometimes interpret as not only meaning that you think they’re wrong but that you think they’re a bad person for believing it!–you can say “<attitude Y> suffers from <general misconception Z>” or “<attitude Y> is actually harmful to express for <reason W>.”
    > 
    > I’m not arguing that you should _believe_ the second statement over the first, only that purely as an empirical matter it seems to create a more effective and thoughtful discussion. And I’m also not necessarily arguing that one should never shame, just that if one is specifically worried about not-shaming people, it’s possible to dissent without shaming.
