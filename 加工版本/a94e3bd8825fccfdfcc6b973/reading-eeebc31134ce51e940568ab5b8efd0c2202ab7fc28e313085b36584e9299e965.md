# 文化很重要｜Culture matters

---

## 正文

companies 用来 influence behavior 的三种主要 tools 是 incentives、process 和 culture。人们谈到这些时，所指的往往不是一回事，所以我会各举一个例子，以便我们达成共同理解（如果你认为我应该用另一个词来指这个 concept，不妨在心里替换成那个词）。

-   让人们准时参加 meetings

    -   Incentive：对迟到的人 dock pay
    -   Process：不允许迟到的人进入 meeting
    -   Culture：人们非常重视准时到场
-   让人们 build complex systems

    -   Incentive：在 promo criteria 中 require complexity
    -   Process：让 creating 或 executing on 一个 work item 的 process 如此 heavyweight，以至于人们不再 doing simple work
    -   Culture：人们喜欢 building complex systems，and/or building complex systems 会带来 peers 的 respect，and/or prestige
-   避免 manufacturing defects

    -   Incentive：按每个 created 的 good item 给人们 pay，and/or 因 bad items 而 dock pay
    -   Process：让 QA 在 shipment 前 check items，并 discard bad items
    -   Culture：人们重视 excellence，并非常努力地 avoid defects

如果你读过那些“老派”thought leaders 的文章，会发现其中许多人提倡 culture-only approach，例如，[Ken Thompson 说，要 reduce bug rate，tools（在本文中，我们将其称为 process）并不是答案，让人们在意并因此决定 avoid writing bugs 才是答案](https://mobile.twitter.com/danluu/status/885214004649615360)，或者 Bob Martin 所说的：“[software apocalypse 的 solution 不是更多 tools。solution 是更好的 programming discipline](https://www.hillelwayne.com/post/uncle-bob/)。”

这类夸张言论引发的情绪反应，加上它们很容易被反驳，导致了对 cultural solutions 的反弹，让人们说出这样的话：“你绝不应该说人们需要更多 discipline，而应该看看 underlying system 的 incentives。”同样，10x programmer meme 及相关评论引发的反弹，也让人们说出[velocity 根本不重要](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/)，或者 programmers 之间的 velocity 完全没有 difference 这样的话（[正如 Jamie Brandon 所指出的，velocity 在很大程度上取决于是否在意 velocity，以及是否为 velocity 付诸努力](https://scattered-thoughts.net/writing/moving-faster/)，所以这也是反对 culture 的反弹的一部分）。

但如果看看 quantifiable output，我们就能发现，即使 processes 和 incentives 是 company 应该首先采用的 first-line tools，culture 也有很大的 impact。例如，如果看看 manufacturing defect rate，就会发现，在长达数十年的 timescale 上，一些国家的 defect rates 持续低于其他国家[1](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:C)，而且这种情况在不同 companies 中通常都是 robust 的。即使 companies 在多个国家 operating factories，并尽可能向各个 factory importing 相同的 process 和 incentives，这种情况依然存在，原因在于 cultural differences 会 impact 人们如何 work。

回到 programming，Jamie 在关于“moving faster”的文章中指出：

> 真正有所帮助的最主要一点，是确实想要变得 faster。
> 
> 起初，相比真正 solving problems，我显然更在意 writing 'elegant' code 或 using 时髦的 tools。这或许不是一种明确的信念，但从我的行动中可以清楚地看出这些 priorities。
> 
> 我大概也没有意识到，自己究竟有可能变得多么 faster。职业生涯早期，我一直和那些与我一样 slow、一样 inexperienced 的人共事。
> 
> 随着时间推移，我开始注意到，有些人正在 producing 的 projects，远远超过我一辈子所能做到的。我想弄清楚该怎样做到，这意味着放下已有的信念，尝试发现真正有效的方法。

我很幸运，起步时的经历恰好相反，因为我的第一份全职工作是在 Centaur，当时这家 company 的 velocity/productivity 非常高。我可以说，我只在另一个 productivity 达到类似 level 的 team 工作过，那就是我现在的 team；但以 tech company 的 team 而言，我现在的 team 相当不同寻常（例如，我们 team 的 median level 是“senior staff”）[2](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:S)。在这样一家 high velocity company 开始职业生涯的一个副作用是，我通常觉得 big companies 的 development 节奏很 slow，也看不出有什么理由仅仅因为 move slowly 被视为正常，就跟着 move slowly。我在 big companies 交谈过的一些人，之前曾在并非 dysfunctional、但也算不上特别 fast 的 startups 工作过，我经常听到他们发表类似评论。当下最时髦的 companies 之一会在定期 survey 中问：“你觉得自己的 dev speed 比上一份工作 faster 还是 slower？”responses 呈 bimodal，取决于 respondent 来自 small company 还是 big company（TrendCo 的 dev speed 比 startups 的 slower，比 larger companies 的 faster）。

有一个故事，[如果我没记错，是 Brian Enos 讲的](https://amzn.to/3EZXykS)：他当时正在 practicing timed drills，目标是一直练到能够在平常的 time 或更短的 time 内 complete 某个 specific task。他很难达到自己平常的 time，因为比平常 slower 而对自己感到恼火，于是一直坚持，直到达到 target。这时他才意识到，自己记错了 target，无意间 targeting 的竟然是一个 new personal best time，比他原本以为可能达到的还要好。虽然说只要下定决心就能做到任何事过于简单，但[我们几乎没有人 operating 在接近自身 capacity 的水平上，而我们认为自己能做到什么，往往是一个主要的 limiting factor](https://twitter.com/nickcammarata/status/1362261305357393920)。当然，到了 limit，velocity 和 quality 之间存在 tradeoff，你不可能“免费”获得 velocity；但就 programming 而言，[我们距离 Pareto frontier 还如此遥远，以至于仍有 free wins](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/)，只要你[意识到它们确实存在](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1442945072144678914)。

culture influences 这件事的一种方式是，人们往往会从自己所在的 culture 中吸收对于什么是可能的看法。举一个与 velocity 无关的例子：参加 [RC](https://www.recurse.com/scout/click?t=b504af89e87b77920c9b60b2a1f6d5e8) 后，我注意到，在 Deconstruct 和 Strange Loop 这类备受尊重的 non-academic non-enterprise tech conferences 上，许多 speakers 也参加过 RC。大多数人在参加 RC 之前并没有发表过 talks；当我询问时，许多人表示，他们一直想发表 talks，却不知道成为“大型”conferences 的 speaker，其 process 竟然如此简单（有一个想法，把它写下来，然后把写下的内容作为 proposal 提交）。事实证明，在 conferences 上发表 talks 很容易做到，对许多人来说，一个主要 blocker 只是知不知道这是可能的。在一个许多人都发表 talks 的环境里，当有人犹豫着询问如何起步时，得到的回答也是这很简单，那么最终就会有许多人发表 talks。blogging 也是如此，这就是为什么在那些拥有广泛读者的 programming bloggers 中，有着不成比例的高比例是在参加 RC 后才开始认真 blogging 的。对许多人而言，开始一个 blog 的 barrier，在某种程度上是尚未意识到开始一个 blog 是 feasible 的，也没有意识到，从 technical standpoint 来看，只要随便选一个还算合理的 toolchain，并完成 setup process，开始一个 blog 就非常容易。而后，因为人们发表 talks、撰写 blog posts，他们也会越来越擅长发表 talks 和撰写 blog posts。因此，on average，RC alums 大概比随意挑选的 programmers 更善于演讲和写作，尽管 RC 几乎没有 skill transfer 或 instruction。

culture 真正能够 drive skills 的另一类情况，是那些高度 attitude dependent 的 skills。debugging 就是一个例子。正如 Julia Evans 所指出的，[良好的态度是 debugging effectiveness 的一个主要 component](https://twitter.com/b0rk/status/1249715842708844544)。Centaur 非常擅长向人们灌输这一点，以至于按照 tech company 的 standards，我在 Centaur 所属 org 中几乎每个人都会被认为是非常强的 debugger。

在 big tech companies，经常能看到有人尝试了几种没有奏效的随意方法后，就放弃处理 bugs。一个极端例子是，我认识的一位在一家 mid-10-figure tech company 工作的人说，任何需要超过几个小时来 debug 的 bug，都根本不值得 debug，因为 engineer time 太宝贵，不应该浪费在需要更长时间才能 debug 的 bugs 上。这种态度是他从自己工作的第一个 team 学来的。一个人如果对 debugging 形成了这样的态度，那么在改变态度之前，就不太可能成为好的 debugger；而包括此人在内的许多人，会在相当长的时间里保留他们在第一份工作中养成的态度和习惯[3](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:N)。

按照 tech standards，Centaur 是另一个方向上的极端例子。如果你正在 designing 一个 CPU，那么对一个你不理解的 bug 撒手不管，会被认为是不可接受的。即使这个 bug 的 symptom 并不 serious，其 underlying cause 也可能实际上很 serious，而你可能直到 shipped 一个 chip 之后，才会 observe 到更 serious 的 symptom，所以即使是看似 trivial 的 bugs，你也必须追查。另外，一个 bug 没有好的、甚至没有 deterministic reproduction 的情况也相当常见。repro 往往是这样的：“在这个 system 上，用这些 settings 来 run 这些 programs，然后 system 会在若干小时或若干天后 hang，and/or corrupt data。”在 debugging 这样的 bug 时，会有无数弯路和死胡同，其中一些可能耗去数周乃至数月。作为一个观察大家处理这类 bugs 的新员工，我所看到的是，人们日复一日地来上班，track down 这样的 bugs，不沮丧，也不放弃。当 culture 如此，而且周围每个人都抱有这种态度时，自然而然就会形成同样的态度。另外，许多 practical debugging skill，都是 applying 那些从 debugged 大量 problems 中学到的 tactical skills；而只要以积极的态度花上相当多的时间 debugging problems，尤其是接触 hard debugging problems，这些 skills 自然就会形成。

当然，tech companies 的大多数 bugs 都不值得花上数月处理，但因为有些 bugs 不值得 fixing 而有意让一些 bugs 保持 undebugged，与从未 debugged 过 serious bug、因而 debugging skills 很差，继而无法 debug 任何并非完全 trivial 的 bug，两者之间存在巨大 difference。

Cultural attitudes 能够 drive 的远不止 debugging 这样的 individual skills。在我工作过的所有 company 中，Centaur 的 serious production bug rate 按 per capita 计算，遥遥领先地处于 lowest 水平：在有 ~100 engineers 的情况下，每年远不到一个。相比之下，我从未在一个只有其 1/10th 规模、却没有至少 10x 的 serious production issues rate 的 team 工作过。和大多数 startups 一样，Centaur 的 process 很少，其 incentives 也比我工作过的 big tech companies 少得多。

其中一个 component 是，这里有一种 owning problems 的 culture，不论你属于哪个 team。如果你发现一个 problem，就会 fix 它；或者，如果有一个非常明确的 owner，你就会把 problem 告诉对方，对方会 fix 它。这里没有 roadmaps、standups、kanban，也没有其他任何用来推动人们处理 important problems 的东西。人们无需提醒或催促，就会去做。

这与我在任职过的三家 big tech companies 中的两家所见到的情况恰好相反：在那里，median person 对 touching 其 team 的 mandate 之外的 problems 避之唯恐不及，而一个不懂政治门道的人如果向另一个 team 提出 problem，就会得到这样的 default answer：“抱歉，这不在我们这个 quarter 的 roadmap 上，也许我们可以把它放到 \[从现在起两个 quarters 后\] 的 roadmap 上”，而两个 quarters 后，任何天真到再次提出同一个 issue 的人，都会听到同样的答复。在我任职过的每一家 tech company，巨大且 extremely costly 的 problems 总是 slip through the cracks，因为没有人愿意接手它们。我从未在 Centaur 见过这种情况。

big company tech culture 的一个 side effect 是，如果一个人确实想做正确的事，只要愿意忽略 organizational processes 和 incentives，就可以仅仅通过四处 fixing 任何 intern 都能 solve 的 problems，轻松做出 very high (positive) impact work。这样的 problems 随处可见，你随便一碰就会[碰到一个对 company 而言 worth 超过我 expected lifetime earnings 的 problem](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/802971209176477696)，而每年解决多个这样的 problems 并不难。[当然，导致这么多 trivial problems 没有得到 solved 的同样那些 forces，也意味着 solve 这些 problems 的人不会因自己的 work 得到 rewarded](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/#appendix-misaligned-incentive-hedgehog-defense-part-3)[4](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:B)。

相反，在 Centaur 的八年里，我只发现过一个 trivial problem，其 fix 的 worth 超过我一生将赚到的钱，因为通常 problems 在发展到那个地步之前就会得到 solved。我见过 big company 各种尝试，用 incentives（例如，为 solving important problems 提供 monetary rewards）和 process（例如，[为所有 projects/problems 制作一份巨大的 list，大约有 1000 个 projects，并让一个人对它们进行 order，同时配上一套 bureaucratic system，要求每个人不断通过 JIRA 提供自己 progress 的 updates，以便 PMs 能持续向那个为数千名 engineers 的 work 提供 total order 的人发送 progress updates](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1497980098107953152)[5](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:R)）来 fix 这个 problem，但这些尝试没有一个能达到拥有 culture of ownership 的一半效果（公平地说到 incentives，我听说 FB 运用 monetary rewards 取得了良好效果，但[我三次都未能通过 FB 的 interview](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1447268693075841024)，所以一直没能亲自观察它如何运作）。

另一个带来 relatively low severe bug rate 的 component 是，在 Centaur 整个 company 中，人们对 quality 的重视，是我在 big tech company 的 team level 都未曾见过的，更不用说 org level。当你拥有一群重视 quality、并且觉得没有任何 issue 不在自己职责范围之内的人时，你就会得到 quality。而当你 onboard 新人时，只要速度没有快到让 new hires 淹没原有 culture，他们通常也会养成同样的 habits 和 values，尤其是在你 hire new grads 时。虽然这算不上常见，但确实有不少 small firms 拥有 culture of excellence，而且通常无须 heavyweight processes 或强大的 incentives 就能维持下去；但这在 big tech companies 行不通，因为它们都经历过 hypergrowth period，在那样的时期，不可能维持如此极端的（以 mainstream standards 而言）cultural values。

到目前为止，我们主要讨论的是 companies 向人们 transmitting culture，但我认为同样重要的是，人们离开时如何把这种 culture 也带走。自从[从 hardware 转行到 software 以来，我取得了 reasonably successful 的发展](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1444034823329177602)，而我认为，在自己能控制的 factors 中，最重要的因素之一就是，我从第一份全职工作的地方学到了 effective cultural values，并且一直以同样的方式行事，而这种方式是 highly effective 的。我也在其他人身上见过这一点：他们在职业生涯中于 culture of excellence 中“成长”，然后转到另一个 direct skill transfer 甚至更少的 field，例如从 skiing 转到 civil engineering。与此相关的是，如果你读过一些书，作者在其中讨论自己为何能在所属 field 中 very effective，例如 Brian Enos 的 [Practical Shooting](https://amzn.to/3EZXykS)、Dan Sirlin 的 [Playing to Win](https://www.sirlin.net/ptw) 等，你会发现这些书往往包含相同的 core ideas（对 skills 的认真 observation 和 improvement、避免 emotional self-sabotage 的重要性、intuition 的重要性，等等）。

总之，我认为，values 和 skills 的 cultural transmission 是选择工作时被 underrated 的一个方面（我认为有些被 overrated 的东西是 [prestige](https://www.patreon.com/posts/25835707) 和 [general reputation](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1275191896097189888)，而且人们应该认真考虑自己要置身于什么样的 cultures 中，因为没有多少人能避免至少在某种程度上 absorbing 周围的 cultural values[6](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:L)。

虽然这篇文章围绕 tech 展开，但这件事并没有任何仅适用于 tech 的地方。一个 classic example 是，怀抱理想的 students 会带着从事“拯救世界”类工作的意图进入 law school，随后却 absorb 了周围 students 的 [prestige-transmitted cultural values](https://www.patreon.com/posts/25835707)，然后选择自己能获得的最 prestigious 的 job；如果不是 clerkship，那通常就是一份与“拯救世界”工作恰好相反的“BIGLAW” job。作为 first approximation，每个人都觉得“这种事绝不会发生在我身上”，但我看过许多人加入一些 organizations，他们[起初觉得那里的 values 和 culture 非常不对](https://danluu.com/wat/)，然而几乎没有人能在留下来的同时，完全不在某种程度上 absorbing 周围的 values；[很少有人能接受周围所有人都因为自己的 values 不对而把自己当傻子看](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/)。

### 附录：Bay area culture

bay area 让我欣赏的一点，是人们试图改变世界的 attitudes 非常具有感染力。在我住过的每个地方，人们都会抱怨各种问题（mortgage industry 糟透了，selling a house 的 friction 很高，等等）。在 bay area 以外，这些只是抱怨，但在 bay，当我与一年前还在抱怨某件事的人交谈时，他们很可能已经创办了一家 startup，试图解决自己抱怨的某个问题。我不认为 bay area 的人与其他地方的人有什么根本不同，更多是因为，当你周围都是愿意离开自己的工作、尝试 disrupt 某个 entrenched industry 的人时，自己也这样做似乎就相当合理（这也会带来 network effects，让这件事从“technical”角度看变得更容易，例如 fundraising 更容易）。[这些抱怨以及解决问题的尝试中，有一种很容易被嘲笑的认真劲](https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/05/11/silicon-valley-a-reality-check/)，但[这种认真劲正是我非常欣赏的](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/)。

当然，[bay area culture 并非全都是 positive 的](https://twitter.com/pushcx/status/1442860058660913166)。除此之外，bay 还有[出了名的 flaky culture](https://devonzuegel.com/post/why-is-flaking-so-widespread-in-san-francisco)，其程度在我搬过去时让我震惊。我刚到那里不久，有一次与几位老朋友约好吃晚饭，我发短信告诉他们，我大概要迟到 15 分钟。等我出现时，他们非常惊讶，因为他们以为我说自己会迟到，其实就是不会来了（另一个让我惊讶的 norm 是一个更极端的版本：对很多人而言，如果没有在约定开始前不久确认 plans，就意味着那个人已经取消，也就是说，plans 默认是取消的）。

我听人抱怨过的另一个相关 norm，是 management 和 leadership 为了避免 conflict，会以“people pleasing”的方式对所有事情都说好；这实际上增加了 conflict，因为那些把“好”理解成“好”、而不是“我说好是为了避免说不，但其实并不是答应”的人，后来会惊讶地发现，“好”原来意味着“不”。

### 附录：Centaur 的 hiring process

我谈起 Centaur 时，有人有时会说，他们一定有某种极其 rigorous 的 hiring process，才能 hiring 到 elite engineers；但这个 hiring process 的 selective 程度远低于我工作过的任何一家“名牌”big tech company（Google、MS 和 Twitter），也没有明显高于我工作过的那些乏味、老派的 companies（IBM 和 Micron）。那个“奇招”是 onboarding，而不是 hiring。

在 new grad hiring 方面（而且按比例来说，我们 hired 了很多 new grads），recruiting 比我工作过的任何其他 company 都更困难。Senior hiring 并不困难，因为 Centaur 在当地 Austin 有很好的 reputation，但在 new grads 中，没有人听说过我们，也没有人想为我们工作。我在 career fairs 上 recruited 时，必须站到展位前面，招呼路过的人，才会有人与我们交谈。这意味着，我们无法挑剔 interviewed 的对象。我们大幅 ramped up 对 new grads 的 hiring，大约就在 Jeff Atwood 通过他那篇影响很大的文章 [为什么 Programmers 不能.. Program？](https://blog.codinghorror.com/why-cant-programmers-program/) 普及这样一种观点的时候：有一大批 fake programmers 正在 applying for jobs，如果不用 basic coding questions 来 screen people out，最终就会招到不会 program 的 programmers（下面的粗体是他加的）：

> **任何所谓的 programmer，竟然在无法 write 最 simplest 的 programs 的情况下就来 apply for a job，这让我感到不安和震惊**。这对任何以 writes software 为生的人来说，都是一记耳光。……你竟然得做这么多 pre-screening，才能**有幸 interviewing 那些真正能够 program 的 programmers**，实在可悲。如果这事没有那么令人沮丧得要命，倒还挺好笑的

由于我们是一家相对 coding oriented 的 hardware shop（verification engineers 主要 wrote software，而 design engineers wrote 了大量 tooling），我们尝试问一道 simple coding question，要求人们根据一段如何 compute Fibonacci numbers 的说明，code up 一个 function 来 output 它们（naive solution 就可以；并不需要 linear time 或 faster solution）。我们后来放弃了这道题，因为如果不详细地带着他们走完整个过程，就没有人能做出来，这意味着这道 question 对我们而言的 discriminatory power 为 zero。

尽管实际上没有问 coding question，人们仍然会 write 复杂难缠的 concurrent code（internal processor microcode，常常 used barriers 作为 concurrency control mechanism），以及 create tools，而其 velocity 更高、bug rate 更低，超过了我在其他任何工作过的地方所见的水平。

不按其他所有人的方式 hiring，让我们受益良多，因为这意味着，我们尝试并确实 hire 到了其他 companies 没有争抢的人。如果其他 companies 使用的 techniques 是 highly effective 的，这样做就没有道理；但其他 companies 做的事情，是让人们 code FizzBuzz，然后在白板上写一些 algorithms。虽然[人们或许会预期，进行 algorithms interviews 会使 hiring 到的人能够 solve interviews 中所问的那些确切的 problems，但事实并非如此](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/)。[我们做的另一件事，是采用远少于大多数 companies 的 prestige filter](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1425514112642080773)，这也让我们能够 hire 到其他 companies 甚至不会考虑的 great engineers。

我们确实有一些人最终不合适，但从来不是因为他们是无法“write 最 simplest 的 programs”的“所谓的 programmers”。我确实知道两个案例，都是 hiring 了根本不会 program 的“fake programmers，但这两件事都发生在 prestigious companies，而这些 companies 的 coding interviews 在 tech companies 中属于最 rigorous 的一批。其中一个案例中，人们很快就发现那个人不会 code，于是回头查看 interview 时的监控录像，才意识到参加 interviewed 的人与前来上班的并不是同一个人。另一个案例中，那个人在 Google 蒙混了好几年，才有人意识到此人实际上从未 wrote 过任何 code，只有在让别人代做 task 时，tasks 才会被 completed。发现这一点的人最终安排了一次 pair programming session，期间他们发现，此人无法 write 一个 loop，不知道 `=` 与 `==` 之间的 difference，等等，尽管此人已经在 Google 当了多年的“senior SWE”（L5/T5）。

我不会说，采用 coding questions 绝不会帮助你避免 hiring 到 fake programmer；但 fake programmers 的 rate 看起来足够低，以至于一家 small company 即使不问 coding question，也可以十年都不 hire 到 fake programmer，而被骗子盯上的 larger companies，即使问了 coding questions，也仍然无法真正避免他们。

### 附录：importing culture

虽然这篇文章讨论的是 company culture 如何 impacts employees，但 employees 当然也会 impact company culture。在 hiring 中，尤其是在 senior leadership 和 senior ICs 的 hiring 中，他们会如何 impact culture 似乎没有得到足够重视。无论是近距离还是远距离，我都反复见过这样的情况：新 hiring 的 senior person 成功地 import 了自己的 culture，而这种 culture 与现有的 company 的 culture 并不 compatible，在局面逐渐稳定下来的过程中，造成了 serious problems，而且往往还伴随着 high attrition。

如今我在这一行也待了一段时间，已经参与过不少关于非常 senior 的 hires 的讨论，但除了某些实在明显的问题，比如这个人是个混蛋，或在 interview 中发表 racist 或 sexist comments，我从未见过其他人提出：某个人是否会 import incompatible cultural values。

## 原文

Three major tools that companies have to influence behavior are incentives, process, and culture. People often mean different things when talking about these, so I'll provide an example of each so we're on the same page (if you think that I should be using a different word for the concept, feel free to mentally substitute that word).

-   Getting people to show up to meetings on time

    -   Incentive: dock pay for people who are late
    -   Process: don't allow anyone who's late into the meeting
    -   Culture: people feel strongly about showing up on time
-   Getting people to build complex systems

    -   Incentive: require complexity in promo criteria
    -   Process: make process for creating or executing on a work item so heavyweight that people stop doing simple work
    -   Culture: people enjoy building complex systems and/or building complex systems results in respect from peers and/or prestige
-   Avoiding manufacturing defects

    -   Incentive: pay people per good item created and/or dock pay for bad items
    -   Process: have QA check items before shipment and discard bad items
    -   Culture: people value excellence and try very hard to avoid defects

If you read "old school" thought leaders, many of them advocate for a culture-only approach, e.g., [Ken Thompson saying, to reduce bug rate, that tools (which, for the purposes of this post, we'll call process) aren't the answer, having people care to and therefore decide to avoid writing bugs is the answer](https://mobile.twitter.com/danluu/status/885214004649615360) or Bob Martin saying "[The solution to the software apocalypse is not more tools. The solution is better programming discipline](https://www.hillelwayne.com/post/uncle-bob/)."

The emotional reaction those kinds of over-the-top statements evoke combined with the ease of rebutting them has led to a backlash against cultural solutions, leading people to say things like "you should never say that people need more discipline and you should instead look at the incentives of the underlying system", in the same way that the 10x programmer meme and the associated comments have caused a backlash that's led to people to say things like [velocity doesn't matter at all](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/) or there's absolutely no difference in velocity between programmers ([as Jamie Brandon has noted, a lot of velocity comes down to caring about and working on velocity](https://scattered-thoughts.net/writing/moving-faster/), so this is also part of the backlash against culture).

But if we look at quantifiable output, we can see that, even if processes and incentives are the first-line tools a company should reach for, culture also has a large impact. For example, if we look at manufacturing defect rate, some countries persistently have lower defect rates than others on a timescale of decades[1](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:C), generally robust across companies, even when companies are operating factories in multiple countries and importing the same process and incentives to each factory to the extent that's possible, due to cultural differences that impact how people work.

Coming back to programming, Jamie's post on "moving faster" notes:

> The main thing that helped is actually wanting to be faster.
> 
> Early on I definitely cared more about writing 'elegant' code or using fashionable tools than I did about actually solving problems. Maybe not as an explicit belief, but those priorities were clear from my actions.
> 
> I probably also wasn't aware how much faster it was possible to be. I spent my early career working with people who were as slow and inexperienced as I was.
> 
> Over time I started to notice that some people are producing projects that are far beyond what I could do in a single lifetime. I wanted to figure out how to do that, which meant giving up my existing beliefs and trying to discover what actually works.

I was lucky to have the opposite experience starting out since my first full-time job was at Centaur, a company that, at the time, had very high velocity/productivity. I'd say that I've only ever worked on one team with a similar level of productivity, and that's my current team, but my current team is fairly unusual for a team at a tech company (e.g., the median level on my team is "senior staff")[2](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:S). A side effect of having started my career at such a high velocity company is that I generally find the pace of development slow at big companies and I see no reason to move slowly just because that's considered normal. I often hear similar comments from people I talk to at big companies who've previously worked at non-dysfunctional but not even particularly fast startups. A regular survey at one of the trendiest companies around asks "Do you feel like your dev speed is faster or slower than your previous job?" and the responses are bimodal, depending on whether the respondent came from a small company or a big one (with dev speed at TrendCo being slower than at startups and faster than at larger companies).

There's a story that, [IIRC, was told by Brian Enos](https://amzn.to/3EZXykS), where he was practicing timed drills with the goal of practicing until he could complete a specific task at or under his usual time. He was having a hard time hitting his normal time and was annoyed at himself because he was slower than usual and kept at it until he hit his target, at which point he realized he misremembered the target and was accidentally targeting a new personal best time that was better than he thought was possible. While it's too simple to say that we can achieve anything if we put our minds to it, [almost none of us are operating at anywhere near our capacity and what we think we can achieve is often a major limiting factor](https://twitter.com/nickcammarata/status/1362261305357393920). Of course, at the limit, there's a tradeoff between velocity and quality and you can't get velocity "for free", but, when it comes to programming, [we're so far from the Pareto frontier that there are free wins](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/) if you just [realize that they're available](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1442945072144678914).

One way in which culture influences this is that people often absorb their ideas of what's possible from the culture they're in. For a non-velocity example, one thing I noticed after attending [RC](https://www.recurse.com/scout/click?t=b504af89e87b77920c9b60b2a1f6d5e8) was that a lot of speakers at the well-respected non-academic non-enterprise tech conferences, like Deconstruct and Strange Loop, also attended RC. Most people hadn't given talks before attending RC and, when I asked people, a lot of people had wanted to give talks but didn't realize how straightforward the process for becoming a speaker at "big" conferences is (have an idea, write it down, and then submit what you wrote down as a proposal). It turns out that giving talks at conferences is easy to do and a major blocker for many folks is just knowing that it's possible. In an environment where lots of people give talks and, where people who hesitantly ask how they can get started are told that it's straightforward, a lot of people will end up giving talks. The same thing is true of blogging, which is why a disproportionately large fraction of widely read programming bloggers started blogging seriously after attending RC. For many people, the barrier to starting a blog is some combination of realizing it's feasible to start a blog and that, from a technical standpoint, it's very easy to start a blog if you just pick any semi-reasonable toolchain and go through the setup process. And then, because people give talks and write blog posts, they get better at giving talks and writing blog posts so, on average, RC alums are probably better speakers and writers than random programmers even though there's little to no skill transfer or instruction at RC.

Another kind of thing where culture can really drive skills are skills that are highly attitude dependent. An example of this is debugging. As Julia Evans has noted, [having a good attitude is a major component of debugging effectiveness](https://twitter.com/b0rk/status/1249715842708844544). This is something Centaur was very good at instilling in people, to the point that nearly everyone in my org at Centaur would be considered a very strong debugger by tech company standards.

At big tech companies, it's common to see people give up on bugs after trying a few random things that didn't work. In one extreme example, someone I know at a mid-10-figure tech company said that it never makes sense to debug a bug that takes more than a couple hours to debug because engineer time is too valuable to waste on bugs that take longer than that to debug, an attitude this person picked up from the first team they worked on. Someone who picks up that kind of attitude about debugging is unlikely to become a good debugger until they change their attitude, and many people, including this person, carry the attitudes and habits they pick up at their first job around for quite a long time[3](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:N).

By tech standards, Centaur is an extreme example in the other direction. If you're designing a CPU, it's not considered ok to walk away from a bug that you don't understand. Even if the symptom of the bug isn't serious, it's possible that the underlying cause is actually serious and you won't observe the more serious symptom until you've shipped a chip, so you have to go after even seemingly trivial bugs. Also, it's pretty common for there to be no good or even deterministic reproduction of a bug. The repro is often something like "run these programs with these settings on the system and then the system will hang and/or corrupt data after some number of hours or days". When debugging a bug like that, there will be numerous wrong turns and dead ends, some of which can eat up weeks or months. As a new employee watching people work on those kinds of bugs, what I observed was that people would come in day after day and track down bugs like that, not getting frustrated and not giving up. When that's the culture and everyone around you has that attitude, it's natural to pick up the same attitude. Also, a lot of practical debugging skill is applying tactical skills picked up from having debugged a lot of problems, which naturally falls out of spending a decent amount of time debugging problems with a positive attitude, especially with exposure to hard debugging problems.

Of course, most bugs at tech companies don't warrant months of work, but there's a big difference between intentionally leaving some bugs undebugged because some bugs aren't worth fixing and having poor debugging skills from never having ever debugged a serious bug and then not being able to debug any bug that isn't completely trivial.

Cultural attitudes can drive a lot more than individual skills like debugging. Centaur had, per capita, by far the lowest serious production bug rate of any company I've worked for, at well under one per year with ~100 engineers. By comparison, I've never worked on a team 1/10th that size that didn't have at least 10x the rate of serious production issues. Like most startups, Centaur was very light on process and it was also much lighter on incentives than the big tech companies I've worked for.

One component of this was that there was a culture of owning problems, regardless of what team you were on. If you saw a problem, you'd fix it, or, if there was a very obvious owner, you'd tell them about the problem and they'd fix it. There weren't roadmaps, standups, kanban, or anything else to get people to work on important problems. People did it without needed to be reminded or prompted.

That's the opposite of what I've seen at two of the three big tech companies I've worked for, where the median person avoids touching problems outside of their team's mandate like the plague, and someone who isn't politically savvy who brings up a problem to another team will get a default answer of "sorry, this isn't on our roadmap for the quarter, perhaps we can put this on the roadmap in \[two quarters from now\]", with the same response repeated to anyone naive enough to bring up the same issue two quarters later. At every tech company I've worked for, huge, extremely costly, problems slip through the cracks all the time because no one wants to pick them up. I never observed that happening at Centaur.

A side effect of big company tech culture is that someone who wants to actually do the right thing can easily do very high (positive) impact work by just going around and fixing problems that any intern could solve, if they're willing to ignore organizational processes and incentives. You can't shake a stick without [hitting a problem that's worth more to the company than my expected lifetime earnings](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/802971209176477696) and it's easy to knock off multiple such problems per year. [Of course, the same forces that cause so many trivial problems to not get solved mean that people who solve those problems don't get rewarded for their work](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/#appendix-misaligned-incentive-hedgehog-defense-part-3)[4](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:B).

Conversely, in eight years at Centaur, I only found one trivial problem whose fix was worth more than I'll earn in my life because, in general, problems would get solved before they got to that point. I've seen various big company attempts to fix this problem using incentives (e.g., monetary rewards for solving important problems) and process (e.g., [making a giant list of all projects/problems, on the order of 1000 projects, and having a single person order them, along with a bureaucratic system where everyone has to constantly provide updates on their progress via JIRA so that PMs can keep sending progress updates to the person who's providing a total order over the work of thousands of engineers](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1497980098107953152)[5](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:R)), but none of those attempts have worked even half as well as having a culture of ownership (to be fair to incentives, I've heard that FB uses monetary rewards to good effect, but [I've failed FB's interview three times](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1447268693075841024), so I haven't been able to observe how that works myself).

Another component that resulted in a relatively low severe bug rate was that, across the company at Centaur, people cared about quality in a way that I've never seen at a team level let alone at an org level at a big tech company. When you have a collection of people who care about quality and feel that no issue is off limits, you'll get quality. And when you onboard people, as long as you don't do it so quickly that the culture is overwhelmed by the new hires, they'll also tend to pick up the same habits and values, especially when you hire new grads. While it's not exactly common, there are plenty of small firms out there with a culture of excellence that generally persists without heavyweight processes or big incentives, but this doesn't work at big tech companies since they've all gone through a hypergrowth period where it's impossible to maintain such extreme (by mainstream standards) cultural values.

So far, we've mainly discussed companies transmitting culture to people, but something that I think is no less important is how people then carry that culture with them when they leave. I've been [reasonably successful since changing careers from hardware to software](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1444034823329177602) and I think that, among the factors that are under my control, one of the biggest ones is that I picked up effective cultural values from the first place I worked full-time and continue to operate as in the same way, which is highly effective. I've also seen this in other people who, career-wise, "grew up" in a culture of excellence and then changed to a different field where there's even less direct skill transfer, e.g., from skiing to civil engineering. Relatedly, if you read books from people who discuss the reasons why they were very effective in their field, e.g., [Practical Shooting by Brian Enos](https://amzn.to/3EZXykS), [Playing to Win by Dan Sirlin](https://www.sirlin.net/ptw), etc., the books tend to contain the same core ideas (serious observation and improvement of skills, the importance of avoiding emotional self-sabotage, the importance of intuition, etc.).

Anyway, I think that cultural transmission of values and skills is an underrated part of choosing a job (some things I would consider overrated are [prestige](https://www.patreon.com/posts/25835707) and [general reputation](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1275191896097189888) and that people should be thoughtful about what cultures they spend time in because not many people are able to avoid at least somewhat absorbing the cultural values around them[6](https://danluu.com/culture/#fn:L).

Although this post is oriented around tech, there's nothing specific to tech about this. A classic example is how idealistic students will go to law school with the intention of doing "save the world" type work and then absorb the [prestige-transmitted cultural values](https://www.patreon.com/posts/25835707) of students around then go into the most prestigious job they can get which, when it's not a clerkship, will be a "BIGLAW" job that's the opposite of "save the world" work. To first approximation, everyone thinks "that will never happen to me", but from having watched many people join organizations where they [initially find the values and culture very wrong](https://danluu.com/wat/), almost no one is able to stay without, to some extent, absorbing the values around them; [very few people are ok with everyone around them looking at them like they're an idiot for having the wrong values](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/).

### Appendix: Bay area culture

One thing I admire about the bay area is how infectious people's attitudes are with respect to trying to change the world. Everywhere I've lived, people gripe about problems (the mortgage industry sucks, selling a house is high friction, etc.). Outside of the bay area, it's just griping, but in the bay, when I talk to someone who was griping about something a year ago, there's a decent chance they've started a startup to try to address one of the problems they're complaining about. I don't think that people in the bay area are fundamentally different from people elsewhere, it's more that when you're surrounded by people who are willing to walk away from their jobs to try to disrupt an entrenched industry, it seems pretty reasonable to do the same thing (which also leads to network effects that make it easier from a "technical" standpoint, e.g., easier fundraising). [There's a kind of earnestness in these sorts of complaints and attempts to fix them that's easy to mock](https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/05/11/silicon-valley-a-reality-check/), but [that earnestness is something I really admire](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/).

Of course, [not all of bay area culture is positive](https://twitter.com/pushcx/status/1442860058660913166). The bay has, among other things, [a famously flaky culture](https://devonzuegel.com/post/why-is-flaking-so-widespread-in-san-francisco) to an extent I found shocking when I moved there. Relatively early on in my time there, I met some old friends for dinner and texted them telling them I was going to be about 15 minutes late. They were shocked when I showed up because they thought that saying that I was going to be late actually meant that I wasn't going to show up (another norm that surprised me that's an even more extreme version was that, for many people, not confirming plans shortly before their commencement means that the person has cancelled, i.e., plans are cancelled by default).

A related norm that I've heard people complain about is how management and leadership will say yes to everything in a "people pleasing" move to avoid conflict, which actually increases conflict as people who heard "yes" as a "yes" and not as "I'm saying yes to avoid saying no but don't actually mean yes" are later surprised that "yes" meant "no".

### Appendix: Centaur's hiring process

One comment people sometimes have when I talk about Centaur is that they must've had some kind of incredibly rigorous hiring process that resulted in hiring elite engineers, but the hiring process was much less selective than any "brand name" big tech company I've worked for (Google, MS, and Twitter) and not obviously more selective than boring, old school, companies I've worked for (IBM and Micron). The "one weird trick" was onboarding, not hiring.

For new grad hiring (and, proportionally, we hired a lot of new grads), recruiting was more difficult than at any other company I'd worked for. Senior hiring wasn't difficult because Centaur had a good reputation locally, in Austin, but among new grads, no one had heard of us and no one wanted to work for us. When I recruited at career fairs, I had to stand out in front of our booth and flag down people who were walking by to get anyone to talk to us. This meant that we couldn't be picky about who we interviewed. We really ramped up hiring of new grads around the time that Jeff Atwood popularized the idea that there are a bunch of fake programmers out there applying for jobs and that you'd end up with programmers who can't program if you don't screen people out with basic coding questions in his very influential post, [Why Can't Programmers.. Program?](https://blog.codinghorror.com/why-cant-programmers-program/) (the bolding below is his):

> **I am disturbed and appalled that any so-called programmer would apply for a job without being able to write the simplest of programs**. That's a slap in the face to anyone who writes software for a living. ... It's a shame you have to do so much pre-screening to **have the luxury of interviewing programmers who can actually program**. It'd be funny if it wasn't so damn depressing

Since we were a relatively coding oriented hardware shop (verification engineers primarily wrote software and design engineers wrote a lot of tooling), we tried asking a simple coding question where people were required to code up a function to output Fibonacci numbers given a description of how to compute them (the naive solution was fine; a linear time or faster solution wasn't necessary). We dropped that question because no one got it without being walked through the entire thing in detail, which meant that the question had zero discriminatory power for us.

Despite not really asking a coding question, people did things like write hairy concurrent code (internal processor microcode, which often used barriers as the concurrency control mechanism) and create tools at a higher velocity and lower bug rate than I've seen anywhere else I've worked.

We were much better off avoiding hiring the way everyone else was because that meant we tried to and did hire people that other companies weren't competing over. That wouldn't make sense if other companies were using techniques that were highly effective but other companies were doing things like asking people to code FizzBuzz and then whiteboard some algorithms. While [one might expect that doing algorithms interviews would result in hiring people who can solve the exact problems people ask about in interviews, but this turns out not to be the case](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/). [The other thing we did was have much less of a prestige filter than most companies](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1425514112642080773), which also let us hire great engineers that other companies wouldn't even consider.

We did have some people who didn't work out, but it was never because they were "so-called programmers" who couldn't "write the simplest of programs". I do know of two cases of "fake programmers being hired who literally couldn't program, but both were at prestigious companies that have among the most rigorous coding interviews done at tech companies. In one case, it was discovered pretty quickly that the person couldn't code and people went back to review security footage from the interview and realized that the person who interviewed wasn't the person who showed up to do the job. In the other, the person was able to sneak under the radar at Google for multiple years before someone realized that the person never actually wrote any code and tasks only got completed when they got someone else to do the task. The person who realized eventually scheduled a pair programming session, where they discovered that the person wasn't able to write a loop, didn't know the difference between `=` and `==`, etc., despite being a "senior SWE" (L5/T5) at Google for years.

I'm not going to say that having coding questions will never save you from hiring a fake programmer, but the rate of fake programmers appears to be very low enough that a small company can go a decade without hiring a fake programmer without asking a coding question and larger companies that are targeted by scammers still can't really avoid them even after asking coding questions.

### Appendix: importing culture

Although this post is about how company culture impacts employees, of course employees impact company culture as well. Something that seems underrated in hiring, especially of senior leadership and senior ICs, is how they'll impact culture. Something I've repeatedly seen, both up close, and from a distance, is the hiring of a new senior person who manages to import their culture, which isn't compatible with the existing company's culture, causing serious problems and, frequently, high attrition, as things settle down.

Now that I've been around for a while, I've been in the room for discussions on a number of very senior hires and I've never seen anyone else bring up whether or not someone will import incompatible cultural values other than really blatant issues, like the person being a jerk or making racist or sexist comments in the interview.
