# Attention is your scarcest resource

---

## 正文

和许多人一样，我大多数最好的点子都是在洗澡时想到的。

这有时很烦人：我每天需要的好点子，可能不止洗一次澡所能想到的那么多，但我也不想最后变成一颗干瘪却富有洞见的西梅。不过，大多数时候，洗澡时的点子正是激励我保持身上气味还算正常的动力，所以我也只好不情不愿地接受这个限制。

这个限制最让我束手束脚的时候，是我第一次成为 manager 时。我当时只有几个 reports，所以 managing 他们并不是一份 full-time job。但我实在做得很差，因此这本来_就应该_是我洗澡时所有灵感都花在上面的事情。

不幸的是，我把 non-management 的时间都花在了 programming 上。即使我试着在洗澡时思考那些棘手又尴尬的 people issues，思绪也总是不知不觉地跑去 tackle 那些美妙诱人的 software design problems。

Management Attempt #1 进行得不太顺利；由于被 programming distracted，我犯下了许多令人难堪的错误，让我的 reports 日子更不好过，这又让我感到压力和内疚。几个月后，我最终又回去做了 individual contributor。

我的这段经历，与 Byrne Hobart 的[关于 knowledge work 中 focus 的 observation](https://diff.substack.com/p/the-future-of-remote-work-is-not)有着强烈的共鸣：

> knowledge workers 的 output 会随着 focus 呈现极其 skewed 的分布。productivity tiers 似乎是这样的：
> 
> 1.  对手头工作的 focused 程度 <10%：有相当大的被解雇的风险。
>     
> 2.  10-50% 的 focus：“meets expectations”，能定期加薪。
>     
> 3.  50%+ 的 focus：是 superstar、10x engineer，注定大有作为。
>     

“50%+ focus”大致就是某件事成为[你脑海中最重要的念头](http://www.paulgraham.com/top.html)的时候。那时，你开始在意它，在意到洗澡时也会想着它。那时，你开始习惯性地问：“这怎样才能更快？”那时，你变得[relentlessly resourceful](http://www.paulgraham.com/relres.html)。也差不多是你开始[惹 coworkers 和/或另一半烦](https://www.benkuhn.net/impatient/)的时候，不过，经过练习，这一点是可以避免的。

最重要的是，同一时间，你只能对一件事保持 50%+-focused——情况不好时，一件也没有。因此，conserve 你的 attention 至关重要，这样你才能把它花在真正重要的事情上。

我发现了几种不同的策略，能在这方面帮到我：

### 发自内心地在乎

仅仅因为觉得自己“应该”做某件事，就想调动 50%+ 的 brain 来做它，是很难的。你的 superego 或许会听从“logic”和“reason”这些新潮玩意儿，但你 brain 中猴子时代的那些部分，可没那么容易被糊弄。要想能够可靠地 focus 于某件事，你需要从 intuitively、emotionally 的层面 invested 于 outcome。

对我来说，这不会自动发生——例如，尽管[我对 Wave 正在做的事情兴奋不已](https://www.wave.com/en/blog/world/)，但如果我 work remotely 太久，没有去 Senegal 看看我们的 operations，我日常的 motivation 就会变差。除非我能隔一段时间就亲眼看到，人们确实在使用我们正在 building 的东西，否则这一切就开始显得有点虚假，仿佛所谓的“现实世界”是一个 simulation，而我只能通过 Slack channels 和 `git push` 与之 communicate。

### 只做一件事（Monotask）

作为 programmer，我曾尽量确保自己任何时候都只做一件事。即使我在那件事上卡住了——比如，我因等待 tech partner 给我 API documentation 而被 blocked——我也会让自己继续卡着，而不是溜去做别的事情。

短期来看，这让我变得不那么 efficient，因为我花在 programming 上的时间少了，盯着天花板发呆的时间多了。但如果发呆足够久，我最终就会气到忍不住，比如，在一阵怒火中 reverse-engineer 那位 partner 的 API。这使我能更快地 shipping 我最重要的 projects，从而获得[更快的 compounding growth](https://www.benkuhn.net/impatient/#startups)。

### 躲避 obligations

成为 manager 后，“只做一件事”变得不可能了：有太多小 tasks，也有太多 projects 在 in parallel 进行。但我有一条类似的原则：我只能有一个_对他人的 obligation_。“给这个 project 提点建议？”随时都可以！“Review 我这个 1,000-line change？”只有在 review 不会 blocking 你 shipping 它的情况下才行。“时不时来看看，确保这件事不会失控？”抱歉，我得，呃，去补个妆。“Figure out 这个 process 应该是什么样？”过几年我再答复你。（最好的 process 就是没有 process，对吧？）

在 Management Attempt #1 期间，我的 programming project 之所以如此让我 distracted，是因为其他人非常在意我能否完成它——如果我不得不 de-prioritize 它，就会让那些人难过。这使得即使我没在做它，它也会占据我的 attention（因为我担心让别人失望）。不幸的是，由于 management work 总是一阵阵紧急地涌来，我常常最终陷入不得不 delay programming 或 management 其中一项的处境——也就是说，要在让两拨不同的人难过之间做选择——而在那些情况下，我很难 control 自己的 focus。

现在我注意到了这一点，便允许自己 in parallel 为多件事情 contribute，只要它们不会考验我的 object permanence skills——如果我今天完全忘记了这个 project，会发生什么坏事吗？只要答案是否定的（通常是因为另有其人在主动推动事情），我就能相当确定，这个 project 不会在我洗澡时试图占领我的脑袋。

### 给破事设定时间框（Timebox）

即使我只有一个其他人依赖的 major project，也总是不知怎么就有一堆琐事要做——回复 emails、推掉 secondary obligations、购买生活必需品、补妆、提交 expense reports，等等。

对于这些事，我采用一个“破事 timebox”——每周几次，每次一个小时，在这段时间里处理[所有那些人生苦短、不值得花时间的事情](http://paulgraham.com/vb.html)。在每周剩下的 165 小时里，我都会坚决尽力不去想这些破事，无论别人多么纠缠我，问一个 [1.5" tungsten cube](https://thume.ca/2019/03/03/my-tungsten-cube/) 到底算不算 legitimate work expense。

为了不让破事在这一周剩下的时间里 distract 我，我会尽量 minimize 自己的“open loops”数量——也就是我已经 started、但尚未 completed 的 projects 或 processes。就像我会对 programming 进行 single-task 直到完成一样，我会选择打电话，而不是用 email threads，更不用说——老天保佑——短信或 instant messaging 了。我花了一段时间才让朋友们养成不向我 instant message 的习惯，不过如今，他们几乎能一字不差地记住我对它多么 distracting 的那些抱怨。

* * *

Management Attempt #2 对我来说也不太顺利。但到了 Management Attempt #3，我终于做得还算可以。部分原因是，我避免了重复 Attempts #1 和 #2 中那些具体的错误。但还有一部分原因是，在 Attempt #3 时，我们的 engineering team grew 得足够快，让我从“不做 managing”直接进入“full-time managing”，中间没有一个让我 distracted、搞砸事情并深陷痛苦的 transitional period。

一旦我开始 full-time managing，就没有什么闪闪发亮的东西来分散我的注意力了，我也能把洗澡的时间花在 focusing 如何成为更好的 manager 上。而当我达到 50%+ focused 之后，嗯，我还没有成为“superstar 10x manager”，但很快就不再是 0.1x 了。

## 原文

Like many people, I have most of my best ideas in the shower.

This is sometimes annoying: I could use more than one shower’s worth of good ideas a day, but I’d rather not end up as a shrivelled yet insightful prune. Mostly, though, shower ideas are the incentive that keeps me smelling okay, so I grudgingly accept the constraint.

The time when it was most constraining was the first time I became a manager. I only had a few reports, so managing them wasn’t a full-time job. But I was very bad at it, and so it _should have_ been what I spent all my shower insights on.

Unfortunately, I was spending my non-management time on programming. And even if I tried to use my showers to think about my thorny and awkward people issues, my mind somehow always wandered off to tackle those nice, juicy software design problems instead.

Management Attempt #1 didn’t go very well; being distracted by programming, I made lots of embarrassing mistakes that made my reports’ lives harder, which in turn made me feel stressed and guilty. I ended up bouncing back to being an individual contributor after a few months.

That experience of mine resonates strongly with Byrne Hobart’s [observation about focus in knowledge work](https://diff.substack.com/p/the-future-of-remote-work-is-not):

> The output of knowledge workers is extremely skewed based on focus. The productivity tiers seem to be:
> 
> 1.  <10% focused on the job at hand: meaningful risk of getting fired.
>     
> 2.  10-50% focus: “meets expectations,” gets regular raises.
>     
> 3.  50%+ focus: superstar, 10x engineer, destined for greatness.
>     

“50%+ focus” is roughly when something becomes [the top idea in your mind](http://www.paulgraham.com/top.html). It’s when you start caring enough to think about it in the shower. It’s when you start habitually asking “how could this go faster?” It’s when you get [relentlessly resourceful](http://www.paulgraham.com/relres.html). It’s around when you start [annoying your coworkers and/or significant other](https://www.benkuhn.net/impatient/), although that part is avoidable with practice.

Most importantly, you can only be 50%+-focused on one thing at a time—or zero, in bad cases. That makes it critical to conserve your attention, so that you can spend it on what matters.

I’ve found a few different strategies that help me with this:

### Care viscerally

It’s hard to recruit 50%+ of your brain to work on something just because you feel like you “should” do it. Your superego might listen to this newfangled “logic” and “reason” stuff, but the monkey-era parts of your brain can’t be fooled so easily. To be reliably able to focus on something, you need to be intuitively, emotionally invested in the outcome.

For me, that’s not automatic—for instance, even though [I’m incredibly excited about what Wave is doing](https://www.wave.com/en/blog/world/), my day-to-day motivation gets worse if I work remotely for too long without visiting our operations in Senegal. Unless I can periodically see for myself that people are using the things we’re building, it starts feeling a little bit fake, like the so-called “real world” is a simulation that I can only communicate with via Slack channels and `git push`.

### Monotask

As a programmer, I tried to make sure that I was only ever working on one thing at a time. Even if I got stuck on that one thing—say I was blocked on waiting for a tech partner to give me API documentation—I’d let myself stay stuck instead of sliding off to work on something else.

In the short term, this made me less efficient, because I’d spend less time programming and more time staring vacantly at the ceiling. But if I stared vacantly for long enough, I’d eventually get mad enough to, e.g., reverse-engineer the partner’s API in a fit of rage. This resulted in me shipping my most important projects faster, hence getting [faster compounding growth](https://www.benkuhn.net/impatient/#startups).

### Evade obligations

As a manager, it became impossible to “only work on one thing:” there were too many small tasks and too many projects going on in parallel. But I have a similar principle: I can only have one _obligation to other people_. “Give advice on this project?” Whenever you want! “Review my 1,000-line change?” Only if review isn’t blocking you from shipping it. “Check in every so often to make sure this doesn’t go off the rails?” Sorry, I have to, uh, powder my nose. “Figure out what the process should be?” I’ll get back to you in a couple years. (The best process is no process, right?)

During Management Attempt #1, my programming project distracted me so much because other people cared a lot that I finished it—if I ever had to de-prioritize it, I would make those people sad. This made it take up my attention (by worrying about letting people down) even when I wasn’t working on it. Unfortunately, since management work comes in urgent bursts, I’d often end up in a situation where I had to delay either the programming or the management—i.e., choose between making two different sets of people sad—and in those cases it was very hard for me to control my focus.

Now that I’ve noticed this, I allow myself to contribute to multiple things in parallel, as long as they don’t tax my object permanence skills—if I totally forgot about this project today, would anything bad happen? As long as the answer is no (usually because someone else is the one taking initiative to move things forward), I can be pretty sure that project won’t try to colonize my brain in the shower.

### Timebox bullshit

Even if I have only one major project that other people depend on, I somehow always end up with a bunch of minor chores to do—responding to emails, deflecting secondary obligations, buying household necessities, powdering my nose, filing expense reports, etc.

For these, I employ a “bullshit timebox”—a one-hour period a few times a week when I do [all the things life is too short for](http://paulgraham.com/vb.html). During the remaining 165 hours per week, I do my resolute best not to think about any of the bullshit, no matter how much people bug me about whether a [1.5" tungsten cube](https://thume.ca/2019/03/03/my-tungsten-cube/) is really a legitimate work expense.

In order for bullshit not to distract me for the rest of the week, I try to minimize my number of “open loops”—projects or processes that I’ve started but not completed. Much like how I single-task on programming until it’s done, I’ll make phone calls instead of email threads, or, God forbid, texts or instant messaging. It took a while for me to train my friends not to instant message me, but by now they remember my rants about how distracting it is, practically word-for-word.

* * *

Management Attempt #2 didn’t go great for me either. But I finally did an okay job at Management Attempt #3. Part of this was because I avoided repeating the particular mistakes of Attempts #1 and #2. But part of it was that on Attempt #3, our engineering team grew quickly enough that I went from “no managing” straight to “full-time managing” with no transitional period in which to be distracted, screw up and be miserable.

Once I was full-time managing, I had no shiny distractions and was able to spend my showers focusing on how to be a better manager. And once I was 50%+ focused, well, I haven’t become a “superstar 10x manager” yet, but I quickly stopped being 0.1x.
