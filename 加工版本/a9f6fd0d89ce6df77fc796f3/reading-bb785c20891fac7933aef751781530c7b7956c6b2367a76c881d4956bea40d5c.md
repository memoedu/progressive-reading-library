## Tabooing words（禁用词语）

很多 arguments 实际上是在争 definitions。总体而言，这类 argument 并没有用。

例如，“如果一棵树在森林里倒下，却没有人 hears 它，它是否会 make a sound？”这个 question 完全是在讨论 words。以下出自 Eliezer Yudkowsky 的经典 essay _[Taboo Your Words（禁用你的词语）](http://lesswrong.com/lw/nu/taboo_your_words/)_：

> > Albert：“一棵树在无人的森林里倒下，会 makes a sound。”
> > 
> > Barry：“一棵树在无人的森林里倒下，不会 make a sound。”
> 
> 显然，既然一个人说“sound”，另一个人说“not sound”，我们就一定有了 contradiction，对吧？但假设他们都在说话前先对各自的 pointers 进行 dereference：
> 
> > Albert：“一棵树在无人的森林里倒下，会 matches \[membership test：这个 event generates acoustic vibrations\]。”Barry：“一棵树在无人的森林里倒下，不会 match \[membership test：这个 event generates auditory experiences\]。”
> 
> 现在，表面上的 collision 已经不存在了——他们所需做的，只是不让自己使用 sound 这个 word。如果“acoustic vibrations”又引发争议，我们就再玩一次 Taboo，改说“pressure waves in a material medium”；如果有必要，我们还可以对“wave”这个 word 再玩一次 Taboo，用 wave equation 替换它。（对“auditory experience”进行 Play Taboo，你就会得到“human brain 内的那种 sensory processing，其 takes as input 的是 frequency mixes 的 linear time series……”）

关键在于，并没有某个特定的东西是 _sound_ “应该”表示的。Language 是一种 tool；definitions 并非来自什么 Platonic realm；它们之所以存在，是因为它们有用，能将 reality 划分成方便使用的 categories。除非有人对 definitions 的滥用实在太过分，否则，如果你发现自己在 meanings 上想得太费劲，大概不如就此打住，回去想之前那些更有成效的事情。对相关 word 进行 Tabooing，就是实现这一点的好办法。

## Sneaking in connotations（暗中夹带内涵）

有些 definitional arguments，正是由 Sneaking in connotations 引起的。

我最喜欢的 use case，是围绕“什么是 art？”展开的 debates。表面上，这类 arguments 似乎是在讨论 art 的 definition 应该是什么。但还是那句话，除了在 delineating categories 方面对我们有什么用，这个“应该”没有别的来源。然而，考虑到 art 的各种 definitions 有多么含混，似乎没有哪个 definition 真能帮助你 communicate。那么，为什么还要 argue 呢？

但“art”这样的 words 很特别，因为任何被称作“art”的东西，都会立刻笼罩上一层 goodness 和 worthiness 的 halo。人们不停地争论“video games 能否成为 art”，是因为有些人听到“video games”，想到的是五岁小孩玩 Mortal Kombat；他们觉得，这不配与 Rembrandt 苦干数月的作品享有同样的 halo。

其他会引起这类 argument 的 words 还包括“love”“happiness”“good”“evil”“right”“wrong”等等。这样的 words 真的很多。

## Steelmanning（钢人化）

Steelmanning 是 strawmanning 的 opposite。如果你_确实_在进行 truth-seeking argument，而不只是装装样子，那么这就是你会对自己的“opponent”做的事。

Paul Graham 写过一篇 essay，在其中 [proposing](http://www.paulgraham.com/disagree.html) 了 quality 逐步提高的 seven types of argumentation，从“name-calling”（DH0）一直到“refuting the central point”（DH6）。Black Belt Bayesian 的 Steven 又加上了[第八个 level](http://www.acceleratingfuture.com/steven/?p=155)：

> 当有人提出一个 argument 时，你会了解到那个 argument。但你通常也会了解到一些本可以提出、却没有提出的 arguments。有时，那些 arguments 能 work，而原来的 argument 却不能。
> 
> 如果你关心的是在 disputes 中站在 right 的一边，你就会 refute 你的 opponents 的 arguments。但如果你关心的是 producing truth，你就会替你的 opponents 修好他们的 arguments。
> 
> 要获胜，你不仅必须与遇到的生物战斗；还必须与能从它的尸体构造出来的最可怕的东西战斗。

顾名思义，对一个 argument 进行 steelman，就是在你 refute 它之前，先把它变成那个 argument 的 strongest possible version。

## 最不方便的 possible world

听到 doctor parable 的人，往往会设法钻空子，绕开它。以下出自[同名的 LessWrong post](http://lesswrong.com/lw/2k/the_least_convenient_possible_world/)：

> > 你是一家乡村小 hospital 的 doctor。你有 ten 位 patients，每个人都因缺少一种不同的 organ 而奄奄一息……。一位旅人走进 hospital……。你意识到，通过 killing 这位旅人，并把他的 organs 分给你的 patients，你就可以 save ten lives。这样做究竟是不是 moral？
> 
> 今天，我不想讨论这个 problem 的 answer。我想讨论我的一位朋友给出的 answer，因为我认为，它揭示了一种很有意思、rationalists 需要警惕的 defense mechanism。我的朋友说：
> 
> > 这不会是 moral。毕竟，人们经常会 reject 来自 random donors 的 organs。这位旅人对你的 patients 而言，很可能是 genetic mismatch；而那些 transplantees 的余生都得使用 immunosuppressants，结果却还是会在几年内因 drugs failed 而死去。
> 
> ……$$H$$e 完全没抓住重点，错失了一次宝贵的、用来 examine morality 的 nature 的机会。
> 
> 所以我问他：“在最不方便的 possible world 里，也就是每个人都与其他所有人 genetically compatible、这个 objection 是 invalid 的那个 possible world 里，你会怎么做？”

当你害怕自己可能不得不作出一个无法让所有人满意的 judgment 时，Fighting the hypothetical 是一种自然反应。但通常，拒绝 judge 就等于默认 siding with one side；通过逃避思考来拖延 decision，也无济于事。况且，提出这个 hypothetical question 的目的，并不是弄清在现实生活中该怎么做，而是 probe ethics 的 [corner cases](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_case)，以弄清一个人的 morals 究竟如何 work。所以，fighting 这个 hypothetical 不仅没用，而且 counterproductive，还是 [logically rude](http://legacy.earlham.edu/~peters/writing/rudeness.htm) 的。要预先避免这种情况，就在你的 hypotheticals 后补上一句：“在最不方便的 possible world 里。”

## 打破直觉（Breaking intuition）

Intuition-breaking thought experiments 或多或少是 fighting the hypothetical 的另一面。

Simplified thought experiments 是 moral debate 的主要 tools 之一。其思路是，如果你的 ethics 在某些 hypothetical scenarios 中给出 repugnant answer，那它就没有 capturing morality 的某个重要 aspect，因此需要 refinement。但有时，如果 experiment 的 structure breaks 我们的 intuitions，就不应因为一个 answer 显得 repugnant 而责难它。

例如，许多人把上面的 doctor question 用作反对 utilitarianism 的 counter-argument：utilitarian answer（在 least convenient possible world 中，你 morally 应该 kill 那位 healthy traveller，以 save 那 ten 位 transplantees）如此 counterintuitive，以至于令人觉得 repugnant，所以 utilitarianism 是 unacceptable 的。

但我们的 moral intuitions 并不是为 deal with simple、clear-cut scenarios 而 structured 的。作用于这些 intuitions 的 Selective pressures 是在 real world 中 act 的，而在那里，没有什么是 simple 且 clear-cut 的，出人意料或不寻常的事情很可能出岔子。因此，即使从 logically 来说，我们已被告知自己在 hypothetical 中是 completely sure 的，我们的 intuitions 仍倾向于 add uncertainty，并 favor 那些在 uncertainty 下依然 hold up well 的 solutions。

在 doctor parable 的 least convenient possible world 中，你有 100% 的把握，harvesting 那位 healthy traveller 的 organs 将 save 全部 10 人，而且不会有 ill effects。但一个普通人几乎永远不会有这样的 certainty，因此，在那种情况下，_不应指望我们的 intuitions 给出“right” answer_。

## 有理由怀疑与有理由相信（Reason to suspect vs. reason to believe）

Argument from authority（“Einstein believes toves 是 slithy 的，因此 toves 是 slithy 的”）被广泛视为一种 fallacy。如果 Einstein believed toves 是 slithy 的，那就告诉我使他 believe 这一点的 process，而不只是 results！

然而，当 Andrew Wiles 告诉我[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/debate/#fn:1) `$$\forall n \in \mathbb{N}: n > 2 \implies \not\exists a, b, c \in \mathbb{N} : a^n + b^n = c^n$$`（[Fermat’s Last Theorem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_Last_Theorem)）时，我 believe 他，即使我并不理解他的 proof。这是为什么？这不正是 argument from authority 吗？

嗯，事实表明，experts 对事情的判断确实常常是 right 的，所以“experts 说 toves 是 slithy 的”通常是 toves 是 slithy 的 [evidence](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference)。当人们说 appeal to authority 是 fallacious 的时，他们真正想表达的是，与给出 convincing argument 相比，它是 _strictly worse evidence_：如果 Einstein 的 beliefs 与 convincing arguments 指向不同方向，那么，比起他碰巧是 right 的，更有可能是他受到了某种 bias 的影响。如果你想在谈论 experts 的 beliefs 时避免 semantic confusion，可以说 Einstein 的 opinion 给了你 _reason to suspect_，使你怀疑 toves 是 slithy 的；而 convincing argument 给了你 _reason to believe_ 这一点。

说句技术宅式的题外话：使用 [belief networks](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_network) 可以把这一点表述得非常 precise。令 `$E_T$` 表示“Einstein believes toves 是 slithy 的”；`$A_T$` 表示“存在一个说明 toves 是 slithy 的 good argument”；`$T$` 表示“toves 是 slithy 的”。那么 causal diagram 就是 `$T \to A_T \to E_T$`（另外还有一些 factors，比如“Einstein 希望 toves 是 slithy 的”，它们 pointing to `$E_T$`）。所以，如果我们 observe `$E_T$`，它可能会被其他 factors confounded；但如果我们 observe `$A_T$`，我们对 `$T$` 就会 certain 得多：事实上，那个 observation 会完全 [screens off](http://lesswrong.com/lw/lx/argument_screens_off_authority/) `$E_T$`。

需要注意的是，observe 是否存在 good argument，往往比 observe experts 的 opinions 要 costlier 得多——例如，要 observe Wiles 的 argument 是否 good，我就需要一个 math 的 PhD。所以，仅仅把 argument from authority 称作“fallacy”，并不能 capture 其中的 subtlety。它是 weaker evidence，但有时仍然有用。

## Tabooing words

Lots of arguments are secretly about definitions. Broadly, this kind of argument is not useful.

For example, the question “If a tree falls in the forest, and nobody hears it, does it make a sound?” is entirely about words. From Eliezer Yudkowsky’s classic essay _[Taboo Your Words](http://lesswrong.com/lw/nu/taboo_your_words/)_:

> > Albert: “A tree falling in a deserted forest makes a sound.”
> > 
> > Barry: “A tree falling in a deserted forest does not make a sound.”
> 
> Clearly, since one says “sound” and one says “not sound”, we must have a contradiction, right? But suppose that they both dereference their pointers before speaking:
> 
> > Albert: “A tree falling in a deserted forest matches \[membership test: this event generates acoustic vibrations\].” Barry: “A tree falling in a deserted forest does not match \[membership test: this event generates auditory experiences\].”
> 
> Now there is no longer an apparent collision—all they had to do was prohibit themselves from using the word sound. If “acoustic vibrations” came into dispute, we would just play Taboo again and say “pressure waves in a material medium”; if necessary we would play Taboo again on the word “wave” and replace it with the wave equation. (Play Taboo on “auditory experience” and you get “That form of sensory processing, within the human brain, which takes as input a linear time series of frequency mixes…”)

The point is that there’s not a specific thing that _sound_ “should” mean. Language is a tool; definitions don’t come from some Platonic realm; they exist because they’re useful and carve up reality into convenient categories. Unless someone is abusing definitions really egregiously, if you find yourself thinking too hard about meanings, it’s probably more interesting to abort and go back to whatever more productive stuff you were thinking about before. Tabooing the relevant word is a good way to do this.

## Sneaking in connotations

Sneaking in connotations is what causes some definitional arguments.

My favorite use case is during debates about “what is art?” On the surface, such arguments seem like they’re about what what the definition of art should be. But again, there’s nowhere for the “should” to come from except for what’s useful to us, in terms of delineating categories. But given how muddy are the various definitions of art, it doesn’t seem like any definition is going to actually help you communicate. So why argue?

But words like “art” are special, because anything that gets called “art” gets an instant halo of goodness and worthiness around it. People keep arguing about “whether video games can be art” because some people hear “video games” and think five-year-olds playing Mortal Kombat, and they don’t think that that deserves the same halo as something Rembrandt slaved over for months.

Other words subject to this kind of argument include “love”, “happiness”, “good”, “evil”, “right”, “wrong”, etc. Lots of them, really.

## Steelmanning

Steelmanning is the opposite of strawmanning. It’s what you do to your “opponent” if you’re _actually_ having a truth-seeking argument, rather than just pretending to.

Paul Graham wrote an essay [proposing](http://www.paulgraham.com/disagree.html) seven types of argumentation, of increasing quality, ranging from “name-calling” (DH0) to “refuting the central point” (DH6). Steven of Black Belt Bayesian added [an eighth level](http://www.acceleratingfuture.com/steven/?p=155):

> When an argument is made, you learn about that argument. But often you also learn about arguments that could have been made, but weren’t. Sometimes those arguments work where the original argument doesn’t.
> 
> If you’re interested in being on the right side of disputes, you will refute your opponents’ arguments. But if you’re interested in producing truth, you will fix your opponents’ arguments for them.
> 
> To win, you must fight not only the creature you encounter; you must fight the most horrible thing that can be constructed from its corpse.

As the name suggests, to steelman an argument is to make the strongest possible version of that argument before you refute it.

## The least convenient possible world

People who are given the doctor parable tend to try and weasel their way out of it. From the [eponymous LessWrong post](http://lesswrong.com/lw/2k/the_least_convenient_possible_world/):

> > You are a doctor in a small rural hospital. You have ten patients, each of whom is dying for the lack of a separate organ…. A traveller walks into the hospital…. You realize that by killing this traveller and distributing his organs among your patients, you could save ten lives. Would this be moral or not?
> 
> I don’t want to discuss the answer to this problem today. I want to discuss the answer one of my friends gave, because I think it illuminates a very interesting kind of defense mechanism that rationalists need to be watching for. My friend said:
> 
> > It wouldn’t be moral. After all, people often reject organs from random donors. The traveller would probably be a genetic mismatch for your patients, and the transplantees would have to spend the rest of their lives on immunosuppressants, only to die within a few years when the drugs failed.
> 
> …\[H\]e completely missed the point and lost a valuable effort to examine the nature of morality.
> 
> So I asked him, “In the least convenient possible world, the one where everyone was genetically compatible with everyone else and this objection was invalid, what would you do?”

Fighting the hypothetical is a natural reaction when you’re afraid you might have to make a judgment that won’t please everyone. But usually, refusing to judge is siding with one side by default; and delaying the decision by avoiding thinking about it doesn’t help. And anyway, the point of asking this hypothetical question isn’t to figure out what to do in real life. It’s to probe the [corner cases](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_case) of ethics in order to figure out how one’s morals actually work. So fighting they hypothetical isn’t just useless, but counterproductive and [logically rude](http://legacy.earlham.edu/~peters/writing/rudeness.htm). To forestall it, postscript your hypotheticals with “in the least convenient possible world.”

## Breaking intuition

Intuition-breaking thought experiments are more or less the flip side of fighting the hypothetical.

Simplified thought experiments are one of the primary tools of moral debate. The idea is that if your ethics gives a repugnant answer in certain hypothetical scenarios, it’s not capturing some important aspect of morality and therefore needs refinement. But sometimes, if the structure of the experiment breaks our intuitions, a repugnant-seeming answer shouldn’t be held against it.

For instance, many people use the doctor question above as a counter-argument against utilitarianism: the utilitarian answer (in the least convenient possible world, you morally should kill the healthy traveller to save the ten transplantees) is so counterintuitive as to be repugnant, so utilitarianism is unacceptable.

But our moral intuitions aren’t structured to deal with simple, clear-cut scenarios. Selective pressures on these intuitions act in the real world, where nothing is simple and clear-cut, and surprising or unusual things are likely to go wrong. So our intuitions tend to add uncertainty even when logically we’ve been told that we’re completely sure in the hypothetical, and favor solutions that hold up well under uncertainty.

In the least convenient possible world of the doctor parable, you’re 100% sure that harvesting the healthy traveller’s organs will save all 10 of the people with no ill effects. But a mere human will almost never have such certainty, and so our intuitions _shouldn’t be expected to give the “right” answer_ in that situation.

## Reason to suspect vs. reason to believe

Argument from authority (“Einstein believes that toves are slithy, therefore toves are slithy”) is widely regarded as a fallacy. If Einstein believed toves are slithy, then tell me the process that got him to believe it, not just the results!

Yet when Andrew Wiles tells me[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/debate/#fn:1) `$$\forall n \in \mathbb{N}: n > 2 \implies \not\exists a, b, c \in \mathbb{N} : a^n + b^n = c^n$$` ([Fermat’s Last Theorem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_Last_Theorem)), I believe him, even if I don’t understand his proof. Why is that? Isn’t this just an argument from authority?

Well, it turns out that experts are quite often right about things, so “experts say toves are slithy” is usually [evidence](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference) that toves are slithy. When people say that appeal to authority is a fallacious, what they mean really mean is that it’s _strictly worse evidence_ than giving a convincing argument: If Einstein’s beliefs and the convincing arguments point in different directions, it’s much more likely that Einstein has fallen prey to some bias than that he’s right by accident. If you want to avoid semantic confusion when talking about experts’ beliefs, you can say that Einstein’s opinion gives you _reason to suspect_ that toves are slithy; a convincing argument gives you _reason to believe_ it.

Nerdy aside: this can be made very precise using [belief networks](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_network). Let `$E_T$` be “Einstein believes toves are slithy”; `$A_T$` be “there exists a good argument that toves are slithy”; and `$T$` be “toves are slithy”. Then the causal diagram goes `$T \to A_T \to E_T$` (plus other factors like “Einstein wants toves to be slithy” pointing to `$E_T$`). So if we observe `$E_T$`, it might be confounded by other factors, but if we observe `$A_T$`, we’re much more certain about `$T$`: in fact, that observation [screens off](http://lesswrong.com/lw/lx/argument_screens_off_authority/) `$E_T$` completely.

The thing to note is that it’s often much costlier to observe whether there’s a good argument than to observe experts’ opinions—for example, to observe whether Wiles’s argument was good I’d need a PhD in math. So just calling argument from authority a “fallacy” doesn’t capture the subtlety involved. It’s weaker evidence, but sometimes still useful.
