# 为什么硬件开发很难｜Why hardware development is hard

---

## 正文

在 CPU design 中，大多数 successful teams 都有相当长的 lineage，并且高度依赖 experienced engineers。当我们考察 CPU startups 时，拥有 successful exist 的 teams 往往有一个已经共事数十年的 core team。例如，PA Semi 被 Apple acquisition 是一次 moderately successful exit，但那个 team 是从哪里来的呢？他们原本是 SiByte team，在 SiByte 被 Broadcom acquired 后离开，而 SiByte 由许多来自 DEC、已经共事十多年的人员组成。我以前的 company 也类似：一位 IBM fellow 召集了[他在 IBM 共事过的最优秀的人](http://conservancy.umn.edu/handle/120173)，此人很早就是 Dell 的 employee，后来成为 exec（那时 Dell 还在做有意思的 design work），随后独立出来，create 一家 chip startup。相当多的 CPU startups raised 了数千万至数亿资金，并且高度依赖 inexperienced labor；也就是刚毕业的 PhDs，以及只有几年 experience 的 hardware engineers。我所知道的每一家这样的 startup 都 failed[1](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/#fn:2)。

这与 software startups 形成了鲜明对比：由刚离开学校（或者中途辍学）的人 founded 的 successful startups 很常见。microprocessors 为什么就应该不同呢？一个新组建的年轻 team 能够成功 making 一个 high-performance microprocessor，这样的事闻所未闻，尽管这并没有阻止人们 funding 这些尝试。

在 software 领域，经常能听到对 experience 的轻视，例如 Zuckerberg 的评论：“我想强调年轻且 technical 的重要性，年轻人就是更聪明。”即使人们没有明确贬低 experience，他们往往也并不重视它。在写作本文时，Joel Spolsky 的《[聪明而且能把事情做好](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/GuerrillaInterviewing3.html)》大概是对 software hiring 最有影响的一篇文章。请注意，它说的不是“聪明、experienced，而且能把事情做好”。似乎只要“聪明而且能把事情做好”就足够了，不需要 experience。如果你更倾向于 Paul Graham 阵营而非 Joel Spolsky 阵营，你的 hire 方式会有许多不同，但 Paul's 建议在这一点上是一样的：[experience 并不位列他最重要的 criteria 之中](http://www.paulgraham.com/founders.html)，[除非是拿来贬损别人](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1356462960172883969)。

假设你想 hire 一位 plumber 或 carptener，你会怎么选？“聪明而且能把事情做好”，还是“experienced 而且 effective”？在 Ceteris paribus 的情况下，我会选择“experienced 而且 effective”，如果情况紧急，就更是如此。

Physical work 不是那种无论你多聪明，都能 derive from first principles 的事情。想想 WWII 后的 South Korea。[它的 GDP per capita lower than Ghana、Kenya，而且只是 barely above the Congo](http://www.nationmaster.com/graph/eco_gdp_per_cap_in_195-economy-gdp-per-capita-1950)。由于种种原因，新的 regime 不必应付 legacy institutions；而且他们希望 Korea 成为 first-world nation。

[我听到的](http://delong.typepad.com/aeh/)说法是，government 最初从 subsidizing concrete 开始。在 making concrete 多年之后，他们想沿着 chain 向上发展，开始更 complex 的 manufacturing。他们最终开始 building ships，因为 shipping 是他们想要 create 的 export economy 中的一个 critical part。

他们找来了一些最优秀的 business people，这些人已经在其他 manufacturing 中学到了 management 和 operations 等 skills。这些人知道，自己没有亲自 build ships 所需的 expertise，因此将其 contracted it out。他们选择与 Scottish firms 合作，因为 Scotland 有悠久的 shipbuilding 历史。这听起来很合理，对吧？

结果却没能成功。由于 historical 和 geographic 原因，Scotland 的 shipyards 并非 full-sized；他们把 ships 分成 two halves 来 built，然后再 assembled。这种做法对他们而言很顺利，因为他们从 1800s 起就在 at scale 地这样做，到 1900s 时已经拥有了 [world renowned expertise](http://www.educationscotland.gov.uk/scotlandshistory/makingindustrialurban/shipbuilding/index.asp)。但当缺乏实践的 Koreans 试图使用 Scottish plans 和详细的 step-by-step directions 来 build ships 时，得到的却是无法完全 fit together 的 two ship halves，在 assembled 后便 sunk 了。

Koreans 最终通过 hiring foreign companies 前来本地 build ships、向人们展示具体做法，成功建立了 shipbuilding industry。而且，即便有人可能认为，所有 requisite knowledge 都已经存在于书中，在 university courses 中传授，也能支付少量费用从 experts 那里获得，他们仍然花了数十年，才让我们看来属于 basic manufacturing 的事情 working smoothly。如今，他们的 manufacturing industries 已达到 world class，例如，据 Consumer Reports 所述，Hyundai 和 Kia produce reliable cars。从 producing unreliable econoboxes 到生产你可以买的 reliable cars，花了十多年，就像 Toyota 在数十年前完成同样转变时一样。如果除了 hiring 许多以前做过这件事的人之外，还存在通向 quality 的捷径，那么至今还没有人发现它。

如今，任何 programmer 都可以学习 Geoffrey Hinton 的[关于 neural networks 和 deep learning 的 course](https://www.coursera.org/course/neuralnets)，并开始 applying state of the art machine learning techniques。在 software 领域，你可以 in real time 地 fix minor bugs。如果 run 你的 regression test suite 需要整整一天，你会觉得自己很幸运，因为这意味着你处于少数认真对待 testing 的 environments 之一。如果 architecture 是 fundamentally flawed，你就拿出自己那本 Feathers 的《[Working Effectively with Legacy Code（有效处理遗留代码）](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B005OYHF0A/ref=as_li_tf_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=B005OYHF0A&linkCode=as2&tag=abroaview-20)》，然后 repeatedly apply fixes。

这并不是说 software 不难，而是有许多 valueable problems，并不需要十年艰难积累的 experience 才能去 attack。但如果你想 build a ship，而你“只有”十年的 carpentry、milling、metalworking 等方面的 experience，那么，祝你好运。你会需要这份好运的。对于一艘 large ship，“minor” fixes 可能需要数天乃至数周，而一个 fundamental flaw 就意味着你的 ship sinks，你会损失半年的工作和数千万美元。等到你面对具有 modern high-performance microprocessor 那种 complexity 的东西时，在 production 中 discovered 的一个 minor bug 就要花费三个月和数百万美元。architecture 中的一个 fundamental flaw 则会让你付出五年时间和数亿美元[2](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/#fn:1)。

Physical mistakes 的代价很高。这里没有 undo，editing 也不仅仅是按几个键的事；changes 会 consume 真正的 physical resources。你需要足够的 wisdom 和 experience，才能 entirely avoid common mistakes——尤其是那些无法 fixed 的错误。

回头来看，我觉得自己在这篇文章中对 software 过于乐观了。如果我们谈的是 product-market fit 和 success，我不认为文中的态度有错，而且经验很少乃至毫无经验的人确实经常能创造出热门产品。但如今我已经在这个行业待了一段时间，也和许多人聊过各种 startups 以及大公司的 infra，我认为，creating high quality software infra 所需的经验，并不比 creating high quality physical items 少。那些认定事实并非如此、从顶尖学校 hire 一群聪明人来 build 自己的 infra 的公司，最终得到的却是 low quality、unreliable、expensive 而且 difficult to operate 的 infrastructure。事实只不过是，如果你的 product-market fit 非常好，就不需要你的 infra 能 work。你的公司可以生存，甚至可以[在 infra 的 uptime 只有 2 9s、costs 比竞争对手的 infra 高出一个 order of magnitude 的情况下 thrive](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/)，或者[在你的 product 的 architecture 意味着它根本不可能 work correctly 的情况下也能如此](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/)。你赚的钱会比原本能赚到的少，但 high order bits 全都在 product 这一边。如果把这种情况与那些拥有 inexperienced engineers、却没能 produce 出 working product 的 chip companies 对比，那么，即使你努力尝试，也确实没法 sell 一个不能 work 的 product。如果你非常幸运，比如恰好在合适的时机创办了一家 deep learning chip company，你或许能让一家大公司 acquire 你的 non-working product。但对于 microprocessor 来说，要获得这样的 exit 难得多。

## 原文

In CPU design, most successful teams have a fairly long lineage and rely heavily on experienced engineers. When we look at CPU startups, teams that have a successful exist often have a core team that's been together for decades. For example, PA Semi's acquisition by Apple was a moderately successful exit, but where did that team come from? They were the SiByte team, which left after SiByte was acquired by Broadcom, and SiByte was composed of many people from DEC who had been working together for over a decade. My old company was similar: an IBM fellow collected [the best people he worked with at IBM](http://conservancy.umn.edu/handle/120173) who was a very early Dell employee and then exec (back when Dell still did interesting design work), then split off to create a chip startup. There have been quite a few CPU startups that have raised tens to hundreds of millions and leaned heavily on inexperienced labor; fresh PhDs and hardware engineers with only a few years of experience. Every single such startup I know of failed[1](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/#fn:2).

This is in stark contrast to software startups, where it's common to see successful startups founded by people who are just out of school (or who dropped out of school). Why should microprocessors be any different? It's unheard of for a new, young, team to succeed at making a high-performance microprocessor, although this hasn't stopped people from funding these efforts.

In software, it's common to hear about disdain for experience, such as Zuckerberg's comment, "I want to stress the importance of being young and technical, Young people are just smarter.". Even when people don't explicitly devalue experience, they often don't value it either. As of this writing, Joel Spolsky's ”[Smart and gets things done](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/GuerrillaInterviewing3.html)” is probably the most influential piece of writing on software hiring. Note that it doesn't say "smart, experienced, and gets things done.". Just "smart and gets things done" appears to be enough, no experience required. If you lean more towards the Paul Graham camp than the Joel Spolsky camp, there will be a lot of differences in how you hire, but Paul's advice is the same in that [experience doesn't rank as one of his most important criteria](http://www.paulgraham.com/founders.html), [except as a diss](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1356462960172883969).

Let's say you wanted to hire a plumber or a carptener, what would you choose? "Smart and gets things done" or "experienced and effective"? Ceteris paribus, I'll go for "experienced and effective", doubly so if it's an emergency.

Physical work isn't the kind of thing you can derive from first principles, no matter how smart you are. Consider South Korea after WWII. [Its GDP per capita was lower than Ghana, Kenya, and just barely above the Congo](http://www.nationmaster.com/graph/eco_gdp_per_cap_in_195-economy-gdp-per-capita-1950). For various reasons, the new regime didn't have to deal with legacy institutions; and they wanted Korea to become a first-world nation.

The story [I've heard](http://delong.typepad.com/aeh/) is that the government started by subsidizing concrete. After many years making concrete, they wanted to move up the chain and start more complex manufacturing. They eventually got to building ships, because shipping was a critical part of the export economy they wanted to create.

They pulled some of their best business people who had learned skills like management and operations in other manufacturing. Those people knew they didn't have the expertise to build ships themselves, so they contracted it out. They made the choice to work with Scottish firms, because Scotland has a long history of shipbuilding. Makes sense, right?

It didn't work. For historical and geographic reasons, Scotland's shipyards weren't full-sized; they built their ships in two halves and then assembled them. Worked fine for them, because they'd be doing it at scale since the 1800s, and had [world renowned expertise](http://www.educationscotland.gov.uk/scotlandshistory/makingindustrialurban/shipbuilding/index.asp) by the 1900s. But when the unpracticed Koreans tried to build ships using Scottish plans and detailed step-by-step directions, the result was two ship halves that didn't quite fit together and sunk when assembled.

The Koreans eventually managed to start a shipbuilding industry by hiring foreign companies to come and build ships locally, showing people how it's done. And it took decades to get what we would consider basic manufacturing working smoothly, even though one might think that all of the requisite knowledge existed in books, was taught in university courses, and could be had from experts for a small fee. Now, their manufacturing industries are world class, e.g., according to Consumer Reports, Hyundai and Kia produce reliable cars. Going from producing unreliable econoboxes to reliable cars you can buy took over a decade, like it did for Toyota when they did it decades earlier. If there's a shortcut to quality other than hiring a lot of people who've done it before, no one's discovered it yet.

Today, any programmer can take Geoffrey Hinton's [course on neural networks and deep learning](https://www.coursera.org/course/neuralnets), and start applying state of the art machine learning techniques. In software land, you can fix minor bugs in real time. If it takes a whole day to run your regression test suite, you consider yourself lucky because it means you're in one of the few environments that takes testing seriously. If the architecture is fundamentally flawed, you pull out your copy of Feathers' “[Working Effectively with Legacy Code](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B005OYHF0A/ref=as_li_tf_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=B005OYHF0A&linkCode=as2&tag=abroaview-20)” and repeatedly apply fixes.

This isn't to say that software isn't hard, but there are a lot of valueable problems that don't need a decade of hard-won experience to attack. But if you want to build a ship, and you "only" have a decade of experience with carpentry, milling, metalworking, etc., well, good luck. You're going to need it. With a large ship, “minor” fixes can take days or weeks, and a fundamental flaw means that your ship sinks and you've lost half a year of work and tens of millions of dollars. By the time you get to something with the complexity of a modern high-performance microprocessor, a minor bug discovered in production costs three months and millions of dollars. A fundamental flaw in the architecture will cost you five years and hundreds of millions of dollars[2](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/#fn:1).

Physical mistakes are costly. There's no undo and editing isn't simply a matter of pressing some keys; changes consume real, physical resources. You need enough wisdom and experience to avoid common mistakes entirely – especially the ones that can't be fixed.

In retrospect, I think that I was too optimistic about software in this post. If we're talking about product-market fit and success, I don't think the attitude in the post is wrong and people with little to no experience often do create hits. But now that I've been in the industry for a while and talked to numerous people about infra at various startups as well as large companies, I think creating high quality software infra requires no less experience than creating high quality physical items. Companies that decided this wasn't the case and hire a bunch of smart folks from top schools to build their infra have ended up with low quality, unreliable, expensive, and difficult to operate infrastructure. It just turns out that, if you have very good product-market fit, you don't need your infra to work. Your company can survive and even [thrive while having infra that has 2 9s of uptime and costs an order of magnitude more than your competitor's infra](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/) or [if your product's architecture means that it can't possibly work correctly](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/). You'll make less money than you would've otherwise, but the high order bits are all on the product side. If you contrast that chip companies with inexperienced engineers that didn't produce a working product, well, you can't really sell a product that doesn't work even if you try. If you get very lucky, like if you happened to start deep learning chip company at the right time, you might get big company to acquire your non-working product. But, it's much harder to get an exit like that for a microprocessor.
