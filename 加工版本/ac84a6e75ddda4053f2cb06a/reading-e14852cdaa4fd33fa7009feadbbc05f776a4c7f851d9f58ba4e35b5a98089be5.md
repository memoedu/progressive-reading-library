# 市场、歧视与“降低标准”｜Markets, discrimination, and "lowering the bar"

---

## 正文

关于 tech 中 discrimination 的公共讨论，常常会以有人声称由于 market forces，discrimination 不可能存在而告终。下面这段 Marc Andreessen 的引语概括了一种常见观点[1](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:1)。

> 我们就直入主题吧。我认为，指责 Silicon Valley companies 是 deliberately, systematically discriminatory 并不正确，而且有两个理由可以相信事实确实如此。……第 2 点，我们的 companies 极度渴求 talent。极度渴求。我们的 companies 对 talent 求之若渴。它们就像躺在海滩上喘不过气来，因为无法为这些 jobs 招到足够的 talented people。无论 talent 在哪里，去找到他们的 motivation 都高得令人难以置信。

Marc Andreessen 的意思是，market 太过 competitive，以至于 discrimination 不可能存在。但 VC funded startups 并不是世界上最先面对 competitive hiring market 的 companies。不妨看看，比如说，1958 至 1987 年间 PhD economists 的 market。Alan Greenspan 曾这样描述他的 firm，Townsend-Greenspan，眼中的那个 market。

> Townsend-Greenspan 作为一家 economics firm，特别之处在于 men 为 women 工作（我们总共约有二十五名 employees）。我 hiring women economists，并不是出于 women's liberation。这只是很符合 business sense。我对 men 和 women 同等 valued，并且发现，由于其他 employers 并非如此，优秀的 women economists 比 men 更 less expensive。Hiring women . . . 让 Townsend-Greenspan 用同样的 money 获得了 higher-quality work . . .

competition 不仅没有终结 discrimination，而且 discrimination 的程度还足以让不 discriminating 这一行为为 Townsend-Greenspan 带来 significant competitive advantage。而这发生在以 cutthroat 著称的 finance。并且还不是 finance 中随便哪个部分，而是 PhD economists hiring 其他 PhD economists 的领域。在这些 industries 中，负责 hiring 的人最有可能同时熟悉 theoretical models 和 empirical research；这些研究表明，discrimination 会通过 suppressing 某些 groups 的 wages 来 opens up market opportunities。但即便如此，在 1958 年 Greenspan 接管 Townsend-Greenspan 时，也不足以让 men 和 women 的 wages 得以 equalize；直到 1987 年 Greenspan 离开、出任 Fed 的 chairman 时，仍然不足以做到这一点。discrimination 就是这样。当它是某种根深蒂固的 belief 的一部分时，人们很难察觉自己正在 discriminating。

然而，在关于 tech hiring 的讨论中，人们常常声称，既然 markets 和 hiring 是 perfectly competitive 或 efficient，companies 就必定已经在 hiring 出现在它们面前的 best people。由此得到的一个 corollary 是，anti-discrimination 或 diversity oriented policies 必然意味着 "lowering the bar，因为这些 policies 意味着 diverging from 现有的 optimal hiring practices。反过来，即便讨论中没有涉及 "market forces"，声称 increasing hiring diversity 必然意味着 "lowering the bar"，也依赖于对 hiring 中某种 optimality 的 assumption。我认为，只要审视一下 tech hiring practices，就很清楚：[practices 是](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/) [far from optimal](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/)。但我不打算根据 practices 来回应这一说法（链接中的文章已经这样做了），而是想看看 market forces 使 discrimination 不可能存在这一 meta-claim。人们会对 market efficiency 和 economics 作出含糊的 claims，例如[这位颇有影响力的 serial founder，他在关于 hiring 的发言结尾说道：“Capitalism 是真实的，markets 是 efficient。”](https://twitter.com/rogerdickey/status/1268327765905772546)[2](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:A)。人们似乎很喜欢含糊地援引“market”或“economists”。

但如果我们真正去读 economists 对 hiring markets 如何运作的论述，就会发现，他们一般并不声称 markets 是 perfectly efficient，也不声称在那些日常可能被称为 highly competitive 的 markets 中不会发生 discrimination。既然我们谈的是 discrimination，那么不妨从 [Becker 关于 discrimination 的开创性著作](http://web.archive.org/web/20150401024833/http://economics.mit.edu/files/553)开始。Becker 说的是，markets 会对 discrimination impose a cost，而且在某些 market conditions 下，Becker 所说的 "taste-based"[3](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:T) discrimination 的 occuring on average，并不意味着存在 discrimination at the margin。这是一个相当 specific 的 statement；如果你阅读 discrimination 相关 literature 中的其他 papers，也会看到类似 specific 的 statements。你不会看到的，是 tech 讨论中那些含糊的 claims，声称 "market forces" 或 "competition" 与 discrimination 或 non-optimal hiring 是 incompatible。坦率地说，我从未与那些说着“Capitalism 是真实的，markets 是 efficient”之类话的人讨论时，发现他们对 Becker 在 economics of discrimination 领域的开创性著作有哪怕粗浅的了解；甚至对这个主题的其他任何主要著作，也同样如此。

在更广泛的 tech community 的讨论中，我从未见过有人论证 tech industry（或任何 industry）满足这样的 conditions：在这些 conditions 下，taste-based discrimination on average 并不 imply marginal taste-based discrimination。我也从未见过有人论证，我们只有 taste-based discrimination，或者我们也满足不存在其他 forms of discrimination 的 conditions。当人们针对 hiring 或 tech 的其他部分援引 "efficient markets" 时，通常只是含糊其辞，听起来像是在 appeal to authority；但这个 authority，不过是某些人可能会称之为一个 teenage libertarian 对 markets 会如何运作的想象。

既然人们往往不觉得 Becker 著作中那类 abstract reasoning 有说服力，我们就来看看几个 concrete examples。你可以在许多 fields 中看到 discrimination。一个问题是，很难将 discrimination 的 effect 与 confounding variables separate out，因为很难获得关于 employee performance v. compensation 随时间变化的良好 data。幸运的是，有一类 fields 能提供这种 data：sports。在进入这些 examples 之前，值得指出的是，从方向上来说，我们应该预期 sports 中的 discrimination 比 tech 中少得多。不仅有好得多的 employee performance data，而且根据过去的 performance 来 predict 未来的 employee performance 更容易；employee performance 对“company” performance 的 impact 更大，也更容易 quantify；market 也更 competitive。相对于 tech，这些 forces 既 increase discrimination 的 cost，也让这个 cost 更 visible。

在 baseball 中，[Gwartney and Haworth (1974)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v82y1974i4p873-81.html) 发现，在 de-segregation 后的十年里，对 non-white players 较少 discriminated 的 teams，其 performed 更好。对之后几十年的 Studies 使用了“classical” productivity metrics，大多发现 salaries 会 equalize。然而，[Swartz (2014)](http://www.hardballtimes.com/searching-for-racial-earnings-differentials-in-major-league-baseball/) 使用更新且更 accurate 的 productivity metrics 后，发现相对于自身的 productivity level，Latino players 是 significantly underpaid。Compensation 并不是 discriminate 的唯一方式——[Jibou (1988)](http://sf.oxfordjournals.org/content/67/2/524.short) 发现，在 controlling for age 和 performance 后，black players 离开 baseball 的 exit rates 更 higher。任何思考过 [tech fields 中 exit rates](http://leanangry.tumblr.com/post/125716699460/your-pipeline-argument-is-bullshit) 的人，对此应该都不会感到陌生。

market 的这种缓慢 effect 并不局限于 baseball；在其他 sports 中，情况实际上似乎更糟。[Kahn (1991)](http://www.jstor.org/stable/2524152) 的一篇 review article 指出，在 basketball 中，最新的 studies（截至该 review 的日期）发现，black players 存在 11%-25% 的 salary penalty，以及更 higher 的 exit rate。Kahn 还提到多项 studies，显示 hockey 中存在针对 French-Canadians 的 discrimination；人们认为，这源于认为 French-Canadian men 比其他 men 更不 masculine 的 stereotypes[4](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:E)。

在 tech 中，有些人担心 increasing diversity 会 "lower the bar"；但在 hiring market 比 tech 更 competitive 的 sports 中，我们看到的恰恰相反：increasing diversity 带来的是 raised the level，而不是 lowering it，因为这意味着根据人们的 qualifications 而不是外貌来 hiring。有人说，tech companies 不 hiring qualified minorities，从而放着 money 不赚，这很荒谬；我并不反对这一说法。但这正是我们在所考察的 sports 中看到的情况，而在那里，由于 quantifying performance 相对容易，这种情况甚至更加荒谬。然而，几十年来，teams 通过 favoring white players（在 hockey 中，则是 non-French Canadian players），放弃了巨额 money；而这些 players 说白了，就是比他们的 peers 更 less qualified。[世界是个荒谬的地方](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1270181411534761984)。

在那些有足够 data 可以判断是否可能存在 discrimination 的 fields 中，我们常常会发现 discrimination。即使在现有最 competitive 的 fields 之中也是如此，例如 major professional sports。关于 discrimination 的 Studies 并不局限于 empirical studies 和 data mining。已有 experiments 显示，从最初的 resume screening，到 phone screening、job offers、salary negotiation，再到 workplace promotions，每个 level 都存在 discrimination。而这些 studies 大多来自某种程度上接近 gender parity 的 fields。在 discrimination 足够 weak、使 entrance rates 达到 gender parity 或 racial parity 的 fields 中，我们能看到，过去两代人间 discrimination 的 levels 一直在 steadily decreasing。Discrimination 尚未被 eliminated，但已经大幅 reduced。

![按 gender 展示 med school、law school、sciences 和 CS 的 enrollment 的 Graph。Graph 由 NPR 提供。](https://danluu.com/images/tech-discrimination/cs_enrollment.webp)

然后，我们还有 computer science。其 entrance rates 的 disparity，大致相当于 medicine、law 和 physical sciences 在 70s 时的情况。恰好，为这种 gender disparity 找的借口，与 70s 被反复拿出来为 women 不愿进入、或无法胜任 medicine、economics、finance 和 biology 等 technical fields 辩解的借口，完全相同。

一种常见的 argument 是，women 天生对 "harder" sciences 的兴趣更少，因此你会预期，进入 biology 或 medicine 的 women 比进入 programming 的更多。我觉得这条 reasoning 不具说服力，主要有两个原因。首先，按 proportionally 而言，进入 math 和 chemical engineering 等 fields 的 women 比进入 programming 的更多。我认为，根据 math 和 sciences 有多么 "hard science" 来对其 rank 毫无意义；但如果你请人们 rank 这些东西，大多数人会把 math 排在 programming 之上，而如果他们知道 chemical engineering degree 涉及什么，我想他们也会把 chemical engineering 排在 programming 之上。然而，这些 fields 中 women 的比例却比 programming 中更高。其次，如果你看看其他国家，会发现 study computer science 的人所占的 proportions 差异极大，而其原因似乎主要是 cultural。既然我们确实看到了所有这些 variation，我看不出有什么理由认为，U.S. 反映的是 women 想要 study programming 的 "true" rate，而在那些有更多 women（按 proportionally 而言）想要 study programming 的国家，其 rates 却是被 cultural biases 扭曲、偏离了 "true" rate 的结果。

先把 theoretical arguments 放在一边，我想知道，为什么我的 lived experience 会与 Andreessen 如此不同。他的 reasoning 在他自己看来一定很合理，而 women 和 minorities 讲述的 discrimination 经历，在他听来一定不像是真的。但对我来说，恰恰相反。

就在前几天，我和朋友 John 聊天（这个名字和其他所有名字都是随机选取的，以保持匿名），他是一位扎实的 programmer。他花了两年才找到工作；在如今的 job market 中，对于我这个年纪的人来说，这令人震惊，但令人难过的是，对于像他这样年龄是我两倍的人来说，这却很正常。

你可能会想，John 除了年龄之外，是不是还有别的问题，但当我和一位 Google 同事对他进行 mock interviewed 时，他表现得很好。我在 Google 接受过 standard interview training，也曾为 Google 进行 interviewed，而把他与那个 bar 相比，我会说，他能否在 Google getting hired，基本上就是抛硬币。状态好的一天能过；状态不好的一天就过不了。而他在 Google interviewed 时，虽然没有拿到 offer，却 passed 了 phone screen，而且在 on-site 之后，他们强烈建议他一年后再次 apply，这是个好迹象。但大多数地方甚至不愿意和 John 谈一谈。

即使在 Google——这家公司大力宣扬要从自己的 processes 中 removing bias——这些 processes 也经常做不到这一点。我把 Mary referred 给 Google 时，她在 recruiter phone screen 中因为不够 technical 而被 rejected；我还看到 William 因为一个 medical problem，面对一位 manager 越来越强烈的怒火，最终导致他 quit。

当然，在 online discussions 中，人们会质疑 Mary 这样的人所具有的 technical competency。可是，Mary 是我在任何 field 中见过的最令人钦佩的 engineers 之一。人们这么说时，意思各不相同，所以让我给出一个 frame of reference：对我而言，同属这一类的其他人包括一位 [IBM Fellow](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow)、那位 IBM Fellow 称为 IBM 最好的 engineer 的人、一位如今在 CMU 担任 professor 的 Math Olympiad medalist、一位 Sun 的 distinguished engineer，以及另外几位类似的人。

总之，Mary 和一位 Google recruiter 通了电话。这位 recruiter 说了些话，提到 Mary 的 degree 是 math 而不是 CS，可能不够 technical，还质疑 Mary 的 programming experience：那是“algorithms”，还是“只是 coding”？从那里开始，事情就每况愈下。

Google 有许多没有 CS degree 的 engineers，有些人的 degrees 是 history、music 和 arts，还有很多 engineers 根本没有任何 degree，甚至连 high school diploma 都没有。但不知怎么，一份 math degree，加上我在 internal referral 中提到她是我见过的最好的 engineers 之一，最终却得出了 Mary 不够 technical 的决定。

你可能会说，这和 John 的 example 一样，只是某种偶然。也许吧。但据我所见，如果 Mary 是 man 而不是 woman，这种偶然发生的 odds 就会更低。

这种 dynamic 不仅仅 limited to hiring。每次阅读 Anna 某篇 blog posts 下的 comments 时，我都会注意到它。经常会有人质疑 Anna 的 technical chops。甚至不是他们在当下这篇文章里找到了一个“嗯，其实”（虽然这种事有时也会发生）；通常，他们会翻出六个月前的某篇文章，并声称那篇文章不够 technical。

我并不比 Anna 更 technical，但这种事真的从来没有发生在我身上。我见过它发生在 men 身上，但只有那些知名度极高的人（位居最知名 tech bloggers 的前 N 名之列，比如 Steve Yegge 或 Jeff Atwood），或者那些正在推动某种经常被居高临下地对待的主张的人（比如 dynamic languages）。但对于像 Anna 这样有一定知名度的 female bloggers，这种事却经常发生。

对 women 和 minorities 的 Differential treatment 不仅仅 limited to hiring 和 blogging。我已经记不清有多少次，一位 woman 随口告诉我，某个男人以为她是 recruiter、front-end dev、妻子、女朋友，或者 UX consultant。这样的事到处都在发生。在 conferences 上。在满是 devs 的聚会上。在工作中。无处不在。这种事不仅从未发生在我身上，相反的事情倒是经常发生——如果我和 physics 或 math grad students 待在一起，人们就会以为我也是一位 grad student。

当人们在这类讨论中提起 market 时，他们说得仿佛它是一种自然力量。它不是。它只是一个描述人们在某些 circumstances 下的 collective actions 的词。Mary 的处境并没有因为这是一个 free market 就自动得到解决。Mary 被 recruiter rejection 的结果之所以被撤销，是因为我向自己的 engineering director 投诉，他让我联系上一位 HR director，后者耐心听完事情经过，并 overturned 了那个 decision[5](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:B)。market 不过就是人。归根结底，全都是人。

只要我们不再假定 market 会替我们解决这个问题，我们就能解决它。

-   [人们真的会从自己阅读的 studies 中读出相反的 result](https://danluu.com/gender-gap/)
-   在今年的 [FSE](http://fse22.gatech.edu/) 上，一位演讲者指出，如果你给一群 programmers 看 random data，他们就会将那些 data interpret 为支持他们对 best practices 已有的 prior beliefs
-   [人们越是 biased，就越觉得自己 objective](https://ed.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/uhlmann_et_2005.pdf)

## 原文

Public discussions of discrimination in tech often result in someone claiming that discrimination is impossible because of market forces. Here's a quote from Marc Andreessen that sums up a common view[1](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:1).

> Let's launch right into it. I think the critique that Silicon Valley companies are deliberately, systematically discriminatory is incorrect, and there are two reasons to believe that that's the case. ... No. 2, our companies are desperate for talent. Desperate. Our companies are dying for talent. They're like lying on the beach gasping because they can't get enough talented people in for these jobs. The motivation to go find talent wherever it is unbelievably high.

Marc Andreessen's point is that the market is too competitive for discrimination to exist. But VC funded startups aren't the first companies in the world to face a competitive hiring market. Consider the market for PhD economists from, say, 1958 to 1987. Alan Greenspan had this to say about how that market looked to his firm, Townsend-Greenspan.

> Townsend-Greenspan was unusual for an economics firm in that the men worked for the women (we had about twenty-five employees in all). My hiring of women economists was not motivated by women's liberation. It just made great business sense. I valued men and women equally, and found that because other employers did not, good women economists were less expensive than men. Hiring women . . . gave Townsend-Greenspan higher-quality work for the same money . . .

Not only did competition not end discrimination, there was enough discrimination that the act of not discriminating provided a significant competitive advantage for Townsend-Greenspan. And this is in finance, which is known for being cutthroat. And not just any part of finance, but one where it's PhD economists hiring other PhD economists. This is one of the industries where the people doing the hiring are the most likely to be familiar with both the theoretical models and the empirical research showing that discrimination opens up market opportunities by suppressing wages of some groups. But even that wasn't enough to equalize wages between men and women when Greenspan took over Townsend-Greenspan in 1958 and it still wasn't enough when Greenspan left to become chairman of the Fed in 1987. That's the thing about discrimination. When it's part of a deep-seated belief, it's hard for people to tell that they're discriminating.

And yet, in discussions on tech hiring, people often claim that, since markets and hiring are perfectly competitive or efficient, companies must already be hiring the best people presented to them. A corollary of this is that anti-discrimination or diversity oriented policies necessarily mean "lowering the bar since these would mean diverging from existing optimal hiring practices. And conversely, even when "market forces" aren't involved in the discussion, claiming that increasing hiring diversity necessarily means "lowering the bar" relies on an assumption of a kind of optimality in hiring. I think that an examination of tech hiring practices makes it pretty clear that [practices are](https://danluu.com/programmer-moneyball/) [far from optimal](https://danluu.com/algorithms-interviews/), but rather than address this claim based on practices (which has been done in the linked posts), I'd look to look at the meta-claim that market forces make discrimination impossible. People make vauge claims about market efficiency and economics, like [this influential serial founder who concludes his remarks on hiring with "Capitalism is real and markets are efficient."](https://twitter.com/rogerdickey/status/1268327765905772546)[2](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:A). People seem to love handwave-y citations of "the market" or "economists".

But if we actually read what economists have to say on how hiring markets work, they do not, in general, claim that markets are perfectly efficient or that discrimination does not occur in markets that might colloquially be called highly competitive. Since we're talking about discrimination, a good place to start might be [Becker's seminal work on discrimination](http://web.archive.org/web/20150401024833/http://economics.mit.edu/files/553). What Becker says is that markets impose a cost on discrimination, and that under certain market conditions, what Becker calls "taste-based"[3](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:T) discrimination occuring on average doesn't mean there's discrimination at the margin. This is quite a specific statement and, if you read other papers in the literature on discrimination, they also make similarly specific statements. What you don't see is anything like the handwave-y claims in tech discussions, that "market forces" or "competition" is incompatible with discrimination or non-optimal hiring. Quite frankly, I've never had a discussion with someone who says things like "Capitalism is real and markets are efficient" where it appears that they have even a passing familiarity with Becker's seminal work in the field of the economics of discrimination or, for that matter, any other major work on the topic.

In discussions among the broader tech community, I have never seen anyone make a case that the tech industry (or any industry) meets the conditions under which taste-based discrimination on average doesn't imply marginal taste-based discrimination. Nor have I ever seen people make the case that we only have taste-based discrimination or that we also meet the conditions for not having other forms of discrimination. When people cite "efficient markets" with respect to hiring or other parts of tech, it's generally vague handwaving that sounds like an appeal to authority, but the authority is what someone might call a teenage libertarian's idea of how markets might behave.

Since people often don't find abstract reasoning of the kind you see in Becker's work convincing, let's look at a few concrete examples. You can see discrimination in a lot of fields. A problem is that it's hard to separate out the effect of discrimination from confounding variables because it's hard to get good data on employee performance v. compensation over time. Luckily, there's one set of fields where that data is available: sports. And before we go into the examples, it's worth noting that we should, directionally, expect much less discrimination in sports than in tech. Not only is there much better data available on employee performance, it's easier to predict future employee performance from past performance, the impact of employee performance on "company" performance is greater and easier quantify, and the market is more competitive. Relatively to tech, these forces both increase the cost of discrimination while making the cost more visible.

In baseball, [Gwartney and Haworth (1974)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v82y1974i4p873-81.html) found that teams that discriminated less against non-white players in the decade following de-segregation performed better. Studies of later decades using “classical” productivity metrics mostly found that salaries equalize. However, [Swartz (2014)](http://www.hardballtimes.com/searching-for-racial-earnings-differentials-in-major-league-baseball/), using newer and more accurate metrics for productivity, found that Latino players are significantly underpaid for their productivity level. Compensation isn't the only way to discriminate -- [Jibou (1988)](http://sf.oxfordjournals.org/content/67/2/524.short) found that black players had higher exit rates from baseball after controlling for age and performance. This should sound familiar to anyone who's wondered about [exit rates in tech fields](http://leanangry.tumblr.com/post/125716699460/your-pipeline-argument-is-bullshit).

This slow effect of the market isn't limited to baseball; it actually seems to be worse in other sports. A review article by [Kahn (1991)](http://www.jstor.org/stable/2524152) notes that in basketball, the most recent studies (up to the date of the review) found an 11%-25% salary penalty for black players as well as a higher exit rate. Kahn also noted multiple studies showing discrimination against French-Canadians in hockey, which is believed to be due to stereotypes about how French-Canadian men are less masculine than other men[4](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:E).

In tech, some people are concerned that increasing diversity will "lower the bar", but in sports, which has a more competitive hiring market than tech, we saw the opposite, increasing diversity raised the level instead of lowering it because it means hiring people on their qualifications instead of on what they look like. I don't disagree with people who say that it would be absurd for tech companies to leave money on the table by not hiring qualified minorities. But this is exactly what we saw in the sports we looked at, where that's even more absurd due to the relative ease of quantifying performance. And yet, for decades, teams left huge amounts of money on the table by favoring white players (and, in the case of hockey, non-French Canadian players) who were, quite simply, less qualified than their peers. [The world is an absurd place](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1270181411534761984).

In fields where there's enough data to see if there might be discrimination, we often find discrimination. Even in fields that are among the most competitive fields in existence, like major professional sports. Studies on discrimination aren't limited to empirical studies and data mining. There have been experiments showing discrimination at every level, from initial resume screening to phone screening to job offers to salary negotiation to workplace promotions. And those studies are mostly in fields where there's something resembling gender parity. In fields where discrimination is weak enough that there's gender parity or racial parity in entrance rates, we can see steadily decreasing levels of discrimination over the last two generations. Discrimination hasn't been eliminated, but it's much reduced.

![Graph of enrollment by gender in med school, law school, the sciences, and CS. Graph courtesy of NPR.](https://danluu.com/images/tech-discrimination/cs_enrollment.webp)

And then we have computer science. The disparity in entrance rates is about what it was for medicine, law, and the physical sciences in the 70s. As it happens, the excuses for the gender disparity are the exact same excuses that were trotted out in the 70s to justify why women didn't want to go into or couldn't handle technical fields like medicine, economics, finance, and biology.

One argument that's commonly made is that women are inherently less interested in the "harder" sciences, so you'd expect more women to go into biology or medicine than programming. There are two major reasons I don't find that line of reasoning to be convincing. First, proportionally more women go into fields like math and chemical engineering than go into programming. I think it's pointless to rank math and the sciences by how "hard science" they are, but if you ask people to rank these things, most people will put math above programming and if they know what's involved in a chemical engineering degree, I think they'll also put chemical engineering above programming and yet those fields have proportionally more women than programming. Second, if you look at other countries, they have wildly different proportions of people who study computer science for reasons that seem to mostly be cultural. Given that we do see all of this variation, I don't see any reason to think that the U.S. reflects the "true" rate that women want to study programming and that countries where (proportionally) many more women want to study programming have rates that are distorted from the "true" rate by cultural biases.

Putting aside theoretical arguments, I wonder how it is that I've had such a different lived experience than Andreessen. His reasoning must sound reasonable in his head and stories of discrimination from women and minorities must not ring true. But to me, it's just the opposite.

Just the other day, I was talking to John (this and all other names were chosen randomly in order to maintain anonymity), a friend of mine who's a solid programmer. It took him two years to find a job, which is shocking in today's job market for someone my age, but sadly normal for someone like him, who's twice my age.

You might wonder if it's something about John besides his age, but when a Google coworker and I mock interviewed him he did fine. I did the standard interview training at Google and I interviewed for Google, and when I compare him to that bar, I'd say that his getting hired at Google would pretty much be a coin flip. Yes on a good day; no on a bad day. And when he interviewed at Google, he didn't get an offer, but he passed the phone screen and after the on-site they strongly suggested that he apply again in a year, which is a good sign. But most places wouldn't even talk to John.

And even at Google, which makes a lot of hay about removing bias from their processes, the processes often fail to do so. When I referred Mary to Google, she got rejected in the recruiter phone screen as not being technical enough and I saw William face increasing levels of ire from a manager because of a medical problem, which eventually caused him to quit.

Of course, in online discussions, people will call into question the technical competency of people like Mary. Well, Mary is one of the most impressive engineers I've ever met in any field. People mean different things when they say that, so let me provide a frame of reference: the other folks who fall into that category for me include an [IBM Fellow](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow), the person that IBM Fellow called the best engineer at IBM, a Math Olympiad medalist who's now a professor at CMU, a distinguished engineer at Sun, and a few other similar folks.

So anyway, Mary gets on the phone with a Google recruiter. The recruiter makes some comments about how Mary has a degree in math and not CS, and might not be technical enough, and questions Mary's programming experience: was it “algorithms” or “just coding”? It goes downhill from there.

Google has plenty of engineers without a CS degree, people with degrees in history, music, and the arts, and lots of engineers without any degree at all, not even a high school diploma. But somehow a math degree plus my internal referral mentioning that this was one of the best engineers I've ever seen resulted in the decision that Mary wasn't technical enough.

You might say that, like the example with John, this is some kind of a fluke. Maybe. But from what I've seen, if Mary were a man and not a woman, the odds of a fluke would have been lower.

This dynamic isn't just limited to hiring. I notice it every time I read the comments on one of Anna's blog posts. As often as not, someone will question Anna's technical chops. It's not even that they find a "well, actually" in the current post (although that sometimes happens); it's usually that they dig up some post from six months ago which, according to them, wasn't technical enough.

I'm no more technical than Anna, but I have literally never had that happen to me. I've seen it happen to men, but only those who are extremely high profile (among the top N most well-known tech bloggers, like Steve Yegge or Jeff Atwood), or who are pushing an agenda that's often condescended to (like dynamic languages). But it regularly happens to moderately well-known female bloggers like Anna.

Differential treatment of women and minorities isn't limited to hiring and blogging. I've lost track of the number of times a woman has offhandedly mentioned to me that some guy assumed she was a recruiter, a front-end dev, a wife, a girlfriend, or a UX consultant. It happens everywhere. At conferences. At parties full of devs. At work. Everywhere. Not only has that never happened to me, the opposite regularly happens to me -- if I'm hanging out with physics or math grad students, people assume I'm a fellow grad student.

When people bring up the market in discussions like these, they make it sound like it's a force of nature. It's not. It's just a word that describes the collective actions of people under some circumstances. Mary's situation didn't automatically get fixed because it's a free market. Mary's rejection by the recruiter got undone when I complained to my engineering director, who put me in touch with an HR director who patiently listened to the story and overturned the decision[5](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/#fn:B). The market is just humans. It's humans all the way down.

We can fix this, if we stop assuming the market will fix it for us.

3.  "taste-based" discrimination is discrimination based on preferences that are unrelated to any actual productivity differences between groups that might exist. Of course, it's common for people to claim that [they've never seen racism or sexism in some context](https://lwn.net/Articles/824148/), often with the implication and sometimes with an explicit claim that any differences we see are due to population level differences. If that were the case, we'd want to look at the literature on "statistical" discrimination. However, statistical discrimination doesn't seem like it should be relevant to this discussion. A contrived example of a case where statistical discrimination would be relevant is if we had to hire basketball players solely off of their height and weight with no ability to observe their play, either directly or statistically.

    In that case, teams would want to exclusively hire tall basketball players, since, if all you have to go on is height, height is a better proxy for basketball productivity than nothing. However, if we consider the non-contrived example of actual basketball productivity and compare the actual productivity of NBA basketball players vs. their height, there is (with the exception of outliers who are very unusually short for basketball players), no correlation between height and performance. The reason is that, if we can measure performance directly, we can simply hire based on performance, which takes height out of the performance equation. The exception to this is for very short players, who have to overcome biases (taste-based discrimination) that cause people to overlook them.

    While measure of programming productivity are quite poor, the actual statistical correlation between race and gender and productivity among the entire population is zero as best as anyone can tell, making statistical discrimination irrelevant.
