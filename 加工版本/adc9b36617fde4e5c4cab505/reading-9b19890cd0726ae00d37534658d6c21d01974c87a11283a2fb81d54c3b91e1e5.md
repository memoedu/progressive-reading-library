# The is-ought problem

---

## 正文

_感谢 Gautam Mohan 启发我写下这篇文章，并提供极有帮助的修改。_

我一直苦恼于一个问题：我觉得自己的 moral beliefs 没有 firm foundation。事实上，我不确定这是否可能，即使是在 principle 上。基本上，这是因为 [is-ought problem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is-ought_problem)：从 _descriptive_ claims（关于 facts 以及世界如何运作）到 _normative_ claims（关于人应该采取什么 actions），似乎不可能进行 logical transition。下面这个 analogy，我认为能帮助阐明 is-ought problem，希望你能原谅我开启数学宅模式。

任何 abstract thought 的 [system](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_system)，例如我们用来对 facts 或 morals 进行 reason 的 system，都是基于 _rules of inference_ 的。这些 rules 是 argument 中的 steps，只要之前的 steps hold，就始终可以合法进行。例如，在 deductive logic 中，有这样一条 rule of inference：如果你知道 `$P$` 是 true，并且知道 `$P$` implies `$Q$`，那么你就知道 `$Q$` 是 true。这条 rule of inference 被称为 _modus ponens_（因为比搬出 formal logic 更装腔作势的，只有搬出拉丁语）。

不过，Rules of inference 只告诉你如何从一个 valid statement 得到另一个。它们可以让你从 `$P$` 得到 `$Q$`，却不能给你一个 valid `$P$` 作为起点。所以，abstract thought 的 system 还必须有 axioms：即被 fundamentally asserted 为 true 的东西。例如，natural-number arithmetic 中的一条常见 axiom 是 `$x=x$`，对任何 natural number `$x$` 都成立。arithmetic 和 descriptive logic 的美妙之处在于，我们可以从看似如此 common-sense、如此 trivial 的 axioms 和 rules of inference，作出惊人地 complex 的 inferences。

一个重要问题是，为什么我们用于 math 和 descriptive logic 的 axioms 显得如此 natural？为什么大家对于用来对世界如何运作作出 inferences 的某个特定 system，（大体上）都表示认同？毕竟，你完全可以想象有人 rejecting 像 modus ponens 这样的 rule；一个很好的例子，请看 Lewis Carroll 的 _[乌龟对阿喀琉斯说了什么](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_the_Tortoise_Said_to_Achilles)_。或者，有人可能会 deny descriptive reasoning 的某些 axioms，例如 [Occam’s Razor](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor)——其 idea 是，在 hypotheses 之间 choosing 时，应该 favor 其中 simplest 的那个。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:1) [2](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:2) 不过，虽然可能存在这样奇怪的 beliefs，但在实践中，人们对于 factual reasoning 的 system 似乎有着广泛共识。

我认为，这部分可以由 evolution 来 explained。不管出于什么原因，我们生活的世界都是按照某一特定 set 的 axioms 和 rules of inference 运作的。“believe” 这个 system 的人（更一般地说，是 organisms）——也就是 brains 恰好按照同样的 axioms 和 rules 进行 reason 的那些——更擅长 modeling 世界，因此也更擅长 predicting 和 responding to novel situations。结果，他们 survive 得更久，拥有更多 offspring，并在 gene pool 中 propagate 自己特有的 reasoning systems，而不是那些不 conform to 世界运作方式的“bad” systems。[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:3)

不过，当我们思考的是 morals 而不是 facts 时，情况就有些不同了。我们仍然可以保留自己的 rules of inference：我们并不 deny 一个人的 moral system 应该是“logical”的。但 logic 的 rules of inference 无法将一个“is”变成一个“ought”。Moral reasoning requires 一套 independent 的“ought” axioms，而麻烦正是从这里开始的。Factual reasoning evolved，是为了帮助我们理解某种在最 basic level 上不会改变的东西——governing 世界如何运作的 laws。但从 evolutionarily 的角度看，moral reasoning 的存在主要是为了帮助 individual organisms 以 beneficial to 整个 species 的方式 navigate 一个 complex social landscape。这个 landscape 不断 shifting，而且要难理解得多得多。结果，我们并没有像在 descriptive reasoning 上那样，converged 到单一的、evolutionarily favored moral system 上（如果这样的 optimal system 真的存在的话）。

这就是为什么我们会看到如此繁多的 moral systems，也解释了为什么像 [consequentialism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism) 与 [deontology](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontological_ethics) 这样的 normative debates，常常显得 fundamentally irreconcilable，而 factual debates 则并非如此。在 factual debates 中，我们的 species 对 rules 大体有共识，但在 moral debates 中，我们就没有这么幸运了。

## 原文

_Thanks to Gautam Mohan for inspiring this post and providing massively helpful edits._

A problem I’ve struggled with is the feeling that I don’t have a firm foundation for my moral beliefs. In fact, I’m not sure it’s possible to, even in principle. Basically, this is because of the [is-ought problem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is-ought_problem): it appears to be impossible to make a logical transition from _descriptive_ claims (about facts and how the world works) to _normative_ claims (about what actions one should take). Here’s an analogy that I think sheds some light on the is-ought problem, if you’ll forgive the math-nerd mode.

Any [system](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_system) of abstract thought, such as one we use to reason about facts or morals, is based on _rules of inference_. These rules are steps of an argument that are always legal to make if the previous steps hold. For example, in deductive logic it is a rule of inference that if you know `$P$` is true, and you know that `$P$` implies `$Q$`, then you know that `$Q$` is true. This rule of inference is called _modus ponens_ (because the only thing more pretentious than breaking out the formal logic is breaking out the Latin).

Rules of inference only tell you how to get from one valid statement to another, though. They can get you from `$P$` to `$Q$`, but they can’t give you a valid `$P$` to start with. So a system of abstract thought also has to have axioms: things fundamentally asserted to be true. For instance, a common axiom in natural-number arithmetic is that `$x=x$` for any natural number `$x$`. The beauty of arithmetic, and of descriptive logic, is that we can make astoundingly complex inferences from axioms and rules of inference that seem as common-sense and trivial as this.

A major question is, why do our axioms for math and descriptive logic seem so natural? Why does everyone agree (largely) on one particular system for making inferences about how the world works? After all, you could perfectly well imagine someone rejecting a rule like modus ponens; for a great example, see Lewis Carroll’s _[What the Tortoise Said to Achilles](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_the_Tortoise_Said_to_Achilles)_. Alternately, someone might deny certain axioms of descriptive reasoning, like [Occam’s Razor](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor)—the idea that when choosing between hypotheses one should favor the simplest.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:1) [2](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:2) But while such strange beliefs are possible, in practice there seems to be a broad consensus on a system for factual reasoning.

I think is partly explained by evolution. For whatever reason, the world we live in operates by one particular set of axioms and rules of inference. People (more generally, organisms) who “believe” in this system—i.e., whose brains happen to reason according to the same axioms and rules—are better at modeling the world, so they’re better at predicting and responding to novel situations. As a result, they survive longer, have more offspring, and propagate their particular reasoning systems in the gene pool, rather than “bad” systems that don’t conform to how the world works.[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/is-ought/#fn:3)

When we’re thinking about morals and not facts, though, things change a bit. We’re still able to keep our rules of inference: we don’t deny that one’s moral system should be “logical”. But logic’s rules of inference can’t turn an “is” into an “ought”. Moral reasoning requires an independent set of “ought” axioms, and it’s here that the trouble begins. Factual reasoning evolved to help us understand something that, at its most basic level, doesn’t change—the laws governing how the world works. But evolutionarily, moral reasoning exists largely to help individual organisms navigate a complex social landscape in the a way beneficial to the species as a whole. This landscape is constantly shifting and much, much harder to understand. As a result, we haven’t converged on a single evolutionarily favored moral system (if such an optimal system even exists) the same way we have for descriptive reasoning.

This is why we see such a massive profusion of moral systems, and why normative debates like [consequentialism](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism) vs. [deontology](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontological_ethics) often seem fundamentally irreconcilable in a way that factual debates don’t. In factual debates, our species largely agrees on the rules, but in moral debates we have no such luck.
