# I. 支持 UAPR 的 case

---

## 正文

Antinatalism 是这样一种 view：bring 新的 conscious beings into existence 是 wrong 的。在这里，我将 present 并 refute 似乎最流行的、支持它的 arguments，具体采用 [David Benatar](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0199549265/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0199549265&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21) 所 present 的版本。

这篇文章的标题指的是 Benatar 提及他的 hypothetical critics 时那些自鸣得意的方式，尽管他自己也批评 hypothetical critics 以自鸣得意的态度回应他的 arguments。我不会这样做，而会把俏皮话限制在这些开场白里。接下来是一个来自 n\_on-self-deluded, cheerful Pollyannaist optimist\_ 的 argument。

开始之前，先说说关于这篇文章的一些考虑。我平时的文章会进行 extensive literature reviews，一直查到发表前一天，但这篇文章只有少数 sources。具体来说，就是上面链接的书，以及 Sarah Perry 的书 [Every cradle is a Grave](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0989697290/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0989697290&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21)（书评见[这里](https://nintil.com/2016/11/30/suicide-fast-and-slow-book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave-sarah-perry/)）。原因是 ~我懒~ 我相当确信自己在这件事上是对的，因此希望不必完成 full literature review，就能让所有人信服我的 position。我对 [anti-natalism](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2011/12/a_cursory_rejec.html) 的反应与 Bryan Caplan 相近，但我会提供一些更详细的 arguments，说明我为何 rejection。

这当然使我比平时更容易受到潜在批评者的质疑，因此，我也会比平时更愿意——而我平时已经很愿意——回应对这里所 present 的 arguments 的任何 critique，并在必要时反复修改这篇文章。

我将考虑两种支持 antinatalism 的 arguments。我会先 define antinatalism 的 varieties，以及它们 entail 什么

-   Universal a priori antinatalism (UAPR)：Bringing 任何新的 life into existence 都是 wrong 的（或者，用较弱的说法，是不 good 的），无论这些 lives 的 goodness 或 badness 如何。对它的辩护使用 asymmetry argument，我稍后会 present 这个 argument。
-   Universal a posteriori antinatalism (UAPO)：Bringing 任何新的 life into existence 都是 wrong 的（或者不 good），因为每一个将会存在的 life 都会是 awful，尽管它成为 awful 并没有 metaphysical necessity。只是我们的 world 的 specific features 恰好使这一点为 true。书中通过提及少数 suffering 和 bad things 的 cases，以及 arguing 持不同看法的人都是 deluded，来为它辩护。
-   Individual a posteriori antinatalism：Bringing 某个特定的新的 life into existence 是 wrong 的，而这需要逐个 case 判断。例如，如果我们 expect 某个特定的 life 会是 awful，那么 bring 那个 life into existence 就是 morally wrong。但如果这个 life 并不 awful，或者是 good 的，那就没有问题。书中没有为这一版本辩护，而且在 Benatar 看来，它几乎算不上 antinatalism，但它是最接近普通人 beliefs 的 antinatalism 版本。

## I. 支持 UAPR 的 case

asymmetry argument 如下。Benatar 通常谈论 pleasure 和 pain，但我恰好认为这些东西的 moral (dis) value 被 overrated 了，因此我将 expand 这个 definition——而不损害他的 argument——使之包括我们可能认为是 good 或 bad 的任何东西。

1.  good things 的 presence 是 good 的
2.  bad things 的 presence 是 bad 的
3.  bad things 的 absence 是 good 的，即使这种 good 不被任何人 enjoyed
4.  good things 的 absence 并不 bad，除非对某个人而言，这种 absence 是一种 deprivation

前两个 premises 似乎是 uncontroversial 的。Benatar 说，rejecting 3-4 并没有任何 logically incoherent 之处，但他支持它们的理由是，accepting 它们能够 explains 一系列其他普遍被 accepted 的 asymmetries。他还说，rejecting 它会使我们 accept 可怕的 conclusions。

1.  ![关于 benatar asymmetry 的图片结果](https://nintil.com/old_assets/2017/02/15-1-1.png)

支持这个 Asymmetry 的四个 asymmetries 是：

1.  Procreational duties：我们有 duty 去 avoid bringing miserable people into existence，但我们没有 duty 去 bring into existence happy people
2.  Prospective beneficence：把孩子会因此 be benefitted 作为 having a child 的 reason 来 cite，是奇怪的。但把这个孩子会 suffer 作为不 having 那个孩子的 reason 来 cite，并不奇怪。
3.  Restrospective beneficence：当一个 suffering child 已经被 brought into existence 时，为那个孩子着想而 regret 这样做，是说得通的。但当一个人没有 bring 一个 happy child into existence 时，就不能为那个孩子着想而 regret 这件事
4.  Distance suffering and absent happy people：我们为那些 distance people 的 suffer 而 sad。但我们不会为 uninhabited regions 中 absent happy people 而 sad

说 pain 的 absence 是 good 的，听起来可能很奇怪，因为没有任何人能够 enjoy 那种 pain 的 absence——如果这甚至是可能的话——所以 Benatar 说（p. 30），这种 judgement 是参照一个 exist 或不 exist 的 person 的 potential interests 作出的。例如，如果有一个 existing person 正处于 pain 之中，那么这种 pain 的 absence 本会是 good 的，即便实现它的方式是从一开始就不让那个人存在。

现在，关于为什么 Asymmetry 能 explains 这些 asymmetries，请考虑 asymmetry 1。这里的意思是，bringing 某个人 into 一个 awful existence 会是 bad 的（对那个 hypothetical person 而言），而 bringing 某个人 into 一个 nice existence 则不会是 good 的（对那个 hypothetical person 而言）。第一个 case 会是 bad 的，这一事实赋予我们不 bring 那个 person into existence 的 duty；但在第二个 case 中，由于 good things 的 absence 并不 bad（对那个 hypothetical person 而言），所以没有 duty。诚然，bring into existence 一个有 zero bad things 的 life 并不会是 wrong 的。但我们也没有 reason 这样做，无论那个人会遇到多少 good things。而只要那个人遇到一件 tiny bad thing，整个 life 就不再 worth starting。

Benatar 随后说，Asymmetry 是唯一 plausible explanation。他考虑并 dismisses 了其他解释。

例如，如果这些 asymmetries 是因为我们有 duties 去 avoid harming 别人，却没有 duties 去 benefit 别人呢？但是，许多人认为我们确实有一些 positive duties 去 do good，而那些 duties 并不包括 bringing happy people into existence。另一个 possible explanation 是，我们没有 duty 去 bring happy people into existence，是因为这涉及 substantial cost（pregnancy，加上 raising the child）。Benatar 认为这存在问题，因为如果没有那些 costs，我们就会有 duty 去 create 更多 happy people。他还说，这种 duty 不能借助 potential people 的 preference 来 justified。原因是还有另一个 asymmetry（“procreative moral reasons 的 asymmetry”）：potential people 的 interests 确实可以作为一个 reason 的 grounding，使我们 avoid bringing into existence unhappy people，但这些 interests 对 happy people 并不起 equivalent purpose。

Benatar 进一步通过考虑两个 symmetric alternatives 来为 Asymmetry 辩护：一个 case 是 pleasure 的 absence 为 bad（而 pain 的 absence 为 good），另一个 case 是两个 absences 既非 good 也非 bad。

在第一个 case 中，他 rejects 它，理由是如果我们 accepted 它，就必须为 happy people 着想而 regret 没有 bringing 他们 into existence。但这并不 regrettable，因此这个 symmetry 并不 tenable。

在第二个 case 中，Benatar 似乎只是在 beg the question：

> 对 existence 的 pains 进行 Avoiding，不仅仅是“不 bad”。它是 good 的。把 pleasure 的 absence Judging 为“不 good”也太 weak，因为这种说法说得不够充分。当然，pleasure 的 absence 并不是我们会称为 ood 的东西。然而，当 pleasure 的 absence 对任何人都不涉及 deprivation 时，重要的 question 是，它是否也“不 bad”，还是说它是“bad”。我认为，answer 是它“不 good，但也不 bad”，而不是“不 good，而是 bad”。因为“不 bad”比“不 good”是一个更 informative evaluation，所以我更倾向于前者。

因此，更 formally 地说，

1.  Asymmetry 是 true 的，所以
2.  potential people 的 lives 中的 good things 对他们而言并不 good
3.  potential people 的 lives 中的 bad things 对他们而言是 bad 的
4.  因此，coming into existence 没有任何 good，而且会有 bad things。
5.  因此，never have been born 是 better 的（没有 good reasons 去 bring people into existence），并且
    1.  就不再有更多 people 被 brought into existence 而言，具体来说 human race 的 extintion，以及一般来说 sentient life 的 extintion，都是 good 的
    2.  abortion 中的 pro-death position，即 abortion 在 pregnancy 的 early stages 是 morally mandatory 的，是 correct 的

请注意，这些 arguments 意在 apply 于任何 possible life。从 UAPR 的 perspective 来看，一个充满 perfect bliss、happiness 和 meaningfulness 的 world，只要包含某个人在某处 experiencing 一次 minor itch 的 mere possibility，就不 worth bringing into existence。

此外，我们是否应该 commit suicide？不一定。Benatar 区分了 lives worth starting 和 lives worth continuing。

> 我的 argument 的一个 implications 是：一个充满 good、只包含最微量 bad 的 life——一个只有一次 pin-prick 的 pain 掺杂其中的 utter bliss 的 life——比完全没有 life 更 worse。objection 是，这并不 plausible。Understanding 以下两者之间的 distinction，就能看出为什么这个 implication 并非那么 implausible：(a) coming into existence 是一种 harm，以及 (b) 它是多大的 harm。

Benatar present 了另一位作者的一个 related argument：

> 她 \[Shiffrin\] 说，在没有 evidence 表明某个 person 的 wishes 与此相反时，为 prevent 一个 greater harm 而对那个人 inflict 一个 lesser harm，是 permissible 的，甚至可能是 obligatory 的。相比之下，inflict 一个会 yield 更大 (pure) benefit 的 harm，则会是 wrong 的。40 因此，我们认为，为 prevent 那个人遭受 greater harm，例如 death，而 break 一个 unconscious（non-consenting）person 的 arm，是 acceptable 的。（这就是“rescue case”。）
> 
> 然而，如果 break 那个人的 arm 是为了 secure 某种 greater benefit，例如“supernormal memory、有用的 encyclopedic knowledge 储备、相当于 twenty IQ points 的额外 intellectual ability，或 consume immoderate amounts of alcohol or fat 而没有 side effects 的 ability”，我们就会 condemn 这种做法。41（把这称为“pure benefit case”。）由于所有 existers 都 suffer harm，procreation 总是 causes harm。Shiffrin 教授愿意 grant（for the sake of argument？）“being created 可以 benefit 一个 person”。42 然而，按照刚才提到的 asymmetry，我们不能为了 secure 这个 benefit 而 inflict 这个 harm。
> 
> 尽管 existing people 有时可以 authorize 我们 inflicting harm，以便为他们 secure 某种 benefit，但对于那些我们 bring into existence 的人，我们永远无法在 create 他们之前 obtain 他们的 consent。她 argue 说，我们也不能 presume hypothetical consent。这有四个 reasons。43 首先，如果我们没有 create 他或她，那个人并不会被 harmed。其次，existence 的 harms 可能是 severe 的。第三，life 的 harms 无法在不付出 considerable cost 的情况下被 escaped。最后，hypothetical consent 并不是 based on 那个 individual 的 values 或 attitudes towards risk。

## II. 为什么 UAPR 是 wrong 的

如果你觉得上文晦涩曲折得没有必要，那么你并不是唯一有这种感受的人。它当然还没有达到 Rawls 那种晦涩曲折的程度，但终究仍然如此。这并不 disqualify Benatar 的 arguments，只是让 understand 和 criticise 它们变得更加困难。

要 showing 为什么 UAPR 是 wrong 的，有两种方式。第一种是为 non-UAPR 提供一个 positive case，另一种是指出支持 UAPR 的 arguments 在哪里 go wrong。第一个 argument 是一个 argument ad myself：

1.  我的 life 是 nice 的
2.  不 bringing me into existence，就不会让我拥有如此 good life，甚至根本不会有 life
3.  如果你能通过按下一个按钮来 cause me to exist——这对你自己只是一个 small cost——那么你就 ought to 这样做。不这样做，就意味着我无法 enjoy my life
4.  因此，有一个 life 是 worth starting 的，而且我们有 duty 去 start 它
5.  但这 contradicts UAPR
6.  因此，UAPR 是 wrong 的

上述 argument 也可以用于这样一个 life：我们知道它将与我的相似，但它尚未 exist。这个 argument 并不 refute 所有版本的 antinatalism。UAPO 仍然是 tenable 的，因为或许我的 case 很特殊，而其他大多数人确实是 miserable 的。

antinatalist 会用支持 UAPA 的 arguments 来 criticise 我的 premise 1，说我的 life 没有那么 good。我稍后会回答这一点。第二个 premise 会受到借助 Asymmetry 而提出的 criticise，因此我现在转向它。如果一个人哪怕只 accepts 一种关于 positive duties 的 minimalistic conception，Premise 三似乎也是 trivially true 的。

我对 Asymmetry 的 critique 有两个方面。首先，请注意，Asymmetry 的 literal presentation 是 misleading 的。也就是说，当 Benatar 说 ‘pain 的 absence 是 good 的’ 时，他并不是说一块空荡荡的 vacuum 中有什么 good。我认为，literal presentation 意味着 bad 或 good things 的 absence 既不是 good，也不是 bad。它什么都不是。它不是 good，也不是 bad。这些 categories 并不 apply 于空无一物的东西。

但 Benatar 实际上想表达的并不是这个意思。他的意思是，对一个 potential person 而言，good things 不会是 good 的，但 bad things 会是 bad 的。当然，我 reject 这一点，而 Benatar 在一篇[后来的 paper](https://philpapers.org/rec/BENSBN) 中说，rejecting 这一点确实并没有什么 logically wrong，但他说我们不应该这样做，理由是据说这会带来奇怪的 consequences。

> 我们当然可以（logically）state，正如 Scenario B 中 absent pains 是 good 的，absent pleasures 也是 bad 的。我已经指出，问题在于我们不应该 claim 这一点。其中一个 reasons 是，这样一来，我们就无法作出那些 value judgments，而我们在我认为由 basic asymmetry 所 explained 的四个 asymmetries 中，确实作出了这些 judgments。

那么，我们就必须转向 sub-asymmetries。

关于第一个 asymmetry，

> 虽然我们有 duty 去 avoid bringing into existence 那些会 lead miserable lives 的人，但我们没有 duty 去 bring into existence 那些会 lead happy lives 的人。

我不同意不 bring people into existence 的 duty 有什么特殊之处。Duties 大多是 negative 的（To avoid harming），少数是 positive 的（beneficence）。这源于这样一种 idea：我们 ought 不去 interfere with 他人的 life-plans，而应让他们按照自己的意愿 live their lives。然而，对于 unborn people，并没有 pre-existing life-plan 可供 interfere with。但如果一个 new person 被 generated 出来，结果此人后来 regret 自己的 existence，那么我们就 wronged 了此人，因为我们让此人经历了一个并未请求得到的 life，所以这仍然是 wrong 的。

话虽如此，如果我们 accept 我们有一项 broad duty of beneficence（对他人 do good），那么 discharging 这项 duty 的一种方式就是 bringing more people into existence。Creating 那些将会对自己的 lives 感到 satisfied 的人，是一件 good thing。

但这项 duty 比 duty of non-maleficence，也就是不 harming 的 duty 更 weaker，因此仍然存在一个 asymmetry。不过，在这里，我们已经去掉了一个专门针对 procreation 的 specific asymmetry，它可以由 negative 和 positive duties 之间的 general asymmetry 来 explained。Benatar 对此有一个 reply：

> 在谈论 duties 而非 reasons 时，我指出，‘‘对于那些 deny 我们有任何 positive duties 的人来说，这确实会是我所提供的 explanation 的一个 alternative explanation。然而，即使在那些确实认为我们有 positive duties 的人当中，也只有少数人认为，其中还包括一项 bring happy people into existence 的 duty’’ (Benatar 2006, p. 32).12 换句话说，用我们有更 stronger reasons 去 avoid suffering，而非 cause pleasure 来作 explanation，是一种较不令人满意的 explanation，因为它只适用于 accept asymmetry of procreational duties 的部分人，而不是所有人。

遗憾的是，他的 argument fails。首先，一个人可以说，我们没有 specific duty 去 create new people，尽管这种 intuition 的适用范围是否像 Benatar 所说的那么有限，是 disputable 的。许多人认为 bringing new life into existence 是一项 duty。但我们不认为存在一项 bring new people into existence 的 specific duty，这个事实并不构成 reason，让我们 claim 这件事不属于 duty of beneficence 的广泛范畴。同样，我们认为我们没有 duty 给我钱，尽管这样做属于 duty of beneficence（如果你想通过这种方式 discharge 你的 duty，右侧栏里有一个 Patreon 链接）

但更重要的是，procreation 的特殊之处在于，它涉及 very high cost（pregnancy 和抚养孩子）。如果 creating 拥有 good life 的人像按下一个按钮一样容易，那么我们就会有非常 strong reasons 这样做。不这样做甚至会是 wrong 的。

Benatar 考虑过这个 argument：

> 人们通常认为，如果 create 大量 pleasure 会要求我们作出 significant sacrifice，那么我们的 positive duties 就不能包括这样一项 duty。既然 having children 涉及 considerable sacrifice（至少对于 pregnant woman 是如此），那么这才是为什么没有 duty 去 bring happy people into existence 的最佳 explanation，而不是 asymmetry。然而，这个 alternative explanation 的问题在于，它 implies，如果没有这种 sacrifice，我们就有 duty 去 bring happy people into existence。换句话说，如果我们能够 create 这样的人，而自己无需付出 great cost，那么不 create 他们就是 wrong 的。

不过，我并不觉得这有什么问题。

> 然而，这个 alternative explanation 的问题在于，它 implies，如果没有这种 sacrifice24，我们就有 duty 去 bring happy people into existence。换句话说，如果我们能够 create 这样的人，而自己无需付出 great cost，那么不 create 他们就是 wrong 的。但这 presupposes，正在讨论的 duty 是一项 all-things-considered duty。
> 
> 然而，potential people 的 interests 甚至不能 ground 一项 bring them into existence 的 defeasible duty。换一种说法，procreative (all-things-considered) duties 的 asymmetry rests on 另一个 asymmetry——procreative moral reasons 的 asymmetry。根据这个 asymmetry，虽然我们有一个 strong moral reason，它 grounded in potential people 的 interests，25 去 avoid creating unhappy people，但我们没有 strong moral reason（grounded in potential people 的 interests）去 create happy people

但这不过是在 begging the question！为什么一个 future person 的 interests 可以 ground 一个 reason 去 avoid 他们的 potential suffering，却不能 ground 一个 reason 去给他们一个 life？Benatar 在这里没有解释。他只是含糊地指向第二个 asymmetry：

> 把孩子将由此 be benefited 当作 having a child 的 reason 来 cite，是奇怪的。但把孩子将会 suffer 当作不 having a child 的 reason 来 cite，却并不具有同样的奇怪之处。

但我并不觉得这奇怪。问题解决了。恕我不同意 Benatar，having a child 的 motivation 有一部分是、也 ought to 是 altruistic 的。

我们继续看下一个 asymmetry

> 当一个人已经 brought a suffering child into existence，regret 自己 having brought that child into existence 是有道理的——而且是为了那个孩子而 regret。相比之下，当一个人没有 bring a happy child into existence 时，他不能为了那个人而 regret 这次未能做到。

这里，我们应该避开在 moral evaluations 中使用 emotional language（regretting）的陷阱，所以我将它改述如下：

> 当一个人已经 brought a suffering child into existence，持有这样一个 belief 是有道理的：having brought that child into existence 是一件 bad thing——而且是为了那个孩子而这样想。相比之下，当一个人没有 bring a happy child into existence 时，他不能为了那个人而持有这样一个 belief：未能 bring that child into existence 是一件 bad thing。

现在清楚了一些，但这个 argument 仍然是 defective 的，因为它是在 comparing 一个 actual case（一个 existing suffering child）与一个 potential case（一个 potential happy child）。如果一个 happy child 被 brought into existence，那么 believe 当初 having brought it into existence 是一个 good idea，确实是有道理的。对于一个 potential child，持有任何一方的 belief 都没有道理：这个人甚至还不 exist。

于是，我们转向最后一个 asymmetry：

> 我们为远方那些 suffer 的人感到 sad，是 rightly 如此。相比之下，我们无需为 uninhabited planets 上，或我们自己 planet 上的 uninhabited islands 或其他 regions 中 absent happy people 而流泪。

再一次，让我们去掉其中的 emotion，好让它对像我这样从不 sad 的人也有意思：

> 我们认为，远方人们的 suffering 是 bad 的。相比之下，我们并不认为 uninhabited planets 上，或我们自己 planet 上的 uninhabited islands 或其他 regions 中 happy people 的 absence 是 bad 的。

但这又是一个 unfair comparison 的 case：把 actuality 与 potentiality 放在一起。如果我们考虑完整的 cases 列表，就会发现 existing suffering 是 bad 的，而 existing good things 是 good 的。对于不 exist 的东西，我们无话可说。真要说什么的话，那就是，如果有一片 empty space，我们有 reasons 去 avoid creating 那些会 suffer 的 beings 来填满它，也有 reasons 去 create 那些将拥有 lives worth living 的 beings。

因此，我 reject 最后三个 asymmetries，并用一项 general duty of beneficence，以及这项 duty 取决于 benefit others 对我们而言有多 costly 这一事实，来 explain 第一个。

现在，可以针对我提出一个 general counter argument：bringing new beings into existence 的 reasons 比不 bring 他们 into existence 的 reasons 更 weaker。对此，我提出下面这个 thought experiment：

假设你有一个神奇的拳头，只要你朝别人的胳膊打一拳，就能使他们拥有 greater intelligence，并能 enjoy 一系列他们之前无法 enjoying 的 pleasures（比如 understanding quantum mechanics、learning History 和 doing phlosophy）。进一步假设，过去被打过的人几乎一致庆幸自己挨了这一拳。那么，你随意朝别人打拳有什么 wrong 吗？

我 argue，没有，而且事实上你有一项 duty——作为 duty of beneficence 的一部分——去朝别人的胳膊打拳，以 improve their lives。

不过，这个 case 并不是完全 analogous 的。你可以 obtain consent。虽然在这个 particular case 中，大多数人庆幸自己 undergone the procedure 这一事实，或许可以 defeat consent；这与父母对孩子 impose 某些 rules 有些相似，其 grounds 是孩子将来会认为这些 rules 是 justified 的，因为他们目前没有足够的 information 去 understand 自己的 choice。（然而，如果他们表现出对 relevant information 有足够的 knowledge，那么他们的 consent 就会 trump 我们打拳的 duty）。

对于 non-beings，他们通过 hypothetical consent 来 consent。假如他们能够说愿意，他们就会这样说。我们可以通过询问人们来知道这一点。如果 regretted being born 的人数很多，那么我们就会处于一个 UAPO 为 true 的 world 中，因此，sentient life 的 extinction 是一项 ethical duty 这一 conclusion 就会 hold。

上面的 argument ad myself 无法 defeat UAPO，因此我需要 argue，life 实际上是 nice。David Benatar 在书中第 3 章通过 arguing，life 实际上是 awful，对此提出了 critique。现在我就转向这一章。

和之前一样，我会先介绍 Benatar 的 arguments，然后提出 critique。

## III. 我们是 biased 的

那么，Benatar 对 life 说了些什么？

他首先就 goods 和 bads 在 time 中的 addition 的 non-conmutativity 提出了一个有意思的 point。

> 例如，一种 life，其所有 good 都发生在前一半，而后一半的特征是 uninterrupted bad，会比 good 和 bad 更 evenly distributed 的 life 要 worse 得多。即使每种 life 中 good 和 bad 的 total amount 相同，这一点也为 true。同样，achievement 和 satisfaction steadily inclining 的 life，比最初几年 bright、但此后 progressively worse 的 life 更 preferable。4 这些 alternative lives 中 good 和 bad 的 amount 可能相同，但 trajectory 可以使一种 life 比另一种 better。

他还 argues，pleasure 和 pain 内部的 additivity 也存在 non-linearity：

> 另一个 distributional consideration 是 good 和 bad 的 intensity。一种 life，其中的 pleasures 是 extraordinarily intense 的，却相应地 few、infrequent 且 short-lived，可能比具有相同 total amount 的 pleasure、但 individual pleasures 是 less intense 且在 life 中 more frequently distributed 的 life 更 worse。不过，pleasures 和其他 goods 也可能在一种 life 中 distributed too widely，从而使它们如此 mild，以至于几乎无法与 neutral states distinguishable。

接着是一句有意思的话，显露了 Benatar 的 negative utilitarian-leaning 立场：

> 可以认为，一旦一种 life 达到某个 badness 的 threshold（同时考虑其 badness 的 amount 和 distribution），任何 quantity 的 good 都不能 outweigh 它，因为任何 amount 的 good 都不可能 worth 那样的 badness。

不过，除了一个小例子之外，他没有为这个 point 进一步 argue；那个例子只是用 numerical quantities 重述了上面引用的段落。

接下来，他继续 arguing，我们对自己的 lives 的 quality 所作的 self-assessments 是 unreliable 的。他需要这个 point 来 argue，反对我的 argumentum ad myself，也反对绝大多数 deny 自己的 lives 是 bad 的人。也就是说，他承认，大多数人 prefer exist，并认为 never have been 并不会更 better。

> 我们有非常充分的 reason 怀疑，这些 self-assessments 是否是 life 的 quality 的 reliable indicator。human psychology 有若干众所周知的 features，可以 account for 人们通常对自己的 life 的 quality 作出的 favourable assessment。能够 explain positive assessment（的 extent）的，是这些 psychological phenomena，而非 life 的 actual quality。

首先是他所称的 Pollyanna Principle，即一种倾向于 optimism 的 tendency。它表现为：

1.  倾向于 recall positive 而非 negative experiences。 “例如，在多项 studies 中，当 subjects 被要求 recall 自己整个 lives 中的 events 时，他们 listed 的 positive experiences 的数量远多于 negative experiences”
2.  我们认为事情会比实际情况发展得更 better。

他说，对 well-being 的 assessments 是 skewed towards spectrum 的 positive end 的。也就是说，很少有人将自己描述为不太 happy。大多数人说自己相当 happy 或非常 happy，而且大多数人 believe，自己比 average person 更 better off。

此外，

> 大多数可以合理认为会 improve 一个人的 life 的 quality 的 factors，并不会 commensurately influence 对这种 quality 的 self-assessments（如果它们确实有 influence 的话）。例如，尽管人们自己对 health 的 rankings 与其对 well-being 的 subjective assessments 之间存在 correlation，但根据 physical symptoms 作出的关于人们 health 的 objective assessments，并不是人们对其 well-being 的 subjective evaluations 的同样有效的 predictor。14 即使在那些对自身 health 的 dissatisfaction 确实 leads to 较低 self-reported well-being 的人中，大多数人 report 的 satisfaction 的 levels 仍处于 spectrum 的 positive end

而且，

> 在任何一个给定的 country 内，16 poor 几乎（但并不完全）和 rich 一样 happy。

而且，

> education 和 occupation 也不会产生很大的 difference（尽管它们确实会产生一些 difference）。

他提到的另一个被算作 bias 的 phenomenon，是 adaptation，或者说 habituation。

> 当一个人的 objective well-being 恶化时，起初会出现 significant subjective dissatisfaction。不过，此后往往会有一种 tendency，使人 adapt 到新的情况，并相应地 adjust 自己的 expectations。19 尽管人们对 adaptation 发生的程度，以及 adaptation 的 extent 如何在 life 的不同 domains 中 varies 存在一些争议，但大家一致认为 adaptation 确实会发生。因此，即使 well-being 的 subjective sense 没有 return 到原来的 level，它也会比人们可能以为的更接近那个 level，而且在某些 domains 中会比在其他 domains 中更接近。

还有一个 biasing factor，是与他人的 well being 所作的 comparisons

> determines 一个人对自己的 life 状况的 judgement 的，与其说是其 life 过得如何，不如说是其 life 在与他人 comparison 时过得如何。因此，self-assessments 更是一个人的 life 的 comparative quality、而非 actual quality 的 indicator。这产生的一个 effect 是，life 中人人都有的那些 negative features，在人们对自身 well-being 的 judgements 中是 inert 的。由于这些 features 非常 relevant，忽视它们会 leads to unreliable judgements。

在这些 factors 中，他指出 Pollyannaism 是最重要的，因为 adaptation 对 negative 和 positive events 都会起作用，而我们也会把自己与 better 和 worse off 的人 compare。但 Pollyannaism 使这两种 mechanisms 都变得 optimistically biased，

> 例如，人们更倾向于将自己与 worse off 的人 comparing，而不是与 better off 的人 comparing。22 因此，在最好的情况下，adaptation 和 comparison 会 reinforce Pollyannaism。在最坏的情况下，它们会 mitigate 它，但不会 entirely negate 它。
> 
> 从 evolutionary perspective 来看，上述 psychological phenomena 并不令人意外。23 它们不利于 suicide，而有利于 reproduction。如果我们的 lives 确实如我接下来仍将指出的那样 bad，而且如果人们倾向于如实看待自己的 lives 的这种 true quality，那么他们可能会更倾向于 kill themselves，或者至少不再 produce 更多这样的 lives。因此，Pessimism 往往不会被 naturally selected。

所以，回顾一下，他为支持我们是 optimistically biased 这一观点而提出的 arguments 是：

1.  Pollyannaism（盲目乐观倾向）
    1.  我们 recall 的 positive events 多于 negative events
    2.  我们 expects 事情会比实际情况发展得更 better
2.  Adaptationism（适应主义）
3.  那些本应在 happiness 上 make a difference 的 Factors，并没有完全做到这一点（education、wealth 等）
4.  我们倾向于 relative to 他人 report 自己的 happiness，而不是以 absolute 方式。General harms 没有被 perceived。
5.  而且，optimism bias 有一个 evolutionary explanation

让我们在这里停下来，考虑这些 claims。

## IV. 我们并没有\_那么\_ biased

我的第一个 critique 是，即使我们承认 Benatar 所说的一切，这个 argument 也并不 follow，因此并没有那么需要对这个问题进行 detailed empirical examination。也就是说，确实，如果 biases 的 levels 极高，那么他就是对的。然而，从 heuristically 来看，涉及 good 和 bad lived experiences 这样 salient 的事物的 biases，很少会那么 strong。

此外，这些并不是我们具有的唯一的 biases，而我们具有它们这一事实，并不会 disqualify 我们在其他领域的 beliefs。举个例子，考虑 optical illusions。我们可以 argue，大多数人 perceive 那些 illusions 的方式并不 consistent with reality。但我们不能据此 justified 地声称，所有 perception 都是 wrong，或者我们所见与实际存在的东西之间有很大差距。而且，即使我们确实 perceive 到那些 illusions，我们仍然可以作出 rational judgement：我们的 visual system 在某些情况下是 flawed 的，因此可以说，即使你看见 X，你面前的东西也不是 X。

同样，我们可以尝试仔细审视自己的 lives，并尝试 account for Benatar 指出的 biases。如果我们仍然认为自己的 lives 是 worth it 的，那么我们这样想就是 justified 的。仔细阅读他提供的 evidence，并不能改变我的想法。Benatar 必须克服的 burden of proof 很大，而他的论述并未做到这一点。

不过，为求完整，我会简要讨论他提出的 evidence。

-   habituation 这一 phenomenon 并不是 bias。我看不出它怎么会是。对你的 welfare 而言，重要的是你对它的 perception。你可能 poor 而 happy，也可能 rich 而 miserable。再进一步说，这是一种非常好的生活方式：习惯生活中那些背景性的烦扰，并强调和关注 good things。如果我们能做到这一点，我们就会 better off——通常如此——。这并不是在呼吁 self-delusion。必须先 acknowledged 那些 bad things，才能将它们视为 minor things 而不再计较。你很少会发现有人比我更强烈地反对 self-delusion，我甚至主张，建立在 falsehood 上的 life 大半或完全是 worthless 的 \[1\]；而要过上 good life，你就需要 examine 一切可能让你相信自己处于这种境况的东西。
-   human languages 中 positive words 占多数这一事实，被称为 Pollyanna hypothesis。但请注意，这并不是相对于 reality 的 bias（也就是我们通常所说的 bias），而是相对于 neutrality 的 bias。Life 总体上很棒，我们应当预期 language 会 reflect 这一点。
-   Pollyanna Principle 则是在上述基础上的 extension，将我们 recall 的 positive events 比 negative events 更多、也更加 vividly 这一事实纳入其中（[Matlin, 2016](https://books.google.co.uk/books?hl=es&lr=&id=0Ge3DAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA315&dq=pollyanna+principle&ots=ibCHoAuTly&sig=Bb0gWSSoH97NA8QFSkdUhPqgXtM&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=pollyanna%20principle&f=false)）。同样，这可以部分地 explained 为：我们经历的 positive events 本来就比 negative events 更多。但这不能 explain，为什么 positive events 会被 more vividly remembered。平均而言，optimistic bias 确实存在，但根据我对 literature 的阅读，它不足以克服 Benatar 要 argue life 是 terrible 时首先必须克服的 burden of proof。
-   depressive people 对世界的看法是否更 realistic，目前并不清楚。我们对这个问题最新的了解来自这篇 meta-analysis（[Moore and Fresco, 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/David_Fresco/publication/227708596_Depressive_realism_A_meta-analytic_review/links/0fcfd500162b4821ca000000.pdf)），其中研究者发现，depressive 和 non-depressive patients 都是 positively biased 的，并发现 depressed 的人具有非常小的优势（[Cohen's d](https://nintil.com/2016/05/09/how-to-give-them-the-cohens-d/) = -0.07）。不过，即便这一 slight bias 也是 inconclusive 的，因为作者指出，不同的 methodologies 会 yield 相当不同的 results，其中也包括 depressed 的人更 biased 这样的 results。具体而言，使用 reality 的 objective measures 和 depression 的 objective measures（而不是 self-report）的 studies，发现的 depressive realism effects 更低。(d~-0.03)。
-   也存在 [negative biases](http://www.seekingbalance.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/BadStrongerThanGood.pdf)，[这](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b074/3ad11614cf1f4861dc2eca0e4a264b528990.pdf)似乎与 uniform positivity bias 的观念 inconsistent。对于这种 negativity bias，也有人提出了 evolutionary explanation。
-   从日常观察来看，人们似乎有时 optimistic，有时 pessimistic。Benatar 给出了一些 unwarranted optimism 的例子。但在现实世界中，我们发现，人们往往认为世界总体上正在变得更糟，而那些确实知道真相的人，不得不不断提醒人们 reality 究竟是什么样子（[这里](http://www.vox.com/the-big-idea/2016/12/23/14062168/history-global-conditions-charts-life-span-poverty)、[这里](http://www.vox.com/2014/11/24/7272929/charts-thankful)、[这里](http://reason.com/archives/2016/10/14/theres-never-been-a-better-time-to-be-al)）
-   Relative income 确实 affect 我们如何 perceive 自己的 welfare。[但 absolute income 也同样如此](https://nintil.com/2016/01/27/the-milanovician-master-argument-you-cant-care-about-poverty-only-about-inequality/)。也就是说，无论你周围是什么情况，如果你喝不上水，你都会感觉不适。同样，无论身边其他人拥有多少 wealth，好吃的食物仍然是好吃的食物。它或许会被视为具有更低或更高的 status，但 experience 本身不会有太大改变。

这些是否足以 prove，Benatar 的 evidence 并不足够？我希望如此。如果还不够，我会再提出一个小小的 argument，让天平进一步向我这边倾斜。

这里的想法是，要了解人们的 welfare 极其困难，而最适合对此作出判断的，是他们自己。这是因为，给定一组 external circumstances，并没有必然随之产生的 emotion。当然，有些情况很容易判断。一般而言，用锤子砸人的脸会 cause 他们遭受巨大的 suffering，我们既可以从他们的 self report，也可以从他们 expressing suffering 的 behaviour（喊叫、哭泣等）中 infer 这一点。\[2\]。同样，你也可以把过去经历的 suffering 与当下的 good things 编织成一个 achievement story。那些 good things 似乎会因过去为 achieve 它们所必需的 suffering 而被 magnified。Benatar 确实同意，bad things 之后接着 good things，比只有 good things 更好。但这是因为，我们能够从中创造出 meaningful 的东西。理想情况下，我们能够在任何境况中创造 meaning，甚至达到 [Viktor Frankl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Frankl) 那样的程度。如果一个人无论出于什么原因不愿意这样做，那么他可以 commit suicide。对于那些 rationally 希望如此的人，应当允许 Suicide。（参见我对 Sarah Perry 的书的讨论，以及对“free disposal society”的讨论）

同为 anti-natalist 的 David Pearce 同意 Benatar 的 framework，但提出了不同的 solution：eliminate suffering，并以 gradients of bliss 取而代之。这样一来，即使在他们看来，仍然没有 start a new life 的 reasons，至少也不会有反对这样做的 reasons。我在这里要说的是，如果接受 Pearce 的 approach，我们也应该接受通过 thought 使 suffering 不复存在这一想法。只凭 thought 的 act，如果我们 alter 自己的感受方式，就可以 suffer less。例如，一位亲爱的朋友去世时，我们不应感到任何 negative 的东西，例如如此。既然已经失去了朋友，为什么还要通过 feeling bad 来增添额外的 suffering？这解决不了任何问题。而且，既然我们坚持 suffering 是 bad 的这一观念，那么如果我们为此 felt bad，就会是 irrational 的。大多数人只是说自己控制不了。但无论如何，这只是一个例子。大多数人确实会使用类似的 mechanisms，使自己的 emotions 适应世界，从而过上更 pleasant 的 lives。这是 bad thing 吗？这是看待世界的 wrong way 吗？我不这么认为。在一个——据说——wretched 而 awful 的世界里，感觉自己的 life 是 worth it 的，这并没有什么 irrational 或 biased 之处。真要说的话，在逆境中维持这种感觉，反而会在做到时让它更加 meaningful。

在结束这一节之前，我再次重申我的核心 argument。

我们认为自己的 lives 是 worth living 的这一 intuition 相当 strong。我们认为，good things vastly outweigh bad things，而这使我们的 lives 既 worth continuing，也 worth starting。要 counter 这一点，需要 massive bias 的 evidence。仅有一些 bias 并不够。如果我们说，自己的 life 有 95% 是完全 worth living 的，Benatar 的 arguments 会将这一比例降低到 93 或 92%。argue 仅仅因为我们具有一些 biases，就应该 completely reject 一个 strong intuition，就如同 argue 因为 [confabulation 确实存在](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confabulation)，我们就应该 discard 自己关于 memory 总体上 reliable 的 belief 一样。

## V. Conclusion（结论）

到这里，我已经 established 两个 points。其一，支持 antinatalism 的 Asymmetry argument fails。其二，life 终究是 nice 的（对大多数人而言）。

但那些确实 regret having being born 的人呢？第一个 response 是 Caplan-Perry synthesis：你可以自由地 reject life（意思是，如果你 rationally 地想这样做，就不应受到阻止），而我们都应努力建设一个向人们开放这一 option 的 society（例如，在 approved medical centers 中）。这样，prefer non-existence 的人可以回到 non-existence，而 prefer existence 的人可以保有 existence。

现在，考虑 bringing someone new into existence 时，确实存在某些 terrible 的事情发生在那个人身上的 chances。仅仅为了允许其他人的 existence，就应该让一个 future person 承受这些事情的 mere possibility 吗？因为显然，如果我们 accept，在一个 life 内，无论多少 good 都不能 compensate 某个给定的 bad，那么，在某个地方、某个时刻，拥有这样一个 life 的人可能 born 这一事实，就会成为 stop 任何 procreation 的 reason。这就是我们必须 reject 那一点的原因。

如果一边是 universe 中所有 conscious life 的 cessation，另一边是极少数人将 live awful lives，在我看来，显然应该选择后者。此外，我们还应该致力于建设一个 regret existing 的人数越来越少的 world。幸运的是，我们并不处在一个 suffering 是 metaphysical necessity 的 world 中。

我就写到这里，等待 critiques，希望它们会来。

## 原文

Antinatalism is the view that it is wrong to bring new conscious beings into existence. Here I present and refute what seems to be the most popular arguments for it, as presented by [David Benatar](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0199549265/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0199549265&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21).

The title of this post is a reference to the many ways Benatar refers smugly to his hypothetical critics, even though he himself criticises hypothetical critics of addressing his arguments smugly. I will not do that and confine my cheesy remarks to these introductory remarks. What follows is an argument from n\_on-self-deluded, cheerful Pollyannaist optimist\_.

Before starting, some considerations about this post. Unlike my usual articles, which involve  extensive literature reviews up to the day before of publication, this one has only a few sources. Specifically, the book linked above, and Sarah Perry's book  [Every cradle is a Grave](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0989697290/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1634&creative=6738&creativeASIN=0989697290&linkCode=as2&tag=nintil-21) (reviewed [here](https://nintil.com/2016/11/30/suicide-fast-and-slow-book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave-sarah-perry/)). The reason for that is ~my laziness~ that I am quite sure that I am right in this matter, and so I hope to be able to convince everyone of my position without having to go through the full literature review. My reaction to [anti-natalism](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2011/12/a_cursory_rejec.html) is close to Bryan Caplan's, but I will offer some more detailed arguments for my rejection.

This of course leaves me more open than usual to potential critics, so I will be more willing than usual - and typically I'm very willing - to address any critique of the arguments presented here, and to amend this post the number of times that is necessary.

The arguments for antinatalism that I will consider are two. I will first define varieties of antinatalism and what they entail

-   Universal a priori antinatalism (UAPR): Bringing any new life into existence is wrong (or, weakly, not good), regardless of the goodness or badness of those lives. This is defended using the asymmetry argument, which I shall later present.
-   Universal a posteriori antinatalism (UAPO): Bringing any new life into existence is wrong (or not good) given that every life that will come to be will be awful, even though there is no metaphysical necessity that it will be. So it happens that specific features of our world make that true. This is defended in the book by mentioning a few cases of suffering and bad things, and by arguing that who thinks otherwise is deluded.
-   Individual a posteriori antinatalism: Bringing some specific new life into existence is wrong, and this varies on a case by case basis. For example, if we expect a life in particular to be awful, then it is morally wrong to bring that life into existence. But if the life is not awful or good, then there is no problem. This is not defended in the book, and it quite wouldn't count as antinatalism in the eyes of Benatar, but is the closest version of antinatalism to the average person's beliefs.

## I. The case for UAPR

The asymmetry argument is as follows. Benatar typically talks about pleasure and pain, but I happen to think that the moral (dis) value of those things is overrated, and thus expand the definition -without loss for his argument- to include anything we might consider good or bad.

1.  The presence of good things is good
2.  The presence of bad things is bad
3.  The absence of bad things is good even if that good is not enjoyed by anyone
4.  The absence of good things is not bad unless there is somebody for whom this absence is a deprivation

The two first premises seem uncontroversial. Benatar says that there is nothing logically incoherent in rejecting 3-4, but he supports them by saying that accepting them explains a series of other asymmetries that are commonly accepted. Also, rejecting it, says he, leads us to accept terrible conclusions.

1.  ![Image result for benatar asymmetry](https://nintil.com/old_assets/2017/02/15-1-1.png)

The four asymmetries that support the Asymmetry are:

1.  Procreational duties: We have a duty to avoid bringing miserable people into existence, but we have no duty to bring into existence happy people
2.  Prospective beneficence: It is strange to cite as a reason for having a child that that child will be benefitted. But is is not strange to cite as a reason for not having that child that the child will suffer.
3.  Restrospective beneficence: When a suffering child has been brought into existence, it makes sense to regret having done so for the sake of that child. But when one does not bring a happy child into existence, one cannot regret that for the sake of that child
4.  Distance suffering and absent happy people: We are sad for distance people who suffer. But we are not sad  for absent happy people on uninhabited regions

It can sound weird to say that the absence of pain is good, given that there is no one to enjoy that absence of pain -if that is even possible- so Benatar says (p. 30) that such judgement is made with reference to the potential interests of a person who either does or does not exist. As an example, if there is an existing person in pain, the absence of such pain would have been good even if the way to achieve it was not to have that person in the first place.

Now, as to why the Asymmetry explains the asymmetries, consider asymmetry 1. The idea here is that bringing someone into an awful existence would be bad (For that hypothetical person), while bringing someone into a nice existence wouldn't be good (for the hypothetical person). The fact that the first case would be bad gives us a duty not to bring that person into existence, but in the second case, as the absence of good things is not bad (for that hypothetical person), there is no duty. It wouldn't be wrong, to be sure, to bring into existence a life that has zero bad things. But we wouldn't have a reason to do it, no matter how many good things happened to that person. And as soon as a tiny bad thing happened to that person, that whole life would not be worth starting.

Benatar then says that the Asymmetry is the only plausible explanation. He considers and dismisses others.

For example, what if the asymmetries are due to the fact that we have duties to avoid harming others, but no duties to benefit others? But then, many people think we have some positive duties to do good, but those duties do not include bringing happy people into existence. Other possible explanation is that the reason why we don't have  duty to bring happy people into existence is that it involves a substantial cost (pregnancy, plus raising the child). Benatar finds this problematic, saying that absent those costs, we would have the duty to create more happy people. And he says that such a duty cannot be justified with recourse to the preference of potential people. The reason being that there is yet another asymmetry ('of procreative moral reasons'): that the interests of potential people do serve as grounding of a reason to avoid bringing into existence unhappy people, but that such interests do no serve the equivalent purpose for happy people.

Benatar further defends the Asymmetry by considering two symmetric alternatives: the case where the absence of pleasure is bad (and the absence of pain is good), and the case where both absences are neither good or bad.

In the first case, he rejects it saying that if we accepted it, we would have to regret not bringing happy people into existence, for their sake. But it is not regrettable, and thus this symmetry is not tenable.

In the second case, Benatar just seems to beg the question:

> Avoiding the pains of existence is more than merely ‘not bad’. It is good. Judging the absence of pleasure to be ‘not good’ is also too weak in that it does not say enough. Of course the absence of pleasure is not what we would call  ood. However, the important question, when the absence of pleasure involves no deprivation for anybody, is whether it is also ‘not bad’ or whether it is ‘bad’. The answer, I suggest, is that it is ‘not good, but not bad either’ rather than ‘not good, but bad’. Because ‘not bad’ is a more informative evaluation than ‘not good’, that is the one I prefer.

Thus, more formally,

1.  The Asymmetry is true, so
2.  The good things in potential people's lives is not good for them
3.  The bad things in potential people's lives is bad for them
4.  Hence, there is nothing good in coming into existence and there will be bad things.
5.  Hence, it is better to never have been born (there are no good reasons to bring people into existence), and
    1.  The extintion of the human race in particular and sentient life in general is good in so far as no more people are brought into existence
    2.  The pro-death position in abortion, that abortion is morally mandatory in the early stages of pregnancy, is correct

Note that these arguments are meant to apply to any possible life. A world of perfect bliss and happiness and meaningfulness, that contains the mere possibility of someone somewhere experiencing a minor itch is not worth bringing into existence, from UAPR's perspective.

Should we, in addition, commit suicide? Not necessarily. Benatar makes a difference between lives worth starting and lives worth continuing.

> One of the implications of my argument is that a life filled with good and containing only the most minute quantity of bad—a life of utter bliss adulterated only by the pain of a single pin-prick—is worse than no life at all. The objection is that this is implausible. Understanding the distinction between (a) coming into existence being a harm and (b) how great a harm it is, enables one to see why this implication is not so implausible.

Benatar presents a related argument from another author:

> She \[Shiffrin\] says that in the absence of evidence of a person’s wishes to the contrary, it is permissible, perhaps obligatory, to inflict a lesser harm on that person in order to prevent a greater harm. By contrast, it would be wrong to inflict a harm that would yield a greater (pure) benefit.40 Thus, we take it to be acceptable to break an unconscious (non-consenting) person’s arm in order to prevent a greater harm, such as death, to that person. (This is the ‘rescue case’.)
> 
> However, we would condemn breaking that person’s arm in order to secure some greater benefit, such as ‘supernormal memory, a useful store of encyclopedic knowledge, twenty IQ points worth of extra intellectual ability, or the ability to consume immoderate amounts of alcohol or fat without side effects’.41 (Call this the ‘pure benefit case’.) Since all existers suffer harm, procreation always causes harm. Professor Shiffrin is prepared to grant (for the sake of argument?) that ‘being created can benefit a person.’42 However, in accordance with the asymmetry just mentioned, we may not inflict the harm in order to secure the benefit.
> 
> Although existing people can sometimes authorize our inflicting harm in order to secure some benefit for them, we can never obtain the consent of those whom we bring into existence before we create them. Nor can we presume hypothetical consent, she argues. There are four reasons for this.43 First, the person is not harmed if we fail to create him or her. Secondly, the harms of existence may be severe. Thirdly, the harms of life cannot be escaped without considerable cost. Finally, the hypothetical consent is not based on the individual’s values or attitudes towards risk.

## II. Why UAPR is wrong

If you found the above unnecessarily obscure and convoluted, you are not the only one. It surely is not Rawls-level obscure and convoluted, but it is so nonetheless. This does not disqualify Benatar's arguments, it only makes it more difficult to understand and criticise them.

There are two ways of showing why UAPR is wrong. The first one is to offer a positive case for non-UAPR, and the other one is show where the arguments for UAPR go wrong. The first argument is an argument ad myself:

1.  My life is nice
2.  Not bringing me into existence wouldn't have allowed me to have such a good life, or a life at all
3.  If you can cause me to exist by pressing a button - a small cost to yourself - you ought to do it. Failing to do so will mean that I wouldn't enjoy my life
4.  Hence, there is one life that is worth starting, and for which we have a duty to start it
5.  But this contradicts UAPR
6.  Hence, UAPR is wrong

The above argument can also be used with a life that we know will be like mine, but that doesn't exist yet. This argument doesn't refute all versions of antinatalism. UAPO is still tenable, as perhaps my case is weird and most other people are indeed miserable.

The antinatalist will criticise my premise 1 with arguments for UAPA, saying that my life is not that good. I'll answer that later. The second premise will be criticise by means of the Asymmetry, so to it I turn. Premise three seems trivially true if one accepts even a minimalistic conception of positive duties.

My critique of the Asymmetry is twofold. First, note that the literal presentation of the Asymmetry is misleading. That is, when Benatar says that 'The absence of pain is good' he does not mean that there is anything good in an empty piece of vacuum. The literal presentation, I say, means that the absence of bad or good things is neither good or bad. It isn't anything. It is not good and not bad. Those categories do not apply to that which is empty.

But Benatar actually means something different with that. He means that for a potential person, good things wouldn't be good, but that bad things would be bad. Of course, I reject this, and Benatar says in a [later paper](https://philpapers.org/rec/BENSBN) that indeed there isn't anything logically wrong in rejecting this, but he says that we shouldn't, the reason being supposedly weird consequences.

> We certainly can (logically) state that just as the absent pains in Scenario B are good, so the absent pleasures are bad. The problem, I have suggested, is that we should not claim this. Among the reasons for this is that we would then not be able to make all the value judgments we do in the four asymmetries that I say are explained by the basic asymmetry.

So then, we must turn to the sub-asymmetries.

Regarding the first asymmetry,

> While we have a duty to avoid bringing into existence people who would lead miserable lives, we have no duty to bring into existence those who would lead happy lives.

I disagree that there anything special regarding a duty not to bring people into existence. Duties are mostly negative (To avoid harming), and a few are positive (beneficence). This stems from the idea that we ought not interfere with the life-plans of other and let them live their lives as they want. However, there is no pre-existing life-plan to interfere with in the case of unborn people. But if a new person is generated such that the person comes to regret its existence, then we would have wronged that person, for we would have put that person through a life that was not asked for, so it would still be wrong.

That said, if we accept that we have a broad duty of beneficence (to do good to others), then one way of discharging that duty is bringing more people into existence. Creating people who will be satisfied with their lives is a good thing.

But that duty is weaker than a duty of non-maleficence, of not harming, so an asymmetry remains. But here we have gotten rid of an specific asymmetry for procreation, which is explained by the general asymmetry between negative and positive duties. Benatar has a reply to this:

> Speaking about duties rather than reasons, I noted that ‘‘for those who deny that we have any positive duties, this would indeed be an alternative explanation of the one I have provided. However, even of those who do think that we have positive duties only a few also think that amongst these is a duty to bring happy people into existence’’ (Benatar 2006, p. 32).12 In other words, the explanation that we have stronger reasons to avoid suffering than to cause pleasure is a less satisfactory explanation because it is open to only some rather than all people who accept the asymmetry of procreational duties.

Unfortunately, his argument fails. First, one can say that we have no specific duty to create new people, although it is disputable the extent of this intuition is as limited as Benatar says. Many people think bringing new life into existence is a duty. But the fact that we don't think there's a specific duty to bring new people into existence is no reason to claim that such a thing doesn't fall under the broad umbrella of a duty of beneficence. We similarly think that we have no duty to give me money, although doing that falls within a duty of beneficence (There is a link to Patreon in the right hand panel if you want to discharge your duty that way)

But more importantly, the specialness of procreation is that it involves a very high cost (pregnancy and raising the kid). If creating people with a good life was as easy as pressing a button, we would have very strong reasons to do so. It would even be wrong not to do it.

Benatar has considered this argument:

> It is usually thought that our positive duties cannot include a duty to create lots of pleasure if that would require significant sacrifice on our part. Given that having children involves considerable sacrifice (at least to the pregnant woman), this, and not asymmetry, is the best explanation for why there is no duty to bring happy people into existence. The problem, though, with this alternative explanation is that it implies that in the absence of this sacrifice we would have a duty to bring happy people into existence. In other words, it would be wrong not to create such people if we could create them without great cost to ourselves.

Altough I don't see this as a problem.

> The problem, though, with this alternative explanation is that it implies that in the absence of this sacrifice24 we would have a duty to bring happy people into existence. In other words, it would be wrong not to create such people if we could create them without great cost to ourselves. But this presupposes that the duty under discussion is an all-things-considered duty.
> 
> However, the interests of potential people cannot ground even a defeasible duty to bring them into existence. Put another way, the asymmetry of procreative (all-things-considered) duties rests on another asymmetry—an asymmetry of procreative moral reasons. According to this asymmetry, although we have a strong moral reason, grounded in the interests of potential people,25 to avoid creating unhappy people, we have no strong moral reason (grounded in the interests of potential people) to create happy people

But this is just begging the question! Why can the interests of a future person ground a reason to avoid their potential suffering but not ground a reason to give them a life? Benatar doesn't explain here. He sort of handwaves in the direction of the second asymmetry:

> It is strange to cite as a reason for having a child that that child will thereby be benefited. It is not similarly strange to cite as a reason for not having a child that that child will suffer.

But it doesn't seem strange to me. Problem solved. Part of the motivation for having a child, pace Benatar is, and ought to be, altruistic.

We move onto the next asymmetry

> When one has brought a suffering child into existence, it makes sense to regret having brought that child into existence—and to regret it for the sake of that child. By contrast, when one fails to bring a happy child into existence, one cannot regret that failure for the sake of the person.

Here we should move away from the trap of using emotional language in moral evaluations (regretting), so I rephrase it:

> When one has brought a suffering child into existence, it makes sense to have the belief that having brought that child into existence was a bad thing—and to think that for the sake of that child. By contrast, when one fails to bring a happy child into existence, one cannot have the belief that failing to bring that child into existence is a bad thing, for the sake of the person.

Now we get a bit clearer, but the argument is still defective, for it is comparing an actual case (an existing suffering child) with a potential case (a potential happy child). If a happy child is brought into existence, it does make sense to believe that it was a good idea to having brought it into existence. With a potential child, it doesn't make sense to have a belief either way: the person doesn't even exist.

And so we move to the final asymmetry:

> We are rightly sad for distant people who suffer. By contrast we need not shed any tears for absent happy people on uninhabited planets, or uninhabited islands or other regions on our own planet.

Again, let's purge this of emotion to make it  interesting to someone who is never sad like me:

> We think that the suffering of distant people is bad. By contrast we do not think the absence of happy people on uninhabited planets, or uninhabited islands or other regions on our own planet is bad.

But this is yet another case of an unfair comparison: an actuality with a potentiality. If we consider the full list of cases, we see that existing suffering is bad, and existing good things are good. For things that do not exist, we have nothing to say. If anything, that if there is an empty space, we have reasons to avoid creating beings that will suffer to populate it, and reasons to create beings that will have lives worth living.

Thus I reject the last three asymmetries and I explain the first once by means of a general duty of beneficence, and the fact that such a duty depends on how costly it is to us to benefit others.

There is one general counter argument that could be made now against me: that the reasons for bringing new beings into existence are weaker than the reasons not to bring them. Against this, I offer the following thought experiment:

Suppose you have a magic fist such that if you punch people in their arm you cause them to have greater intelligence, and be able to enjoy a range of pleasures that they weren't enjoying before (Say, understanding quantum mechanics, learning History, and doing phlosophy). Furthermore, assume that people who have been punched in the past have all almost unanimously been glad to be punched. Is there anything wrong with you randomly punching people?

I argue that no, and in fact you have a duty -as part of a duty of beneficence - to punch people in the arm to improve their lives.

This case, however, is not totally analogous. You could obtain consent. Although in this particular case, the fact that most people are glad to have undergone the procedure could perhaps defeat consent, in a similar way to how parents impose certain rules to their kids, on grounds that they will come to see them as justified, because in the present they don't have enough information to understand their choice. (If however, they manifested enough knowledge of the relevant information, then their consent would trump our duty to punch).

For non-beings, they consent via hypothetical consent. Had they been able to say yes, they would have. We can know this by asking people. If the number of people who regretted being born was high, then we would be in the world were UAPO is true, and so the conclusion that extinction of sentient life is an ethical duty would hold.

The argument ad myself above isn't able to defeat UAPO, and so I would need to argue that life is actually nice. David Benatar offers a critique of this, by means or arguing that life is actually awful in chapter 3 of the book. To it I now turn.

As before, first I will present Benatar's arguments, then offer a critique.

## III. We are biased

What does then Benatar say about life?

He first makes an interesting point about the non-conmutativity of the addition in time of goods and bads.

> For instance, a life in which all the good occurred in the first half, and uninterrupted bad characterized the second half, would be a lot worse than one in which the good and bad were more evenly distributed. This is true even if the total amount of good and bad were the same in each life. Similarly, a life of steadily inclining achievement and satisfaction is preferable to one that starts out bright in the very earliest years but gets progressively worse.4 The amount of good and bad in each of these alternative lives may be the same, but the trajectory can make one life better than the other.

And he also argues that there is also a non-linearity of additivity within pleasure and pain:

> Another distributional consideration is the intensity of the good and the bad. A life in which the pleasures were extraordinarily intense but correspondingly few, infrequent, and short-lived might be worse than a life with the same total amount of pleasure, but where the individual pleasures were less intense and more frequently distributed across the life. However, pleasures and other goods can also be distributed too widely within a life, thereby making them so mild as to be barely distinguishable from  neutral states.

And then an interesting remark that reveals Benatar's negative utilitarian-leaning credentials:

> Arguably, once a life reaches a certain threshold of badness (considering both the amount and the distribution of its badness), no quantity of good can outweigh it, because no amount of good could be worth that badness.

He, however, does not argue for that point beyond a minor example that is just restating  the paragraph above quoted with numerical quantities.

Next, he continues by arguing that self-assessments of the quality of our own lives is unreliable. He needs this point to argue againt my argumentum ad myself, and against the vast majority of people who deny that their lives are bad. That is, he grants that most people prefer to exist and think that it wouldn't have been better to never have been.

> there is very good reason to doubt that these self-assessments are a reliable indicator of a life’s quality. There are a number of well-known features of human psychology that can account for the favourable assessment people usually make of their own life’s quality. It is these psychological phenomena rather than the actual quality of a life that explain (the extent of) the positive assessment.

The first of this is what he calls the Pollyanna Principle, a tendency towards optimism. This manifests itself in:

1.  Inclination to recall positive rather than negative experiences. "For example, when asked to recall events from throughout their lives, subjects in a number of studies listed a much greater number of positive than negative experiences"
2.  We think that things will go better than they actually go.

He says that assessments of well-being are skewed towards the positive end of the spectrum. That is, very few people describe themselves as not too happy. Most peope say they are pretty happy or very happy, and most people believe they are better off than the average person.

Also,

> Most of the factors that plausibly improve the quality of a person’s life do not commensurately influence self-assessments of that quality (where they influence them at all). For example, although there is a correlation between people’s own rankings of their health and their subjective assessments of well-being, objective assessments of people’s health, judging by physical symptoms, are not as good a predictor of peoples’ subjective evaluations of their well-being.14 Even among those whose dissatisfaction with their health does lead to lower self-reported well-being, most report levels of satisfaction toward the positive end of the spectrum

And

> Within any given country,16 the poor are nearly (but not quite) as happy as the rich are.

And

> Nor do education and occupation make much (even though they do make some) difference.

Another phenomenon he mentions that counts as a bias is adaptation, or habituation.

> When a person’s objective well-being takes a turn for the worse, there is, at first, a significant subjective dissatisfaction. However, there is a tendency then to adapt to the new situation and to adjust one’s expectations accordingly.19 Although there is some dispute about how much adaptation occurs and how the extent of the adaptation varies in different domains of life, there is agreement that adaptation does occur. As a result, even if the subjective sense of well-being does not return to the original level, it comes closer towards it than one might think, and it comes closer in some domains than in others.

Yet another biasing factor is comparisons with the well being of others

> It is not so much how well one’s life goes as how well it goes in comparison with others that determines one’s judgement about how well one’s life is going. Thus self-assessments are a better indicator of the comparative rather than actual quality of one’s life. One effect of this is that those negative features of life that are shared by everybody are inert in people’s judgements about their own well-being. Since these features are very relevant, overlooking them leads to unreliable judgements.

Out of those factors, he points to Pollyannaism as the most important one, as adaptation works for both negative and positive events, and we compare ourselves with people who are better and worse off. But Pollyannaism makes it so that these two mechanisms are optimistically biased,

> For example, people are more prone to comparing themselves with those who are worse off than with those who are better off.22 Thus, in the best cases, adaptation and comparison reinforce Pollyannaism. In the worst cases, they mitigate it but do not negate it entirely.
> 
> The above psychological phenomena are unsurprising from an evolutionary perspective.23 They militate against suicide and in favour of reproduction. If our lives are quite as bad as I shall still suggest they are, and if people were prone to see this true quality of their lives for what it is, they might be much more inclined to kill themselves, or at least not to produce more such lives. Pessimism, then, tends not to be naturally selected.

So to recap, the arguments he offers in support of the idea that we are optimistically biased are:

1.  Pollyannaism
    1.  We recall more positive rather than negative events
    2.  We expects things to go better than they do
2.  Adaptationism
3.  Factors that should make a difference in happiness doesn't quite do so (education, wealth, etc)
4.  We tend to report our happiness relative to others, not in an absolute way. General harms are not perceived.
5.  And there is an evolutionary explanation for an optimism bias

Let us stop here and consider these claims.

## IV. We are not\_that\_ biased

My first critique is that even if we grant Benatar everything he is saying, the argument doesn't follow, so a detailed empirical examination of the issue is not that needed. That is, it is true that if the levels of biases were huge, then he would be right. However, heuristically, biases regarding something as salient as good and bad lived experiences are rarely that strong.

Furthermore, these are not the only biases we have, and the fact that we have them does not disqualify our beliefs in other areas. As an example, consider optical illusions. We can argue that most people perceive those illusions in a way that is not consistent with reality. But from here we would not be justified in claiming that all perception is wrong, that between what we see and what there is there is a big gap. And on top of that, even when we perceive the illusions as such, we can make the rational judgement that our visual system is flawed in some cases, and thus say that even if you see X, what is in front of you isn't X.

Similarly, we can try to carefully consider our lives, and try to account for the biases Benatar points out. If we still think that our lives are worth it, then we have justified to think it. If one peruses through the evidence he provides, this fails to change my mind. The burden of proof Benatar has to overcome is huge, and it fails to do it.

But for completion, I will briefly discuss the evidence he is presenting.

-   The phenomenon of habituation is not a bias. I don't see how it could be. What matters for your welfare is your perception of it. You could be poor and happy or rich and miserable. And to take it beyond, this is a very good approach to life: to get used to the background nuisances of life, end emphasise and focus on the good things. If we can do that, we'll be better off -typically-. This is not a call for self-delusion. The bad things have to be acknowledged before being dismissed as minor things. You will rarely find some who is more aggressively against self-delusion than me, to the point where I claim that a life built upon falsehood is mostly or totally worthless \[1\], and that to live a good life you need to examine anything that could lead you to believe that you are in such a situation.
-   The fact that there is a majority of positive words in human languages has been called the Pollyanna hypothesis. But note that this is not a bias with respect to reality (What we usually call a bias), but a bias with respect to neutrality. Life is generally great, and we should expect language to reflect that.
-   The Pollyanna Principle is then an extension of the above to include the fact that we recall more and more vividly positive rather than negative events ([Matlin, 2016](https://books.google.co.uk/books?hl=es&lr=&id=0Ge3DAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA315&dq=pollyanna+principle&ots=ibCHoAuTly&sig=Bb0gWSSoH97NA8QFSkdUhPqgXtM&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=pollyanna%20principle&f=false)). Again, this can be partially explained by the fact that we live more positive, rather than negative, events. What cannot be explained by that is that positive events are more vividly remembered. An optimistic bias does exist, on average, but from my reading of the literature it doesn't suffice to overcome the initial burden of proof that Benatar has to overcome to argue that life is terrible.
-   It is unclear whether depressive people are more realistic in their view of the world. The latest that we know about the matter is this meta-analysis ([Moore and Fresco, 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/David_Fresco/publication/227708596_Depressive_realism_A_meta-analytic_review/links/0fcfd500162b4821ca000000.pdf)) where they found both depressive and non-depressive patients to be positively biased, and they found an very small advantage in favour of the depressed ([Cohen's d](https://nintil.com/2016/05/09/how-to-give-them-the-cohens-d/) = -0.07). But then, even this slight bias is inconclusive, as the authors note that different methodologies yield quite different results, including that the depressed are more biased. Specifically, studies that use objective measures of reality and objective measures of depression (rather than self-report) find lower depressive realism effects. (d~-0.03).
-   There are also [negative biases](http://www.seekingbalance.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/BadStrongerThanGood.pdf) , [which](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b074/3ad11614cf1f4861dc2eca0e4a264b528990.pdf) seems inconsistent with the idea of a uniform positivity bias. This negativity bias also has a proposed evolutionary explanation.
-   Casually, it seems that people are sometimes optimistic and sometimes pessimistic. Benatar gives some examples of unwarranted optimism. But in the real world, we find that people tend to think that the world in general is getting worse, and those who do know the truth have to be constantly reminding people of what reality looks like ([Here](http://www.vox.com/the-big-idea/2016/12/23/14062168/history-global-conditions-charts-life-span-poverty), [here](http://www.vox.com/2014/11/24/7272929/charts-thankful), [here](http://reason.com/archives/2016/10/14/theres-never-been-a-better-time-to-be-al))
-   Relative income does affect how we perceive our own welfare. [But so does absolute income](https://nintil.com/2016/01/27/the-milanovician-master-argument-you-cant-care-about-poverty-only-about-inequality/). That is, you are going to feel unwell if you can't drink, whatever is in your surroundings. And equally, good food will remain good food regardless of the wealth others have around you. It perhaps will be seen as lower or higher status, but the experience itself won't be much changed.

Will this be enough to prove that Benatar's evidence is not enough?  I hope so. If it isn't, I'll offer one small argument to tip the scale even more towards my side.

The idea here is that it is extremely difficult to know about people's welfare, and the best judges of it are themselves. This happens because there is no necessary emotion given a set of external circumstances. Of course, some are easy. Generally, hitting people with a hammer in the face will cause them great suffering, which we can infer both from their self report and their suffering-expressing behaviour (yelling, crying, etc.). \[2\]. Equally, you could take suffering you have had in the past and weave it with present good things in an achievement story. Those good things seem to be magnified by the past suffering necessary to achieve them. Benatar does agree with the fact that bad things followed by good things are better than good things. But this is because we are able to make up something meaningful out of it. Ideally, we would be able to make up meaning in every circumstance, to the extreme of [Viktor Frankl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Frankl). If one doesn't want to, for whatever reason, then one can commit suicide. Suicide should be allowed to those who rationally want it. (See my discussion on Sarah Perry's book, and the discussion of a 'free disposal society')

Fellow anti-natalist David Pearce agrees with Benatar's framework, but proposes a different solution: eliminate suffering and replace it with gradients of bliss. That way, even if for them there won't still be reasons to start a new life, at least there won't be reasons against it either. What I say here is that if one buys Pearce's approach, we should also buy the idea of thinking suffering out of existence. By mere act of thought, we can suffer less if we alter the way we feel. If a dear friend dies, for example, nothing negative should be felt, for example. To the loss of the friend, why add extra suffering by feeling bad? It won't solve anything. And given that we are committed to the idea that suffering is bad, we would be irrational if we felt bad for it. Most people just say that they can't help it. But regardless, this is just an illustration. Most people do use similar mechanisms to adapt their emotions to the world in order to live more pleasant lives. Is this a bad thing? Is this a wrong way of seeing the world? I don't think so. There is nothing irrational or biased about feeling that one life is worth it in a -supposedly- wretched and awful world. If anything, sustaining that feeling in face of adversity makes it even more meaningful when achieved.

To close this section, I restate once more my core argument.

Our intuition that our lives are worth living is quite strong. We think that the good things vastly outweigh the bad things and that makes our lives worth continuing and worth starting. To counter this, one would need evidence of massive bias. Some bias is not enough. If we say that our life is 95% totally worth living, Benatar's arguments would reduce that to 93 or 92%. To argue that just because we have some biases we should completely reject a strong intuition is like arguing that we should discard our belief that our memory is generally reliable because [confabulation is a thing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confabulation).

## V. Conclusion

So far I have established two points. One, that the Asymmetry argument for antinatalism fails. And two, that life is, after all, nice (For most people).

But what about those who genuinely do regret having being born? One first response is the Caplan-Perry synthesis: You are free to reject life (in the sense that if you rationally want it, you shouldn't be stopped), and we should all work towards a society where that option is open to people (In approved medical centers, for example). That way, those who prefer non-existence can go back to it and those who prefer existence can keep it.

Now, when thinking about bringing someone new into existence, chances of something terrible happening to that person exist. Should a future person be subjected to the mere possibility of such things just to allow the existence of other people? Because clearly, if we accept that no amount of good can compensate a given bad within a life, the fact that somewhere, at some point, someone with such a life could be born would be a reason to stop any procreation. Which is why we have to reject that.

If on the hand hand we have the cessation of all conscious life from the universe and in the other having  a minuscule minority of people who will live  awful lives, it seems clear to my that we should opt for the latter. And in addition, we should also aim for a world were the number of people who regret existing is smaller and smaller. It is fortunately the case that we are not in a world where suffering is a metaphysical necessity.

I leave this here, and wait for critiques, which will hopefully come.
