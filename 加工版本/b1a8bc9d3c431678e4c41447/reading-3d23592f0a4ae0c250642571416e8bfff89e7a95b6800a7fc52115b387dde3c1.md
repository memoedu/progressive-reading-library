# 这重要吗？

---

## 正文

Adam Smith Institute 的 Executive Director Sam Bowman 最近写了一篇文章（好吧，是几周前），解释了他给自己贴上的标签 [neoliberalism 是什么](https://medium.com/@s8mb/im-a-neoliberal-maybe-you-are-too-b809a2a588d6#.5o4yg7ilo)。

我们都应该成为 neoliberals 吗？

至少我不会。我一向不给自己贴标签，尽量让自己的 identity 保持较小。这就是我写的那篇 '[Non-non-libertarian FAQ](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/03/24/nnlibertarianfaq/)' 取这个名字的原因。它不是对 libertarianism 的 defense，而是对 libertarianism 的 critique 的 critique。不成为 neoliberal 的另一个原因是，这个标签带有 negative connotations。Sam 正试图通过赋予这个标签一个 positive project 来改变这一点。一些 leftie 类型的人在试图找出世界上一切问题的 root cause 时，往往会使用 Neoliberalism。他们往往忘了，如果 neoliberalism 确实是 ruling ideology，那么 neoliberalism 就[已经 presided over](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/the-failure-of-neoliberalism-is-being-grossly-overstated)了人类 History 中最好的时期。

好吧，不过这听起来有点吹毛求疵，现在具体说说我为什么不会称自己为 neoliberal：

Sam 的 neoliberal package 中有九个主要 points，总的来说，我认为，如果存在一个采用他那一版 neoliberalism 的 Bowmanocracy，我会立刻搬过去，因为在我看来，它是一个 superior system，胜过目前任何国家现有的任何 system。

我 unqualifiedly agree with 第 1,3,4,5,6,7 和 8 个 points。

我对第 2 点的 disagreement 在于，我不是 consequentialist，尽管我认为 consequences 非常重要。事实上，我在那些用来 measure 一个人有多 utilitarian vs deontological 的 moral psychological tests 中给出的答案，把我归在了 highly utilitarian 的一边。我确实认为 rights 很重要，但这些 rights 的 strength 部分 depend 于设立这类 rights 的 consequences。Absolute rights 会导致 nonsensical outcomes。我不认为 rights 是有助于 overall welfare 的 rules of thumb，但 overall welfare 应当 shape rights 能延伸到什么程度。除了在奇怪的 thought experiments 中，这一点似乎并不明显。在实践中，我会说，我希望 rights 成为什么样，与 consequentialists 希望 ''rights'' 成为什么样，会大致 match。

我对第 2 点的 disagreement 更像是一种 moral uncertainty：我不确定 redistribution 是否 allowable。我倾向于认为，如果每个人都履行了自己的 moral obligation of charity——而今天并非如此——或者出于任何原因不再需要 redistribution，那么它就不是 allowable。但接下来，我们还有如何 structure redistribution 的问题：第 4 点，即 caring about 世界上的每个人，似乎表明，我们应当先向 poorer countries 进行 redistribute（例如通过 GiveWell），再在 West 实施 welfare；只是为了 avoid riots 而需要达到的某个 threshold 以上的部分除外（如果存在这样的 threshold）。

和 Sam 一样，我认为人们并不 deserve 自己的 talents。但我们应当把[这番话说完整](http://bleedingheartlibertarians.com/2016/08/rawls-and-desert/)：在这个意义上，没有人 deserves 任何东西。我们并不 deserve 自己的 intelligence、父母、朋友、internal organs，甚至按 Rawls 的说法，也不 deserve [我们的 _beliefs_](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/02/01/rawls-redistribucion-de-organos-e-ingenieria-psiquica/)。我会说，大多数 academic philosophers，包括那些持 liberal/libertarian 倾向的人，都 reject desert。我唯一接受的 deserts 是甜点。从这里走到“因此，一切都必须 equalised”（或者说 redistributed），还有一些 philosophical work 要做。

## 这重要吗？

既然我 agree with Sam 的 neoliberalism 中的 90%，我就希望世界更多地朝那个方向前进，因为它更接近我对于世界应有模样的 ideal。

此外，我认为主要的 debate 不应该不是关于 values（redistribution、给 the poor 多大 weight、什么才是 correct ethical theory），而应该是关于 facts。我在[这里](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/07/20/empirical-questions-at-the-root-of-ideological-disagreements/)写过这件事。

[Laurie MacFarlane](https://medium.com/@larred/im-not-a-neoliberal-you-may-not-be-either-a4b53cdd64f4#.86kwo8ywz) 对 Sam 那篇文章的一篇回应说明了这一点。他并不 criticise consequentialism，而是提出了一些 factual questions：markets 是 government interventions 吗 \[1\]（它们需要是吗？）。他认同如今已被 discredited 的观点，即 enclosures 与 capitalism 的 rise 有很大关系（[它们](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/enclosure-and-the-yeoman-9780198282969?lang=en&cc=de) [并没有](https://www.amazon.com/Enlightened-Economy-Economic-History-1700-1850/dp/0300189516)）。markets 需要在 State 内 work 吗（[它们](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/private-governance-9780199365166?cc=de&lang=en&)[不需要](http://www.cambridge.org/us/academic/subjects/economics/economics-general-interest/anarchy-unbound-why-self-governance-works-better-you-think)（至少有些不需要））。labour markets 会让 workers 容易遭受 exploitation 吗？我还没有 researched 这个问题，但这里有一项 data：economics historians 的 [consensus](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/726767781161623552) 是，Great Depression 之前 American manufacturing 的 workweek 的 reduction，是由于 economic growth，而不是 labour unions。

至于 water，它和 railways 一样，最初是 private enterprise。London 当时有几家 companies 在做这件事。在 Japan，有许多 railway providers 同时 own rolling stock _和 railtracks_。我们可以主张，有些 industries 需要 special regulation（那些存在 natural monopolies 的 industries）。但这些只是 minor exceptions，而不是 rule，而且其 regulation 可以 bottom up 地在 city/town level 完成，逐步建立用于 collective water resource management 的 federated structures。

MacFarlane 提出的另一个 claim 是，capitalism 并不 stable。这同样是一个 empirical claim。但这同样是 [false](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0164070412000304) 的。在 US，1913（FED）之后的时期，比此前更加 unstable。要建立 robust financial system，free market 的 alternative 是 free banking。[而它](http://www.alt-m.org/2015/04/28/what-you-should-know-about-free-banking-history/) [works](http://www.alt-m.org/2015/07/01/why-free-banking/) 得[很好](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/money-banking/new-paper-sound-money-an-austrian-proposal-for-free-banking-ngdp-targets-and-omo-reforms)。

最后一个 claim 是，最大的 breakthroughs 都是通过 society 的 collective mobilisation achieved 的（例如登月，或 universal healthcare）。但在这里，我也必须再次表示 disagreement。当然，如果没有几十年前 US 的 government investments \[2\]，就不会有人登上月球。但几十年前，private access to space 同样难以想象，而现在 private companies 正在 leading 一场重新展开的 Space Race。Universal healthcare 在许多情况下，是通过 nationalising 一个此前已经存在的 private healthcare system 而出现的。我们应当提出的问题——同样是一个 factual 问题——是，healthcare 领域的 deregulated market 是否会比 public universal healthcare system do better。有些人认为答案显而易见：a) US 的 system 很糟糕，而且它是 private 的，而 NHS 很好，而且它是 public 的（[这是一个糟糕的 critique](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/03/24/nnlibertarianfaq/#healthcare)）b) 大多数 economists 都认为 private healthcare 存在 problems。那么我们就应该做一次 literature review，看看 problems 是什么，以及我们知道什么 works best。

我说的可能不对，也许 capitalism 确实是 inherently unstable 的。但关键在于，这些 debates 并不要求我们进行困难的 philosophical argumentation，而是要求我们进行（困难的）fact checking、paper reading，以及对 History 的了解。

[Political disagreements 大多](http://www.owl232.net/irrationality.htm)围绕 biases、facts，以及如果愿意这样说的话，moods，而不是 fundamental values。让我们先解决这些，逐步改善世界，然后再回到 ethics 的 ultimate answer 是什么这个问题上。

## 原文

Adam Smith Institute's Executive Director, Sam Bowman recently wrote (well, a few weeks ago) a post explaining [what neoliberalism](https://medium.com/@s8mb/im-a-neoliberal-maybe-you-are-too-b809a2a588d6#.5o4yg7ilo), a label he applies to himself, is.

Should we all be neoliberals?

At least, not me. I have a policy of not labeling myself to try to keep my identity small. This is the reason why the '[Non-non-libertarian FAQ](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/03/24/nnlibertarianfaq/)' I wrote has such a name. It's not a defense of libertarianism, but a critique of a critique of libertarianism. Another reason not to be a neoliberal is that the label has negative connotations., something that Sam is trying to fix by filling the label with a positive project. Neoliberalism tends to be used by some leftie types when they try to find out the root cause of all the problems of the world. They tend to forget that if neoliberalism is actually the ruling ideology, then neoliberalism [has presided over](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/the-failure-of-neoliberalism-is-being-grossly-overstated)the best period in the History of mankind.

Okay, but that sounds a bit nitpicky, now to the details of why I wouldn't call me a neoliberal:

There are nine main points in Sam's neoliberal package, and overall I think that if a Bowmanocracy existed, with his version of neoliberalism, I would move immediately there, as I see it as a superior system over anything we have right now in any country.

I unqualifiedly agree with points 1,3,4,5,6,7, and 8.

My disagreement with 2 is that I am not consequentialist, though I think consequences matter greatly. As a matter of fact, my answers to moral psychological tests to measure how utilitarian vs deontological one is puts me in the highly utilitarian side. I do think rights matter, but that the strength of those rights depend partly on the consequences of setting up rights like that. Absolute rights are a recipe for nonsensical outcomes. I do not think that rights are rules of thumb that contribute to overall welfare, but overall welfare should shape how far rights go. This doesn't seem obvious except in weird thought experiments. In practise, I would say that what I would like rights to be will match  more or less with what consequentialists will want ''rights'' to be.

My disagreement with 2 is more of a case of moral uncertainty, I'm not sure if redistribution is allowable. I tend to think that it is not if everyone fulfilled their moral obligation of charity -which is not the case today-, or if there were no need for redistribution for whatever reason. But then, we have the issue of how to structure redistribution: Point 4, caring about everyone in the world, seems to suggest that we should first redistribute towards poorer countries (via GiveWell, for example) before doing welfare in the West, except above some threshold needed to avoid riots (If there's such threshold).

Like Sam, I think people don't deserve their talents. But we should say [the full thing](http://bleedingheartlibertarians.com/2016/08/rawls-and-desert/): no one deserves anything in that sense. We don't deserve our intelligence, parents, friends, internal organs, or even for Rawls, [our _beliefs_](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/02/01/rawls-redistribucion-de-organos-e-ingenieria-psiquica/). Most academic philosophers, I'd say, including those of a liberal/libertarian persuasion, reject desert. The only deserts I accept are desserts. From here there is some philosophical work to do to get to 'And thus everything has to be equalised' (Or redistributed for that matter)

## Does this matter?

Given that I agree with 90% of Sam's neoliberalism, I want the world to move more in that direction, because it is closer to my ideal of how the world should look like.

Furthermore, I think that the main debate shouldn't not be about values (redistribution, how much weight to give to the poor, what's the correct ethical theory), but about facts. I wrote about that [here](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/07/20/empirical-questions-at-the-root-of-ideological-disagreements/) .

One reply to Sam's article, by [Laurie MacFarlane](https://medium.com/@larred/im-not-a-neoliberal-you-may-not-be-either-a4b53cdd64f4#.86kwo8ywz) illustrates this. He doesn't criticise consequentialism, but some factual questions: Are markets government interventions \[1\] (Do they need to be?). He subscribes to the now discredited view that enclosures had much to do with the rise of capitalism ([They](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/enclosure-and-the-yeoman-9780198282969?lang=en&cc=de)  [didn't](https://www.amazon.com/Enlightened-Economy-Economic-History-1700-1850/dp/0300189516)) Do markets need to work within a State ([They](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/private-governance-9780199365166?cc=de&lang=en&) [don't](http://www.cambridge.org/us/academic/subjects/economics/economics-general-interest/anarchy-unbound-why-self-governance-works-better-you-think) (at least some)). Do labour markets leave workers vulnerable to exploitation? I haven't researched that one, but here's one piece of data: t[he consensus](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/726767781161623552)of economics historians is that the reduction of the workweek in American manufacturing before the Great Depression was due to economic growth, not labour unions.

Regarding water, like railways, it began as a private enterprise. In London there were a few companies doing the job. In Japan, there are lots of railway providers who own both rolling stock _and railtracks_. We could argue that some industries require special regulation (those with natural monopolies). But these are minor exceptions, not the rule, and its regulation can be done bottom up, at the city/town level, building up federated structures for collective water resource management.

Another claim made by MacFarlane, is that capitalism isn't stable. Again, this is an empirical claim. But this is again, [false](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0164070412000304). In the US, the post 1913 (FED) period was more unstable than what came before it. The free market alternative for a robust financial system is free banking. [And it](http://www.alt-m.org/2015/04/28/what-you-should-know-about-free-banking-history/) [works](http://www.alt-m.org/2015/07/01/why-free-banking/) [well](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/money-banking/new-paper-sound-money-an-austrian-proposal-for-free-banking-ngdp-targets-and-omo-reforms).

A final claim is that the biggest breakthroughs have been achieved via the collective mobilisation of society (Like going to the moon, or universal healthcare). But here I must again disagree. Surely, without the US government investments back a few decades \[2\], there wouldn't have been a man on the moon. But private access to space was also unthinkable a few decades ago, and now private companies are leading a renewed Space Race. Universal healthcare came in many cases by nationalising a pre-existing private healthcare system. The question -again, a factual one - we should be asking is whether a deregulated market in healthcare would do better than a public universal healthcare system. Some people think the answer is obvious: a) The US' system is terrible and it's private, and the NHS is great and it's public ([That's a bad critique](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/03/24/nnlibertarianfaq/#healthcare)) b) Most economists see problems with private healthcare. We should then do a literature review to see what problems are, and what do we know works best.

I can be wrong in what I said, maybe capitalism is inherently unstable. But the point is that these debates do not require us to engage in difficult philosophical argumentation, but on (difficult) fact checking, paper reading, and History knowing.

[Political disagreements are mostly](http://www.owl232.net/irrationality.htm) about biases, facts, and, if we wish, moods, rather than fundamental values. Let us solve that first, gradually fix the world, and then come back to the question of what's the ultimate answer to ethics.
