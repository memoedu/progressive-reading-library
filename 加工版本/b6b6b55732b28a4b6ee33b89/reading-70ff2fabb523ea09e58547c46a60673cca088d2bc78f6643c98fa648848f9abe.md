最近，我一直在与许多人讨论如何 structure 和 staff teams。我反复提到的一个 framework，是将 team leadership 的 responsibility 分成几个不同的 categories。

这样做有几个好处。其一，它能帮助你更具体地理解 leading a team 涉及哪些事情；对于新的 managers，一份 exhaustive 的 job responsibilities 清单，有助于确保你在 tracking 所有这些 responsibilities。

不过，更重要的是，我们经常希望以某种方式在人们之间 split 这些 responsibilities。Team leadership 涵盖的事情极其广泛——从这篇文章的篇幅就能看出来——而想找到一个各方面都很出色的人，往往如同寻找独角兽。即使真能找到一个各方面都 good-enough 的人，他们通常也会在 1-2 个 areas 尤为突出，让他们 fully focus 于这些 areas，可能会更 higher-leverage。

下面是我经常使用的一种 breakdown：✻✻ 这些 categories 是思考如何 divide team leadership work 的良好起点，但现实当然更加模糊、更加杂乱，responsibility 不会完全沿着这些 axes 来 break down。另外，对某个 area 负责，并不意味着只有自己一个人 contributes；这些事情都能从其他人的 input 中获益良多！

## Categories（类别）

### Overall direction（总体方向）

一个 team 的 leadership 最重要的 responsibility，是确保这个 team 朝着正确的 direction 前进——也就是说，他们是否在朝着正确的 high level goal 努力，是否有 achievable plan 来实现它？Overall direction 通常会得到 team 内外许多人的 input，但对它负有最大 accountability 的人可能有所不同；参见下文的 [responsibility 的 Example divisions](https://www.benkuhn.net/leadcats/#example-divisions-of-responsibility)。

Overall direction 涉及的工作包括：

-   为 team 进行 mission、vision 或 charter 的 Setting
-   对 team 的 goals、plans 和 roadmap 进行 Choosing
-   对 team 可以承担的各种 projects 进行 Prioritizing
-   向 team members 和外部人员 Communicating 上述内容

做好这些事情最重要的 skill，是拥有 _good predictive models_（既包括 team 的 domain，也包括 organization）——因为 prioritization 归根结底是在问：“如果我们 pursue 这个 project，会有什么 impact？”善于向其他 stakeholders communicating 这些 predictive models，以及 team 的 priorities 和 goals，也很重要。

好的 team direction，大多表现为 team 持续 producing big wins。差的 direction 最常表现为_被意外打个措手不及_或_落在后面_——也就是说，mispredicting 哪些 work 将最为重要，并且做得太少，例如开始得太晚、under-hiring，或没有让人员成长到合适的 skillset 或 role。差的 direction 还有其他迹象，包括 team members 不理解自己为什么要做某件事；team 所做的 projects 只能 deliver little value；与 peer teams 存在 friction，或对 scope 争论不休；又或者，重要的 projects 在 teams 之间的缝隙中被遗漏。

### People management（人员管理）

People management 意味着对 team 中人员的 success 负责，最常见的事情包括：

-   对人员进行 Coaching，帮助他们在 careers 中 improve 和 grow
-   为其 team 进行 hiring processes 的 Designing 和 overseeing
-   对 performance expectations 进行 Setting 和 communicating，并据此 evaluating

日常工作中，这方面最重要的 responsibility 是 [recurring 1:1s（属于 coaching 的那种，而不是 status update 的那种）](https://www.benkuhn.net/11/)。其他事情包括 writing job descriptions、setting up interview loops、sourcing candidates、gathering feedback、writing performance reviews、帮助人员 navigate org policies、提供 career coaching，等等。

people management 最重要的 skill 是 _understanding people_——既包括传统意义上的“high EQ”，即富有 empathetic 特质、善于看到他人的 perspectives，也包括知道什么有助于在某个 domain 中实现 high performance（例如，是什么让一个人成为出色的 engineer 或 researcher）。善于以 compassionate 但 firm 的方式进行 tricky conversations，也很重要。

people management 的主要 outcome，是 team 中的人员是否 high-performing、是否快乐。拥有最佳 people management 的 Teams 会 hire 出色的人，对任何不起作用的事情迅速给予 feedback，迅速帮助他们 course-correct，随着时间推移帮助他们 grow 自己的 impact，并且总体上帮助他们在工作中度过愉快的时光。糟糕的 people management，则表现为人员长期 underperforming 或 low morale。

这里一个常见的问题是，people manager 需要有多 technical。对此，人们的看法差异很大。我通常建议的 bar 是：people manager 不需要拥有 team 中最深的 technical depth，但其 depth 必须足够，使他们能够跟上大多数 discussions 而不拖慢进程，在大多数 debates 中无需依赖信任便能理解谁是正确的，并且总体上能够轻松地 stay oriented。

people manager 有责任确保其 reports 在需要时得到 mentorship 和 feedback，但他们_不_需要亲自成为提供 mentorship 或 feedback 的主要人员。通常，domain-specific mentorship 来自负责 [technical direction](https://www.benkuhn.net/leadcats/#technical-leadership) 的人，但也可以来自 team 中其他任何 senior 人员，或者较少见地，来自 org 中的其他地方。

### Project management（项目管理）

Project management 意味着确保 team _executes well_：也就是说，每个人都朝着 team 的 top priorities 进行 works efficiently，同时保持 unblocked，并对其他正在发生的事情保持 situationally aware。在短期内，它是 team 的 productivity 的 key determinant。

日常的 project management 是这样的：

-   对 team 的“operating cadence”进行 Setting 和 running，也就是那套帮助完成 work 的 recurring meeting/rituals（standups、planning/prioritization meetings、retrospectives 等）
-   弄清如何在 team 内 split up work，将其 delegating 给实际执行的人，并 monitoring progress，以确保它保持 unblocked
-   通过 making work visible 来 Keeping team oriented，例如让 Slack channels 保持 organized、maintaining 一个 task tracker，等等
-   担任 team 与 company 其余部分之间的 point of contact——来回 conveying important updates，等等。

Project management 不只是 administrative；要做好它，需要相当多的 domain expertise（以便 follow project discussions、understand status updates、track dependencies 等）。除此之外，做到 organized 和 detail-oriented，以及拥有关于人的良好 mental models，也都有帮助（谁会擅长哪些 types of work？哪些 coordination rituals 对这个 team 有帮助？）。

好的 project management 几乎不会被察觉——只会让人觉得“一切都在顺畅运转”。当它做得不好时，就会更加明显，主要表现为 _inefficient work_：人们被 blocked，由于 priority thrash 而频繁 context-switching，因为所做的 project 是 bad fit 而无所适从，因为不理解 root goal 而把 work 做错，错过出现在错误 Slack channel 中的 important information，等等。

当 teams 变大时，project management 是最容易 delegate 和 split up 的领域之一。例如，当 Anthropic 的 inference team 达到 10+ 人时，我们将其 split up 为多个专注于不同领域的“pods”，每个 pod 都有一位“pod lead”，负责该 pod 的 project management。

### Technical leadership（技术领导）

Technical leadership 意味着对 team 的 technical work 的 quality 负责。在 integrating 多种 technical skillsets 的 complex orgs 中，你可以认为 teams 往往在每一种 skillset 上都需要一定程度的 tech leadership——例如，Anthropic 的 research teams 既需要 research，也需要 engineering leadership，尽管具体 balance 因 team 而异。

具体 work 包括：

-   进行 Setting technical direction（例如，某个 topic 的 research agenda，或某个 system 的 architecture）
-   对照该 direction 进行 Reviewing execution（reviewing experimental designs 和 results、technical design docs、code review 等）
-   为 team 中的 ICs 提供其他 technical mentorship，例如 1:1s、pairing 等。
-   通常还包括一定量的 individual execution，不过这会因 technical lead 有多忙而有所不同。

由于亲自从事 execution 所带来的 detailed context 和 feedback loops 对 technical leadership 大有裨益，tech leads 是 individual contributors 的情况相当常见。†† 不过值得注意的是，technical leadership 并不是作为 individual contributor 实现 high impact 的唯一途径！关于其他 archetypes 的 software-engineering-specific 剖析，可参阅 Will Larson 关于 [Staff engineer archetypes](https://staffeng.com/guides/staff-archetypes/) 的文章；其中的内容部分适用于其他 technical domains，但并非全部适用。在实践中，许多 teams 的 surface area 足够广，因此最终会在不同 domains 中有多位 technical leads——或者按 project 进行“vertically”划分，或者按 skillset 进行“horizontally”划分，或者采用二者的某种组合。

也许显而易见，对 tech lead 而言最重要的 skill 是 domain expertise。Technical communication 大概是其次最重要的，也是将这一 senior IC archetype 与其他 archetypes 区分开来的因素。

当 technical leadership 做得不好时，最常见的表现是 accumulating debt，或产生其他会 slows down execution 的 friction：bogus research results、uninformative experiments、creaky systems、frequent outages 等。

## responsibility 的 divisions 示例

下面是几个不同的现实例子，说明这些 responsibilities 可以如何 divided up。‡‡ 这些例子远非 exhaustive，而且仍然只是 oversimplified schematic——对于任何一个 team，具体 division 都取决于相关 leaders 的 skillsets，并且会存在大量 fuzziness 和 overlap！

### “tech lead manager”这一角色

当一家新 company 首次引入 technical managers 时，通常会把最强的 technical person（或多个人）调入 management role，并期望他们履行全部四项 responsibilities。有些人在这样的 roles 中表现得很好，但更常见的情况是，新 manager 并不擅长其中一项或多项 responsibilities——最常见的是 people management——而且由于还有许多其他事情需要负责，很难有所改善。（延伸阅读：[Tech Lead Management roles 是一个陷阱](https://lethain.com/tech-lead-managers/)）

虽然 TLM roles 存在一些陷阱，但并非不可能做好。以下几个 protective factors 能让它们更有可能成功：

-   team 是 small 或 low-pressure 的，因此他们有更多时间专注于自己的 growth areas
-   TLM 有一位 highly engaged manager-of-managers，能在他们需要帮助的地方提供支持
-   team 的 domain 足够 simple，或 ICs 足够 senior，因此对 technical oversight 的需求是 limited 的
-   TLM 在 tech leadership 和 people management 两方面都有扎实的既往 experience
-   TLM 愿意长时间工作（当然，这只是 descriptive，而非 normative）

### Engineering manager / tech lead（工程经理 / 技术负责人）

这种 split 在较大的 tech companies 中很常见，由 EM 负责 overall direction、people 和 project management，由 TL 负责 technical leadership（并且也可能 contributing to overall direction）。这里的“Tech lead”不一定非得是 formal title，有时一个 team 会在不同领域有多位 tech leads。

在 Anthropic，我们的 inference team 就是一个很好的例子，其中的 managers 自己并不怎么 set technical direction，而是专注于 hiring、organizing、coaching、establishing priorities，以及与 team 的众多 client teams 之间[充当纽带，即 being glue](https://noidea.dog/glue)。由于这个 domain 是 highly complex 的，而 team 是 senior-heavy 的，service 不同部分的 tech leadership 由多位不同的 ICs 提供（model implementation、server architecture、request scheduling、capacity management 等）。

### Product manager / tech lead（产品经理 / 技术负责人）

这是一个较少见的 split 的例子。在 [Wave](https://www.wave.com/en/)，我们采用了与上文所述 EM/TL split 类似的 division，但 team managers（我们称他们为 Product Managers，尽管这对于 PM role 而言是一种有些 atypical 的形态）往往具有 non-technical backgrounds。

我们期望 PMs 担任某个 product area（例如我们的 bill payment product、agent network 等）的“mini CEO”，并拥有相当广泛的 autonomy，可以在该 area 内开展工作。由于“mini CEO”这个 role 涉及许多其他 competencies，我们认为他们不必同时像普通 engineering manager 可能需要的那样 technical。

虽然并不常见，但这种做法效果很好，主要有以下几个原因：

-   Wave 在 technically 方面相对 simple，但从 operational 和 product 的角度来看却很 complex，因此，对于 accountable 于 overall direction 的人而言，technical skill 相对没那么重要，而 operational/product skill 则相对更重要。
-   我们为 PM role hired 了非常 strong 的人，主要通过在内部从其他 teams transferring 最 strong 的人来实现。
-   每个 team 的 PM 和 TL 都有非常 strong 的 working relationship，因此他们能够就 velocity 与 tech quality 之间的 tradeoffs 等问题 communicate effectively，而最终不会通过诸如 PM fiat 的方式 resolving 这些问题。

值得注意的是，这违背了我上文提到的建议，即 people managers 应当具备合理程度的 technical 能力。这种做法之所以奏效，主要是因为对于 people management 中需要 technical context 的部分，我们能够高度依靠 tech leads。Tech lead 是一个 formal role，并有向一位 engineering manager-of-managers 进行 secondary reporting 的安排；而且，尽管 PMs 最终 responsible 于 people management，TL 也在其中发挥了 major role。他们两人都会与每位 team member 进行 1:1s，performance reviews 则由 PM 和 TL 共同 co-written。

### People manager / research lead（人员经理 / 研究负责人）

Anthropic 有几个将 people management 与 research leadership 分开的例子；持续时间最长的一个是在我们的 Interpretability team，其中 Chris Olah owned overall direction 和 technical leadership，而 Shan Carter owned people 和 project management。（如今 Interpretability 已有多个 sub-teams，这种安排也发生了一些变化。）

在这种 split 中，与 engineering team 的 EM/TL split 不同，让 research lead 对 overall direction 承担 accountable 的责任更为合理，因为它非常依赖于针对应当 pursue 哪个 research direction 所作的 high-context intuitive judgment calls（例如，大力押注 [superposition hypothesis](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)，从而带来了几项 [major](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/index.html) [results](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html)）。许多（尽管并非所有！）engineering teams 的 prioritization 对这种 highly technical judgment call 的依赖较少。

这个例子很有意思，因为在这种 setup 中，people manager 并不（主要）responsible 于 overall direction。它与某些 tech companies 的 CTO / VP Engineering split 有些 analogous：CTO responsible 于 overall direction，但大部分 people-leadership responsibility 由向其 reports 的 VPE 承担。

Recently I’ve been having a lot of conversations about how to structure and staff teams. One framework I’ve referenced repeatedly is to break down team leadership into a few different categories of responsibility.

This is useful for a couple reasons. One is that it helps you get more concrete about what leading a team involves; for new managers, having an exhaustive list of job responsibilities is helpful to make sure you’re tracking all of them.

More importantly, though, we often want to somehow split these responsibilities between people. Team leadership covers a huge array of things—as you can see from how long this post is—and trying to find someone who can be great at all of them is often a unicorn hunt. Even if you do find someone good-enough at all of them, they usually spike in 1-2 areas, and it might be higher-leverage for them to fully focus on those.

Here’s a breakdown I use a lot:✻✻ These categories are a good starting point for figuring out how to divide team leadership work, but of course reality is fuzzier and messier, and responsibility won't break down exactly along these axes. Plus, being responsible for an area doesn’t mean being the only person that contributes; all of these benefit a lot from input from other people!

## Categories

### Overall direction

The most important responsibility a team’s leadership is to ensure that the team is headed in the right direction—that is, are they working towards the right high level goal and do they have an achievable plan to get there? Overall direction tends to get input from many people inside and outside a team, but who is most accountable for it can vary; see [Example divisions of responsibility](https://www.benkuhn.net/leadcats/#example-divisions-of-responsibility) below.

Overall direction involves working on things like:

-   Setting the team’s mission, vision, or charter
-   Choosing the team’s goals, plans and roadmap
-   Prioritizing the various different projects the team could take on
-   Communicating the above, both to team members and to people outside

The most important skill for getting this right is having _good predictive models_ (of both the team’s domain and the organization)—since prioritization is ultimately a question about “what will be the impact if we pursue this project.” Being great at communicating those predictive models, and the team’s priorities and goals, to other stakeholders is also important.

Good team direction mostly looks like the team producing a steady stream of big wins. Poor direction most commonly manifests as _getting caught by surprise_ or _falling behind_—that is, mispredicting what work will be most important and doing too little of it, for example by starting too late, under-hiring, or not growing people into the right skillset or role. Other signs of poor direction include team members not understanding why they’re working on something; the team working on projects that deliver little value; friction with peer teams or arguments about scope; or important projects falling through the cracks between teams.

### People management

People management means being responsible for the success of the people on the team, most commonly including things like:

-   Coaching people to improve and grow in their careers
-   Designing and overseeing hiring processes for their team
-   Setting and communicating performance expectations and evaluating against them

Day to day, the most important responsibility here is [recurring 1:1s (the coaching kind, not the status update kind)](https://www.benkuhn.net/11/). Others include writing job descriptions, setting up interview loops, sourcing candidates, gathering feedback, writing performance reviews, helping people navigate org policies, giving career coaching, etc.

The most important skill for people management is _understanding people_—both in the traditional “high EQ” sense of being empathetic and good at seeing others’ perspectives, but also in the sense of knowing what contributes to high performance in a domain (e.g. what makes someone a great engineer or researcher). It’s also important to be good at having tricky conversations in a compassionate but firm way.

The main outcome of people management is whether people on the team are high-performing and happy. Teams with the best people management hire great people, give them fast feedback on anything that’s not working, course-correct them quickly, help them grow their impact over time, and generally help them have a great time at work. Bad people management looks like people chronically underperforming or having low morale.

A common question here is how technical a people manager needs to be. Opinions vary widely. The bar I typically suggest is that the people manager doesn’t need to have the most technical depth on the team, but they need enough depth that they can follow most discussions without slowing them down, understand who’s correct in most debates without needing to rely on trust, and generally stay oriented easily.

The people manager is responsible for making sure their reports get mentorship and feedback if needed, but they _don’t_ need to be the primary person doing the mentorship or feedback themselves. Often, domain-specific mentorship comes from whoever is responsible for [technical direction](https://www.benkuhn.net/leadcats/#technical-leadership), but it can also come from anyone else senior on the team, or less commonly, somewhere else in the org.

### Project management

Project management means making sure the team _executes well_: i.e., that everyone works efficiently towards the team’s top priorities while staying unblocked and situationally aware of what else is going on. In the short run, it’s the key determinant of a team’s productivity.

Day to day, project management looks like:

-   Setting and running the team’s “operating cadence,” i.e. the set of recurring meeting/rituals that help get work done (standups, planning/prioritization meetings, retrospectives, etc.)
-   Figuring out how to split up work across the team, delegating it to whoever will do it, and monitoring progress to make sure it stays unblocked
-   Keeping the team oriented by making work visible, through e.g. keeping Slack channels organized, maintaining a task tracker, and so on
-   Being the point of contact between the team and the rest of the company—conveying important updates back and forth, etc.

Project management isn’t just administrative; doing it well requires a significant amount of domain expertise (to follow project discussions, understand status updates, track dependencies, etc.). Beyond that, it’s helpful to be organized and detail-oriented, and to have good mental models of people (who will be good at what types of work? What kinds of coordination rituals are helpful for this team?).

Good project management is barely visible—it just feels like “things humming along.” It’s more visible when it’s going badly, which mostly manifests as _inefficient work_: people being blocked, context-switching frequently due to priority thrash, flailing around because they’re working on a project that’s a bad fit, doing their work wrong because they don’t understand the root goal, missing out on important information that was in the wrong Slack channel, and so on.

When teams get big, project management is one of the areas that’s easiest to delegate and split up. For example, when Anthropic’s inference team got up to 10+ people, we split it up into multiple “pods” focused on different areas, where each pod had a “pod lead” that was responsible for that pod’s project management.

### Technical leadership

Technical leadership means being responsible for the quality of a team’s technical work. In complex orgs integrating multiple technical skillsets, you can think of teams as often needing some amount of tech leadership in each one—for example, research teams at Anthropic need both research and engineering leadership, although the exact balance varies by team.

Specific work includes:

-   Setting technical direction (e.g. the research agenda for a topic, or the architecture of a system)
-   Reviewing execution against that direction (reviewing experimental designs and results, technical design docs, code review, etc.)
-   Other technical mentorship of ICs on the team, e.g. 1:1s, pairing, etc.
-   Often some amount of individual execution, though this can vary depending on how busy the technical lead is.

Because technical leadership benefits a lot from the detailed context and feedback loops of working on execution yourself, it’s fairly common for tech leads to be individual contributors.†† It’s worth noting though that technical leadership is not the only way to achieve high impact as an individual contributor! For a software-engineering-specific unpacking of other archetypes, which translates partly but not entirely to other technical domains, see Will Larson’s post on [Staff engineer archetypes](https://staffeng.com/guides/staff-archetypes/). In practice, many teams have a wide enough surface area that they end up with multiple technical leads in different domains—split either “vertically” by project, “horizontally” by skillset, or some combination of the two.

Perhaps obviously, the most important skill for a tech lead is domain expertise. Technical communication is probably next most important, and what separates this archetype of senior IC from others.

When technical leadership isn’t going well, it most often manifests as accumulating debt or other friction that slows down execution: bogus research results, uninformative experiments, creaky systems, frequent outages, etc.

## Example divisions of responsibility

Here are a few different real-world examples of how these responsibilities can be divided up.‡‡ These are extremely non-exhaustive, and are still an oversimplified schematic—on any given team, the exact division will depend on the skillsets of the leaders involved, and there will be lots of fuzziness and overlap!

### The “tech lead manager”

When a new company introduces their first technical managers, they often do it by moving their strongest technical person (or people) into a management role and expecting them to fulfill all four responsibilities. Some people do just fine in such roles, but more commonly, the new manager isn’t great at one or more of the responsibilities—most often people management—and struggles to improve due to the number of other things they’re responsible for. (Further reading: [Tech Lead Management roles are a trap](https://lethain.com/tech-lead-managers/))

Although TLM roles have some pitfalls, they’re not impossible. Here are a few protective factors that make them more likely to succeed:

-   the team is small or low-pressure so that they have more time to focus on their growth areas
-   the TLM has a highly engaged manager-of-managers who can support them where they need help
-   the team’s domain is simple enough, or the ICs senior enough, that the need for technical oversight is limited
-   the TLM has strong prior experience in both tech leadership and people management
-   the TLM is up for working a large number of hours (this is, of course, descriptive and not normative)

### Engineering manager / tech lead

This type of split is common in larger tech companies, with the EM responsible for overall direction, people and project management, and the TL responsible for technical leadership (and potentially also contributing to overall direction). “Tech lead” doesn’t have to be a formal title here, and sometimes a team will have multiple tech leads in different areas.

At Anthropic, a good example of this is our inference team, where the managers don’t set much technical direction themselves, and instead are focused on hiring, organizing, coaching, establishing priorities, and [being glue](https://noidea.dog/glue) with the team’s many many client teams. Since the domain is highly complex and the team is senior-heavy, tech leadership is provided by multiple different ICs for different parts of the service (model implementation, server architecture, request scheduling, capacity management, etc.).

### Product manager / tech lead

This is an example of a less-common split. At [Wave](https://www.wave.com/en/), we used a division similar to the EM/TL split described above, but the team managers (which we called Product Managers, although it was a somewhat atypical shape for a PM role) often came from non-technical backgrounds.

PMs were expected to act as the “mini CEO” of a product area (e.g. our bill payment product, our agent network, etc.) with fairly broad autonomy to work within that area. Because the “mini CEO” role involved a bunch of other competencies, we decided they didn’t also need to be as technical as a normal engineering manager might.

Although unusual, this worked well for a couple main reasons:

-   Wave was relatively technically simple, but complex from an operational and product perspective, so for the person accountable for overall direction, technical skill was relatively less important and operational/product skill relatively more so.
-   We hired very strong people into the PM role, mostly by transferring the strongest people internally from other teams.
-   Each team’s PM and TL had a very strong working relationship, such that they were able to communicate effectively about things like tradeoffs between velocity and tech quality, and didn’t end up resolving those via e.g. PM fiat.

Notably, this broke the suggestion I mentioned above that people managers should be reasonably technical. This worked mostly because we were able to lean heavily on tech leads for the parts of people management that required technical context. Tech lead was a formal role, with secondary reporting into an engineering manager-of-managers; and while PMs were ultimately responsible for people management, the TL played a major role as well. Both of them would have 1:1s with each team member, and performance reviews would be co-written between the PM and the TL.

### People manager / research lead

Anthropic has a few examples of splitting people management from research leadership; the longest-running one is on our Interpretability team, where Chris Olah owned overall direction and technical leadership, and Shan Carter owned people and project management. (This has changed a bit now that Interpretability has multiple sub-teams.)

In this split, unlike an EM/TL split on an engineering team, it made more sense for the research lead to be accountable for overall direction because it depended very heavily on high-context intuitive judgment calls about which research direction to pursue (e.g. betting heavily on the [superposition hypothesis](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html), which led to several [major](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/index.html) [results](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html)). Many (though not all!) engineering teams’ prioritization depends less on this kind of highly technical judgment call.

This is interesting as an example of a setup where the people manager wasn’t (primarily) responsible for overall direction. It’s somewhat analogous to the CTO / VP Engineering split in some tech companies, where the CTO is responsible for overall direction but most people-leadership responsibility lies with the VPE who reports to them.
