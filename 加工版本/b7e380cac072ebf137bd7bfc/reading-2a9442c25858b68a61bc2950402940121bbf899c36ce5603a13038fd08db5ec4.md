# Why and how to start a startup serving emerging markets

---

## 正文

[Wave](https://www.wave.com/) 是一家 for-profit、venture backed startup，正在 Africa 的多个国家内 building cheap、instant money transfer。自 2018 年 launching 以来，我们已经成为 Senegal 遥遥领先的最大 money transfer service，每月有超过 50% 的 adult population 使用，迄今已为我们的 users saving 超过 \$100 million。我们的 roadmap 是 expand 至整个 Africa，并 launch 我们的 users 所需的其他每一种 financial service；我们预计，如果成功，这将产生 [orders-of-magnitude bigger impact](https://www.jefftk.com/p/estimating-the-value-of-mobile-money)。

Wave 的 mission 是改善世界，而不是赚钱。尽管如此，我们的 operate 方式更像一家 tech company，而不是 social enterprise。我们的 investors 是试图获得 high return 的 venture capitalists，他们对我们的 growth rate 和 unit economics 采用与任何 developed-world startup 相同的 standards。这或许看起来是个坏处（直接 optimize for impact，难道不比承受 investors 要求赚钱的压力更容易吗？），但对我们而言，这实际上从两个方面 _increased_ 了我们的 impact。首先，grow quickly 的压力迫使我们把 product 做得更好并 scale faster，从而让我们帮助更多的人，也让每个人受益更多。其次，由于按照 for-profit investors 的 standards 来看，我们做得非常好，因此我们能够比 nonprofit 或 social enterprise raise 多得多的 money。

在我看来，Wave 的路径——把 US startup playbook importing 到 developing countries——在 ex ante 就可以预见是 high-expected-impact 的。首先，starting a company 通常具有 high expected impact：innovation 的 social benefit [通常是 private return 的 large multiple](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10433)。Serving 一个 emerging market 又 adds 了一个 multiplier，因为你可以着手解决的 problems 要严重得多。（为某人 Providing \$5 of value means a lot more when \$5 就是他们一天的工资！）最后，developing countries 有更多可供 companies 着手处理的 low-hanging fruit，因为 skilled entrepreneurs 的 supply 更小。

* * *

因此，On the margin，更多拥有在 developed world 工作经验的 altruists 应该尝试这条路径。我觉得可以放心这么说，因为目前似乎很少有人在尝试，无论他们是否 altruistic。

这令人意外，因为许多 developing countries 如今已经具备 support tech companies 的 infrastructure。在 Dakar、Nairobi、Addis 或 Lagos 这样的大城市，有 mostly-reliable electricity、decent internet、high smartphone penetration、driveable roads 等等。但利用这些条件的优秀 startups 却不多。（据我们那些最关注 Africa 的 investors 所说，Wave 是其中遥遥领先、最有前景的一家。）

为什么这个 space 没有更多参与者？我猜，这是因为 creating 一款优秀的 product 需要两样东西：maniacally perfectionist，以及 deeply understanding 你的 users。要做到 maniacally perfectionist，你需要置身于一种具有极高 product standards 的文化之中（例如 Silicon Valley）。要 understand 你在 Africa 的 users，你需要住在 Africa。这两个 groups 的 intersection 几乎没有人，因为大多数能够住在 Silicon Valley 的人，都远不愿意搬到比如沙漠腹地的一处 former tank base（我的许多同事曾在那里住了多年）。

理解 Wave 的一种方式，是把它看成 high standards 的 importer。例如，在 Africa 的大多数 mobile money systems 中，如果你试图进行 large withdrawal，你的 local agent 可能没有足够的 cash——他们可能需要几小时甚至几天才能凑齐钱。在 Wave，我们意识到这会让 users 难过，因此开始 predicting 我们的 agents 将需要多少 cash，并与他们合作，确保他们绝不会用尽 cash。这给我们带来了大量额外工作和 risk，却促成了 traders 的 massive adoption——由于 funds 可以从 Wave instantly available，他们的 turn over inventory 速度往往真的能达到两倍。Wave beats 其他 mobile money systems 的每一个方面，都遵循了类似的 playbook：发现一个 problem，然后即使它看起来极其困难，也要_竭尽全力去 solve 它_。

“local context 加 high standards”这一 theory 提出了一种简单（但并不容易！）的 strategy，用来 build 一家帮助 global poor 的 high-quality business：

1.  搬到一个 developing country，去 understand 你未来的 users。
2.  学习 startup playbook（例如参加 Y Combinator）。
3.  Start 一家 users 位于你居住地的 business。

本文其余部分将详细阐述这一 strategy。我对 location 的讨论将主要限于 sub-Saharan Africa，因为那是我最熟悉的地区，但我猜它在其他 developing countries 也会奏效。

* * *

这一 strategy 中第一个重要的 decision 是搬到哪里。在这里，我会将最重要的 criteria（大致）排序如下：

1.  language barrier 要 Minimal，以便你能 understand 你的 users 和同事
2.  infrastructure 要足够好，能够 support 一家 tech startup
3.  是你喜欢居住的地方，以 minimize 你的 burnout risk
4.  institutions 要足够 Strong，使你的 expropriation/corruption risks 保持 low
5.  具有 large addressable market

对于一个说英语的人，排在前面的几个地方大概会是 Lagos、Accra、Nairobi、Kampala 或 Dar es Salaam（不过我对此并没有太大把握，因为这些地方我都没住过）。如果你正在考虑这一 strategy，我建议把它们都研究一下，实地去其中几个地方看看，再选择你最向往的那个。

在尝试 start a business 之前，你大概应该先花些时间单纯地学习。在这期间，先在别处工作会有帮助——理想情况下，那个地方能让你接触到大多数人的日常生活，而不是让你与之隔绝。为此，NGO 或“voluntourism”类型的工作实际上可能有用。例如，Wave 的 CEO Drew 在 Tanzania 经营了一家销售手推车的 nonprofit 数年，亲身经历了试图向自己的 business 汇款的痛苦，之后才开始思考 money transfer。

* * *

第二个重要的 decision 是做什么。如果你花几个月时间，与许多人谈论他们的 problems，那么在这方面，你会比我更像一个 expert——我在 Africa 住过几年，但当时我已经在为 Wave 工作，所以没有特别努力地寻找其他 ideas。话虽如此，我还是会给出一些自己的猜想。

developing world 中最大的 business gaps，出现在 developed world 所 built 的 products 无法很好地 generalize 到新 contexts 的地方。例如，credit/debit cards 在 developing countries 几乎不存在，因为大多数 customers 没有 bank accounts，而大多数 merchants 买不起 card terminal。你还会发现许多其他这类 gap 的例子。

下面是我注意到的几个。它们偏向 financial services，但应该能让你大致了解这些 problems 的类型。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/emco/#fn:1)

-   大多数人没有 computers，因此 developed world 的许多 computer-based business software 应该被 rebuilt，以便在 smartphones 上 work well。例如，根据 Wave 的经验，大多数 small businesses 不 keep 任何形式的 financial records。一款 easy-to-use mobile cash register 或 bookkeeping system，可以帮助 small businesses 更 effectively 地 run。

-   Africa 的 chain stores 似乎少得多，而同时也在 developed world operate 的尤其少。我不确定这是为什么，但我猜，在 Africa operating successfully 所需的 playbook 非常不同，而且无法很好地 transfer。不过，chain stores 仍然应该在 economies of scale 和 branding 方面拥有 advantages。

-   在 developing countries，处于 informally employed 或 self-employed 状态的人多得多。这意味着他们有许多 developed-world tech companies 尚未 solve 的 problems，例如每个月差别很大的 income，或需要与同一 line of business 中的其他 informal entrepreneurs coordinate。帮助应对这些 challenges 的 Products，将帮助极其庞大的一群人。

-   在 US 依赖 custom hardware 的东西，可以被 rebuilt，改用 cheaper/no hardware 加一部 smartphone——例如，ATM 或 cash register 可以通过 Bluetooth 与 phone 通信，而不是使用自己的 screen。

-   developed world 中的许多 systems（例如 shipping）依赖 well-known street names 和 addresses，而许多 African cities 并没有这些。但这些 systems 也是在 GPS 尚未 ubiquitous 时 built 的。“Logistics，但采用 GPS”大概就能形成好几个不同的 company ideas。

-   如今的 e-commerce 是 built 在许多在 Africa 并不成立的 assumptions 之上的，例如上面提到的 street names，以及 easy electronic payments、functional postal system、相对 easy credit、well-known large-scale brands 等等。一家 African e-commerce company Jumia 最近 went public 了，但他们似乎在 product 方面 executing 得并不特别好。我认为，应该有可能做得更好。

-   在我住过的许多城市，要找到好的 skilled tradespeople（plumbers、electricians 等）极其困难。我猜，US 通过 high-quality vocational school 与 occupational licensing 的某种组合 solves 了这个问题，而在许多 developing countries，这两者都不存在（或没有得到 enforced）。

-   大多数“API for X”companies 并不 serve developing countries，或只是事后才想到它们。例如，[Africa’s Talking](https://africastalking.com/) 就是“[Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/)，但面向 Africa”，而在 Wave，我们已经在几个 Africa’s Talking 尚未 operate 的国家 built 了我们自己的 micro-Twilio。这些 businesses 依赖 strong tech ecosystem，因此我还不确定它们有多 viable，但它们也能 enable 那一类 ecosystem，所以如果确实能奏效，就会尤其 beneficial！

* * *

最后，谈几点“Silicon Valley startup playbook”在 developing-country context 中需要如何改变的想法。

如果你不是当地人，你会犯多得多的错误，因为你并不 understand 你的 users 或你的 context。当你的 users 不识字，或者你必须花两周时间追着一家 telecom，才能获得 send text messages 的 permission 时，build 一款好的 product 要困难得多。这意味着，迅速从错误中学习更加重要，而且你应该预期，在 finding product/market fit 之前，需要 iterate 很长时间。

即使你身处一个 Anglophone country，你也需要在 local 与 tech-startup norms 之间做到“bilingual”。在 Wave，我们的 internal culture 强调 honesty、transparency 和 autonomy，这与典型的、比如 Senegalese work environment 非常不同。我们一些最重要的 hires，就是那些特别擅长在两者之间“code-switching”的人——他们帮助我们与 local partners 合作得顺畅得多，也对我们的 product 和 strategy 产生了巨大影响。

如果你所在的国家具有 weaker institutions，那么 corruption 或 expropriation 损害你的 business 的 risk 就高得多。与 developed world 相比，要在 government 或 competitors 面前“悄然行事、不引起注意”要困难得多，而且他们也很可能更不择手段。要比你认为有必要的时间更早，开始着手 mitigating 这一 risk（通过拥有 powerful、well-connected investors 或 business partners）。

## 原文

[Wave](https://www.wave.com/) is a for-profit, venture backed startup building cheap, instant money transfer within various countries in Africa. Since launching in 2018, we’ve become by far the biggest money transfer service in Senegal, used by over 50% of the adult population every month, and saving our users over $100 million so far. Our roadmap is to expand throughout Africa and launch every other type of financial service our users need, which we expect to have an [orders-of-magnitude bigger impact](https://www.jefftk.com/p/estimating-the-value-of-mobile-money) if we succeed.

Wave’s mission is to improve the world, not to make money. Despite that, we operate more like a tech company than a social enterprise. Our investors are venture capitalists trying to make a high return, and they hold us to the same standards of growth rate and unit economics as any developed-world startup. This might seem like a downside (surely it would be easier to directly optimize for impact rather than have pressure from investors to make money?), but for us it’s actually _increased_ our impact in two ways. First, the pressure to grow quickly forces us to make our product better and scale faster, so we help more people by a larger amount. Second, since we’ve done really well by for-profit investors’ standards, we can raise much more money than a nonprofit or social enterprise.

In my opinion, Wave’s path—importing the US startup playbook to developing countries—was predictably high-expected-impact ex ante. First, starting a company generally has high expected impact: the social benefit of innovation is [usually a large multiple of the private return](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10433). Serving an emerging market adds another multiplier, because the problems you could work on are much worse. (Providing someone $5 of value means a lot more when $5 is their day’s wages!) Finally, there’s more low-hanging fruit for companies to work on in developing countries, because the supply of skilled entrepreneurs is smaller.

* * *

On the margin, then, more altruists with experience working in the developed world should try this approach. I feel safe saying this because very few people (altruistic or not) currently seem to.

This is surprising, since lots of developing countries now have the infrastructure to support tech companies. In big cities like Dakar, Nairobi, Addis or Lagos, there’s mostly-reliable electricity, decent internet, high smartphone penetration, driveable roads and so on. But there aren’t many great startups taking advantage of them. (According to our investors who pay the most attention to Africa, Wave is by far the most promising.)

Why isn’t the space more crowded? I’d guess it’s because creating a great product requires two things: being maniacally perfectionist, and deeply understanding your users. To be maniacally perfectionist, you need to be immersed in a culture with really high product standards (for instance, Silicon Valley). To understand your users in Africa, you need to live in Africa. The intersection of these two groups is practically no one, because most people who could live in Silicon Valley would much rather not move to, say, a former tank base in the middle of the desert (where many of my coworkers lived for years).

One way to think of Wave is as an importer of high standards. For instance, in most mobile money systems in Africa, if you try to make a large withdrawal, your local agent may not have enough cash—it could take them hours or days to come up with the money. At Wave, we realized this made users sad, so we started predicting how much cash our agents would need and working with them to make sure they never ran out. This was a lot of extra work and risk for us, but led to massive adoption from traders—with funds available instantly from Wave, they could often turn over inventory literally twice as fast. Every way in which Wave beats other mobile money systems has followed a similar playbook: notice a problem, then _try really hard to solve it_ even when it seems extremely difficult.

The “local context plus high standards” theory suggests a simple (though not easy!) strategy to build a high-quality business that helps the global poor:

1.  Move to a developing country to understand your future users.
2.  Learn the startup playbook (for instance, by doing Y Combinator).
3.  Start a business whose users are in the place you live.

The remainder of this post fleshes out this strategy. I’ll restrict my discussion of location mostly to sub-Saharan Africa, since that’s the region I’m most familiar with, but I’d guess it would work in other developing countries too.

* * *

The first important decision in this strategy is where to move to. Here, I’d (roughly) rank the most important criteria as:

1.  Minimal language barrier so that you can understand your users and coworkers
2.  Good enough infrastructure to support a tech startup
3.  Somewhere you like living, to minimize your risk of burnout
4.  Strong enough institutions that your expropriation/corruption risks are low
5.  A large addressable market

For an English speaker, the top few places would probably be Lagos, Accra, Nairobi, Kampala or Dar es Salaam (though I’m not too confident in this because I’ve never lived in any of these). If you’re considering this strategy, I’d recommend researching them all, visiting multiple and choosing the one you’re most excited about.

You should probably spend some time just learning before trying to start a business. While you’re doing that, it will help to work somewhere else—ideally somewhere that will expose you, not insulate you, from what daily life is like for most people. For this, NGO or “voluntourism” type work might actually be useful. For instance, Drew, Wave’s CEO, started thinking about money transfer after running a nonprofit selling hand-carts in Tanzania for a few years, and experiencing the pain of trying to send money to his own business.

* * *

The second important decision is what to work on. If you talk to a lot of people about their problems for a few months, you’ll be more of an expert than me on this—I’ve lived in Africa for a couple years, but I was already working for Wave at the time so didn’t look too hard for other ideas. That said, I’ll give some of my guesses.

The biggest business gaps in the developing world are the ones where products built in the developed world don’t generalize well to new contexts. For instance, credit/debit cards barely exist in developing countries, because most customers don’t have bank accounts and most merchants can’t afford a card terminal. You’ll find many other instances of that kind of gap.

Here are a few I’ve noticed. They’re skewed towards financial services, but should give you a general idea of the types of problems.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/emco/#fn:1)

-   Most people don’t have computers, so lots of computer-based business software in the developed world should be rebuilt to work well on smartphones. In Wave’s experience, for instance, most small businesses don’t keep any sort of financial records. An easy-to-use mobile cash register or bookkeeping system could help small businesses run much more effectively.

-   There seem to be way fewer chain stores in Africa, and especially few that also operate in the developed world. I’m not sure why this is, but I’d guess that the playbook for operating successfully in Africa is very different and doesn’t transfer well. But chain stores still should have advantages in economies of scale and branding.

-   Many more people in developing countries are informally employed or self-employed. That means they have a lot of problems that developed-world tech companies have yet to solve, like income that’s very different from month to month, or needing to coordinate with other informal entrepreneurs in the same line of business. Products to help with these challenges would help a huge number of people.

-   Things that rely on custom hardware in the US could be rebuilt to use cheaper/no hardware plus a smartphone—for instance, an ATM or cash register could talk to a phone over Bluetooth instead of using its own screen.

-   Lots of systems in the developed world (shipping, for instance) rely on well-known street names and addresses, which don’t exist in many African cities. But they were also built before GPS was ubiquitous. “Logistics, but with GPS” is probably several different company ideas.

-   Today’s e-commerce is built on a lot of assumptions that are false in Africa, like street names mentioned above but also easy electronic payments, a functional postal system, relatively easy credit, well-known large-scale brands, etc. One African e-commerce company, Jumia, recently went public, but they don’t seem to be executing particularly well on product. I think it should be possible to do better.

-   It’s extremely hard to find good skilled tradespeople (plumbers, electricians, etc.) in many cities I’ve lived in. My guess is that the US solves this with some combination of high-quality vocational school and occupational licensing, neither of which exists (or is enforced) in many developing countries.

-   Most “API for X” companies don’t serve developing countries, or treat them as an afterthought. For instance, [Africa’s Talking](https://africastalking.com/) is “[Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/), but for Africa,” and at Wave we’ve built our own micro-Twilio in a few different countries where Africa’s Talking doesn’t operate yet. These businesses depend on a strong tech ecosystem, so I’m not sure how viable they are yet, but they also enable that type of ecosystem, so are especially beneficial if they do work!

* * *

Last, a few thoughts on how the “Silicon Valley startup playbook” needs to change in a developing-country context.

If you’re not local, you will make a lot more mistakes because you don’t understand your users or your context. It’s way harder to build a good product when your users can’t read, or when you have to spend two weeks chasing a telecom to get permission to send text messages. That means it’s even more important to learn quickly from your mistakes, and you should expect to iterate for a long time before finding product/market fit.

Even if you’re in an Anglophone country, you’ll need to be “bilingual” between local and tech-startup norms. At Wave, our internal culture emphasizes honesty, transparency and autonomy, which is very different from a typical, say, Senegalese work environment. Some of our most important hires were people who were exceptional at “code-switching” between the two—they helped us work much more smoothly with local partners, and made a huge difference to our product and strategy.

If you’re in a country with weaker institutions, the risk of corruption or expropriation hurting your business is much higher. Compared to the developed world, it’s much harder to “fly under the radar” of either the government or your competitors, and they will also probably be less scrupulous. Start working on mitigating this risk (by having powerful, well-connected investors or business partners) earlier than you think you need to.
