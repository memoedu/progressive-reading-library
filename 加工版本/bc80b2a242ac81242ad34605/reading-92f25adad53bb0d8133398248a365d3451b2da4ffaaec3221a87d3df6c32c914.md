# Exploration and exploitation

---

## 正文

我很喜欢有机会用 computer-science metaphors 来阐明现实生活中的 processes。去年，[我的 algorithms professor](http://mybiasedcoin.blogspot.com/) 向我介绍了其中一个我最喜欢的 metaphors。当时我与他见面，想听听他对于我应该选哪些课的看法。他建议将这件事看作一个 _exploration-exploitation trade-off_，这个 concept 来自一种用于 solving difficult optimization problems 的 technique。事实证明，这是一种有力而实用的看待事物的方式。

例如，假设你正在尝试 design 一个 airplane wing，而且希望它尽可能 aerodynamic。你会怎么做？你可以尝试直接 calculate optimal wing shape，但有很多 variables 会 affect aerodynamics，而且它们以 complicated 的方式 interact，所以这恐怕太难了。结果，最好的 solution 恰恰是显而易见的那个：尝试许多不同的 designs，再选出其中效果最好的那个。

难点在于，最初该如何 generating 那些要 test 的 designs。你可以只 generate random 的 designs，但那样几乎不可能找到好的——大多数 randomly generated wings 都会毫无用处。因此，你可能更想采用一个已有的 design，对它做出你知道会让它变得更好的 small modifications。经过足够多的 iterations，每次都 improves 这个 design incrementally，你就有望得到相当不错的结果。你可能已经猜到了，这就是 _exploitation_ 的部分。

但如果你只 exploit，就可能困在一个 _local optimum_ 中：这个 design 很平庸，然而任何 incremental change 都会让它变得更差。只有推翻一切，并对你的 design 进行 [radically rethinking](http://www.airliners.net/aviation-photos/photos/2/5/1/0657152.jpg)，你才可能找到更好的 wing。因此，不时从一个基本上 random 的 design 重新开始，或者做出一个你完全不知道其 effects 的 large change，去 _explore_ “optimization landscape”的另一个部分，是个好主意。

关键的 observation 是，由于 exploration 和 exploitation 都需要付出努力（这里指的是 computer time），它们之间存在 trade-off。如果你只 explore，最后就会得到一堆 misshapen、randomly-generated 的 kludges。但如果你只采用一个 design 并 exploit 它，就有可能困在一个 mediocre local optimum 中。因此，你需要在两者之间取得平衡。

另一个重要的 insight 是，如果你拥有的 computer time 是 finite 的，就应该在过程开始时更多地 explore，在结束时更多地 exploit。最后才 exploring 没有意义，因为即使你找到了好的 designs，也没有时间去 refine 你 generate 的那些 designs。另一方面，在你已经充分 explored、对 airplane wings 的 overall space 有了大致了解之前，你也不该开始深入 exploiting。

通过 analogy，这也很适用于人生选择。Explore 太多，你就会变成一个浅尝辄止的人；exploit 太多，你就会困在无聊或无用的事情中。因此，最好还是取得平衡：年轻时多 explore，随着年龄增长，逐渐转向 exploiting。

根据我的经验，我们不擅长前半部分，原因有几个。第一个是 _availability_——那些停止 exploring、开始 exploiting 的人会取得显而易见的成功，让人很想效仿。我发现，在大学里尤其如此：不论你在哪个领域，总会有某个[出类拔萃的人](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evan_O%27Dorney) 飞快地走在你前面，因为他们全身心投入 math（或者 violin、tennis，或任何你选择的活动）。放眼更广阔的世界，讲述比如 [Terence Tao](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_tao) 这样从两岁起就一直 exploiting math 的人的故事，也远比讲述 [Doug Melton](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_melton) 的故事有趣；后者先 majored in philosophy of science，之后才成为一位明星 biologist。但这些 prodigies 要么很幸运，这么早就找到了 fruitful territory——要么正在走向一个 local optimum。对于我们其他人来说，premature exploitation 虽然诱人，终究不是个好主意。

我们回避 exploration 的另一个主要原因是 _delayed payoffs_。大多数时候，exploration 都不奏效。你那架装着古怪 wings 的 airplane crashes。你做出来的陶罐凹凸不平。你的文章遭到鄙夷（或者更糟，根本无人理会）。而我们对失败的恐惧又让这个问题更加严重。exploration 只有偶尔才真正 pays off，所以我们从不会把 _exploration 本身_ 与好事发生联系起来——只会把其中少数成果与好事联系起来。

我认为，应对这种情况最好的办法，是有意识地以积极的方式看待 exploring。如果你发现自己因为新尝试进展不顺而感到难过或焦虑，就试着用对拥有 explore 机会的感激来替代这种情绪。提醒自己，并没有发生什么真正糟糕的事；没有老虎在吃你。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/exploration/#fn:1) 记住，从长远来看，它是 positive-expectation 的，即使这一次没有成功。回想一次你 explored 并且取得成功的经历。想象未来的自己正在做各种疯狂而有趣的事情，而这些事情都是你通过冒险发现的。也许可以给自己奖励 points，并持续记录 running total。无论你平时用什么小技巧让自己的大脑忽略 availability 和 delayed payoffs，都用上它们，好让自己学会像理应做到的那样热爱 exploration。

## 原文

I love it when I get to use computer-science metaphors to illuminate real-life processes. One of my favorite such metaphors was introduced to me by [my algorithms professor](http://mybiasedcoin.blogspot.com/) last year. I was meeting with him to get his thoughts on what classes I should take. His suggestion was to frame it as an _exploration-exploitation trade-off_, a concept that comes from a technique for solving difficult optimization problems. It turns out to be a powerful and helpful way to view things.

Suppose, for example, that you’re trying to design an airplane wing and you want it to be as aerodynamic as possible. How would you do this? You could try and calculate the optimal wing shape directly, but there are lots of variables that affect the aerodynamics, and they interact in complicated ways, so that’s probably too hard. It turns out the best solution is the obvious one: try a bunch of different designs and pick the one that turns out to work the best.

The hard part is generating the designs to test in the first place. You could just generate random ones, but you’d be very unlikely to find good ones—most randomly generated wings will be useless. So what you probably want to do is take an existing design and make small modifications that you know will make it better. After enough iterations, each of which improves the design incrementally, you’ll hopefully have something quite good. As you may have guessed, this is the _exploitation_ part.

But if you only exploit, you might get stuck in a _local optimum_: a design that’s mediocre, yet any incremental change makes it worse. You could only find a better wing by scrapping everything and [radically rethinking](http://www.airliners.net/aviation-photos/photos/2/5/1/0657152.jpg) your design. So it’s a good idea to sometimes start over from an essentially random design, or make a large change whose effects you have no idea about, to _explore_ a different part of the “optimization landscape.”

The key observation is that, since both exploration and exploitation take effort (in this case computer time), there’s a trade-off between them. If you only explore, you’ll end up with a bunch of misshapen, randomly-generated kludges. But if you only take one design and exploit it, you risk getting stuck in a mediocre local optimum. So you need to strike a balance between the two.

The other important insight is that if you have a finite amount of computer time, you should explore more at the beginning of your process and exploit more at the end. There’s no point in exploring at the end, because you’ll have no time to refine the designs that you generate, even if you find good ones. On the other hand, you don’t want to start exploiting in depth until you’ve explored enough to have a decent idea of the overall space of airplane wings.

This extends very well by analogy to making life choices. Explore too much, and you become a dilettante; exploit too much and you’ll be stuck doing something boring or useless. So again, it’s best to strike a balance: explore a lot when you’re young, and transition to exploiting as you age.

In my experience, we’re bad at the first part, for a couple of reasons. The first one is _availability_—people who stop exploring and start exploiting become visibly successful, and it’s tempting to follow their lead. I’ve found this especially true in college, where no matter what your field, there’s always some [brilliant talent](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evan_O%27Dorney) whizzing ahead of you because they live and breathe math (or violin or tennis or whatever your activity of choice is). And in the rest of the world, it’s much more interesting to tell the story of, say, [Terence Tao](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_tao), who’s been exploiting math since the age of two, than [Doug Melton](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_melton), who majored in philosophy of science before becoming a star biologist. But prodigies are lucky to have found fruitful territory so early—or they’re on their way to a local optimum. For the rest of us, premature exploitation is a seductive but ultimately bad idea.

The other big reason we avoid exploration is _delayed payoffs_. Most of the time, exploration doesn’t work. Your airplane with funky wings crashes. Your clay pots come out lumpy. Your essay is received with scorn (or worse, apathy). And our fear of failure makes the problem even worse. It’s only occasionally that exploration actually pays off, so we never associate _exploration itself_ with good things happening—only a few of its products.

The best way to combat this, I think, is to consciously frame exploring positively. If you notice yourself feeling sad or anxious that something new isn’t going well, try to replace it with gratefulness for the chance to explore. Remind yourself that nothing truly bad is happening; no tigers are eating you.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/exploration/#fn:1) Remember that it’s positive-expectation in the long run, even if this time it didn’t work out. Think back to a time you explored and it worked out. Visualize your future self doing all kinds of crazy, fun things you found by taking risks. Maybe award yourself points, and keep a running total. Use whatever kind of trick you use to get your brain to ignore availability and delayed payoffs, so that you can learn to love exploration as much as you should.
