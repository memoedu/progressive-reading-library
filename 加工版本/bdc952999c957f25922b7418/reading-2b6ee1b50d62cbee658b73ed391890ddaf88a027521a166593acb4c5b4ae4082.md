# 鸡尾酒派对创意｜Cocktail party ideas

---

## 正文

你不必参加聚会，也能看到这种 phenomenon 在现实中发生，不过，我经常在某些社交圈的聚会上看到一件奇怪的事：那里的人重视 intelligence 和 cleverness，却没有同样重视 on-the-ground knowledge 或 intellectual rigor。人们经常讨论那些常见的热门话题（最近我在多场聚会上见到的话题包括如何 build 一个 Google search 的 competitor，以及如何 solve high transit construction costs 这个 problem），解释为什么如今在这个 field 工作的人做错了，然后解释他们自己会怎么做。我偶尔也会有符合这种模式的良好交谈（对方在这个 field 拥有非常深厚的 expertise，多年来一直致力于改变这个 field），但更常见的模式是，某人只对一个 field 有 cocktail-party level knowledge，就会提出如何 fixed 这个 field 的想法。

当我参加的聚会被这种 superficial pseudo-technical discussion 主导时，询问人们为什么认为自己的 solutions 能够 solve 这个 field 中有价值的 problems，已经成了我的一个爱好。在追问 details 时，我发现在自己略有 knowledge 的 areas 中，人们[通常并不知道，为了 solve 他们试图 address 的 problem，需要先 solved 哪些 sub-problems，这使他们的 solution 毫无希望](https://danluu.com/sounds-easy/)。这样做过许多次后，我认为，其 root cause 通常在于，许多对某个 topic 只有 superficial understanding 的人，会假定这个 topic 的 complex 程度就等同于他们对它的 understanding，而没有意识到，对一个 topic 只知道一点，意味着他们缺乏对这个 topic 的 full complexity 的 understanding。

由于我经常与 programmers 一起参加聚会，这意味着我经常听到 [programmers retelling](https://twitter.com/nick_r_cameron/status/1346174149044043776) 他们对[另一个 field 的 cocktail-party level understanding](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1347269793578053632)（尽管上面的 search engine example 并非如此）。如果你想在网上看看类似评论的例子，经常可以在 programmers 讨论 "trad" engineering fields 时看到。我觉得有意思的一个 example 是[这条 Twitter thread，其中 Hillel Wayne 讨论了不了解 trad engineering 的 programmers，如何经常对 trad engineering 的实际情况抱有 incorrect ideas](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1162469763374673920)；其中许多回复来自对 trad engineering 几乎或完全没有 knowledge 的 programmers，他们接着用自己的 misconceptions 回复 Hillel。当 Hillel 完成他的 [crossover project](https://www.hillelwayne.com/tags/crossover-project/) 时，他采访了既在 trad engineering field 工作过、也在 software 领域工作过的人，[他收到了更多这样的评论](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1484268111687663620)。即使已经有人提醒，对一个 field 的 naive conceptions 很可能是 incorrect，许多人仍然忍不住，会立刻就自己基本一无所知的 field 发表意见。

总之，在 crossover project 中，Hillel 将确实在多个 fields 工作过的人的 perceptions，与 pop-programmer 对 trad engineering 的 perceptions 作了 compared。Hillel 给出的许多 examples 之一，是人们谈论 bridge building 时的情形；他指出，programmers 会说这样的话：

> 一个真正的 engineer 的世界所具有的 predictability，真令人羡慕。但我们的世界总是在 flux，其中的 laws of physics 每周都会 change。如果我们不 quickly adapt to 那些 unforeseen 的情况，唯一 foreseeable 的 event 就会是我们自己的 destruction。

以及

> 没有人会想到，在 construction 进行到一半时 moving bridge 的 starting 或 ending point。

但 Hillel 采访的一位 civil engineer 说，他们不得不 move 一座 bridge！当然，civil engineers move bridges 的频率，不如 programmers 处理 software 中的 changes 那么高；但是，如果你与真正从事这项工作的 civil engineers 交谈，就会发现，许多 civil engineers 经常在工作开始后处理 changing requirements，这与 programmers 在工作中不得不处理的事情，并没有 fundamentally different。在这两个 fields 都工作过，或者至少与另一个 field 的人交谈过的人，往往认为这两个 fields 的 engineers 面临的 concerns 都是 complex；但对某个 field 只有 cocktail-party level understanding 的人，却经常声称，自己没有从事的那个 field 是 simple 的，不像自己的 field。

我经常听 programmers 说，programming 就像“不得不在 plane 正 flying 时 build 它”，这隐含着一种论点：programming 比 designing 和 building 一架 plane 更 hard，因为 design 和 build planes 的人可以在 plane 开始 flying 之前完成这些工作[1](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:N)。但当然，designs airplanes 的人也完全可以说：“天哪，如果我能 build uptime 为 4 9s 的 planes，而且允许我的 plane 每周有 1 minute crash，导致所有 passengers 丧生，那我的工作就会非常容易。”当然，不同类型的 projects 和不同 fields 的 constraints，会使不同的事情变得 hard，但人们似乎经常很难看到，其他 fields 有哪些自己的 field 没有的 constraints。人们或许会以为，理解自己的 field 比 outsider 天真设想的更 complex，会帮助人们理解，其他 fields 也可能具有 hidden complexity，但通常似乎并非如此。

如果我们看看 Hillel 所引用的 statement 的其余部分（它来自一个 stack exchange question 的 top & accepted answer），作者接着写道：

> 当你事先确切知道别人要求你 project 什么时，make accurate projections 就容易得多，而不是一边 making guesses，一边处理 constant changes。
> 
> 绝大多数 bridges 使用的都是经过极充分验证的 tried and true materials、architectures 和 techniques。一个 Roman engineer 即使被送到两千年后的未来，通常也能认出一个 modern construction site 上正在发生什么。当然会有 differences，但你仍然是在 building 用于 load balancing 的 arches，仍然在 using 许多相同的 materials，等等。另一方面，如今正在 built 的大多数 software . . .

这正是人们讨论 cocktail-party ideas 时所犯的典型 error。当那些已经十年没当过 programmers、又一无所知的高管，对正在进行的 project 提出 unreasonable changes 时，Programmers 抱怨得有道理；但这与从未当过 civil engineers 的政客，要求在 large scale civil engineering projects 中进行 project changes 的情况，并没有那么不同，而且前者实际上更有可能是 reasonable 的。平均而言，programming projects 的 project changes 比 civil engineering projects 更 frequent 或更 large，这是 plausible 的；但我猜，intra-field variance 至少与 inter-field variance 一样大。

而且，当然，只有从未在 physical world 从事过严肃的 engineering work 的人，才会说出“一个真正的 engineer 的世界所具有的 predictability，真令人羡慕。但我们的世界总是在 flux，其中的 laws of physics 每周都会 change”这样的话，以为 physical laws 的（relative）fixity 就意味着 physical work 是 predictable。我担任 hardware engineer 时，我的 projects 中很大一部分 effort 和 complexity 都用在处理 physical uncertainty 上，而 civil engineering 也没有什么不同（甚至可以说，civil engineers 在 large scale projects 中处理 physical uncertainty 所拥有的 tools 要差得多，造成了更大程度的 uncertainty，也降低了 prevent 因 uncertainty 导致的 delays 的能力）。

如果我们看看 Roman engineering，甚至 300 年前的 engineering，与 modern engineering 有何 differs，就会发现，differences 的一个主要来源，是我们对来自 physical world 的 uncertainty 有了好得多的 understanding。过去，一个 structure 在 built 后不久，没有遇到任何 unusual conditions 或 stimulus 就 failed，并不令人震惊（例如 building collapse，或因 incorrectly constructed rail 导致的 train accident）。如今，这已经罕见到只要在 U.S. 或 Canada 发生，就会成为重大新闻；而这种 understanding 也让我们能够在过去被认为难以或不可能 build moderate-sized structures 的 areas，build gigantic structures。

例如，如果你看一个位于 Vancouver 地区、坐落在 delta 上的 large-scale construction project（Delta、Richmond，以及向 Hope 延伸的大部分土地），我们直到相对较近的时候，才发现了在这类 ground 上 reliably build 某些 large scale structures（例如 tall-ish buildings）所必需的 knowledge；这是 Roman engineer 无法理解的 modern civil engineering 的许多部分之一。这其中有很多来自一个叫作 geotechnical engineering 的 field，它是 civil engineering 的一个 sub-field（换个角度，也可以说它是一个独立的 field，还可以说它是 geological engineering 的一个 subfield），涉及 ground，即 soil mechanics、rock mechanics、geology、hydrology，等等。geotechnical engineering 的一个 fundamental 部分，是你可以 apply [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) 来 reason about soil 这一想法。已知最早将 mechanics application 于 soils 的时间是 1773 年，这是 geotechnical engineering 的 fundamental 部分；而人们通常认为，今天所理解的 geotechnical engineering 始于 1925 年。虽然 Roman engineers 做出了许多令人印象深刻的工作，但[他们所使用的 mental models，使他们无法 understanding modern civil engineering 的许多内容](https://www.patreon.com/posts/61946482)。

自然，这些 knowledge 要能够 change 我们可以 build 什么，就必须 change 我们如何 build。如果我们看看一个使用这些 modern knowledge、位于 compressible Vancouver delta soils 上的 construction site 是什么样子，那么按 wall clock time 来看，大部分时间看起来都像是有人在 construction site 上放了一堆 sand（preload）。虽然 Roman engineer 会知道一堆 sand 是什么，却不会知道，人们是如何确定需要多少 sand，以及它需要在那里放多久的（在某些情况下，Romans 会在我们今天使用 preload 的地方使用 piles 或 rafts；但在许多情况下，对于 preload 今天 solves 的 problems，他们没有答案）。

Geotechnical engineering 及其产生的那堆 sand（preload），只是进行一个 modern、large scale 的 civil engineering project 时需要 expertise 的数十个 sub-fields 之一；Roman engineer 需要接受相当多的教育，才能真正理解它。

回到我听到的 cocktail party solutions，其中一类常见的 solutions，是如何 fix high construction costs 和 slow construction。关于为什么东西这么贵、为什么 projects 耗时比 projected 更长等等，人们经常抛出一套热门想法。有时，这些评论与我从参与这些 projects 的 practicing engineers 那里听到的说法相似，但更多时候，给出的 reasons 相当不同。当 reasons 相同时，似乎[他们也一定只是碰巧 correct，因为他们似乎并不理解，要 reason through engineering tradeoffs 所必需的 body of knowledge](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1420866014493822980)[2](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:D)。

当然，和酒会上那些 theorists 一样，[在该 field 拥有 expertise 的 civil engineers 也认为 modern construction 是 wasteful 的](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1483162978224463872)，但他们提出的 reasons 往往与我在酒会上听到的很不一样[3](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:C)。不去 understanding 那个 problem，assume 那个 problem 是人为地 simple 的，然后为这个想象出来的 problem 提出 solution，就很容易拿出酒会式的 solutions。要 understanding 开展 large scale construction projects 所需的数十个 interacting engineering sub-fields 之间的 tradeoffs，并且就这些 tradeoffs 应该是什么、以及如何促使 engineers 和 policy makers 改变 tradeoffs 的落点展开真正 relevant 的 discussion，则要困难得多。

关于人们对事物如何 work 抱有[极其 oversimplified 且 incorrect 的 models](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1356056202203947008) 这一普遍 phenomena，一项被广泛 cited 的 study 是 [Rebecca Lawson 关于人们如何 understanding bicycles 如何 work 的这项 study](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03195929.pdf)，其中指出：

> 近期的 research 表明，人们经常 overestimate 自己 explain 事物如何 function 的 ability。Rozenblit 和 Keil (2002) 发现，人们 overrated 自己对 complicated phenomena 的 understanding。这种 illusion of explanatory depth 并不仅仅是由 general overconfidence 导致的；相对于其他 knowledge domains，例如 facts（capital cities 的 names）、procedures（baking cakes）或 narratives（movie plots），它专门出现在对 causally complex systems 的 understanding 上，例如 artifacts（crossbows、sewing machines、microchips）和 natural phenomena（tides、rainbows）。

以及

> 如果 nonexperts 无法 explain gears 如何 work 的 intricacies，或者为什么 bicycle 的 front forks 的 angle 是 critical 的，那并不令人意外。事实上，甚至 physicists 对一些看似 simple 的 issues 也存在分歧，例如 bicycles 为什么是 stable 的 (Jones, 1970; Kirshner, 1980)，以及它们如何 steer (Fajans, 2000)。此次 results 令人吃惊的地方在于，竟有如此多的人对 bicycles 如何 function 几乎没有任何 knowledge。​​

在该 study 的“experiment 2”中，人们被要求 draw 一辆 working bicycle，并 focus 于使 bicycle 能够 work 的 mechanisms（而不是让 drawing 看起来漂亮），94 名 participants 中有 60 名至少出现了一个 gross error，导致 drawing 甚至不像一辆 working bicycle。如果我们考察一个 large-scale real-world civil engineering project，单是一个 relevant subfield，例如 geotechnical engineering，其 complexity 就比一辆 bicycle 高出许多 orders of magnitude；我们可以相当有把握地猜测，取到 nearest percent，能够大致 sketch out 其中 relevant moving parts 是什么的 lay people（或 Roman engineers）的比例是 zero percent。

至于 civil engineering 以外的 example，Jamie Brandon 引用了 [Jim Manzi 的 Uncontrolled](https://amzn.to/3HrzkSc) 中的这段 excerpt，它 refutation 了一个我经常在酒会上听人搬出来的“clever”小观点：

> paradox of choice 是一个广为流传的民间故事，讲的是某一个 experiment：在 supermarket display 上摆出更多种果酱，反而 resulted in 更少的 purchases。给出的 explanation 是，choice 会带来压力，所以有些人面对太多可选的果酱，会干脆转身离开，空手回家。这个 experiment 不断被新闻和媒体 cited，通常伴随着“scientists 已经 discovered，choice 对你不好”之类的描述。但如果你走进一家大型超市，你会看到大约 12 million 种果酱。难道他们没有听说过那个果酱 experiment 吗？Jim Manzi 在 [Uncontrolled](https://amzn.to/3HrzkSc) 中讲道：
> 
> > 首先，请注意，所有 inference 都 built on 总共不过三十五罐果酱的 purchase。其次，请注意，如果那个果酱 experiment 的 results 是 valid 且 applicable 的，并且具有作为 economic or social policy 的 basis 所需的那种 generality，那么它将 imply，许多商店可以 eliminate 75 percent 的 products，并使 sales increase by 900 percent。那会是一个相当惊人的 result，也 indicates，这里的 measurement 可能有 problem。
> > 
> > ……original experiment 中的 researchers 本人对于其有关 generalizability 的 explicit claims 相当谨慎，而且人们已经投入大量精力，专门寻找 choice overload 会 consistently occurs 的 conditions；然而，popularizers 却把一家商店在两个星期六进行的一次 coupon-plus-display promotion 所 derived 的 conclusions，一路放大为有关这家商店的果酱 product selection 的 impact 的 assertions，再放大为有关美国所有 grocery stores 的果酱 product selection 的 impact，继而放大为有关每家商店中所有种类 retail products 的 product selection 的 impact 的 claims，最终演变成关于 choice 对 society 的 benefits 的颇为宏大的 claims。但正如我们所见，在不同 situations 下通过五十次 experiments 来 testing 这种 claim，会给这一 assertion 泼上大量冷水。
> > 
> > 以一个 practical business example 来说，即使是对一个构成有用的 forward prediction rule 的 causal mechanism 所做的 simplification，也不太可能类似于“把 QwikMart 商店 Renaming 为 FastMart 将 cause sales to rise”，而更可能类似于“把 high-income neighborhoods 中位于 high-traffic roads 上的 QwikMart 商店 Renaming 为 FastMart 将 cause sales to rise，只要商店因粉刷而关门的时间不超过两天。”在开始 testing 之前，我们极不可能知道所有可能的 hidden conditionals，并且能够 design 和 execute 一次 test，就 discovers 这样一条 condition-laden rule。
> > 
> > 此外，这些 causal relationships 本身也可能经常 change。例如，我们在一次 test 中 discover，某个 specific sales promotion 相对于 no promotion 会 drives a net gain in profit，但到了下一年，出现了大量 changes——我们的 competitors 推出了新的 promotions，overall economy 已经 deteriorated，consumer traffic 有一部分从 malls shifted 到了 strip centers，等等——这条 rule 就不再 holds true 了。延续前面的比喻，我们靠着小腿撞上家具，在漆黑的房间里摸索前进，而看不见的小妖怪却不断在我们身边移动家具。由于这些 reasons，仅仅 run an experiment、find a causal relationship，然后 assume 它是 widely applicable 的，是不够的。我们必须 run tests，然后在 actual implementation 中 measure 从这些 tests 中 developed 的 rules 的 actual predictiveness。

到目前为止，我们讨论的 examples 都是在某个 field 没有 background 的人 explain 一个 field 如何 work 或应该如何 work；但当人们退后一步，以脱离 details 的 high-level view 看待自己的 field 时，也会犯下采用 high-level view、incorrectly assuming 事物是 simple 的这种 error。例如，当年我在 Centaur 工作、我们还没有 shipped 一款 dual core chip 时，一位来自顶尖学校、即将毕业的 computer architecture 方向 PhD student 问我：“你们为什么不干脆像 Intel 和 AMD 那样，把两个 cores 钉在一起，做成一款 dual core chip 呢？那可是个 easy win。”

那时，我们为从 single core 转向 multi core 已经工作了一年多。要让 single core chip 具备 multi-core 甚至 multi-processor capable 的能力，并拥有 decent performance，就需要给 cache and memory hierarchy 增加大量额外的 complexity，而这正是 chip 中最 logically complex 的部分。作为一个 rough estimate，我猜，把一款为 single-core use 而 designed 的 chip 变成 multi-processor capable 的 chip，至少会使 produce 一款 working chip 所需的 testing/verification effort doubles（而一款 chip 的 design effort，大部分都投入在 testing/verification 上）。更一般地说，一名 computer architect 的水平取决于他对自己的 decisions 所 impact 的 tradeoffs 的 understanding。有水平的人对他们必须 interact 的 underlying fields 有很深的 understanding。一名 computer architect 会做出 bad decision 的常见 reason 是，他们对 computer architecture 之下一个或两个 levels 的 fields 只有酒会水平的 understanding。一个在 industry 中出现过多次的 bad decision 的 example 是，一名在职 computer architect 决定给 chip add [SMT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_multithreading)，因为它基本上是一个 free win。你付出百分之几的额外 area，或许就能得到 20% 更好的 performance。我知道有多次这样的尝试，由于 predictable reasons 而彻底 failed，因为 architect 没有 account for adding SMT 所带来的 complexity 和 verification cost。Adding SMT 所 adds 的 complexity 远多于 adding 第二个 core，因为 logic 必须贯穿所有部分；同样的 reason 也会导致 verifying 那款 chip 的 complexity 爆炸式增长。Intel 给 P4 added SMT 是个著名案例，但在它 shipped 的 first generation 中并没有 enable，因为它过于 complex，无法在 single generation 中 verify，而且存在 critical、showstopping 的 bugs。他们利用在一代 architecture 上排除 bugs 的数年时间，fixed 了 SMT implementation，并在下一代 chips 中 shipped 了它。当他们 migrated 到 Core architecture 并给它 added SMT 时，这件事又发生了一次。一名在职 computer architect 应该知道，这在 Intel 发生过两次，这 implying，verifying 一个 SMT implementation 是困难的；然而，仍然有多次 instances，有人对 SMT 的 complexity 只有酒会水平的 understanding，却建议把它 adding 到某个 design 中，而那个 design 根本没有足够的 verification budget 来最终 ship 一款带有 SMT 的 working chip。

[当然，这实际上并不是 computer architecture 所独有的](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)。我用了 dual core 这个 example，是因为它碰巧是我眼下最先想到的一个，但我不假思索就能想出几十个类似的 [examples](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/814167684954738688)，而且我很确定，如果花几天时间思考类似的 examples，我能写出几百个。[在某个 field 工作的人仍然必须非常谨慎，避免对世界抱有一种 incorrect、过于 abstract、elides details 的 view](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1594227349662609408)，并[因此 draws 错得令人发笑的 inferences 或 conclusions](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1484589911873261568)。当一个 field 之外的人 explain 事物应该如何 work 时，他们的 explanations 通常甚至比该 field 中漏掉某个 critical consideration 的人还要糟糕，而且[他们一般会 present](https://www.patreon.com/posts/54329188) [crank ideas](https://yosefk.com/blog/the-high-level-cpu-challenge.html)。

把 Roman engineering 的 example 和 CPU 的 example 放在一起看，从 1 core 到 2（以及一般而言，从 1 到 2，例如从 1 datacenter 到 2 datacenters，或者从 monolith 到 distributed system），是每个 practitioner 都应该明白很 hard 的事，尽管有些人并不明白。与此有些相关的是，如果有人展示了一个 4 THz processor，其 performance 是一个 4 GHz processor 的 1000x，那么任何 practitioner 都应该认识到，那是自己绝对不理解的 alien technology。只有对这个 field 毫无 knowledge 的 lay person，才可能情有可原地对自己说：“它不过是一个以 1000x 的 clock speed running 的 processor；能够 make 一个 4 GHz processor 的 engineer，基本上应该能 understand 一个具有 1000x 的 performance 的 4 THz processor 如何 works。”我们距离通过让 chips running 得 1000x faster 来把 performance scale up 到 1000x 还如此遥远，以至于要做到这一点，需要 technology 上的许多 fundamental breakthroughs，而且极有可能需要 creation 一些全新的 fields，其中包含的 engineering knowledge 比当今世界已有的还要多。同样，只有 lay person 才会看着 Roman engineering 和 modern civil engineering 想：“Romans built 东西，我们 build 的东西只是 bigger、more varied；Roman engineer 应该能够 understand 我们今天如何 build 东西，因为那些东西只是 bigger 而已。”仅 Geotechnical engineering 包含的 engineering knowledge，就比 Roman 时代所有 engineering fields combined 已有的还要多，而它还只是那些[为了能够像我们今天这样 building structures 而不得不 invented 的新 fields](https://www.patreon.com/posts/61946482)之一。

当然，我并不指望随便哪个 programmers 都能 understand geotechnical engineering，但我希望，一个在 programming 与 civil engineering 之间 making a comparison 的人，至少对 civil engineering 有一些 knowledge，而不是在自己对这个 field 基本一无所知的情况下，就 assume 这个 field 中已有的 knowledge 的 amount 与自己对这个 field 的 knowledge roughly equal。

尽管[我似乎比大多数人都更努力地避免陷入因为自己不 understand 某件事就认为它是 simple 的陷阱](https://www.patreon.com/posts/60185075)，我还是经常中招，而我为防止这一点所想到的最好办法，虽然聊胜于无，却并不 reliable。

其中一部分是，我试着培养自己去 noticing“没有真正 understanding 某件事，却在 glossing over 它时的 feeling”。我认为这 analogous to（也许实际上就是同一回事）过去二十年流行起来的一件事：留意 emotions 在身体中的感受，并通过 noticing 身体中的 feelings 来 understanding 自己的 emotional state，例如，某块特定 muscle 中某一种 tight feeling，就是我在生气的确凿信号。

当我对某件事抱着一种 fuzzy、high-level 的 view，并在脑中 glossing over 它时，我的身体会出现一种特定的 feeling。如果不留意，我很容易错过它；而且我怀疑，当我以某种方式 gloss over 某件事，以至于 brain 中产生这种 feeling 的 non-conscious part 甚至都不知道我在 glossing over 某件事时，我也可能错过它。尽管 noticing 这种 feeling 本身就是 unreliable 的，我认为，其他任何我可能用来独自 check 自己的 reasoning 的办法，fundamentally 都 relies on 同一种 mechanism（例如，如果我有一份 checklist，试着 determine 自己在 reasoning 某个 topic 时是否没有 glossed over 某件事，那么这个 process 中的某个部分仍然会 rely on feeling 或 intuition）。我确实会试着对自己错过这种 feeling 的 cases 进行 postmortem，弄清楚发生了什么，而这基本上就是我最初发现自己有一种与这种 error associated 的 feeling 的方式（我回想过去是什么导致了这类 mistake，并注意到自己有一种通常与它 associated 的 feeling），但这永远不会 perfect，甚至不会[很好](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/)。

另一个 component，是做我所说的“checking 进入我脑中的 inputs”。上高中时，我注意到，自己所说的那些“obviously wrong”的话中，有相当大一部分源于让 incorrect information 进入了脑中。我当时没有、现在也仍然没有一种好用又 cheap 的办法，能给一条 information tag 上它有多 reliable，所以我发现，在接收 information 时，要么 fact-check，要么 discard，会容易得多。

我尝试做的另一件事是 [get feedback](https://danluu.com/writing-non-advice/#appendix-getting-feedback)，但这既 unreliable，在 general case 下也 intractable，因为 getting feedback 的 speed 远远慢于 thought 的 speed，把一般 thought 放慢到 feedback 的 speed，会使人只有 relatively few thoughts[4](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:W)。

尽管[与某些 areas 不同，这里并不存在一套 mechanical、systematic 的 steps](https://danluu.com/teach-debugging/)，可以通过教授它们来 solve 这个 problem，我确实认为这是可以 practiced 和 improved 的，而且有些 fields 会教授类似的 skills（通常是 implicitly）。例如，在讨论一本 advanced 或 graduate level textbook 的 prerequisites 时，经常会看到书中写着类似这样的话：“Self contained。除 mathematical maturity 外，没有其他 prerequisites。”这是一种简略的说法，意思是：“本书不要求你掌握任何 high school student 不会学到的特定 mathematical knowledge，但你确实需要消除一种 fuzzy thinking，几乎每个 untrained 的人在 interpreting 和 understanding mathematical statements 时都有这种 thinking。”拥有 math degree 的人，脑中会有大量关于 [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%E2%80%93Schwarz_inequality) 和 [Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolzano%E2%80%93Weierstrass_theorem) 等内容的 explicit knowledge，但对于能够 understand 这本书而言，重要的并不是 explicit knowledge，而是一个人思考 math 的 general way。

尽管在其他 fields 中，与 mathematical maturity equivalent 的东西并没有一个真正的 term，例如，人们通常不会把“systems designs maturity”称作 [systems design interviews](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1470890504833228801) 中所寻求的东西，但这种 analogous skill 即使没有名字，也仍然存在。对于思考自己并非 trained expert 的 topics，也是如此，例如 non-civil engineer 思考为什么某个 construction project cost 了那么多、花了那么长时间，这是一种 general maturity of thought[5](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:R)。

>

> 当你想到一个有着 excellent judgment 的人时，会想到哪些 traits？也许你会想到 intelligence、cleverness、courage 或 patience。这些都是令人钦佩的 virtues，但有一种 trait 应该排在首位，却被忽视得如此严重，甚至还没有正式名称。

>

2.  关于 construction delays 的讨论同样忽略了 delays 的 geotechnical reasons。与前文一样，我用 geotechnical 作为一个能够解释许多 delays 的 sub-field 的例子，是因为我恰好熟悉它，而不是因为它是最重要的因素；但它确实是 delays 的一个 major cause，而且在许多种 projects 中，是 delays 的 largest cause。

    回到我们的那个 Roman engineer 充其量可能只有 superficially understand 的例子：我们之所以在 building 之前把 dirt 堆到 ground 上，是因为 Vancouver 的许多地方对于 building large structures 而言具有 poor geotechnical conditions。这里的 ground 是 soft 的，如果在上面 built 了 heavy 的东西，它就会随着时间 unevenly squished down。sand 放在那里是作为 weight，用来 pre-squish ground。

    按照上一段的描述，这听起来很 straightforward。遗憾的是，实际情况完全不是这样。碰巧，我最近经常和一位 geophysics engineer 一起开车四处走（这个 field 与 geotechnical engineering 有关，但又相当不同）。当我们开车经过路上某个奇怪的 bump 或 dip 时，她通常能够指出造成那个 bump 的 geotechnical issue，或者出于 political 动机而忽视 geotechnical engineer 的 guidance 的 decision。这里让我觉得有意思的是，尽管 civil engineering projects 所做的 de-risking 的 level 通常远高于我参与过的 electrical engineering projects，而后者又远高于我参与过的任何 software project，仍然有足够多的“bugs”进入“production”，以至于只要你开车四处走、具备相关知识并且留心观察，一天就能看到几十甚至几百处 mistakes。

    Fundamentally，issue 在于，对于 major highways 这类 physically large projects，人类并没有能够以接近 reasonable cost 的代价去 understand ground 的 technology。我们拥有的一种 tool 是用 ground penetrating radar 对 ground 进行 image，但这会产生 highly [underdetermined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermined_system) 的 output。我们拥有的另一种 tool 是使用 core drill 或 soil augur 之类的东西，基本上就是向 ground 内 digging，看看里面有什么。这也会产生 inherently underdetermined output，因为我们只能看到恰好 drilled 的位置是什么情况，而 ground 的 composition 有时具有 large spatial variation，从 surface 看并不明显。一个常见例子是存在 unmapped remnant creek bed，它很容易“躲过”soil 被 sampled 的那些 locations。其他 tools 也存在，但类似地，当它们在 practical financial constraints 下使用时，仍会让 engineer 对世界只有 incomplete 且 uncertain 的认识。

    当我听人们在 cocktail party 上讨论某个 construction project 为什么耗时那么久，并将其与 civil engineers 告诉我的 delay 原因进行比较时，cocktail party discussion 几乎总是只讨论那些 civil engineers 告诉我是 incorrect 的 reasons。delays 的 reasons 有很多，“unexpected geotechnical conditions”是常见的一种。Civil engineers 在这里进退两难，因为 drilling cores 既耗时又昂贵，而且人们看到 ground 被 dug up，却没有在做“真正的工作”时，就会生气（在 preload 被 applied 时也是一样——“他们为什么不在 highway 上干活？”）；这会给 politicians 造成压力，间接导致 timelines 没有留出足够时间去 understand geotechnical conditions。这有时会在 project 期间带来 geotechnical surprise（在 technical reports 中通常表述为“unforseen geotechnical conditions”），可能导致 project 的 major parts 不得不改用更慢、更昂贵的 techniques，甚至更糟，可能使 project 的一部分必须 redone，从而造成 cost and schedule overruns。

    我从未听到过讨论 project delays 的 geotechnical reasons 的 cocktail party discussion。相反，人们谈论的是一些 high-level reasons，它们在 lay person 听来似乎 plausible，却完全是 fabricated，是与 reality 脱节的 reasons。但如果你想讨论如何才能更快、更便宜地 built 东西，讨论“[progress studies](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2019/07/we-need-new-science-progress/594946/)”等等，那么如果不对其中涉及的 geotechnical tradeoffs（以及我们尚未讨论的其他 civil engineering fields 中的 tradeoffs）有所了解，就无法 reasonably 地进行这种讨论。

3.  为了把 costs 控制住，我们可以做的一件事是减少 geotechnical work，并在某个 risk bound 以内忽略 geotechnical surprises。如今，所做的部分“amount of work”由 regulations 决定，其中很大一部分则由 case law 决定；case law 大致说明了在发生 building collapse 等各种 bad outcomes 时，为避免 legal liability 需要做哪些 work。

    如果我们不再用 case law 和 risk of liability 来 determine 应该做多少 geotechnical derisking，而是根据每 dollar 的 [QALYs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality-adjusted_life_year) 来 compute，那么 at the margin，与许多其他 interventions 相比，我们似乎在 geotechnical derisking 上花了非常多的钱。

    这不仅适用于 geotechnical work，也适用于 civil engineering 中的其他 fields。例如，U.S. 和 Canada 等地的 builders 所做的 slump testing，远多于某些 construction pace 快得多的国家，这会 reduces building 过早毁坏的 risk。如果一栋 building 因为 builders 没有做 slump testing 而 collapsed，而他们在 U.S. 或 Canada 本来会做，那么这既会是一桩丑闻，也会构成严重的 liability problem，，但即便 builders 没有像 U.S. 和 Canada 通常那样做那么多 slump testing，buildings 通常也不会 collapse。

    一些没有按照大致与 U.S. 或 Canadian standards 同样 rigorous 的 standards 来 build 的国家，有时会有不久前才 built 的 structures 以在 U.S. 和 Canada 会被认为令人震惊的方式 collapse；但与这些钱可以花在的其他地方相比，每 dollar 所 saved 的 lives 数量很少。我们是否应该通过 policy decision 来改变这一点，比起我在 cocktail party 上听到的关于 construction costs 的那些 fabricated reasons，是与 building costs 和 timelines 更相关的讨论，但我从未听到 civil engineering 圈子以外的人提起这一点，或其他关于 project cost 的 concrete reasons。

    即便我们只把范围限定在与 civil engineering 有关的 work，而不是采取更宽泛、更 EA-minded 的 approach，考察所有可能的 interventions 的 QALYs，在 construction 期间用于 derisking 的 resources 与持续用于 derisking 的 resources（inspections、maintenance 等）之间的 tradeoff 中，relative resource levels 也并非由一个理应能够产生哪怕接近 optimal outcome 的 process 所 determined。

## 原文

You don't have to be at a party to see this phenomenon in action, but there's a curious thing I regularly see at parties in social circles where people value intelligence and cleverness without similarly valuing on-the-ground knowledge or intellectual rigor. People often discuss the standard trendy topics (some recent ones I've observed at multiple parties are how to build a competitor to Google search and how to solve the problem of high transit construction costs) and explain why people working in the field today are doing it wrong and then explain how they would do it instead. I occasionally have good conversations that fit that pattern (with people with very deep expertise in the field who've been working on changing the field for years), but the more common pattern is that someone with cocktail-party level knowledge of a field will give their ideas on how the field can be fixed.

Asking people why they think their solutions would solve valuable problems in the field has become a hobby of mine when I'm at parties where this kind of superficial pseudo-technical discussion dominates the party. What I've found when I've asked for details is that, in areas where I have some knowledge, people [generally don't know what sub-problems need to be solved to solve the problem they're trying to address, making their solution hopeless](https://danluu.com/sounds-easy/). After having done this many times, my opinion is that the root cause of this is generally that many people who have a superficial understanding of topic assume that the topic is as complex as their understanding of the topic instead of realizing that only knowing a bit about a topic means that they're missing an understanding of the full complexity of a topic.

Since I often attend parties with programmers, this means I often hear [programmers retelling](https://twitter.com/nick_r_cameron/status/1346174149044043776) their [cocktail-party level understanding of another field](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1347269793578053632) (the search engine example above notwithstanding). If you want a sample of similar comments online, you can often see these when programmers discuss "trad" engineering fields. An example I enjoyed was [this Twitter thread where Hillel Wayne discussed how programmers without knowledge of trad engineering often have incorrect ideas about what trad engineering is like](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1162469763374673920), where many of the responses are from programmers with little to no knowledge of trad engineering who then reply to Hillel with their misconceptions. When Hillel completed his [crossover project](https://www.hillelwayne.com/tags/crossover-project/), where he interviewed people who've worked in a trad engineering field as well as in software, [he got even more such comments](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1484268111687663620). Even when people are warned that naive conceptions of a field are likely to be incorrect, many can't help themselves and they'll immediately reply with their opinions about a field they know basically nothing about.

Anyway, in the crossover project, Hillel compared the perceptions of people who'd actually worked in multiple fields to pop-programmer perceptions of trad engineering. One of the many examples of this that Hillel gives is when people talk about bridge building, where he notes that programmers say things like

> The predictability of a true engineer’s world is an enviable thing. But ours is a world always in flux, where the laws of physics change weekly. If we did not quickly adapt to the unforeseen, the only foreseeable event would be our own destruction.

and

> No one thinks about moving the starting or ending point of the bridge midway through construction.

But Hillel interviewed a civil engineer who said that they had to move a bridge! Of course, civil engineers don't move bridges as frequently as programmers deal with changes in software but, if you talk to actual, working, civil engineers, many civil engineers frequently deal with changing requirements after a job has started that's not fundamentally different from what programmers have to deal with at their jobs. People who've worked in both fields or at least talk to people in the other field tend to think the concerns faced by engineers in both fields are complex, but people with a cocktail-party level of understanding of the field often claim that the field they're not in is simple, unlike their field.

A line I often hear from programmers is that programming is like "having to build a plane while it's flying", implicitly making the case that programming is harder than designing and building a plane since people who design and build planes can do so before the plane is flying[1](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:N). But, of course, someone who designs airplanes could just as easily say "gosh, my job would be very easy if I could build planes with 4 9s of uptime and my plane were allowed to crash and kill all of the passengers for 1 minute every week". Of course, the constraints on different types of projects and different fields make different things hard, but people often seem to have a hard time seeing constraints other fields have that their field doesn't. One might think that understanding that their own field is more complex than an outsider might naively think would help people understand that other fields may also have hidden complexity, but that doesn't generally seem to be the case.

If we look at the rest of the statement Hillel was quoting (which is from the top & accepted answer to a stack exchange question), the author goes on to say:

> It's much easier to make accurate projections when you know in advance exactly what you're being asked to project rather than making guesses and dealing with constant changes.
> 
> The vast majority of bridges are using extremely tried and true materials, architectures, and techniques. A Roman engineer could be transported two thousand years into the future and generally recognize what was going on at a modern construction site. There would be differences, of course, but you're still building arches for load balancing, you're still using many of the same materials, etc. Most software that is being built, on the other hand . . .

This is typical of the kind of error people make when they're discussing cocktail-party ideas. Programmers legitimately gripe when clueless execs who haven't been programmers for a decade request unreasonable changes to a project that's in progress, but this is not so different and actually more likely to be reasonable than when politicians who've never been civil engineers require project changes on large scale civil engineering projects. It's plausible that, on average, programming projects have more frequent or larger changes to the project than civil engineering projects, I'd guess that the intra-field variance is at least as large as the inter-field variance.

And, of course, only someone who hasn't done serious engineering work in the physical world could say something like "The predictability of a true engineer’s world is an enviable thing. But ours is a world always in flux, where the laws of physics change weekly", thinking that the (relative) fixity of physical laws means that physical work is predictable. When I worked as a hardware engineer, a large fraction of the effort and complexity of my projects went into dealing with physical uncertainty and civil engineering is no different (if anything, the tools civil engineers have to deal with physical uncertainty on large scale projects are much worse, resulting in a larger degree of uncertainty and a reduced ability to prevent delays due to uncertainty).

If we look at how Roman engineering or even engineering from 300 years ago differs from modern engineering, a major source of differences is our much better understanding of uncertainty that comes from the physical world. It didn't used to be shocking when a structure failed not too long after being built without any kind of unusual conditions or stimulus (e.g., building collapse, or train accident due to incorrectly constructed rail). This is now rare enough that it's major news if it happens in the U.S. or Canada and this understanding also lets us build gigantic structures in areas where it would have been previously considered difficult or impossible to build moderate-sized structures.

For example, if you look at a large-scale construction project in the Vancouver area that's sitting on the delta (Delta, Richmond, much of the land going out towards Hope), it's only relatively recently that we discovered the knowledge necessary to build some large scale structures (e.g., tall-ish buildings) reliably on that kind of ground, which is one of the many parts of modern civil engineering a Roman engineer wouldn't understand. A lot of this comes from a field called geotechnical engineering, a sub-field of civil engineering (alternately, arguably its own field and also arguably a subfield of geological engineering) that involves the ground, i.e., soil mechanics, rock mechanics, geology, hydrology, and so on and so forth. One fundamental piece of geotechnical engineering is the idea that you can apply [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) to reason about soil. The first known application of mechanics to soils, a fundamental part of geotechnical engineering, was in 1773 and geotechnical engineering as it's thought of today is generally said to have started in 1925. While Roman engineers did a lot of impressive work, [the mental models they were operating with precluded understanding much of modern civil engineering](https://www.patreon.com/posts/61946482).

Naturally, for this knowledge to have been able to change what we can build, it must change how we build. If we look at what a construction site on compressible Vancouver delta soils that uses this modern knowledge looks like, by wall clock time, it mostly looks like someone put a pile of sand on the construction site (preload). While a Roman engineer would know what a pile of sand is, they wouldn't know how someone figured out how much sand was needed and how long it needed to be there (in some cases, Romans would use piles or rafts where we would use preload today, but in many cases, they had no answer to the problems preload solves today).

Geotechnical engineering and the resultant pile of sand (preload) is one of tens of sub-fields where you'd need expertise when doing a modern, large scale, civil engineering project that a Roman engineer would need a fair amount of education to really understand.

Coming back to cocktail party solutions I hear, one common set of solutions is how to fix high construction costs and slow construction. There's a set of trendy ideas that people throw around about why things are so expensive, why projects took longer than projected, etc. Sometimes, these comments are similar to what I hear from practicing engineers that are involved in the projects but, more often than not, the reasons are pretty different. When the reasons are the same, it seems that [they must be correct by coincidence since they don't seem to understand the body of knowledge necessary to reason through the engineering tradeoffs](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1420866014493822980)[2](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:D).

Of course, like cocktail party theorists, [civil engineers with expertise in the field also think that modern construction is wasteful](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1483162978224463872), but the reasons they come up with are often quite different from what I hear at parties[3](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:C). It's easy to come up with cocktail party solutions to problems by not understanding the problem, assuming the problem is artificially simple, and then coming up with a solution to the imagined problem. It's harder to understand the tradeoffs in play among the tens of interacting engineering sub-fields required to do large scale construction projects and have an actually relevant discussion of what the tradeoffs should be and how one might motivate engineers and policy makers to shift where the tradeoffs land.

A widely cited study on the general phenomena of people having [wildly oversimplified and incorrect models of how things work](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1356056202203947008) is [this study by Rebecca Lawson on people's understanding of how bicycles work](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03195929.pdf), which notes:

> Recent research has suggested that people often overestimate their ability to explain how things function. Rozenblit and Keil (2002) found that people overrated their understanding of complicated phenomena. This illusion of explanatory depth was not merely due to general overconfidence; it was specific to the understanding of causally complex systems, such as artifacts (crossbows, sewing machines, microchips) and natural phenomena (tides, rainbows), relative to other knowledge domains, such as facts (names of capital cities), procedures (baking cakes), or narratives (movie plots).

And

> It would be unsurprising if nonexperts had failed to explain the intricacies of how gears work or why the angle of the front forks of a bicycle is critical. Indeed, even physicists disagree about seemingly simple issues, such as why bicycles are stable (Jones, 1970; Kirshner, 1980) and how they steer (Fajans, 2000). What is striking about the present results is that so many people have virtually no knowledge of how bicycles function.​​

In "experiment 2" in the study, people were asked to draw a working bicycle and focus on the mechanisms that make the bicycle work (as opposed to making the drawing look nice) and 60 of the 94 participants had at least one gross error that caused the drawing to not even resemble a working bicycle. If we look at a large-scale real-world civil engineering project, a single relevant subfield, like geotechnical engineering, contains many orders of magnitude more complexity than a bicycle and it's pretty safe to guess that, to the nearest percent, zero percent of lay people (or Roman engineers) could roughly sketch out what the relevant moving parts are.

For a non-civil engineering example, Jamie Brandon quotes this excerpt from [Jim Manzi's Uncontrolled](https://amzn.to/3HrzkSc), which is a refutation of a "clever" nugget that I've frequently heard trotted out at parties:

> The paradox of choice is a widely told folktale about a single experiment in which putting more kinds of jam on a supermarket display resulted in less purchases. The given explanation is that choice is stressful and so some people, facing too many possible jams, will just bounce out entirely and go home without jam. This experiment is constantly cited in news and media, usually with descriptions like "scientists have discovered that choice is bad for you". But if you go to a large supermarket you will see approximately 12 million varieties of jam. Have they not heard of the jam experiment? Jim Manzi relates in [Uncontrolled](https://amzn.to/3HrzkSc):
> 
> > First, note that all of the inference is built on the purchase of a grand total of thirty-five jars of jam. Second, note that if the results of the jam experiment were valid and applicable with the kind of generality required to be relevant as the basis for economic or social policy, it would imply that many stores could eliminate 75 percent of their products and cause sales to increase by 900 percent. That would be a fairly astounding result and indicates that there may be a problem with the measurement.
> > 
> > ... the researchers in the original experiment themselves were careful about their explicit claims of generalizability, and significant effort has been devoted to the exact question of finding conditions under which choice overload occurs consistently, but popularizers telescoped the conclusions derived from one coupon-plus-display promotion in one store on two Saturdays, up through assertions about the impact of product selection for jam for this store, to the impact of product selection for jam for all grocery stores in America, to claims about the impact of product selection for all retail products of any kind in every store, ultimately to fairly grandiose claims about the benefits of choice to society. But as we saw, testing this kind of claim in fifty experiments in different situations throws a lot of cold water on the assertion.
> > 
> > As a practical business example, even a simplification of the causal mechanism that comprises a useful forward prediction rule is unlikely to be much like 'Renaming QwikMart stores to FastMart will cause sales to rise,' but will instead tend to be more like 'Renaming QwikMart stores to FastMart in high-income neighborhoods on high-traffic roads will cause sales to rise, as long as the store is closed for painting for no more than two days.' It is extremely unlikely that we would know all of the possible hidden conditionals before beginning testing, and be able to design and execute one test that discovers such a condition-laden rule.
> > 
> > Further, these causal relationships themselves can frequently change. For example, we discover that a specific sales promotion drives a net gain in profit versus no promotion in a test, but next year when a huge number of changes occurs - our competitors have innovated with new promotions, the overall economy has deteriorated, consumer traffic has shifted somewhat from malls to strip centers, and so on - this rule no longer holds true. To extend the prior metaphor, we are finding our way through our dark room by bumping our shins into furniture, while unobserved gremlins keep moving the furniture around on us. For these reasons, it is not enough to run an experiment, find a causal relationship, and assume that it is widely applicable. We must run tests and then measure the actual predictiveness of the rules developed from these tests in actual implementation.

So far, we've discussed examples of people with no background in a field explaining how a field works or should work, but the error of taking a high-level view and incorrectly assuming that things are simple also happens when people step back and have a high-level view of their own field that's disconnected from the details. For example, back when I worked at Centaur and we'd not yet shipped a dual core chip, a nearly graduated PhD student in computer architecture from a top school asked me, "why don't you just staple two cores together to make a dual core chip like Intel and AMD? That's an easy win".

At that time, we'd already been working on going from single core to multi core for more than one year. Making a single core chip multi-core or even multi-processor capable with decent performance requires significant additional complexity to the cache and memory hierarchy, the most logically complex part of the chip. As a rough estimate, I would guess that taking a chip designed for single-core use and making it multi-processor capable at least doubles the amount of testing/verification effort required to produce a working chip (and the majority of the design effort that goes into a chip is on testing/verification). More generally, a computer architect is only as good as their understanding of the tradeoffs their decisions impact. Great ones have a strong understanding of the underlying fields they must interact with. A common reason that a computer architect will make a bad decision is that they have a cocktail party level understanding of the fields that are one or two levels below computer architecture. An example of a bad decision that's occurred multiple times in industry is when a working computer architect decides to add [SMT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_multithreading) to a chip because it's basically a free win. You pay a few percent extra area and get perhaps 20% better performance. I know of multple attempts to do this that completely failed for predictable reasons because the architect failed to account for the complexity and verification cost of adding SMT. Adding SMT adds much more complexity than adding a second core because the logic has to be plumbed through everything and it causes an explosion in the complexity of verifying the chip for the same reason. Intel famously added SMT to the P4 and did not enable in the first generation it was shipped in because it was too complex to verify in a single generation and had critical, showstopping, bugs. With the years of time they had to shake the bugs out on one generation of architecture, they fixed their SMT implementation and shipped it in the next generation of chips. This happened again when they migrated to the Core architecture and added SMT to that. A working computer architect should know that this happened twice to Intel, implying that verifying an SMT implementation is hard, and yet there have been multiple instances where someone had a cocktail party level of understanding of the complexity of SMT and suggested adding it to a design that did not have the verification budget to ever ship a working chip with SMT.

[And, of course, this isn't really unique to computer architecture](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/). I used the dual core example because it's one that happens to currently be top-of-mind for me, but I can think of tens of similar [examples](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/814167684954738688) off the top of my head and I'm pretty sure I could write up a few hundred examples if I spent a few days thinking about similar examples. [People working in a field still have to be very careful to avoid having an incorrect, too abstract, view of the world that elides details](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1594227349662609408) and [draws comically wrong inferences or conclusions as a result](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1484589911873261568). When people outside a field explain how things should work, their explanations are generally even worse than someone in the field who missed a critical consideration and [they generally present](https://www.patreon.com/posts/54329188) [crank ideas](https://yosefk.com/blog/the-high-level-cpu-challenge.html).

Bringing together the Roman engineering example and the CPU example, going from 1 core to 2 (and, in general, going from 1 to 2, as in 1 datacenter to 2 datacenters or a monolith to a distributed system) is something every practitioner should understand is hard, even if some don't. Somewhat relatedly, if someone showed off a 4 THz processor that had 1000x the performance of a 4 GHz processor, that's something any practitioner should recognize as alien technology that they definitely do not understand. Only a lay person with no knowledge of the field could reasonably think to themselves, "it's just a processor running at 1000x the clock speed; an engineer who can make a 4 GHz processor would basically understand how a 4 THz processor with 1000x the performance works". We are so far from being able to scale up performance by 1000x by running chips 1000x faster that doing so would require many fundamental breakthroughs in technology and, most likely, the creation of entirely new fields that contain more engineering knowledge than exists in the world today. Similarly, only a lay person could look at Roman engineering and modern civil engineering and think "Romans built things and we build things that are just bigger and more varied; a Roman engineer should be able to understand how we build things today because the things are just bigger". Geotechnical engineering alone contains more engineering knowledge than existed in all engineering fields combined in the Roman era and it's only one of the [new fields that had to be invented to allow building structures like we can build today](https://www.patreon.com/posts/61946482).

Of course, I don't expect random programmers to understand geotechnical engineering, but I would hope that someone who's making a comparison between programming and civil engineering would at least have some knowledge of civil engineering and not just assume that the amount of knowledge that exists in the field is roughly equal to their knowledge of the field when they know basically nothing about the field.

Although [I seem to try a lot harder than most folks to avoid falling into the trap of thinking something is simple because I don't understand it](https://www.patreon.com/posts/60185075), I still fall prey to this all the time and the best things I've come up with to prevent this, while better than nothing, are not reliable.

One part of this is that I've tried to cultivate noticing "the feeling of glossing over something without really understanding it". I think of this is analogous to (and perhaps it's actually the same thing as) something that's become trendy over the past twenty years, paying attention to how emotions feel in your body and understanding your emotional state by noticing feelings in your body, e.g., a certain flavor of tight feeling in a specific muscle is a sure sign that I'm angry.

There's a specific feeling I get in my body when I have a fuzzy, high-level, view of something and am mentally glossing over it. I can easily miss it if I'm not paying attention and I suspect I can also miss it when I gloss over something in a way where the non-conscious part of the brain that generates the feeling doesn't even know that I'm glossing over something. Although noticing this feeling is inherently unreliable, I think that everything else I might do that's self contained to check my own reasoning fundamentally relies on the same mechanism (e.g., if I have a checklist to try to determine if I haven't glossed over something when I'm reasoning about a topic, some part of that process will still rely on feeling or intuition). I do try to postmortem cases where I missed the feeling to figure out happened, and that's basically how I figured out that I have a feeling associated with this error in the first place (I thought about what led up to this class of mistake in the past and noticed that I have a feeling that's generally associated with it), but that's never going to perfect or even [very good](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/).

Another component is doing what I think of as "checking inputs into my head". When I was in high school, I noticed that a pretty large fraction of the "obviously wrong" things I said came from letting incorrect information into my head. I didn't and still don't have a good, cheap, way to tag a piece of information with how reliable it is, so I find it much easier to either fact-check or discard information on consumption.

Another thing I try to do is [get feedback](https://danluu.com/writing-non-advice/#appendix-getting-feedback), which is unreliable and also intractable in the general case since the speed of getting feedback is so much slower than the speed of thought that slowing down general thought to the speed of feedback would result in having relatively few thoughts[4](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:W).

Although, [unlike in some areas, there's no mechanical, systematic, set of steps](https://danluu.com/teach-debugging/) that can be taught that will solve the problem, I do think this is something that can be practiced and improved and there are some fields where similar skills are taught (often implicitly). For example, when discussing the prerequisites for an advanced or graduate level textbook, it's not uncommon to see a book say something like "Self contained. No prerequisites other than mathematical maturity". This is a shorthand way of saying "This book doesn't require you to know any particular mathematical knowledge that a high school student wouldn't have picked up, but you do need to have ironed out a kind of fuzzy thinking that almost every untrained person has when it comes to interpreting and understanding mathematical statements". Someone with a math degree will have a bunch of explicit knowledge in their head about things like [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%E2%80%93Schwarz_inequality) and the [Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolzano%E2%80%93Weierstrass_theorem), but the important stuff for being able to understand the book isn't the explicit knowledge, but the general way one thinks about math.

Although there isn't really a term for the equivalent of mathematical maturity in other fields, e.g., people don't generally refer to "systems designs maturity" as something people look for in [systems design interviews](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1470890504833228801), the analogous skill exists even though it doesn't have a name. And likewise for just thinking about topics where one isn't a trained expert, like a non-civil engineer thinking about why a construction project cost what it did and took as long as it did, a sort of general maturity of thought[5](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fn:R).

For a broader and higher-level discussion of clear thinking, see Julia Galef's Scout Mindset:

> WHEN YOU THINK of someone with excellent judgment, what traits come to mind? Maybe you think of things like intelligence, cleverness, courage, or patience. Those are all admirable virtues, but there’s one trait that belongs at the top of the list that is so overlooked, it doesn’t even have an official name.
> 
> So I’ve given it one. I call it scout mindset: the motivation to see things as they are, not as you wish they were.
> 
> Scout mindset is what allows you to recognize when you are wrong, to seek out your blind spots, to test your assumptions and change course. It’s what prompts you to honestly ask yourself questions like “Was I at fault in that argument?” or “Is this risk worth it?” or “How would I react if someone from the other political party did the same thing?” As the late physicist Richard Feynman once said, “The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.”

As a tool to improve thought, the book has [a number of chapters that give concrete checks that one can try](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1477789638387322880), which makes it more (or at least more easily) actionable than this post, which merely suggests that you figure out what it feels like when you're glossing over something. But I don't think that the ideas in the book are a substitute for this post, in that the self-checks the book suggests don't directly attack the problem discussed in this post.

In one chapter, Galef suggests leaning into confusion (e.g., if some seemingly contradictory information gives rise to a feeling of confusion), which I agree with. I would add that there are a lot of other feelings that are useful to observe that don't really have a good name. When it comes to evaluating ideas, some that I try to note, beside the already mentioned "the feeling that I'm glossing over important details", are "the feeling that a certain approach is likely to pay off if pursued", "the feeling that an approach is really fraught/dangerous", "the feeling that there's critical missing information", "the feeling that something is really wrong", along with similar feelings that don't have great names.

1.  Another one I commonly hear is that, unlike trad engineers, [programmers do things that have never been done before](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1162469760900091904) [\[return\]](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/#fnref:N)
2.  Discussions about construction delays similarly ignore geotechnical reasons for delays. As with the above, I'm using geotechnical as an example of a sub-field that explains many delays because it's something I happen to be familiar with, not because it's the most important thing, but it is a major cause of delays and, on many kinds of projects, the largest cause of delays.

    Going back to our example that a Roman engineer might, at best, superficially understand, the reason that we pile dirt onto the ground before building is that much of Vancouver has poor geotechnical conditions for building large structures. The ground is soft and will get unevenly squished down over time if something heavy is built on top of it. The sand is there as a weight, to pre-squish the ground.

    As described in the paragraph above, this sounds straightforward. Unfortunately, it's anything but. As it happens, I've been spending a lot of time driving around with a geophysics engineer (a field that's related to but quite distinct from geotechnical engineering). When we drive over a funny bump or dip in the road, she can generally point out the geotechnical issue or politically motivated decision to ignore the geotechnical engineer's guidance that caused the bump to come into existence. The thing I find interesting about this is that, even though the level of de-risking done for civil engineering projects is generally much higher than is done for the electrical engineering projects I've worked on, where in turn it's much higher than on any software project I've worked on, enough "bugs" still make it into "production" that you can see tens or hundreds of mistakes in a day if you drive around, are knowledgeable, and pay attention.

    Fundamentally, the issue is that humanity does not have the technology to understand the ground at anything resembling a reasonable cost for physically large projects, like major highways. One tool that we have is to image the ground with ground penetrating radar, but this results in highly [underdetermined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermined_system) output. Another tool we have is to use something like a core drill or soil augur, which is basically digging down into the ground to see what's there. This also has inherently underdetermined output because we only get to see what's going on exactly where we drilled and the ground sometimes has large spatial variation in its composition that's not obvious from looking at it from the surface. A common example is when there's an unmapped remnant creek bed, which can easily "dodge" the locations where soil is sampled. Other tools also exist, but they, similarly, leave the engineer with an incomplete and uncertain view of the world when used under practical financial constraints.

    When I listen to cocktail party discussions of why a construction project took so long and compare it to what civil engineers tell me caused the delay, the cocktail party discussion almost always exclusively discusses reasons that civil engineers tell me are incorrect. There are many reasons for delays and "unexpected geotechnical conditions" are a common one. Civil engineers are in a bind here since drilling cores is time consuming and expensive and people get mad when they see that the ground is dug up and no "real work" is happening (and likewise when preload is applied — "why aren't they working on the highway?"), which creates pressure on politicians which indirectly results in timelines that don't allow sufficient time to understand geotechnical conditions. This sometimes results in a geotechnical surprise during a project (typically phrased as "unforseen geotechnical conditions" in technical reports), which can result in major parts of a project having to switch to slower and more expensive techniques or, even worse, can necessitate a part of a project being redone, resulting in cost and schedule overruns.

    I've never heard a cocktail party discussion that discusses geotechnical reasons for project delays. Instead, people talk about high-level reasons that are plausible sounding to a lay person, but completely fabricated, reasons that are disconnected from reality. But if you want to discuss how things can be built more quickly and cheaply, "[progress studies](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2019/07/we-need-new-science-progress/594946/)", etc., this cannot be reasonably done without having some understanding of the geotechnical tradeoffs that are in play (as well as the tradeoffs from other civil engineering fields we haven't discussed).

3.  One thing we could do to keep costs under control is to do less geotechnical work and ignore geotechnical surprises up to some risk bound. Today, some of the "amount of work" done is determined by regulations and much of it is determined by case law, which gives a rough idea of what work needs to be done to avoid legal liability in case of various bad outcomes, such as a building collapse.

    If, instead of using case law and risk of liability to determine how much geotechnical derisking should be done, we compute this based on [QALYs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality-adjusted_life_year) per dollar, at the margin, we seem to spend a very large amount of money geotechnical derisking compared to many other interventions.

    This is not just true of geotechnical work and is also true of other fields in civil engineering, e.g., builders in places like the U.S. and Canada do much more slump testing than is done in some countries that have a much faster pace of construction, which reduces the risk of a building's untimely demise. It would be both scandalous and a serious liability problem if a building collapsed because the builders of the building didn't do slump testing when they would've in the U.S. or Canada,, but buildings usually don't collapse even when builders don't do as much slump testing as tends to be done in the U.S. and Canada.

    Countries that don't build to standards roughly as rigorous as U.S. or Canadian standards sometimes have fairly recently built structures collapse in ways that would be considered shocking in the U.S. and Canada, but the number of lives saved per dollar is very small compared to other places the money could be spent. Whether or not we should change this with a policy decision is a more relevant discussion to building costs and timelines than the fabricated reasons I hear cocktail party discussions of construction costs, but I've never heard this or other concrete reasons for project cost brought up outside of civil engineering circles.

    Even if we just confine ourselves to work that's related to civil engineering as opposed to taking a broader, more EA-minded approach, and looking QALYs for all possible interventions, the tradeoff between resources spent on derisking during construction vs. resources spent derisking on an ongoing basis (inspections, maintenance, etc.), the relative resource levels weren't determined by a process that should be expected to produce anywhere near an optimal outcome.

5.  One part of this that I think is underrated by people who have a self-image of "being smart" is where book learning and thinking about something is sufficient vs. where on-the-ground knowledge of the topic is necessary.

    A fast reader can read the texts one reads for most technical degrees in maybe 40-100 hours. For a slow reader, that could be much slower, but it's still not really that much time. There are some aspects of problems where this is sufficient to understand the problem and come up with good, reasonable, solutions. [And there are some aspects of problems where this is woefully inefficient and thousands of hours of applied effort are required to really be able to properly understand what's going on](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/).
