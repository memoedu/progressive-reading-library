## 正文

## _[A Fire Upon the Deep（深渊上的火）](http://www.amazon.com/Fire-Upon-Deep-Zones-Thought-ebook/dp/B000FBJAGO/)_

_Vernor Vinge_

强烈推荐，尤其是如果你对 Singularity 的 intellectual history 感兴趣。读过之后，[Eliezer’s](http://yudkowsky.net/) worldview 中的一些部分就变得容易理解多了。从头到尾都读得很愉快，主要是因为 Vinge 的 world-building 做得非常出色。顺便说一句，按照 Wikipedia 的说法，他的名字读作“vinjee”。

## _[Quantum Computing Since Democritus（自德谟克利特以来的量子计算）](http://www.amazon.com/Quantum-Computing-since-Democritus-Aaronson-ebook/dp/B00B4V6IZK/)_

_Scott Aaronson_

我原以为这本书会比较浅显，偏向 pop-science-y，结果却惊喜地发现，它所面向的 mathematical maturity 水平相当合理。Aaronson 的语气有时有点让人不舒服——“rational numbers 是 countable 的 proof 是什么？你以前没见过？哦，好吧。”——但总的来说，我很喜欢读到对这些内容更为 denser 的 exposition。到目前为止，我对 complexity theory 有了一点了解，不过我才刚读到“quantum”这一节，我预计真正的学习收获会出现在这里。Aaronson 答应要讲讲他自己对学习 QM 的看法，这已经勾起了我的好奇心：

> 教授 quantum mechanics 有两种方式。第一种方式——对如今大多数 physicists 来说，这仍然是唯一的方式——遵循这些 ideas 被 discovered 的历史顺序。因此，你从 classical mechanics 和 electrodynamics 开始，每一步都要 solving 大量令人精疲力竭的 differential equations。然后，你会学到“blackbody paradox”和各种奇怪的 experimental results，以及这些事情给 physics 带来的巨大 crisis。接下来，你会学到 physicists 在 1900 到 1926 年间为了试图消除这场 crisis 而 invented 的一套复杂、拼凑而成的 ideas。然后，如果你够幸运，在学习多年之后，你终于能接触到那个 central conceptual point：nature 不是由 probabilities（它们总是 nonnegative）来 described 的，而是由称为 amplitudes 的 numbers 来 described 的，这些 numbers 可以是 positive、negative，甚至是 complex。
> 
> 教授 quantum mechanics 的第二种方式不去逐一叙述它的 discovery 过程，而是直接从 conceptual core 开始——也就是对 laws of probability 进行某种 generalization，以允许 minus signs（以及更一般的 complex numbers）。一旦你理解了这个 core，就可以按照自己的喜好掺入一些 physics，并 calculate 你想要的任何 atom 的 spectrum。

## _[Capital in the Twenty-First Century（二十一世纪资本论）](http://www.amazon.com/Capital-Twenty-First-Century-Thomas-Piketty-ebook/dp/B00I2WNYJW/)_

_Thomas Piketty，Arthur Goldhammer 译_

我只读了第一部分，大约占这部大书的 14%。到目前为止，我觉得它格外好读，尤其是考虑到作者有时听起来像是想成为下一个 Marx（不过得承认，是更为谨慎、rigorous 的那一类 Marx）。Piketty 使用跨度达两千年的极其广泛的 data，来 analyzes income、wealth 和 inequality 的 dynamics（不过重点放在 1700-2013 这一时期）。我读过的 economics 还不够多，没什么可以拿来与它比较，但据我了解，它称得上是一部 analytic 的 _杰作_。

第一部分的 arguments 基本上可以 summarized 为这样一个主张：过去一个世纪的大部分时间里出现的 extreme growth，在很大程度上是 anomalous 的，其原因在于 Europe 在 World War II 后重建所产生的“catch-up” effects、前所未有的 demographic growth，以及如今（显然[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/reading/#fn:1)）正在 slowing 的 breakaway innovation。

总的来说，我觉得这些 arguments 很有说服力，但在 meta level 上，我不确定自己应该在多大程度上因此而被说服。我对 economics 了解不多，尤其是 Piketty 所处理的那类 macro 内容，所以我不能确定他没有略去某个显而易见的 counterargument。我也不确定，援引那么久远的 historical precedent 是否很有帮助——我们所处的 regime 与 300 年前的世界完全不同，这似乎相当有可能。

这本书还包含许多有趣的 observations，它们并不处于 Piketty 的 main point 的中心。例如，我不常想到的一件事是，inflation 过去基本上并不存在。直到 20th century 初，nominal prices 都极其 stable——以至于那个时期的小说（例如 Austen、Balzac 的作品）经常会在作品中提到具体的 incomes。后来，World War 1 爆发；各国 accumulated 巨额 public debts，并 floated 自己的 currencies、printed money，以便为这些 debts 提供 finance。当我想到 100 年前的世界与现在有何不同时，通常不会想到这一点。

## _[Uncharted: Big Data as a Lens on Human Culture（未知之境：以大数据透视人类文化）](http://www.amazon.com/Uncharted-Data-Lens-Human-Culture-ebook/dp/B00C5R845Y/)_

_Erez Aiden 和 Jean-Baptiste Michel_

这本书几乎完全是在通俗地介绍他们那篇[关于 culturomics 的 _Science_ paper](http://www.sciencemag.org/content/331/6014/176.short)，他们在其中 invent 了 [Google Books ngram viewer](https://books.google.com/ngrams)，并借助它发现了一些很酷的事实。这个 ngram viewer 非常棒，而且我很赞成在 studying history 和 culture 时，整体上采取更 quantitative 的方式。我对这本书唯一较大的不满是，它大概只需一半的篇幅，就能提供同样多的信息；其中有很多废话。

Aiden 和 Michel 所 ran 的最有趣的 experiment，是尝试 automatically detect censorship regimes。结果发现，一般来说，人们的“fame”逐年的 change（也就是他们的名字在 Google Books corpus 中被提及的 frequency）会 follows a normal distribution——少数人变得有名得多，少数人变得不那么有名，而绝大多数人则处于 mean 约为 zero 的 bell curve 中（所以变得更有名和变得不那么有名的人一样多）。但在例如 Nazi regime 期间，curve 的 tails 会变得 heavier 得多：许多“subversive” artists 因为作品遭到 censored，popular 程度大幅下降，而许多 Nazi officials 则因 propaganda 而 popular 程度大幅上升。如果把同样的 analysis apply 到例如来自 [Sina Weibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sina_Weibo) 的 datasets（它受到 Chinese government 广泛的 censored），看看能否用这种方式 detect 哪些内容正在被 censored，会很有意思。

## _[The Secret Life of Pronouns：Pronouns 的秘密生活](http://www.amazon.com/Secret-Life-Pronouns-Words-About-ebook/dp/B005E8AB16/)_

_James W. Pennebaker_

和 _Uncharted_ 一样，我是为了一门 social psychology 课读这本书的（好吧，只读了一半）。书中介绍了 word frequency 的 minute variations 与各种事物 correlated 的许多不同方式：例如，在一项 study 中，当人们写下自己的 traumas 时，如果他们有以下表现，就会有更好的 health outcomes：

-   used 的 positive words 比 negative 更多（即有 overall positive outlook）；
-   但确实也 use 了一些 negative words（acknowledging 事件的 downsides）；
-   对 cognitive words 的使用从 low levels 转向 high levels（例如 _think_、_feel_、_believe_——也就是说，他们起初并没有 narrative，而是在 writing process 中 constructed 了一个）；
-   在使用 first-person singular/other pronouns 之间 changing（被 interpreted 为“changing perspectives”）

这里有许多极其有趣的内容，但至少在我读过的那一半中，他们并没有 study 很多 interventions，而只是研究 correlations，而且我觉得其中的 causal inferences 有些 questionable。事实上，他们曾尝试用几种办法，为 trauma recovery experiment 中的 causation 提供证明，但全都失败了。作者自己也指出：“evidence 是 convincing 的：Word usage 通常 _reflects_ psychological state，而不是 influences 它。”如果作者们尝试为 underlying mechanisms build 一个更好的 model，我会更感兴趣。

## _[A Fire Upon the Deep](http://www.amazon.com/Fire-Upon-Deep-Zones-Thought-ebook/dp/B000FBJAGO/)_

_Vernor Vinge_

Highly recommended, especially if you’re interested in the intellectual history of the Singularity. Reading it made some of [Eliezer’s](http://yudkowsky.net/) worldview became a lot more understandable. Enjoyable throughout, primarily because Vinge does a great job of world-building. Incidentally, according to Wikipedia it’s pronounced “vinjee.”

## _[Quantum Computing Since Democritus](http://www.amazon.com/Quantum-Computing-since-Democritus-Aaronson-ebook/dp/B00B4V6IZK/)_

_Scott Aaronson_

I expected this to be pretty soft and pop-science-y, but I was pleasantly surprised to find that it was pitched for a pretty reasonable level of mathematical maturity. Aaronson’s tone rankled a bit sometimes—“What’s the proof that the rational numbers are countable? You haven’t seen it before? Oh, alright.”—but in general I appreciated reading a denser exposition of the material. So far I’ve picked up a bit about complexity theory, but I only just got to the “quantum” section, where I expect the meat of the learning to happen. My curiosity’s already been piqued by Aaronson’s promise of his own take on learning QM:

> There are two ways to teach quantum mechanics. The first way – which for most physicists today is still the only way – follows the historical order in which the ideas were discovered. So, you start with classical mechanics and electrodynamics, solving lots of grueling differential equations at every step. Then, you learn about the “blackbody paradox” and various strange experimental results, and the great crisis these things posed for physics. Next, you learn a complicated patchwork of ideas that physicists invented between 1900 and 1926 to try to make the crisis go away. Then, if you’re lucky, after years of study, you finally get around to the central conceptual point: that nature is described not by probabilities (which are always nonnegative), but by numbers called amplitudes that can be positive, negative, or even complex.
> 
> The second way to teach quantum mechanics eschews a blow-by-blow account of its discovery, and instead starts directly from the conceptual core – namely, a certain generalization of the laws of probability to allow minus signs (and more generally, complex numbers). Once you understand that core, you can then sprinkle in physics to taste, and calculate the spectrum of whatever atom you want.

## _[Capital in the Twenty-First Century](http://www.amazon.com/Capital-Twenty-First-Century-Thomas-Piketty-ebook/dp/B00I2WNYJW/)_

_Thomas Piketty, trans. Arthur Goldhammer_

I’ve only read Part One, which is about 14% of this tome. So far I’ve found it remarkably readable for an author who at times sounds like he wants to be the next Marx (if admittedly a Marx of a more cautious and rigorous variety). Piketty analyzes the dynamics of income, wealth, and inequality using an extraordinarily broad array of data spanning two millennia (though with a focus on the period 1700-2013). I haven’t read enough economics to have much to compare it to, but from what I understand it’s something of an analytic _tour de force_.

The arguments of Part One can be summarized basically as claiming that the extreme growth seen for much of the past century is largely anomalous, due to “catch-up” effects as Europe rebuilt after World War II, unprecedented demographic growth, and breakaway innovation which is now (apparently[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/reading/#fn:1)) slowing.

I found the arguments persuasive in general, but on a meta level I’m not sure how swayed I should be by that fact. I don’t know very much about economics, especially the kind of macro stuff that Piketty deals with, so I can’t be sure that he’s not eliding some obvious counterargument. I’m also not sure of the use of historical precedent going back that far is very helpful—it seems pretty plausible that we’re just in a totally different regime than the world 300 years ago.

The book also includes a lot of interesting observations that are noncentral to Piketty’s main point. For instance, something I don’t often think of is that inflation basically used not to be a thing. Until the beginning of the 20th century, nominal prices were rock-stable—so much so that novels of the time period (e.g. Austen, Balzac) frequently mentioned specific incomes in their work. Then World War 1 happened; countries accumulated massive public debts, and floated their currencies and printed money to finance them. That’s not something that typically comes to mind when I think about how the world was different 100 years ago.

## _[Uncharted: Big Data as a Lens on Human Culture](http://www.amazon.com/Uncharted-Data-Lens-Human-Culture-ebook/dp/B00C5R845Y/)_

_Erez Aiden and Jean-Baptiste Michel_

This is almost entirely a popular presentation of their [_Science_ paper on culturomics](http://www.sciencemag.org/content/331/6014/176.short) in which they invent the [Google Books ngram viewer](https://books.google.com/ngrams) and find out some cool facts with it. The ngram viewer is pretty awesome, and I’m a fan of generally being more quantitative about studying history and culture. My only big beef with the book was that it probably could have been half as long and equally informative; there was a lot of fluff.

The most interesting experiment that Aiden and Michel ran was trying to automatically detect censorship regimes. It turns out that generally from year to year people’s change in “fame” (frequency with which their name is mentioned in the Google Books corpus) follows a normal distribution—a few people get a lot more famous, a few get a lot less, and the vast majority are in a bell curve with a mean of about zero (so as many people get more famous as get less). But during e.g. the Nazi regime, the tails of the curve get much heavier: lots of “subversive” artists got massively less popular because their work was being censored, and lots of Nazi officials got massively more popular due to propaganda. It would be interesting to apply the same analysis to e.g. datasets from [Sina Weibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sina_Weibo) (which is censored extensively by the Chinese government) and see if you can detect what’s being censored that way.

## _[The Secret Life of Pronouns](http://www.amazon.com/Secret-Life-Pronouns-Words-About-ebook/dp/B005E8AB16/)_

_James W. Pennebaker_

Like _Uncharted_, I read this (well, half of this) for a social psychology class. It covered many different ways in which minute variations in word frequency correlated with various things: in one study, for example, when people wrote about their traumas, they showed better health outcomes if they

-   used more positive words than negative (i.e. had an overall positive outlook);
-   but did use some negative words (acknowledging the downsides of the event);
-   went from using low to high levels of cognitive words (like _think_, _feel_, _believe_—i.e., they didn’t start out with a narrative but constructed one during the writing process);
-   changing between use of first-person singular/other pronouns (interpreted as “changing perspectives”)

There was a lot of super interesting stuff here, but at least in the half that I read they didn’t study very many interventions, only correlations, and I find the causal inferences somewhat questionable. In fact, they tried several tacks to show causation for the trauma recovery experiment and all failed. The author himself notes that “\[T\]he evidence is convincing: Word usage generally _reflects_ psychological state rather than influences it.” I would have been more interested if the authors had tried to build a better model of the underlying mechanisms.
