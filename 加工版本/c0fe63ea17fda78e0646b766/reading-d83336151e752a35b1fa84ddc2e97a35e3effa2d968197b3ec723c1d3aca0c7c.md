# 为什么买到好用的东西这么难？｜Why is it so hard to buy things that work well?

---

## 正文

我经常听到一种 efficient markets hypothesis 的 [鸡尾酒会版本](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/)，其基本意思是：“markets enforce efficiency，所以一家 company 不可能存在某种重大的 inefficiency 却仍能 survive。”[我们此前在这里讨论过 Marc Andreessen 关于 tech hiring 不可能是 inefficient 的那段话](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/)，也在[这里](https://danluu.com/talent/)讨论过：

> 我们就直入主题吧。我认为，关于 Silicon Valley companies 存在 deliberately, systematically discriminatory 行为的 critique 是 incorrect 的，有两个理由可以相信事实如此。……第 2，我们的 companies 极度渴求 talent。极度渴求。我们的 companies 简直渴求 talent 到了要命的地步。它们就像躺在沙滩上喘不过气来，因为它们无法为这些 jobs 招到足够的 talented people。无论 talent 在哪里，都要去找到他们的 motivation 高得令人难以置信。

我经常听到 engineers 和 VCs 重复这个想法的各种变体：companies 是 efficient 的，和/或 products 基本上已经 as good as possible，因为，如果它们有可能变得 better，就早有人 outcompeted 它们，并且已经做到了[1](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:S)。

这种说法隐约有些 plausibility，因此，[我经常听到人们在闲聊中展开这场争论](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/)：一个人指出某种明显的 company inefficiency 或 product error，另一个人便回应说，如果它那么明显，company 里早就有人 fixed the issue 了，或者早就有另一家 company 出现，凭借 more efficient 或 better 而获胜了。纯粹 abstractly 地谈，很难为这场争论定论；但如果看看具体情况，事情就清楚些了，比如上面两个关于 hiring 的例子：我们能观察到，无论人们提出什么 abstract arguments，inefficiencies 都 persisted 了几十年。

说到 buying products and services，在个人层面，我认识的大多数人，只要检查过自己为 home renovation 或 [accounting](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1477072484092375040) 等事情雇来的人的工作，就发现过严重的 errors。虽然也有可能找到不做 shoddy work 的人，但[对于并非该 field 的 expert 的人来说，通常很难 determine 某个人是否会在该 field 做 shoddy work](https://twitter.com/pushcx/status/1499381895653699585)。你可以尝试通过 paying more 来获得 better quality，但一旦离开 market 的最底端，如何用 money 换取 quality 往往就不清楚了；例如，我那些选择了 large、brand name 的 accounting firms 的朋友和同事，比选择 small、local accountants 的人付了多得多的钱，得到的却是 higher error rate；作为一种 strategy，尝试 expensive local accountants 的效果也并没有好多少。好的 accountants 通常有些 expensive，但他们通常并不 charging the highest rates，而有些 expensive 的 accountants 中，只有 small percentage 是好的。

更一般地说，在许多 markets 中，consumers 是 uninformed 的，而且[要弄清哪些 products 连勉强过得去都算得上，也相当困难，更不用说好的了](https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/#appendix-capitalism)。当人们碰巧 choose 了适合自己的 product 或 service 时，往往出于错误的理由。例如，过去几年，我的社交圈里出现过两波从 iPhones 转用 Android phones 的人。这两波都源于 Apple PR 的失误，导致许多人以为 iPhones 在某方面很糟糕，而事实上，它们在那方面比 Android phones 更好。幸运的是，iPhones 并不 strictly superior to Android phones，而且许多转用的人得到了更适合自己的 device，因为他们此前使用 iPhone，本就是因为良好的 Apple PR，这使他们的 errors 恰好 cancel out。但是，当人们主要依据 marketing 和 PR 来 making decisions，又无法获得 good information 时，我们没有什么特别的理由认为，一个 product 的 generally better、甚至 strictly superior，会使它 winning，而更差的 product losing。在 capital markets 中，我们并不需要很多 informed participants，就可以认为某种形式的 efficient market hypothesis holds，确保“prices reflect all available information”。关于 market inefficiencies 的 published results 一经 published 就不再成立，因为人们会 exploit the inefficiency，直到它 disappears——这是个 truism。但在 job market 的例子中，即使 firms 能够利用 mispriced labor，就像 Greenspan 在成为 Chairman of the fed 之前所做的那件著名的事一样，inefficiencies 仍然可能 persist：

> Townsend-Greenspan 作为一家 economics firm，有一点不同寻常：男性为女性工作（我们总共约有二十五名 employees）。我 hiring women economists 并不是出于 women's liberation。这样做只是非常符合 business sense。我对男性和女性 valued equally，并发现，由于其他 employers 并非如此，好的 women economists 比男性 less expensive。Hiring women . . . 让 Townsend-Greenspan 用 same money 得到了 higher-quality work . . .

但正如我们也看到的，exploiting mispriced labor 的 individual firms 对 labor 的 demand 是 limited 的，而 inefficiencies 可以 persist 几十年，因为那些依据“all available information”采取行动的 firms 并没有 buy enough labor，无法把 mispriced people 的 price 推到这样的位置：如果大多数或所有 firms 都 acting rationally，它本应达到的位置。

从 abstract 的角度看，对于 products and services，inefficiencies 似乎也应该能够 persist 很长时间，因为类似地，也不存在一种 mechanism，让 system 中的 actors 能以直接把 money converts 成更多 money 的方式 exploit the inefficiency；有时甚至根本没有什么 mechanism 能让人赚到哪怕一点钱。例如，如果你观察到，人们因为认为 Apple 正在搞恶意的 planned obsolescence 而从 iPhones 转用 Android phones，这很愚蠢，因为 Android devices 通常反而更快 become obsolete——原因在于，[iPhones 获得 updates 的时间更长](https://danluu.com/android-updates/)，而且 iPhones 在它们 compete 的每个 price point 都 faster，让 phone 能够在 [bloated sites](https://danluu.com/web-bloat/) 上使用[更长时间](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/919423480776613888)——你其实无法靠这一观察赚钱。这不同于 mispriced asset：你可以 buy 它的 derivatives 来 make money（in expectation）。

对于不知道什么 product 或 service 好的问题，一个常见建议是去问该 field 的 expert 或有 credentialed 资质的人，但[这往往也会失败](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1324495890548056065)。例如，我的一个朋友因为自己的 window air conditioner 很 loud，每次 turned on 都会把他吵醒，而睡不好觉。他问一位自己信任、从事 air conditioners 工作的朋友，换一台更新的 air conditioner 能否 improved 这个问题，那位朋友说：“不能；air conditioners 基本上全都一样。”但任何 compared 过内含 motors 的物品的 consumer，都会立刻知道这是 false 的。在 holding power and cost constant 的情况下，Engineers 在 producing quieter devices 方面已经进步了很多。我朋友最终买了一台更新、quieter 的 air conditioner，解决了睡眠问题；但这个问题困扰他的时间超出了本来所需，因为他以为，一个以 air conditioners 为工作对象的人，会给他关于 air conditioners 的不至于太糟的建议。如果我朋友是 air conditioners 方面的 expert，或者曾经随着时间推移 compared 过其他方面 comparable 的 consumer products 的 noise levels，他就能弄清自己不该信任那位朋友；但如果他有那个水平的 expertise，一开始就不需要建议了。

到目前为止，我们看的是在个人层面获得合适的 product 或 service 有多困难，但这个问题也存在于 firm 层面，而且往往更严重，因为 markets 往往 thinner，可选的 products 更少，pricing 也 opaque，要“给我们打电话”才知道。一条常被重复的建议是，firms 应该专注于自己的“core competencies”，并 outsource 其他一切（例如，Joel Spolsky、Gene Kim、Will Larson、Camille Fournier 等人都这么说），但如果看看 mid-sized tech companies，就能发现，它们往往需要拥有远远超出任何人所认为的其 core competency 的 in-house expertise，除非，例如，[每一家 social media company 都把 kernel expertise 作为 core competency](https://danluu.com/in-house/)。原则上，firms 可以 outsource 这类工作；但我认识的那些依赖 outsourcing 的人，例如把 kernel expertise 交给 consultants 或 support contract 下的 application engineers 的人，对于 results 都很不满意，相比之下，他们 hiring dedicated engineers 能得到的结果要好得多。这既体现在 absolute terms 上（support 常常几周乃至几个月都无法提出 satisfactory resolution，即使问题是一名好的 engineer 几天就能 solve 的），也体现在花钱的效果上（尽管 engineers 很 expensive，large support contracts 的 cost 往往可能比一名 engineer 还高，却 delivering 比一名 engineer 更差的 service）。

这个问题不仅存在于 support，也存在于 company 可以 buy 而非 build 的 products。例如，Wave 的 CTO Ben Kuhn 写过[一串 Twitter thread，谈我们在 Wave 遇到的一些问题](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1324493738304036864)，还有[两篇](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1382325921311563779)[followups](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1496167942744346624)。Ben 现在认为，他担任 CTO 时犯下的一个重大错误是，没有在 vendor selection 上投入多得多的精力，即使 decision 看起来毫无悬念也应如此；同时，也没有更认真地考虑更早把许多 systems 转到 custom in-house versions。即便已经从 leading（也就是 largest、most respected）的 firm 中 selecting 了该领域公认最好的 product，并使用该 company 的 main offering，这个 product 往往不仅不 work，而且由于 design 本身，根本不可能 work。

例如，对于一个把 data 从 Postgres syncs 到 Snowflake 的 product，我们尝试了“buy”，而不是“build”。从 Postrgres 进行 Syncing，是一家 leading data sync company 的 main offering（也就是拥有最多 customers 的 offering），而我们发现，它会 lose data、duplicate data 和 corrupt data。深入调查后发现，这个 product 的 design 除了其他问题之外，还 relies on data source 能在自己的 changelog 上 seek backwards。但 Postgres 一旦 consumed 了 changelogs，就会将它们 throws away，因此 Postgres data source 无法 support 这项 operation。当他们的 product 尝试这样做，而 operation fails 时，我们最终得到的是“stuck”的 sync，需要 vendor 的 operator 进行 manual intervention，和/或出现 data loss。由于我们的 data 仍在 Postgres 上，可以通过做一次 full resync 来 recover；但这个 data sync product 最高只能达到 5MB/s，原因似乎连他们自己也不知道，因此即使 databases 并没有那么 large，一次 full resync 也可能花上几天。Resyncs 还会 silently drop and corrupt data，因此有时必须反复进行多个 cycles 的 full resyncs，并随后执行 data integrity checks，才能从 data corruption 中 recover，这可能需要几周。尽管这个 product 被广泛推荐，而且是该领域的 leading product，但它存在多项重大的 design flaws，这意味着它确实根本不可能 work。

这与 Mongo 或其他存在 fundamental design flaws、导致 severe data loss 的 products 并没有那么不同，[主要区别在于，大多数领域都没有一个 Kyle Kingsbury，花上多年为该领域的各种 products publishing tests，耐心回应关于 correctness 的 bogus claims，直到 PR backlash 促使该领域的 companies 开始认真对待 correctness](https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/)。没有这种 pressure，大多数 software products 基本上都不能 work，因此才有上文 Ben 的那些 Twitter threads；他在其中指出，你可能想选择的 "buy" solutions 大多不能 work[2](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:V)。当然，以我们的 scale，有很多东西我们近期都不会 build，比如 CPUs；但对于许多按照惯常看法应该 "buy" 的东西，"build" 似乎是个合理的 option。对于更大的 companies 和 building CPUs，这一点甚至也成立。十五年前，high-performance（也就是 non-embedded level 的 performance）CPUs 是一种 canonical example：大家会觉得，in-house build 这种东西简直是疯了，即使对最大的 software companies 来说也荒唐。但 Apple 和 Amazon 已经能够在它们所 optimizing for 的 dimensions 上 produce best-in-class CPUs，而这背后有 predictable reasons[3](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:Q)。

这不只是一个 impacts tech companies 的问题；我们在许多不同的 industries 都能看到它。例如，任何想向 customers 邮寄物品的 company，都必须要么自行 implement shipping，要么承担 unreliable shipping 带来的后果。作为 user，packages 能否 shipped 到你手上，很大程度上取决于你住在哪里，以及住在什么样的楼里。

我住独栋房屋时，无论 shipper 是谁，packages 通常都能送到（尽管经常迟到）。但搬进公寓楼之后，有些楼就是收不到某些 delivery services 的 deliveries。有一次，我住的楼里，postal service 没有正常 deliver mail，我有很多邮件都没收到（尽管我经常收到寄给楼里其他人以及其他地方的人的邮件）。更常见的是，UPS 和 Fedex 通常根本不会 attempt to deliver，只会在楼门上贴上一堆 notices，对应那些它们没有 deliver 的 packages；notice 上谎称收件人不在家，但有一点倒是说对了：要拿到 package，收件人必须去某个 pick-up location 自取。

有一阵子，我住的城市里，Amazon 使用 3rd-party commercial courier services 来完成 same-day delivery 的 last-mile shipping。它们使用的 services 以把东西标记为 delivered、却好几天都不 delivering 物品而出名，导致 "same day" shipping 比 next day 甚至 two day shipping 还慢。有一次，我天真地联系了 Amazon support，因为我的 package 已被标记为 delivered，却并没有 delivered。Support 按照 Amazon 提供的 standard script 告诉我，应该在 package 被标记为 delivered 三天之后再联系他们，因为 couriers 经常把 packages 标记为 delivered，却没有 delivering，但通常会在几天内 deliver package。Amazon 知道，它们使用的 courier service 在声称已经 deliver packages[4](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:P) 时，其实甚至根本没有尝试这样做，而它们当时唯一可用的 short-term mitigation，就是让 support 告诉人们，不要因为 packages 被标记为 delivered，就以为它们已经送到了。

Amazon 最终通过雇用自己的 delivery people，或使用按 commercial shipping standards 来说极其昂贵的 service，解决了这个问题（Apple 为 same-day delivery 就这么做过）[5](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:A)。在 scale 下，没有任何你付钱就能买到的 commercial service，会 reliably attempt to deliver packages。如果你想要一个确实 works 的 service，通常就得自己负责 building 它，就像 software 世界里一样。我当地的 grocery store 曾试着把这件事 outsource 给 DoorDash。我从这家 grocery store 试过 3 次 delivery，groceries 在 3 次里送到了 2 次，这远低于大多数人认为 grocery delivery 可以接受的 hit rate（而且在 groceries 确实送到时，有些我没有订购、也没有被收费的物品，也被送给了我，而不是订购它们的人）。为了获得 reliability 而不得不 build 而不是 buy，对 productivity 是一种巨大拖累，对较小的 companies 尤其如此（例如，想与 Amazon compete、向 customers 邮寄 products 的小店，不可能拥有 reliable delivery，因为它们无法 build out 自己的 delivery service）。

无法把 services 委托出去所产生的 waste 数量惊人，我在工作过的每个地方都见过。举一个来自另一个 industry 的例子：我在一家小型 chip startup 工作时，我们具备 in-house 完成 end-to-end chip processing 的 capability（但没有自己的 fabs），这对一家小型 chip startup 来说并不常见。当一个新 design 的第一片 wafer 从 fab 出来时，我们会安排航班把 wafer 空运过来，然后有人用 wafer saw 把 wafer cut 成 individual chips，以便我们尽快开始 testing。这经常被认为很荒唐，就像一家小型 software startup 管理自己的 on-prem hardware 会被认为很荒唐一样。毕竟，wafer saw 以及从 wafer 得到 working chip 所需的 expertise，在超过 99% 的时间里都会 idle。常备 equipment 和 expertise，却只有不到 1% 的时间会用到，是那种应该 outsource 的事情的 classic example；但如果你算一算，既要有 competent 的人来做这件事，又要有可用的 equipment，即使 volumes 相当低，in-house 做也更便宜，哪怕相应的 equipment 和 expertise 在 99% 的时间里都 idle。更重要的是，你在 in house 会得到好得多的 service（faster turnaround），从而能以 higher cadence 来 ship。我既在尝试把这类事情 contract out 的 companies 工作过，也和许多这样做过的人聊过；结果就是，你会付出 higher cost，得到 slower、less reliable 的 service。

chip software tooling 也一样；尽管把 tooling outsource 给 [large EDA vendors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_design_automation) 是 standard 做法，我们却从使用自己的 custom tools 中获益良多，而这些 tools 通常由一个人 created 或 maintained。例如，我在那里时，大多数 simulator cycles 都是在一个由一个人 maintained 的 custom simulator 上 run 的，每年节省了数百万美元的 simulator costs（当时 simulator 的 standard pricing 是每个 license 每年几千美元，而我们有一个约含一千台 simulation machines 的 farm）。你可能会想，如果一个人就能 create 或 maintain 一个每年为 company 带来数百万美元价值的 tool，我们的 competitors 也会这样做；就像你可能会想，如果雇一个知道如何把 wafer 切开的人，就能 ship faster 且 at a lower cost，我们的 competitors 也会这样做一样，但它们大多没有。

[Joel Spolsky 在一篇旧文章里说](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/823296808919019520)：

> “Find 那些 dependencies——并 eliminate 它们。”当你在一个非常、非常优秀的 team 中，与出色的 programmers 共事时，坦白说，其他所有人的 code 都是 bug-infested garbage，而且其他人都不知道如何 ship on time。

我们也有类似的态度，不过我会说，我们稍微谦逊一些。我们不认为其他所有人都在 producing garbage，但我们也不会假定，自己不能以十分之一的 cost，produce 出与可以买到的东西 comparable 的东西。通过与一些 competitors 的人交谈，我发现我们的运作方式与它们的运作方式之间存在[相当大的 cultural difference](https://danluu.com/culture/)。它们根本没有想到，自己不必接受那套 standard American business logic，即你应该 focus on 你的 core competencies；你完全可以根据具体事情本身的利弊，想清楚 in-house 做这件事是否合理，而不是把自己的思考 outsourcing 给一句简练的格言。

我曾经从内部看着一家 company 经历这种 cultural shift。leadership 中的几个人决定，company 应该 focus on 它的 core competencies，这意味着放弃用于 infrastructure 的 custom software。这导致了好几次 large migrations，从 custom internal software 转向 SaaS solutions 和 open source software。如果你观察围绕各个 projects “为什么”应该或不应该 migrate 的讨论，就会看到少数 unusually unreasonable 的人，试图根据每个 case 本身的利弊来推敲具体 cases（在一篇讨论如何反对来自上层的命令的文章中，[Yossi Kreinin 把这些人称为 insane employees](https://yosefk.com/blog/people-can-read-their-managers-mind.html)；我在本文中会指称同一个 concept，但会把这样做的人称为 unusually unreasonable）。不过，大多数人都接受了那套统一口径，不管具体情况如何，都推动 migration。

我觉得有趣的是，leadership 并没有告诉特定 teams 它们必须 migrate；对于那些有一个 "unusually unreasonable person" 为了合理的理由提出反对、以便继续 running 现有 system 的 teams，也没有什么实际的负面后果。相反，大多数人接受了这个想法，并试图用一些听起来隐约合理、却与现实无关的理由来 justify migrations，造成了一些可笑的结果，例如“为了省钱”而迁往一个 open source system，但新 system 却相当明显地 less efficient[6](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:M)，而且不出所料，需要高得多的 capex 和 opex。原本设想的 cost savings 来自缩减 team，但 operational cost 的 increase 远远超过了 team 的 cost 变化，而且 operating 这个 system 的 complexity 意味着 team size 不但没有 decreasing，反而 increased。确实有不少 cases 中 migrate 是合理的，但公开陈述的 migration 理由，往往与实际上使 migrate 合理的理由 unrelated 或 weakly related。一旦人们接受了 company 应该 focus on core competencies 的想法，migrations 就由这个 cultural idea 驱动，而不是由任何 technical reasons 驱动。

[像上述那样的 decisions，也就是没有认真进行 technical consideration 就作出的 technical decisions，如此普遍，是 companies 为了 make good products 而承受的 selection pressure 如此 weak 的一个主要原因](https://danluu.com/bad-decisions/)。确实存在一些 pressure，但其中的 noisy 程度足以让 successful companies 经常绕开 making 一个 works 的 product；就像上文 Mongo 的例子，从 business standpoint 来看，Mongo 决定大声重复 [demonstrably bogus performance claims，并作出 demonstrably false correctness claims](https://aphyr.com/tags/mongodb)，优于把精力放在实际的 correctness 和 performance 上；通过把 resources 集中在对 business 真正重要的地方，它们成功 outcompete 了那些犯下错误、把大量 resources 投入 performance 和 correctness 的 companies。

Yossi 的文章讲到，一个 unusually unreasonable 的人如何能在自己所在的 firm 中、在自己看重的 dimension 上产生 outsized impact；这一点也适用于 firm 之外的 impact。上面提到的 Kyle Kingsbury 就是一个例子。按我听说 [Jepsen](https://jepsen.io/) 目前收取的 rates，Kyle 能赚到 BigCo 的 senior developer 那样的收入（是真正的 senior，而不是只有 "senior" 这个 title 的人），但这是他多年来以低于 market rates 的报酬、长时间投身于一项前途未卜的事业，并反驳批评者的 [FUD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear,_uncertainty,_and_doubt) 之后才达到的（如果你读过所链接文章的回复，或者更糟，读过他参与与 developers 讨论的实际 tickets，就会发现，多年来给 Kyle 的回复一直是连篇的胡说八道，包括为 vendors 工作的人[觉得他专门针对自己，诋毁他的人品](https://lobste.rs/s/jgfyep/addio_redis_i_m_leaving_redis_labs#c_i6kcci)[7](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:J)，以及[对他大肆贬损](https://lobste.rs/s/jgfyep/addio_redis_i_m_leaving_redis_labs#c_sae3wo)）。我深深敬佩那些即使 system 与自己作对，仍愿意推动这类问题解决的人；但尽管我敬佩他们，基本上没有人会这么做。如果一个 system 必须等到 Kyle 这样的人挺身而出，successful firms 才肯在 correctness 而不是 correctness marketing 上投入精力，那么它就会产生大量善于 marketing correctness、实际上却没有像样的 correctness properties 的 products（比如本文提到的 data sync product，尽管它的 design 是 fundamentally broken 的，其网站却反复强调这个 syncing product 是多么 reliable 和 safe）。

在 firm 层面也是如此：往往需要一家 unusually unreasonable 的 firm，才能 produce 真正出色的 product，而不只是被 marketed 成出色的 product。例如，[Volvo 这家 car manufacturer 似乎是唯一一家试图 produce 超越 IIHS tests 所能展示水平的 structural safety 的厂商](https://danluu.com/car-safety/)，其 business 表现却如此糟糕，以至于被迫 move upmarket，成了一家 niche、luxury 的 automaker，因为尽管 car accidents 是主要的 cause of death，也是 life expectancy loss 的一个重要来源，consumers 却并不真正关心 safety。而且，Volvo 是否还能继续做一家 unreasonable 的 firm，也并不清楚，因为它未能作为 independent automaker 生存下去。Ford acquired Volvo 后，[Ford 开始把 Volvos 转移到 shared Ford C1 platform，而这个 platform 在 crash tests 中的表现并不特别好](https://danluu.com/car-safety/#quality-may-change-over-time)。Geely acquired Volvo 之后，[现在还太早，无法确定他们是否会维持 Volvo 的承诺，即针对 real-world crash data 进行 designing，而不只是针对 benchmarks 中报告的 crash data](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1458814317948661768)。如果 Geely 不愿延续 Volvo 对 structural safety 的承诺，可能就买不到 designed 为 safe 的 modern car 了。

大多数 markets 都是如此，只不过它们从一开始就没有过 Volvo 这样 unreasonable 的 firm。谈到 unreasonable employees，Yossi 说：

> 谁能、而且有时确实会，从底部让这条鱼不再腐烂？一个 insane employee。这样的人会把 forks、crashes 等视为对自己的冒犯，并会反复冒着惹恼 management 的风险，努力阻止这些事情。尤其是那些把自己的 political capital——通过做 management 真正看重的事情而辛苦积攒起来的——花在 management 并不真正看重的工作上的人，他们可以坚持抗争很久。有些人坚持到 management 真正改变想法并奖励他们，从而成功把这件事做成了一番事业。无论这种结果的 odds 如何，普通人都无法理解一个试图完成这种壮举的人的 motivation。

愿意耗费大量 personal capital 来做正确事情的人很少，无论正确的事情对那个人意味着什么；但 firm 的 leadership 愿意作出这种选择、消耗 firm 的 capital 来做正确事情的情况，就更少了。

Economists 对这样一种情况有一个 term：information asymmetry 使 buyers 无法区分好的 products 和 "lemons"，这被称为 "a market for lemons"，例如 car market（lemons 这个 term 就来自这里），或者 [hiring market 的双方](https://danluu.com/hiring-lemons/)。在 economic discourse 中，出于各种原因，人们对于 cars 究竟是不是 market for lemons 存在争论（允许人们退回坏车的 lemon laws 似乎并未改变 market 的 operates 方式；如果把 lemons defined 为有严重 reliability problems 的 vehicle，那么 modern cars 中很少有 lemons；等等）。但只看人们是否偶尔买到 defective car，是只见树木、不见森林。也许只有一家 car manufacturer 真正在认真尝试制造 structural safety 超越 standards bodies 所 test 范围的 car（而坊间传闻，他们在日益重要的 software testing 方面却有所省略），因为超出少数 standards bodies 所 test 的水平后，consumers 就无法区分 car 是更 safe 还是较不 safe。这就是一个 market for lemons，几乎所有其他 consumer 和 B2B market 也都是如此。

### 附录：culture

American society 中有件事让我觉得有意思：很多人认为，如果一个人因为没能防备所有可能的意外而在一笔交易中吃了亏，那就是他“活该”（希望做到 highly effective 的 orgs 往往通过建立 "blameless" culture 来避免这种情况，但接触过这种 culture 的人很少）。

最近我在以下一些场合见到了这种情况：

-   有人在咖啡馆被偷了笔记本电脑，却被责怪没有一直盯着电脑，因为任何正常人都绝不会在转头和别人聊两句时，让自己的任何随身物品离开视线 10 seconds
-   有人发布了一则 PSA，说自己因为一家公司的 terms of service 发生 change 而吃了亏，提醒其他人也应留意同样的事情；人们却说，吃亏的人没有逐字阅读收到的每一份 terms of service update，实在太蠢了
-   （在 r/idiotsincars 上见过很多次）：有人遇到了一场按常理很难甚至不可能避免的事故，人们却说他没能避免这场事故，说明他是个糟糕的司机
    -   至少有一次，当事人做了 frame-by-frame analysis，表明自己的反应与人类所能达到的最快反应之间，latency 不超过一个 frame，却仍然被说成应该避免那场事故
    -   人们常常会说“我根本就不会让自己陷入那种处境”之类的话；当事人只是开车经过一辆停着的车时，这就会变成“我绝不会以超过 10mph 的速度驶过一辆车”这样的荒唐说法，仿佛发表评论的人每次经过有停放或临时停靠车辆的街道，都会减速到 10mph。
-   有人在 flyertalk 论坛抱怨，Google maps 的导航指示对不是机器人的人来说不够清楚（例如“在 500 meters 后右转”，这可能指多个路口中的任何一个），人们却回复“我去任何地方前都会完全熟悉路线”之类的话，还说你应该提前规划好所有驾车路线，就像在 1992 年拿着纸质地图进行公路旅行一样（他们以此证明提前规划所有行程是合理的——我当年就是这么做的，现在任何不蠢的人也都会这么做）
    -   作出这类负面回复的人得到了大量 upvotes；[没有人建议改用 Apple Maps，而它会提供清楚、基于 landmark 的 directions，比如“过了红绿灯，然后在下一个路口右转”](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1520012665954865152)

如果你读过这类讨论，经常会看到有人声称“世界就是这样的”，甚至进一步说，世界不可能是别的样子，所以任何没有为此做好准备的人都是白痴。

回到笔记本电脑被盗的例子，任何旅行过，甚至只是读过其他 cultures 相关内容的人，都能观察到，North Americans 认为基本上是 large-scale society 所带来的 immutable consequences 的事情，其实是 arbitrary 的。例如，如果你把包和笔记本电脑留在 Korea 一家咖啡馆的桌上，几个小时后再回来，包和电脑极有可能还在那里[我听说 Japan 也是如此](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=30625890)。虽然这样占着一张桌子很不礼貌，但你的包和笔记本电脑不太可能被偷。

而且，事实上，只要稍微调整一下情境，America 基本上也是如此。走进一间空房子并从里面偷走东西（[学会撬锁相当容易](https://www.youtube.com/c/lockpickinglawyer)，直接打破窗户就更容易了），并不比从咖啡馆偷东西难多少。然而，在 America 的大多数社区，人们很少遭遇入室盗窃；有人发帖说自己遭遇入室盗窃时，也不会因为没有看好自己的房子而被痛骂是蠢货。相反，人们大多会表示同情。无人看管的财物在 public spaces 被偷被认为是正常的，在 private spaces 则不是，但这与其说是 technical distinction，不如说是 cultural distinction。

Avery Pennarun 讲过一组相关的故事，谈的是一个 American 在 Korea 经历的 culture shock。其中一个故事说到一种有点像 Amazon 的 online ordering service。在 Amazon 上，你订购东西后，会收到一个上面有多个 bar/QR/other codes 的箱子；打开后，里面还有一个箱子，上面至少有一个另外的 code。当然，外面的箱子需要 barcode，因为它要经过某个 at-scale 运作的 facility，那里的任何人都不知道箱子里是什么、该送往哪里；而里面的箱子也必须经过其他某种 process，而且如果这件商品在 retailer 出售，它还需要能被 checkout machine scanned。商品就在里面那个箱子里。如果需要退货，你要把商品放回带有 barcode 的箱子，再把那个箱子放进 shipping box，然后在 shipping box 上贴上另一个 barcode，再把它寄出去。

而在 Korea，有一种类似 Amazon 的服务，你可以订购一件商品，一两个小时后，就会听到敲门声。走到门口时，你会看到一个没有标签的箱子或袋子，商品就在这个没有标签的容器里。如果想退货，你就在 app 上“告诉”它你要退货，把商品放回容器，放到门口，他们就会把它取走。看到这种与 U.S. 截然不同的 shipping setup 后，他问别人：“他们怎么可能不会弄混哪个箱子是哪个呢？”得到的回答是：“他们为什么会弄混哪个箱子是哪个呢？”他的其他故事也有类似的感觉：他描述某件非常陌生的事情，问当地人事情怎么能以这种陌生的方式运作，而当地人根本想象不出事情还可以有其他运作方式，回答说：“X 为什么会行不通呢？”

和咖啡馆里笔记本电脑的例子一样，Avery 的许多故事归根结底都在说明，对于人和 organizations 可以如何运作，人们有着截然不同的 shared cultural expectations。

这方面的另一个例子是 covid。我的许多朋友在过去几年里的大部分时间都住在 Vietnam 或 Taiwan 这样的 Asian countries，那里有低得多的 covid rates，以至于几乎完全没有实行过 locked down。我在那些国家的朋友基本上能够过正常生活，仿佛 covid 根本不存在（至少在最新的 variants 出现之前是这样；到那时，他们已经 vaccinated，而且发生最 serious outcomes 的 risk 相对较低），同时承担的 getting covid 的 risk 基本为零。

在大多数 western countries，许多人最初的 public opinion 是，locking down 毫无意义，而且我们无法采取任何措施来 prevent covid 的 explosion。我认识的多位 engineers 理解 exponential growth，也知道这意味着什么，却仍在 lockdown 之前继续正常活动，随后 got covid，而且（很可能）spread covid。实行 lockdowns 后，要求尽早 lift 它们的压力极大，结果[出现了类似这篇文章中 "adaptive response" diagram 的情况](http://vihart.com/how-we-reopen/)。此后，许多人（我有一个汇总这方面 public opinion 的 project，但不确定自己是否会把它的优先级提到足以完成的程度）把看法改成了：“曾经实行 locked down 就是愚蠢的，我们最终总归会面临 endemic covid，所有这些 economic damage 都毫无意义。”如果我们查看 in-person retail sales data 或 restaurant data，就很容易发现，在 pandemic 最初大约一年、virus 处于 broad circulation 的时候，许多人在 lockdowns 前后都在自愿限制自己的活动。

与此同时，在 Taiwan 和 Vietnam 等一些 Asian countries，人们在 lockdowns 实行后大多 complied with 它们，这意味着每当 outbreaks 发生时，他们都能够在国内 squash covid，直到相对较近的时候，covid mutated 成了 spread 容易得多的 forms，而人们对 covid risk 的 tolerance 也因 vaccinations 而大幅提高。当然，covid 不断被 reintroduced 到能够 squash 它的国家，因为其他国家做不到，而后者在很大程度上源于一种 self-fulfilling belief：认为 squash covid 是不可能的。

回到什么时候把某项工作 bring something in-house 才合理这个问题：即使有些情况下，乍听之下似乎不该这么做，因为 expertise 有 99% 的时间处于 idle，或者必须由一个人 build 一套 software，而单是一家 firm 就愿意每年为它支付数百万美元，这在很大程度上仍取决于你所处的 culture 是否允许你 trust 另一家 firm 的 promise。如果你所在的 society 普遍预期其他 firms 会针对你谈妥的任何 contract，都把你逼到 letter of the law 的边界，那么，即使只是为了获得相当于 in house 人员所提供的 service 一半水平的 service，花力气 negotiate 一份 contract 也常常不值得。如果你看看这些 contracts 最后是如何措辞的，就会发现 companies [经常试图偷偷加入让 contract 失去意义的 terms](https://www.patreon.com/posts/21164582)；即使你成功排除了所有这些问题，legally enforcing the contract 也代价高昂。在我知道的那些 companies 经常 violated 其 support SLA agreement 的案例中（这只是个例子），最终的 resolution 是 terminate the contract，而不是 pursue legal action，因为 legal action 的 cost 相对于任何可能获得的收益都不值得。

如果你无法 trust 其他 firms，而又希望工作能够 work，那么在是否把它们 bringing things in house 这件事上，你往往别无选择。

虽然这其实是另一篇文章的话题，但我想指出，存在于 companies 之间的 lack of trust，如果也存在于内部，同样会 hamstring companies。[正如我们先前讨论过的，许多 larger scale brokenness 也源于 organizations 内部的 cultural expectations](https://danluu.com/culture/)。其中一个会导致 pervasive organizational problems 的具体例子，就是 organization 内部的 lack of trust。例如，不久前，我曾向一位 director 抱怨一位 VP broke a promise，而且我们正因为类似的原因 losing 很多人。那位 director 的回答是：“那位 VP 不可能 made a promise。”我要求他解释清楚，他解释说：“除非你把它写进 contract，否则那就不是 promise。”也就是说，这家 company 的 VPs lie 的 rate 高到让 VP 的 verbal commitment 毫无价值；只有允许你把他们告上法庭的 legally binding commitment 才有意义。

当然，这很荒谬，因为没有人能够在 BigCo 工作时，四处要求别人为所有 promises 都签订 contracts，否则立刻就会被当成某种极度官僚主义的怪人。不过，我们不妨认真对待这句话的本意：只 trust 与你关系密切的人。在我曾经工作的那家 company，这是个好建议；但对那家 company 而言，不幸的是，它的 implications 与 inter-firm 的例子类似。我们在那里指出过，一种要求你围绕 letter of the law 进行 litigate 的 norm，代价高昂到足以让 firms 经常选择把 expertise bring expertise in house，以免处理那些细节。在 intra-firm 的情况下，你会经常看到 teams 和 orgs 进行 "empire build"，因为他们知道，[至少在 management level，他们无法 trust 自己 fiefdom 之外的任何人](https://amzn.to/3pDnYUr)。

虽然这种 intra-firm lack of trust 的 cost 通常低于 inter-firm lack of trust，因为对于造成 major blocker 的 organization，有更好的 levers 可以促使它采取行动，但[它的 cost 仍然相当高](https://amzn.to/3pDnYUr)。我交谈过的几乎所有 VPs 和 BigCo tech execs，都深深浸润于自己所处的 culture，以至于无法想象另一种可能；但并没有什么 inherent reason，要求 organizations 必须那样 work。我曾在两家 companies 工作，那里的人们确实 trust leadership，而 leadership 即使在你无法把他们告上法庭的情况下，通常也确实会兑现 commitments，其中包括我现在的 employer，Wave。但是，在其他 companies，大家普遍抱有的 expectation——leadership 不可信，而且也不应被 trusted——会“导致”最终进入 leadership roles 的人变得 untrustworthy，从而造成我们刚刚讨论过的 inefficiencies。

人们常常认为，随着 company scales，高度的 internal distrust 不可避免；但我交谈过的一些人，曾在 Intel 和 Google 担任 upper management，或者与最高层相当接近，他们说，这两家 companies 都曾有过一段很长的时期，leadership 会 enforced trustworthiness，而且在 Andy Grove 和 Eric Schmidt 分别领导期间，杜绝 dishonesty 和 "bad politics" 是各自 company 如此 successful 的一个主要原因。当最高层的人换了，新来的人不再 enforced honesty 时，你在大多数大 companies 的 upper levels 所见的那些常见 cultural norms 就渗透进来了，但这并非不可避免。

当我与那些未曾接触过 BigCo leadership culture、也未曾见过 decisions 实际上如何 made 的人交谈时，他们往往觉得这些 decision making processes 难以置信；这与深受 BigCo leadership culture 熏陶的人，觉得一家大 company 居然能够以其他任何方式 operate 也难以置信，几乎如出一辙。

从内部往往很难看出一个 system 有多荒谬。另一个观察角度是，Americans 常常觉得 Japanese universities 和 [Japanese engineering firms 的 work practices](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1428848459071819779) 荒谬，不过通常还不如 [Korean chaebols 的 promotion policies 荒谬，后者以 nepotistic 著称](https://english.hani.co.kr/arti/english_edition/e_business/823025.html)。例如，Chung Mong-yong 是 Hyundai Sungwoo 的 CEO，因为他是 Chung Soon-yung 的儿子；而 Chung Soon-yung 曾是 Hyundai Sungwoo 的 head，因为他是 Hyundai Group 的 founder Chung Ju-yung 的弟弟（Hyundai Group 基本上就是 top-level Hyundai corporation），等等。但是，尽管这些 practices 在 Americans 看来荒谬地 inefficient，在 software industry 之外，Japanese 和 Korean engineering firms 总体上并不比 American engineering firms 更不 efficient。[American firms 并不是在更 efficient 的情况下失去在多个 industries 中的 dominance 的](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1439511588529065987)；真要说的话，恰恰是 market inefficiencies 让它们保住 marketshare 的时间，远远超过了仅看它们 products 的 technical merit 时你会天真预期的时间。

American firms 中也存在相互抵消的 inefficiencies，其荒谬程度不亚于 Korean chaebols 实际上实行的 company leadership 的 familiar succession。只不过，对 system 内部的人来说，American cultural practices 所造成的 inefficiencies，似乎是这个世界不可改变的事实。但当你观察具有完全不同 cultures 的 firms 时，就会清楚地看到，cultural norms 并不是 law of nature。

### 附录：build 的弊端

当然，以 building 代替 buying 并不是万灵药。我经常见到一些 internal designs，它们和本文描述的 data sync product 一样 broken。一般来说，当你看到这样的 design 时，[在 design phase，就已经有不少人解释过为什么这个 design 永远不可能 work](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1470890510998851588)，但他们被忽视了。虽然 "build" 比 "buy" 给你多得多的 control，而且因为你可以 influence 这个 design，让你更有可能得到一个能够 works 的 product，但一个 dysfunctional org 中的 dysfunctional team 也很容易 make 出不能 work 的 products。

Steve Jobs 有一段关于 companies 的话，也适用于 teams：

> 事实证明，同样的事情也可能发生在获得 monopolies 的 technology companies 中，比如 IBM 或 Xerox。假如你是 IBM 或 Xerox 的 product person，你做出了一个更好的 copier 或 computer。那又怎样？当你拥有 monopoly market share 时，company 并不会因此更加 successful。
> 
> 所以，能够让 company 更加 successful 的人是 sales 和 marketing 人员，最后 running 这些 companies 的就成了他们。而 product 人员被赶出了 decision making forums，这些 companies 也就忘记了 make 出出色的 products 意味着什么。那些让它们达到那个 monopolistic position 的 product sensibility 和 product genius，被 running 这些 companies 的人侵蚀殆尽，因为这些人根本不懂 good product 与 bad product 的区别。
> 
> 他们根本不懂，把一个 good idea 变成 good product 需要怎样的 craftsmanship。而且，他们通常也并没有发自内心地真正想要帮助 customers。"

出于 "efficiency" 的考虑，一些 large companies 试图避免 duplicate effort；如果某些 projects 看起来与另一个 project 太过相似，就会 kill 它们，从而让拥有某个 product 的 canonical verison 的 team 获得 monopoly。如果 company 没有努力做正确事情的 culture，就会出现 Steve Jobs 所讨论的同样问题，只不过是在 team 和 org level，而不是 company level。

我曾所在的一个 team 采用的 workaround，基本上就是 re-implement 一套 parallel stack，替代我们所依赖的那些不能 work 的东西。但这之所以可能，只是因为 leadership 基本上什么都没能 enforce。讽刺的是，这还是他们竭尽全力之后的结果——[leadership 曾多次大举尝试 impose top-down control](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1494696655928508418)，但他们不懂如何 influence 一个 organization，所以这些尝试都失败了。假如 leadership 成功了，company 的状况反而会糟糕得多。[当 leadership 有好的 plans 时，effective top-down direction 是有好处的](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1487239027002527744)，但当时这根本不是现实的可能性，所以 leadership 不知道如何 execute，实际上反而更好。

## 原文

There's a [cocktail party version](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/) of the efficient markets hypothesis I frequently hear that's basically, "markets enforce efficiency, so it's not possible that a company can have some major inefficiency and survive". [We've previously discussed Marc Andreessen's quote that tech hiring can't be inefficient here](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/) and [here](https://danluu.com/talent/):

> Let's launch right into it. I think the critique that Silicon Valley companies are deliberately, systematically discriminatory is incorrect, and there are two reasons to believe that that's the case. ... No. 2, our companies are desperate for talent. Desperate. Our companies are dying for talent. They're like lying on the beach gasping because they can't get enough talented people in for these jobs. The motivation to go find talent wherever it is unbelievably high.

Variants of this idea that I frequently hear engineers and VCs repeat involve companies being efficient and/or products being basically as good as possible because, if it were possible for them to be better, someone would've outcompeted them and done it already[1](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:S).

There's a vague plausibility to that kind of statement, which is why it's [a debate I've often heard come up in casual conversation](https://danluu.com/cocktail-ideas/), where one person will point out some obvious company inefficiency or product error and someone else will respond that, if it's so obvious, someone at the company would have fixed the issue or another company would've come along and won based on being more efficient or better. Talking purely abstractly, it's hard to settle the debate, but things are clearer if we look at some specifics, as in the two examples above about hiring, where we can observe that, whatever abstract arguments people make, inefficiencies persisted for decades.

When it comes to buying products and services, at a personal level, most people I know who've checked the work of people they've hired for things like home renovation or [accounting](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1477072484092375040) have found grievous errors in the work. Although it's possible to find people who don't do shoddy work, [it's generally difficult for someone who isn't an expert in the field to determine if someone is going to do shoddy work in the field](https://twitter.com/pushcx/status/1499381895653699585). You can try to get better quality by paying more, but once you get out of the very bottom end of the market, it's frequently unclear how to trade money for quality, e.g., my friends and colleagues who've gone with large, brand name, accounting firms have paid much more than people who go with small, local, accountants and gotten a higher error rate; as a strategy, trying expensive local accountants hasn't really fared much better. The good accountants are typically somewhat expensive, but they're generally not charging the highest rates and only a small percentage of somewhat expensive accountants are good.

More generally, in many markets, consumers are uninformed and [it's fairly difficult to figure out which products are even half decent, let alone good](https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/#appendix-capitalism). When people happen to choose a product or service that's right for them, it's often for the wrong reasons. For example, in my social circles, there have been two waves of people migrating from iPhones to Android phones over the past few years. Both waves happened due to Apple PR snafus which caused a lot of people to think that iPhones were terrible at something when, in fact, they were better at that thing than Android phones. Luckily, iPhones aren't strictly superior to Android phones and many people who switched got a device that was better for them because they were previously using an iPhone due to good Apple PR, causing their errors to cancel out. But, when people are mostly making decisions off of marketing and PR and don't have access to good information, there's no particular reason to think that a product being generally better or even strictly superior will result in that winning and the worse product losing. In capital markets, we don't need all that many informed participants to think that some form of the efficient market hypothesis holds ensuring "prices reflect all available information". It's a truism that published results about market inefficiencies stop being true the moment they're published because people exploit the inefficiency until it disappears. But with the job market examples, even though firms can take advantage of mispriced labor, as Greenspan famously did before becoming Chairman of the fed, inefficiencies can persist:

> Townsend-Greenspan was unusual for an economics firm in that the men worked for the women (we had about twenty-five employees in all). My hiring of women economists was not motivated by women's liberation. It just made great business sense. I valued men and women equally, and found that because other employers did not, good women economists were less expensive than men. Hiring women . . . gave Townsend-Greenspan higher-quality work for the same money . . .

But as we also saw, individual firms exploiting mispriced labor have a limited demand for labor and inefficiencies can persist for decades because the firms that are acting on "all available information" don't buy enough labor to move the price of mispriced people to where it would be if most or all firms were acting rationally.

In the abstract, it seems that, with products and services, inefficiencies should also be able to persist for a long time since, similarly, there also isn't a mechanism that allows actors in the system to exploit the inefficiency in a way that directly converts money into more money, and sometimes there isn't really even a mechanism to make almost any money at all. For example, if you observe that it's silly for people to move from iPhones to Android phones because they think that Apple is engaging in nefarious planned obsolescence when Android devices generally become obsolete more quickly, due to a combination of [iPhones getting updates for longer](https://danluu.com/android-updates/) and iPhones being faster at every price point they compete at, allowing the phone to be used on [bloated sites](https://danluu.com/web-bloat/) [for longer](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/919423480776613888), you can't really make money off of this observation. This is unlike a mispriced asset that you can buy derivatives of to make money (in expectation).

A common suggestion to the problem of not knowing what product or service is good is to ask an expert in the field or a credentialed person, but [this often fails as well](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1324495890548056065). For example, a friend of mine had trouble sleeping because his window air conditioner was loud and would wake him up when it turned on. He asked a trusted friend of his who works on air conditioners if this could be improved by getting a newer air conditioner and his friend said "no; air conditioners are basically all the same". But any consumer who's compared items with motors in them would immediately know that this is false. Engineers have gotten much better at producing quieter devices when holding power and cost constant. My friend eventually bought a newer, quieter, air conditioner, which solved his sleep problem, but he had the problem for longer than he needed to because he assumed that someone whose job it is to work on air conditioners would give him non-terrible advice about air conditioners. If my friend were an expert on air conditioners or had compared the noise levels of otherwise comparable consumer products over time, he could've figured out that he shouldn't trust his friend, but if he had that level of expertise, he wouldn't have needed advice in the first place.

So far, we've looked at the difficulty of getting the right product or service at a personal level, but this problem also exists at the firm level and is often worse because the markets tend to be thinner, with fewer products available as well as opaque, "call us" pricing. Some commonly repeated advice is that firms should focus on their "core competencies" and outsource everything else (e.g., Joel Spolsky, Gene Kim, Will Larson, Camille Fournier, etc., all say this), but if we look mid-sized tech companies, we can see that they often need to have in-house expertise that's far outside what anyone would consider their core competency unless, e.g., [every social media company has kernel expertise as a core competency](https://danluu.com/in-house/). In principle, firms can outsource this kind of work, but people I know who've relied on outsourcing, e.g., kernel expertise to consultants or application engineers on a support contract, have been very unhappy with the results compared to what they can get by hiring dedicated engineers, both in absolute terms (support frequently doesn't come up with a satisfactory resolution in weeks or months, even when it's one a good engineer could solve in days) and for the money (despite engineers being expensive, large support contracts can often cost more than an engineer while delivering worse service than an engineer).

This problem exists not only for support but also for products a company could buy instead of build. For example, Ben Kuhn, the CTO of Wave, has [a Twitter thread about some of the issues we've run into at Wave](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1324493738304036864), with [a couple](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1382325921311563779) of [followups](https://twitter.com/benskuhn/status/1496167942744346624). Ben now believes that one of the big mistakes he made as CTO was not putting much more effort into vendor selection, even when the decision appeared to be a slam dunk, and more strongly considering moving many systems to custom in-house versions sooner. Even after selecting the consensus best product in the space from the leading (as in largest and most respected) firm, and using the main offering the company has, the product often not only doesn't work but, by design, can't work.

For example, we tried "buy" instead of "build" for a product that syncs data from Postgres to Snowflake. Syncing from Postrgres is the main offering (as in the offering with the most customers) from a leading data sync company, and we found that it would lose data, duplicate data, and corrupt data. After digging into it, it turns out that the product has a design that, among other issues, relies on the data source being able to seek backwards on its changelog. But Postgres throws changelogs away once they're consumed, so the Postgres data source can't support this operation. When their product attempts to do this and the operation fails, we end up with the sync getting "stuck", needing manual intervention from the vendor's operator and/or data loss. Since our data is still on Postgres, it's possible to recover from this by doing a full resync, but the data sync product tops out at 5MB/s for reasons that appear to be unknown to them, so a full resync can take days even on databases that aren't all that large. Resyncs will also silently drop and corrupt data, so multiple cycles of full resyncs followed by data integrity checks are sometimes necessary to recover from data corruption, which can take weeks. Despite being widely recommended and the leading product in the space, the product has a number of major design flaws that mean that it literally cannot work.

This isn't so different from Mongo or other products that had fundamental design flaws that caused severe data loss, with [the main difference being that, in most areas, there isn't a Kyle Kingsbury who spends years publishing tests on various products in the field, patiently responding to bogus claims about correctness until the PR backlash caused companies in the field to start taking correctness seriously](https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/). Without that pressure, most software products basically don't work, hence the Twitter threads from Ben, above, where he notes that the "buy" solutions you might want to choose mostly don't work[2](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:V). Of course, at our scale, there are many things we're not going to build any time soon, like CPUs, but, for many things where the received wisdom is to "buy", "build" seems like a reasonable option. This is even true for larger companies and building CPUs. Fifteen years ago, high-performance (as in, non-embedded level of performance) CPUs were a canonical example of something it would be considered bonkers to build in-house, absurd for even the largest software companies, but Apple and Amazon have been able to produce best-in-class CPUs on the dimensions they're optimizing for, for predictable reasons[3](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:Q).

This isn't just an issue that impacts tech companies; we see this across many different industries. For example, any company that wants to mail items to customers has to either implement shipping themselves or deal with the fallout of having unreliable shipping. As a user, whether or not packages get shipped to you depends a lot on where you live and what kind of building you live in.

When I've lived in a house, packages have usually arrived regardless of the shipper (although they've often arrived late). But, since moving into apartment buildings, some buildings just don't get deliveries from certain delivery services. Once, I lived in a building where the postal service didn't deliver mail properly and I didn't get a lot of mail (although I frequently got mail addressed to other people in the building as well as people elsewhere). More commonly, UPS and Fedex usually won't attempt to deliver and will just put a bunch of notices up on the building door for all the packages they didn't deliver, where the notice falsely indicates that the person wasn't home and correctly indicates that, to get the package, the person has to go to some pick-up location to get the package.

For a while, I lived in a city where Amazon used 3rd-party commercial courier services to do last-mile shipping for same-day delivery. The services they used were famous for marking things as delivered without delivering the item for days, making "same day" shipping slower than next day or even two day shipping. Once, I naively contacted Amazon support because my package had been marked as delivered but wasn't delivered. Support, using a standard script supplied to them by Amazon, told me that I should contact them again three days after the package was marked as delivered because couriers often mark packages as delivered without delivering them, but they often deliver the package within a few days. Amazon knew that the courier service they were using didn't really even try to deliver packages[4](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:P) when they said they did and the only short-term mitigation available to them was to tell support to tell people that they shouldn't expect that packages have arrived when they've been marked as delivered.

Amazon eventually solved this problem by having their own delivery people or using, by commercial shipping standards, an extremely expensive service (Apple has done for same-day delivery)[5](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:A). At scale, there's no commercial service you can pay for that will reliably attempt to deliver packages. If you want a service that actually works, you're generally on the hook for building it yourself, just like in the software world. My local grocery store tried to outsource this to DoorDash. I've tried delivery 3 times from my grocery store and my groceries have showed up 2 out of 3 times, which is well below what most people would consider an acceptable hit rate for grocery delivery (and when groceries did arrive, some items I didn't order and didn't get charged were also delivered to me instead to whoever ordered them). Having to build instead of buy to get reliability is a huge drag on productivity, especially for smaller companies (e.g., it's not possible for small shops that want to compete with Amazon and mail products to customers to have reliable delivery since they can't build out their own delivery service).

The amount of waste generated by the inability to farm out services is staggering and I've seen it everywhere I've worked. An example from another industry: when I worked at a small chip startup, we had in-house capability to do end-to-end chip processing (with the exception of having its own fabs), which is unusual for a small chip startup. When the first wafer of a new design came off of a fab, we'd have the wafer flown to us on a flight, at which point someone would use a wafer saw to cut the wafer into individual chips so we could start testing ASAP. This was often considered absurd in the same way that it would be considered absurd for a small software startup to manage its own on-prem hardware. After all, the wafer saw and the expertise necessary to go from a wafer to a working chip will be idle over 99% of the time. Having full-time equipment and expertise that you use less than 1% of the time is a classic example of the kind of thing you should outsource, but if you price out having people competent to do this plus having the equipment available to do it, even at fairly low volumes, it's cheaper to do it in-house even if the equipment and expertise for it are idle 99% of the time. More importantly, you'll get much better service (faster turnaround) in house, letting you ship at a higher cadence. I've both worked at companies that have tried to contract this kind of thing out as well as talked with many people who've done that and you get slower, less reliable, service at a higher cost.

Likewise with chip software tooling; despite it being standard to outsource tooling to [large EDA vendors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_design_automation), we got a lot of mileage out using our own custom tools, generally created or maintained by one person, e.g., while I was there, most simulator cycles were run on a custom simulator that was maintained by one person, which saved millions a year in simulator costs (standard pricing for a simulator at the time was a few thousand dollars per license per year and we had a farm of about a thousand simulation machines). You might think that, if a single person can create or maintain a tool that's worth millions of dollars a year to the company, our competitors would do the same thing, just like you might think that if you can ship faster and at a lower cost by hiring a person who knows how to crack a wafer open, our competitors would do that, but they mostly didn't.

[Joel Spolsky has an old post where he says](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/823296808919019520):

> “Find the dependencies — and eliminate them.” When you're working on a really, really good team with great programmers, everybody else's code, frankly, is bug-infested garbage, and nobody else knows how to ship on time.

We had a similar attitude, although I'd say that we were a bit more humble. We didn't think that everyone else was producing garbage but, we also didn't assume that we couldn't produce something comparable to what we could buy for a tenth of the cost. From talking to folks at some competitors, there was a [pretty big cultural difference](https://danluu.com/culture/) between how we operated and how they operated. It simply didn't occur to them that they didn't have to buy into the standard American business logic that you should focus on your core competencies, that you can think through whether or not it makes sense to do something in-house on the merits of the particular thing instead of outsourcing your thinking to a pithy saying.

I once watched, from the inside, a company undergo this cultural shift. A few people in leadership decided that the company should focus on its core competencies, which meant abandoning custom software for infrastructure. This resulted in quite a few large migrations from custom internal software to SaaS solutions and open source software. If you watched the discussions on "why" various projects should or shouldn't migrate, there were a few unusually unreasonable people who tried to reason through particular cases on the merits of each case (in a post on pushing back against orders from the top, [Yossi Kreinin calls these people insane employees](https://yosefk.com/blog/people-can-read-their-managers-mind.html); I'm going to refer to the same concept in this post, but instead call people who do this unusually unreasonable). But, for the most part, people bought the party line and pushed for a migration regardless of the specifics.

The thing that I thought was interesting was that leadership didn't tell particular teams they had to migrate and there weren't really negative consequences for teams where an "unusually unreasonable person" pushed back in order to keep running an existing system for reasonable reasons. Instead, people mostly bought into the idea and tried to justify migrations for vaguely plausible sounding reasons that weren't connected to reality, resulting in funny outcomes like moving to an open source system "to save money" when the new system was quite obviously less efficient[6](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:M) and, predictably, required much higher capex and opex. The cost savings was supposed to come from shrinking the team, but the increase in operational cost dominated the change in the cost of the team and the complexity of operating the system meant that the team size increased instead of decreasing. There were a number of cases where it really did make sense to migrate, but the stated reasons for migration tended to be unrelated or weakly related to the reasons it actually made sense to migrate. Once people absorbed the idea that the company should focus on core competencies, the migrations were driven by the cultural idea and not any technical reasons.

The pervasiveness of [decisions like the above, technical decisions made without serious technical consideration, is a major reason that the selection pressure on companies to make good products is so weak](https://danluu.com/bad-decisions/). There is some pressure, but it's noisy enough that successful companies often route around making a product that works, like in the Mongo example from above, where Mongo's decision to loudly repeat [demonstrably bogus performance claims and making demonstrably false correctness claims](https://aphyr.com/tags/mongodb) was, from a business standpoint, superior to focusing on actual correctness and performance; by focusing their resources where it mattered for the business, they managed to outcompete companies that made the mistake of devoting serious resources to performance and correctness.

Yossi's post about how an unusually unreasonable person can have outsized impact in a dimension they value at their firm also applies to impact outside of a firm. Kyle Kingsbury, mentioned above, is an example of this. At the rates that I've heard [Jepsen](https://jepsen.io/) is charging now, Kyle can bring in what a senior developer at BigCo does (actually senior, not someone with the title "senior"), but that was after years of working long hours at below market rates on an uncertain endeavour, refuting [FUD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear,_uncertainty,_and_doubt) from his critics (if you read the replies to the linked posts or, worse yet, the actual tickets where he's involved in discussions with developers, the replies to Kyle were a constant stream of nonsense for many years, including people working for vendors [feeling like he has it out for them in particular, casting aspersions on his character](https://lobste.rs/s/jgfyep/addio_redis_i_m_leaving_redis_labs#c_i6kcci)[7](https://danluu.com/nothing-works/#fn:J), and [generally trashing him](https://lobste.rs/s/jgfyep/addio_redis_i_m_leaving_redis_labs#c_sae3wo)). I have a deep respect for people who are willing to push on issues like this despite the system being aligned against them but, my respect notwithstanding, basically no one is going to do that. A system that requires someone like Kyle to take a stand before successful firms will put effort into correctness instead of correctness marketing is going to produce a lot of products that are good at marketing correctness without really having decent correctness properties (such as the data sync product mentioned in this post, whose website repeatedly mentions how reliable and safe the syncing product is despite having a design that is fundamentally broken).

It's also true at the firm level that it often takes an unusually unreasonable firm to produce a really great product instead of just one that's marketed as great, e.g., [Volvo, the one car manufacturer that seemed to try to produce a level of structural safety beyond what could be demonstrated by IIHS tests](https://danluu.com/car-safety/) fared so poorly as a business that it's been forced to move upmarket and became a niche, luxury, automaker since safety isn't something consumers are really interested in despite car accidents being a leading cause of death and a significant source of life expectancy loss. And it's not clear that Volvo will be able to persist in being an unreasonable firm since they weren't able to survive as an independent automaker. When Ford acquired Volvo, [Ford started moving Volvos to the shared Ford C1 platform, which didn't fare particularly well in crash tests](https://danluu.com/car-safety/#quality-may-change-over-time). Since Geely has acquired Volvo, [it's too early to tell for sure if they'll maintain Volvo's commitment to designing for real-world crash data and not just crash data that gets reported in benchmarks](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1458814317948661768). If Geely declines to continue Volvo's commitment to structural safety, it may not be possible to buy a modern car that's designed to be safe.

Most markets are like this, except that there was never an unreasonable firm like Volvo in the first place. On unreasonable employees, Yossi says

> Who can, and sometimes does, un-rot the fish from the bottom? An insane employee. Someone who finds the forks, crashes, etc. a personal offence, and will repeatedly risk annoying management by fighting to stop these things. Especially someone who spends their own political capital, hard earned doing things management truly values, on doing work they don't truly value – such a person can keep fighting for a long time. Some people manage to make a career out of it by persisting until management truly changes their mind and rewards them. Whatever the odds of that, the average person cannot comprehend the motivation of someone attempting such a feat.

It's rare that people are willing to expend a significant amount of personal capital to do the right thing, whatever that means to someone, but it's even rarer that the leadership of a firm will make that choice and spend down the firm's capital to do the right thing.

Economists have a term for cases where information asymmetry means that buyers can't tell the difference between good products and "lemons", "a market for lemons", like the car market (where the term lemons comes from), or [both sides of the hiring market](https://danluu.com/hiring-lemons/). In economic discourse, there's a debate over whether cars are a market for lemons at all for a variety of reasons (lemon laws, which allow people to return bad cars, don't appear to have changed how the market operates, very few modern cars are lemons when that's defined as a vehicle with serious reliability problems, etc.). But looking at whether or not people occasionally buy a defective car is missing the forest for the trees. There's maybe one car manufacturer that really seriously tries to make a structurally safe car beyond what standards bodies test (and word on the street is that they skimp on the increasingly important software testing side of things) because consumers can't tell the difference between a more or less safe car beyond the level a few standards bodies test to. That's a market for lemons, as is nearly every other consumer and B2B market.

### Appendix: culture

Something I find interesting about American society is how many people think that someone who gets the raw end of a deal because they failed to protect themselves against every contingency "deserves" what happened (orgs that want to be highly effective often avoid this by having a "blameless" culture, but very few people have exposure to such a culture).

Some places I've seen this recently:

-   Person had a laptop stolen in a cafe; blamed for not keeping their eye on the laptop the entire time since no reasonable person would ever let their eyes off of any belongings for 10 seconds as they turned their head to briefly chat with someone
-   Person posted a PSA that they were caught out by a change in the terms of service of a company and other people should be aware of the same thing, people said that the person caught out was dumb for not reading every word of every terms of service update they're sent
-   (many times, on r/idiotsincars): person gets in an accident that would've been difficult or impossible to reasonably avoid and people tell the person they're a terrible driver for not having avoided the accident
    -   At least once, the person did a frame-by-frame analysis that showed that they reacted to, within one frame of latency, as fast as humanly possible, and was still told they should've avoided the accident
    -   Often, people will say things like "I would never get into that situation in the first place", which, in the circumstance where someone is driving past a parked car, results in absurd statements like "I would never pass a vehicle at more than 10mph", as if the person making the comment slows down to 10mph on every street that has parked or stopped cars on it.
-   Person griped on flyertalk forum that Google maps instructions are unclear if you're not a robot (e.g., "turn right in 500 meters", which could be one of multiple intersections) and people responded with things like "I never go anywhere without being completely familiar with the route" and that you should map out all of your driving beforehand, just like you would for a road trip with a paper map in 1992 (this was used as a justification for the reasonableness of mapping out all travel beforehand – I did it back then and anyone who isn't dumb would do it now)
    -   People with those kinds of negative responses were highly upvoted; [no one suggested switching to Apple Maps, which gives clear, landmark based directions like "go through the light and then take the next right"](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1520012665954865152)

If you read these kinds of discussions, you'll often see people claiming "that's just how the world is" and going further and saying that there is no other way the world could be, so anyone who isn't prepared for that is an idiot.

Going back to the laptop theft example, anyone who's traveled, or even read about other cultures, can observe that the things that North Americans think are basically immutable consequences of a large-scale society are arbitrary. For example, if you leave your bag and laptop on a table at a cafe in Korea and come back hours later, the bag and laptop are overwhelmingly likely to be there [I've heard this is true in Japan as well](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=30625890). While it's rude to take up a table like that, you're not likely to have your bag and laptop stolen.

And, in fact, if you tweak the context slightly, this is basically true in America. It's not much harder to walk into an empty house and steal things out of the house (it's fairly [easy to learn how to pick locks](https://www.youtube.com/c/lockpickinglawyer) and even easier to just break a window) than it is to steal things out of a cafe. And yet, in most neighbourhoods in America, people are rarely burglarized and when someone posts about being burglarized, they're not excoriated for being a moron for not having kept an eye on their house. Instead, people are mostly sympathetic. It's considered normal to have unattended property stolen in public spaces and not in private spaces, but that's more of a cultural distinction than a technical distinction.

There's a related set of stories Avery Pennarun tells about the culture shock of being an American in Korea. One of them is about some online ordering service you can use that's sort of like Amazon. With Amazon, when you order something, you get a box with multiple bar/QR/other codes on it and, when you open it up, there's another box inside that has at least one other code on it. Of course the other box needs the barcode because it's being shipped through some facility at-scale where no one knows what the box is or where it needs to go and the inner box also had to go through some other kind of process and it also needs to be able to be scanned by a checkout machine if the item is sold at a retailer. Inside the inner box is the item. If you need to return the item, you put the item back into its barcoded box and then put that box into the shipping box and then slap another barcode onto the shipping box and then mail it out.

So, in Korea, there's some service like Amazon where you can order an item and, an hour or two later, you'll hear a knock at your door. When you get to the door, you'll see an unlabeled box or bag and the item is in the unlabeled container. If you want to return the item, you "tell" the app that you want to return the item, put it back into its container, put it in front of your door, and they'll take it back. After seeing this shipping setup, which is wildly different from what you see in the U.S., he asked someone "how is it possible that they don't lose track of which box is which?". The answer he got was, "why would they lose track of which box is which?". His other stories have a similar feel, where he describes something quite alien, asks a local how things can work in this alien way, who can't imagine things working any other way and response with "why would X not work?"

As with the laptop in cafe example, a lot of Avery's stories come down to how there are completely different shared cultural expectations around how people and organizations can work.

Another example of this is with covid. Many of my friends have spent most of the last couple of years in Asian countries like Vietnam or Taiwan, which have had much lower covid rates, so much so that they were barely locked down at all. My friends in those countries were basically able to live normal lives, as if covid didn't exist at all (at least until the latest variants, at which point they were vaccinated and at relatively low risk for the most serious outcomes), while taking basically zero risk of getting covid.

In most western countries, initial public opinion among many people was that locking down was pointless and there was nothing we could do to prevent an explosion of covid. Multiple engineers I know, who understand exponential growth and knew what the implications were, continued normal activities before lockdown and got and (probably) spread covid. When lockdowns were implemented, there was tremendous pressure to lift them as early as possible, resulting [in something resembling the "adaptive response" diagram from this post](http://vihart.com/how-we-reopen/). Since then, many people (I have a project tallying up public opinion on this that I'm not sure I'll ever prioritize enough to complete) have changed their opinion to "having ever locked down was stupid, we were always going to end up with endemic covid, all of this economic damage was pointless". If we look at in-person retail sales data or restaurant data, we can easily see that many people were voluntarily limiting their activities before and after lockdowns in the first year or so of the pandemic when the virus was in broad circulation.

Meanwhile, in some Asian countries, like Taiwan and Vietnam, people mostly complied with lockdowns when they were instituted, which means that they were able to squash covid in the country when outbreaks happened until relatively recently, when covid mutated into forms that spread much more easily and people's tolerance for covid risk went way up due to vaccinations. Of course, covid kept getting reintroduced into countries that were able to squash it because other countries were not, in large part due to the self-fulfilling belief that it would be impossible to squash covid.

Coming back to when it makes sense to bring something in-house, even in cases where it superficially sounds like it shouldn't, because the expertise is 99% idle or a single person would have to be able to build software that a single firm would pay millions of dollars a year for, much of this comes down to whether or not you're in a culture where you can trust another firm's promise. If you operate in a society where it's expected that other firms will push you to the letter of the law with respect to whatever contract you've negotiated, it's frequently not worth the effort to negotiate a contract that would give you service even one half as good as you'd get from someone in house. If you look at how these contracts end up being worded, companies [often try to sneak in terms that make the contract meaningless](https://www.patreon.com/posts/21164582), and even when you managed to stamp out all of that, legally enforcing the contract is expensive and, in the cases I know of where companies regularly violated their agreement for their support SLA (just for example), the resolution was to terminate the contract rather than pursue legal action because the cost of legal action wouldn't be worth anything that could be gained.

If you can't trust other firms, you frequently don't have a choice with respect to bringing things in house if you want them to work.

Although this is really a topic for another post, I'll note that lack of trust that exists across companies can also hamstring companies when it exists internally. [As we discussed previously, a lot of larger scale brokenness also comes out of the cultural expectations within organizations](https://danluu.com/culture/). A specific example of this that leads to pervasive organizational problems is lack of trust within the organization. For example, a while back, I was griping to a director that a VP broke a promise and that we were losing a lot of people for similar reasons. The director's response was "there's no way the VP made a promise". When I asked for clarification, the clarification was "unless you get it in a contract, it wasn't a promise", i.e., the rate at which VPs at the company lie is high enough that a verbal commitment from a VP is worthless; only a legally binding commitment that allows you to take them to court has any meaning.

Of course, that's absurd, in that no one could operate at a BigCo while going around and asking for contracts for all their promises since they'd immediately be considered some kind of hyperbureaucratic weirdo. But, let's take the spirit of the comment seriously, that only trust people close to you. That's good advice in the company I worked for but, unfortunately for the company, the implications are similar to the inter-firm example, where we noted that a norm where you need to litigate the letter of the law is expensive enough that firms often bring expertise in house to avoid having to deal with the details. In the intra-firm case and you'll often see teams and orgs "empire build" because they know they, [at least the management level, they can't trust anyone outside their fiefdom](https://amzn.to/3pDnYUr).

While this intra-firm lack of trust tends to be less costly than the inter-firm lack of trust since there are better levers to get action on an organization that's the cause of a major blocker, [it's still fairly costly](https://amzn.to/3pDnYUr). Virtually all of the VPs and BigCo tech execs I've talked to are so steeped in the culture they're embedded in that they can't conceive of an alternative, but there isn't an inherent reason that organizations have to work like that. I've worked at two companies where people actually trust leadership and leadership does generally follow through on commitments even when you can't take them to court, including my current employer, Wave. But, at the other companies, the shared expectation that leadership cannot and should not be trusted "causes" the people who end up in leadership roles to be untrustworthy, which results in the inefficiencies we've just discussed.

People often think that having a high degree of internal distrust is inevitable as a company scales, but people I've talked to who were in upper management or fairly close to the top of Intel and Google said that the companies had an extended time period where leadership enforced trustworthiness and that stamping out dishonesty and "bad politics" was a major reason the company was so successful, under Andy Grove and Eric Schmidt, respectively. When the person at the top changed and a new person who didn't enforce honesty came in, the standard cultural norms that you see at the upper levels of most big companies seeped in, but that wasn't inevitable.

When I talk to people who haven't been exposed to BigCo leadership culture and haven't seen how decisions are actually made, they often find the decision making processes to be unbelievable in much the same way that people who are steeped in BigCo leadership culture find the idea that a large company could operate any other way to be unbelievable.

It's often difficult to see how absurd a system is from the inside. Another perspective on this is that Americans often find Japanese universities and [the work practices of Japanese engineering firms](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1428848459071819779) absurd, though often not as absurd as the [promotion policies in Korean chaebols, which are famously nepotistic](https://english.hani.co.kr/arti/english_edition/e_business/823025.html), e.g., Chung Mong-yong is the CEO of Hyundai Sungwoo because he's the son of Chung Soon-yung, who was the head of Hyundai Sungwoo because he was the younger brother of Chung Ju-yung, the founder of Hyundai Group (essentially the top-level Hyundai corporation), etc. But Japanese and Korean engineering firms are not, in general, less efficient than American engineering firms outside of the software industry despite practices that seem absurdly inefficient to American eyes. [American firms didn't lose their dominance in multiple industries](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1439511588529065987) while being more efficient; if anything, market inefficiencies allowed them to hang on to marketshare much longer than you would naively expect if you just looked at the technical merit of their products.

There are offsetting inefficiencies in American firms that are just as absurd as effectively having familiar succession of company leadership in Korean chaebols. It's just that the inefficiencies that come out of American cultural practices seem to be immutable facts about the world to people inside the system. But when you look at firms that have completely different cultures, it becomes clear that cultural norms aren't a law of nature.

### Appendix: downsides of build

Of course, building instead of buying isn't a panacea. I've frequently seen internal designs that are just as broken as the data sync product described in this post. In general, when you see a design like that, [a decent number of people explained why the design can never work during the design phase](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1470890510998851588) and were ignored. Although "build" gives you a lot more control than "buy" and gives you better odds of a product that works because you can influence the design, a dysfunctional team in a dysfunctional org can quite easily make products that don't work.

There's a Steve Jobs quote that's about companies that also applies to teams:

> It turns out the same thing can happen in technology companies that get monopolies, like IBM or Xerox. If you were a product person at IBM or Xerox, so you make a better copier or computer. So what? When you have monopoly market share, the company's not any more successful.
> 
> So the people that can make the company more successful are sales and marketing people, and they end up running the companies. And the product people get driven out of the decision making forums, and the companies forget what it means to make great products. The product sensibility and the product genius that brought them to that monopolistic position gets rotted out by people running these companies that have no conception of a good product versus a bad product.
> 
> They have no conception of the craftsmanship that's required to take a good idea and turn it into a good product. And they really have no feeling in their hearts, usually, about wanting to really help the customers."

For "efficiency" reasons, some large companies try to avoid duplicate effort and kill projects if they seem too similar to another project, giving the team that owns the canonical verison of a product a monopoly. If the company doesn't have a culture of trying to do the right thing, this has the same problems that Steve Jobs discusses, but at the team and org level instead of the company level.

The workaround a team I was on used was to basically re-implement a parallel stack of things we relied on that didn't work. But this was only possible beacuse leadership didn't enforce basically anything. Ironically, this was despite their best efforts — [leadership made a number of major attempts to impose top-down control](https://twitter.com/altluu/status/1494696655928508418), but they didn't understand how to influence an organization, so the attempts failed. Had leadership been successful, the company would've been significantly worse off. [There are upsides to effective top-down direction when leadership has good plans](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1487239027002527744), but that wasn't really on the table, so it's actually better that leadership didn't know how to execute.
