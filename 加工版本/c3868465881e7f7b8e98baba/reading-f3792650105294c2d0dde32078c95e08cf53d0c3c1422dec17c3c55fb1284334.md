## 正文

Sarah Perry 是 Ribbonfarm 的特约编辑，也做得一手绝佳的烩饭（不开玩笑，看看那本书就知道），几年前写了一本关于 suicide 和 antinatalism 的书。这本书没有受到多少关注，这使它非常适合成为我的一篇博客文章的主题，也符合我不写别人正在写的东西的原则。

我即将评论的这本书讨论 suicide 和 antinatalism，后者是一种 philosophical view，认为 bringing more people to the world 是 immoral。在我的评论中，我只会涉及第一部分。虽然我已经读完了整本书，但我想以后再写一篇关于 antinatalism 的文章，届时会在那里讨论那一部分。按章节来说，这篇文章涵盖 chapter 1-8，以及 Appendix。

如果用一两句话 summary 这本书的第一部分，那就是：suicide 对某些人而言是 good choice，而各种 biases 使许多人难以看清这一点。

首先，你可以在[这里](http://annsterzinger.com/662-2/)、[这里](https://nickbsteves.wordpress.com/2015/05/26/choose-your-illusion-a-review-of-every-cradle-is-a-grave/)、[这里](http://www.oddthingsconsidered.com/every-cradle-is-a-grave-by-sarah-perry/) ，以及[这里。](http://culturedvultures.com/book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave/) 找到先前的评论。我会尽量不重复其中已经提到的内容，所以请先读那些评论。

**(I) Suicide 就像香草冰淇淋**

Every Cradle is a Grave 的开篇，是我见过的最出色的 captatio benevolentiae 之一。这里，作者请读者认真对待书中的内容，讨论他们为什么可能决定不这样做，并给出认真对待的理由。对于一本讨论 suicide 这种 controversial topic 的书来说，这很重要；当它的 conclusions 甚至更加 controversial 时，就更是如此。从开头的几段中，你就能有所了解，

> _这是一本关于 ethics 的书。人们并不经常改变自己对 ethics 的看法。即使改变，通常也是出于 social reasons，而不是因为接触了 reasoned argument。Reasoned arguments 更多时候使人们得以 cement 自己已有的 opinions。无论如何，Ethical beliefs 在 influence actions 方面的能力都是 extremely limited。我将 advocate 几种 counterintuitive 的 ethical positions，有些人会把它们形容为 evil。这些 ethical positions 包括这样的 view：life——不仅是 human life，而是所有 capable of having experiences 的 life——都是非常 bad 的。我将 argue，have babies 或以其他方式 create aware beings 是非常 immoral 的。我还将 argue，suicide 并不是 wrong，也不是 mental illness 的 product，而是对 life 的 badness 的一种 ethically privileged, rational response。$$...$$_
> 
> _我怀疑，单凭做一个可爱的家庭主妇、做得一手绝佳的鸡油菌烩饭，我就已经让更多人转而 questioning life’s value，_ _比我 constructed 的任何_ _特定 argument 都更有效。既然我没法为_ _你做烩饭，我就尝试以平和_ _而 reasoned 的方式呈现我的 arguments，并始终尊重我们共同拥有的 human-_ _ity。也许在你看来，我就像那种_ _你应该当作 epistemic peer 接受的卡通反派。_ _不过，无论你是否允许我用这些_ _dangerous ideas 来 influence 你，我都希望，当我告诉你_ _我确实站在你这一边时，你会相信我。毕竟，你是一个 aware_ _being，正在 having experiences。无论你_ _是否已经或将会 have children，这一点都成立；无论你_ _want to live 还是 want to die，这一点也都成立。_ _谢谢你阅读我的书。_

我自己对 suicide 的看法值得在这里说一说：suicide 就像香草冰淇淋。有些人喜欢香草冰淇淋，有些人不喜欢。对于从未吃过香草冰淇淋的人，至少尝一尝是件好事，这样才能确保自己拒绝得有道理，没有错过自己可能喜欢的东西。当然，强迫别人吃香草冰淇淋是不对的。

同样，说到 suicide，有些人 like living，另一些人则不喜欢。对于不 like living 的人，仔细想清楚自己是否真的不 like living，并考虑自己将来会不会 like living，是件好事。如果一个人已经经历了这样的 thought process，那么阻止他 committing suicide 就是 wrong 的。

这 assumes，除了个人自己的 preference，并没有支持 being alive vs being dead 的 argument。Utilitarian reasoning 也许能 support 类似的主张：即使你真的 dislike your life，如果继续 living 能 increases net aggregate utility，你或许也应该如此。但我不是 utilitarian，而且很难 argue，仅仅因为有你在这个世界会更好，你就应该做一个 miserable slave。请注意，一个人也可以奉献于他人，甚至在这样做时 suffer，却仍然出于 supererogatory considerations，认为这件事值得做。

又或许 living 是 good in itself，因此一个人必须改变自己看待 life 的方式，以适应必须继续 living 的要求。我承认，我希望能有一个支持这种看法的 argument，但目前没有。因此，虽然我想说，你应该改变自己的 perception 和 preferences，以 accommodate living，但我无法 rationally endorse 这一主张。至少现在还不能。

关键在于，与香草冰淇淋不同，suicide 是 irreversible 的。这就是为什么，阻止一个一时冲动而试图 commit suicide 的人是 justified 的。这里的 justification 是 hypothetical consent，也就是为一个并不 conscious、但需要 surgery 才能 stay alive 的人实施 surgery 时所使用的同一种 justification：如果你曾冷静地思考过这种情况，你就会 consent to that。我们怎么知道？因为大多数被阻止 committing suicide 的人，都会庆幸有人阻止了自己，因为他们并不真的 want to die。

这种看法与流行的 suicide 观念形成了 radically contrasts：在后者看来，无论那个人过着怎样的 life，suicide 都是一件可怕的事。无论发生什么，一个人都_必须 live_，即使这意味着把那个人 incarcerating 在 mental institution 里，直到他 want to live。Suicide 被视为一种 cowardly act，绝不能允许它发生。

**(II) 一些杂评**

下面选录了书中的一些关键段落，并附上我自己的评论。

> _但人并不是作为与 human relationships 和 groups 相 separate 的 individual units 而 exist 的。一个 wanting to die 的人所面对的 committing suicide 的 cost，很大一部分是 social 和 empathetic 的：它体现在他的 death 将给父母、子女、兄弟姐妹、配偶或朋友带来的，或至少加速到来的 loneliness 和 grief 中。作为 social creatures，我们至少从出生起就开始 forming bonds；这些 bonds，虽然往往与我们自己的 births 一样，并非 voluntarily chosen，却是 powerful motivations。那些与我们_ _have formed social bonds 的人……_。

在我看来，这有点奇怪：如果我正在考虑 suicide，我会思考自己的未来，weighting 眼下的 misery 与未来可能的 improvements。但书中阐述的 suicide 观念，全都关乎他人，关乎 groups。在她看来，正是 strong social bonds 阻止了 suicides——或者说，我们所有人？——真正 committing suicide。

> _Simple misery 似乎并不会像 Bryan Caplan 的 naive model 中那样 reliably cause suicide；相反，人们_
> 
> _似乎是在 freed from，或者更确切地说 deprived of 那些使他们保持 alive 的 social bonds 时，才会 commit suicide。_

按照这种看法，life 就像某种 prisoner dilemma：我们之所以会 alive，是因为他人。但如果我们能放下那些 social bonds……那么，我们 commit suicide 时就不会让别人 suffer，于是我们就会这样做。（？）在我看来，这似乎不对。但对其他人来说，会不会确实如此？

在那一段中，Perry 提到了 Bryan Caplan 的 [free disposal](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2007/10/free_disposal.html)[model of suicide](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2011/12/a_cursory_rejec.html)。如果你不 like living，你直接 commit suicide 就好了！面对这份未经请求便送来的 life 礼物，你总可以说不。

但 Perry 说，先别急：Suicide 没那么容易！Society 远非一个 suicide 像 undergoing surgery 一样轻而易举的地方。她用一个有趣的 free disposal society 构想来说明这一点：在这样的 society 中，suicide 不会被视为 evil，而是一个人可以做出的 choice。人们从小就会被教导，自己的 life 属于自己，如果愿意，就可以 end 它。一个人可以去找 doctor，预约，在经过一些 counselling 和讨论之后，按下一个 button，safely and effortlessly inject oneself with 某种 causes instant and painless death 的 drug。在这个 society 中，life 才真正会是一种 free disposal good。而 Perry argue，我们距离生活在这样的 society 中还很远。即使我们想这样做，Committing suicide 也很难，因为我们的思维方式是 biased 和 irrational 的。例如，如果一个人想 jump from a bridge，他就会开始想到这可能 causes 的所有 pain，以及自己可能并不会 die 的 probability（比你想象的 Higher！），从而不得不在床上度过余生，什么也做不了。

第二章开头提到，life 是需要 justification 的。外面有大量的 suffering 和 bad things，我们需要一个充分的 reason，才能让这一切继续下去。也就是说：为什么 live？我的回答是：但世界很美好，而且 suffering 并没有那么多。为什么_不_ live？

Sarah Perry 指出，重要的不是 happiness 或 pleasure，而是 meaning。我同意。但接下来，她（在全书中）转而声称 meaning-perception 是一种 illusion。Meaning 并不真的存在。那么，如果重要的东西甚至根本不存在……

> 认为 life 是 meaningful 的 belief，往往以 strong feeling 而非 reasoned conclusion 的形式出现；事实上，meaning 的 functions 之一，就是通过提供一种 justified for its own sake 的 value，让人免受 reasoning 的 harmful effects；它是 cognition 的 foundational rock，在它之下，不必再回答任何为什么

我对此的回应是：认为 suffering 是 bad 的 belief 也一样，因为所有 reasoning 都始于那些在 reasoner 看来似乎 true、却没有给出为什么的 premises。

> 沉重的 modern self 面临一项艰难的任务：它必须为自己完成过去由 human religion 和 community 完成的事情。它必须为自己 provide meaning。

这也是事实。直到不久前，人们还可以在 God、Nation 或 Community 中 seek meaning。如今，人们必须回答“我为什么要做这件事？”，而回答的人只有自己。也许有些人会发现这个问题难以回答。如果没有答案，另一个问题就会出现：“我应该继续做这件事吗？”。如果答案是否定的，那么我们就有了一位 potential suicide 的 candidate。

第三章用 Jonathan Haidt 的 Moral Foundations Theory 来 analyses suicide：人们如何使用不同的 foundations（Harm, Care, Purity, Authority, Loyalty...）来 reason about suicide。

> 对于 human action 的 coordination、对于 politics、对于 orderly human life，Sacredness 都是 necessary 的。它本质上是一种 valuable illusion，由 social unit 以 created cooperatively 的方式形成，往往历经很长时间、付出很大代价，然后得到 maintained，并受到 defended，以抵御 mutations 和 competing sacredness structures。Sacredness 暗中 informs 我们所有的 judgments，即使那些似乎 purely related to harm 或 fairness 的 judgments 也是如此——事实上，即使那些似乎并不具有 moral dimension 的 judgments 也不例外。

这段文字让我们看到了 Sarah 最近在 ribbonfarm 谈论的一些 themes（rituals、community 等）。不过，我不会说 sacredness 有那么重要，而且我们现有的许多 rules 都源于 self-interest。并非全部如此：我确实认为，modern society 要 work well，就需要一些 altruism。但 other-regarding preferences 的那个小小核心（这里我谈论的是超出 kin 或 reciprocal altruism 的 altruism）并不能算作 sacredness。当然，可以打个比方，说 morality 像 religion，因此 moral belief 像 sacred beliefs；但 philosophy 并不像 religion 那样运作，而且 moral beliefs 是可以受到 critique 和 discussion 的。同样，认为 Law 是 sacred、认为人应当 comply with the Law，因为它就是 Law，这样的 belief 似乎并非必要。认为 Law 是用于 coordination 的 tool，再加上对 breaching the Law 的 penalties，似乎就足够了。

第四章

> 那些使 life 显得 meaningful 的事物，其最 salient features 之一，就是它们经常 rely on illusion：关于 unchanging permanence 的 illusion、关于未来 happiness state 的 illusion，以及关于自己 affect the world 的 ability 的 illusion。我的看法是，meaningfulness 的 sense 本身就是一种 illusion，一种对 individuals 和 groups 非常 adaptive 的 cognitive phenomenon 
> 
> $$...$$ life 是 meaningful 的 feeling，是一种被广泛共有的 pre-rational sensory perception。然而，人们觉得 satisfying 和 convincing 的具体 meanings 是 disparate 的，而且常常是 contradictory 的。这些 underlying realities 应当促使我们 question：life 是 meaningful 的 sense——或者 life 中能够找到某种具体 meaning 的 sense——究竟是 true observation，还是仅仅是一种 illusion。$$…$$
> 
> 那些我们觉得 meaningful 的事物，事实上都是微型的 Experience Machines。它们 rely on illusion，并 filter 传递给我们的 information，使我们能够继续觉得 life 是 meaningful，或者在缺少 meaning 时继续在 life 中 search for meaning。它们非常有用；它们帮助我们 organize 我们的 behavior、与他人 coordinate，以及 manage 我们的 emotions。从 practical sense 来说，它们往往使 life 的 suffering 变得 bearable；但一旦它们被 recognized 为 illusions，它们就和 pleasure 一样，无法在 abstract sense 上 justify suffering。

虽然人们可以用 evolutionary theory 这支矛来 attack meaningfulness，并 argue 它是一种 bias，但我们也可以对 suffering avoidance 做同样的事。事实上，相比拥有 meaningfulness 的 sense，suffering avoidance 更是这类 evolutionary bias 的一个 \_meaningful \_candidate。但 plausibility 并不是 definitive argument。Evolutionary psychology 理应不止是其反对者大加批评的那些 just-so stories。

接下来，这一章介绍了 reverse experience machine 的 idea。通常的 experience machine 是一个最早见于 Robert Nozick 的 Anarchy, State, and Utopia 的 thought experiment，它请读者想象存在一台可以让自己 plug into 的 machine。如果你这样做，就会进入 dreamlike state，在其中继续 living，experiencing 按你自己的标准所能 experience 的最好的 life。在这台 machine 中，你会忘记自己身处梦中。Nozick 得出结论：有理由不 plug into 这台 machine。

reverse experience machine 是指发现我们已经身处这台 machine 之中。我们会出去吗？

> Reverse Experience Machine experiment 很接近我们所处的 situation——如果我们发现自己已经身处一台 Experience Machine 中，我们会选择离开它，前往 real world 吗？Institutions、religions、social communities，甚至 individual people，都起着 Experience Machines 的作用，creating 和 maintaining illusions，帮助我们觉得 life 是 worthwhile。meaning realist 会 reject Experience Machine，但为了保持 consistent，他也必须 reject life 中那些利用 illusion 或 information filters 来 provide meaning 的方面。meaning subjectivist 几乎没有理由 reject Experience Machine。这对以 meaningfulness 为基础的 life’s misery 的 justification 有着 implications。

这一节我没有完全理解，但如果我的理解是正确的，那么我看到了一点问题：这里 assumed，那些被用作 meaningfulness（或 [meaningness](https://meaningness.com/)？）的 building blocks 的事物，实际上像 experience machine 一样，是 fake reality 的 generators。但 experience machine 与她提到的那些事物之间存在 differences：身处 experience machine 时，你不知道自己实际身在何处，而且对周围的一切持有 false beliefs。（例如，你会在与 unconscious robots 而非人 interacting，而且你所 learning 的是 simulation，而不是 actual universe）。至于 institutions 和人，我们确实知道它们是什么，以及它们 _真正_ 在做什么。

另一个 question 是，人究竟应不应该 plug into 或离开 experience machine。我会说，这取决于你有多么 value 对 true beliefs 的认识。有些人不会从这台 machine 中 unplug，因为他们已经习惯在那里 living。对有些人来说，fake parents、fake friends 和 fake life，比被扔进一个完全未知的世界更好。但并非对所有人都是如此。

第五章讨论 suicide 究竟是 impulsive 的，还是 rationally pre-planned 的。大多数人显然赞同前一种 view，而 Sarah 为读者提供了一些相反的 evidence，其中包括 peer-reviewed research。

> 这一 theory 的一个 problem 在于，suicide 事实上似乎并不是 impulsive act，32而是通常涉及一个 plan。而且，考虑到现有的 barriers to suicide，看似“impulsive”的 suicide，实际上可能 reflect 这位 suicide 的 genuine rational desires。一个 rationally prefers to die 的人，可能因 legal 和 practical barriers 而被 unfairly prevented from doing so；他可能需要一个“impulse”将他推过那道坎，enact 他的 rational desire。

那么，究竟是哪一种？Suicide：fast 还是 slow？我自己对这个话题了解不多，因此把自己的判断交给 psychiatrist Scott Alexander（在许多事情上，他都是我信任的 authorities 之一），他主张 suicide 是 [mostly impulsive](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/04/25/in-defense-of-psych-treatment-for-attempted-suicide/) 的，而且人们并不真正想这样做。我甚至给他发过一篇支持 rational suicide thesis 的[近期 literature review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263476087_Reconsidering_the_Link_Between_Impulsivity_and_Suicidal_Behavior)，但他仍未被说服。你可以在[这里](https://t.co/XQXZKnYPve)找到他的回复。

第六章谈论的话题包括 IQ 和 suicide。你可能会认为，IQ 与 suicide 是 negatively correlated 的，因为大多数好事都与 IQ 是 positively correlated 的，而 suicide 可以说是一件坏事（而且 IQ 更高的人会更 succesful，因此 commit suicide 的理由更少）。Sarah Perry 赞同这一点，但有所保留：Lewis Terman 曾对一个 IQ 非常高的 group 做过一项 study（"Terman Termites", N>1500），而这个 group 的 suicidality rates 是 average population 的 [four times as high](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886903003829)。奇怪的是，IQ 以相当 predictive 的方式 reduce suicide 的 chances，

> 在任何 story 之外 Living——生活中没有对未来的 hope，也没有自己是某个 narrative 的一部分这一 belief——是 confusing 的。当什么都没有 point 时，就很难做成任何事情。对于我考虑的任何 not-immediately-pleasurable action（或 inaction）——早晨起床、吸尘、接电话——我脑海中始终存在的那个问题：“为什么？”，都没有现成的答案。至少，没有一个 long-term 的“为什么”。$$...$$
> 
> heaven 中没有 stories；heaven 全是 epilogue。它充当我们 stories 的 bookend；我们甚至可以称它为“hereafter”，就像“从此幸福地生活下去”中的那个“从此”。heaven 中不可能有 conflict，因此也不可能有 stories。
> 
> drugs、prostitution、gambling 和 suicide 明明给 suffering 的人带来了如此多的 relief，为什么却是 illegal 的？我认为，这是因为在 societal level，我们受到蒙蔽，以为没有这些东西，happiness 也仍然是 possible 的，甚至可能是 easy 或 likely 的。我把这称为“cheery social policy”。
> 
> 这种 cheeriness 的 fundamental problem，在于它 assumption 了 good life——pleasant life——是相对容易 achieve 的。Cheery 的人之所以能够持有这样的 belief，是因为他们能够忽视——甚至可能根本无法设想——population 中相当一部分 minority 的 suffering。对我们许多人来说，good life 并不容易 achieved $$...$$
> 
> 这就是从 hell 看到的景象。Hell 并不是 experiencing 大量 suffering、却没有 pleasure 来“balance it out”的 state。Hell 是完全脱离 meaning 这一 notion。而这个 Hell 就是我们所有人都身处其中的 meta-condition，无论我们是否 perceive 到它。

我对这本书的异议，根本上在于我们应该选择什么 default 来 judging life：为什么 live？还是为什么不 live？由于我们缺少一个 argument，说明一个人如果不想 live，为什么仍 ought to live，因此这一点很重要。如果一个人在某种 cosmic 或 universal meaning 中寻找第一个问题的答案，就不会找到答案，于是也就没有了 live 的 reason。正如一句西班牙谚语所说，不要向榆树索要梨子。不要设定高得不可能达到的标准，否则你会失望的。

因此，如果我们想 live，我们就 live；如果不想，rationality 就 dictates 我们应该 commit suicide，尽管 suicide 在实践中很困难。这就是 Gary Becker 和 Eric Posner 在他们 2004 年的 paper《[Suicide: An Economic Approach](http://www.gwern.net/docs/2004-becker.pdf)》中所 argue 的。他们的 model 还 explains 了为什么 suicidal 的人往往会 engage in risky activities（例如 smoking 或 heavy drinking）：他们几乎没什么可 lose，却有很多可以 gain。

那么，Suicide 就是我们可以用自己的 lives 做的一件事，而且它可能是一个 rational decision。我并不确信它通常如此；我仍然认为，它更多是一种 impulsive 的事情。以后的文章可以探讨这样一个问题：是否有可能提出一个支持 existing 而非 not existing 的 argument。

## 原文

Sarah Perry, contributing editor of Ribbonfarm, and killer risotto maker (no joke, check the book), wrote a book about suicide and antinatalism some years ago. It hasn't got much attention, which makes it an excellent choice for one of my blogposts, following my policy of not writing about what others are writing about.

The book I'm about to review deals with suicide and antinatalism, the philosophical view that bringing more people to the world is immoral. In my review I will only cover the first part. Altough I've read the whole thing, I want to write an antinatalism post later on, and I will cover that part there. In terms of chapters, this post covers chapter 1-8, plus the Appendix.

A summary of the first part of the book in one or two lines would be that suicide is a good choice for some people, and that biases of various sorts make it difficult for many people to see this.

First, you can find previous reviews [here](http://annsterzinger.com/662-2/), [here](https://nickbsteves.wordpress.com/2015/05/26/choose-your-illusion-a-review-of-every-cradle-is-a-grave/),[here](http://www.oddthingsconsidered.com/every-cradle-is-a-grave-by-sarah-perry/) , and [here.](http://culturedvultures.com/book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave/) I'll try not to repeat things already mentioned there, so read those first.

**(I) Suicide as vanilla ice cream**

Every Cradle is a Grave opens with one of the best captatio benevolentiae that I've seen. This is when the authors asks the readers to take the content of the book seriously, discussing why they might decide not to do it, and give them reasons to do it. This is important for a book that discusses a controversial topic such as suicide, and more so when the conclusions are on an even higher order of controversy. You can get an idea from some of the first paragraphs,

> _This is a book about ethics. People don’t often change their minds about ethics. When they do, it is generally for social reasons, not because they are exposed to reasoned argument. Reasoned arguments more often allow people to cement their existing opinions. Ethical beliefs are, in any case, extremely limited in their ability to influence actions. I will advocate several ethical positions that are counterintuitive, and that some people would describe as evil. These ethical positions include the view that life—not just human life, but all life capable of having experiences—is very bad. It is very immoral, I will argue, to have babies or to otherwise create aware beings. I will also argue that suicide is not wrong or a product of mental illness, but an ethically privileged, rational response to the badness of life. \[...\]_
> 
> _I suspect that I have made more converts to the cause of questioning life’s value simply by being an adorable house-_ _wife who makes a killer chanterelle risotto than by any_ _particular argument I’ve constructed. Since I can’t make_ _you risotto, I have tried to present my arguments in a calm_ _and reasoned manner, with abiding respect for the human-_ _ity that we all share. Perhaps I will come across as the sort_ _of cartoon villain you should accept as an epistemic peer._ _But whether or not you allow me to influence you with my_ _dangerous ideas, I hope you will believe me when I tell you_ _that I am very much on your side. You are, after all, an aware_ _being having experiences. This is true whether or not you_ _have had or will have children, and this is true whether you_ _want to live or want to die._ _Thank you for reading my book._

My own approach to suicide, which is worth presenting here, is that suicide is like vanilla ice cream. Some people like vanilla ice cream, others do not. It is good that people who never have eaten vanilla ice cream at least try it to ensure that their rejection makes sense and they are not missing out something they could like. And it would be wrong to force-feed someone vanilla ice cream, of course.

Likewise, with suicide, some people like living, and other people don't. It is good that people who don't like living make sure to think twice that they don't really like living, plus that they consider if they will like living in the future. If someone has gone through that thought process, it would be wrong to stop someone from committing suicide.

This assumes that there is no argument in favour of being alive vs being dead beyond one's own preference. Utilitarian reasoning could support something like that: Perhaps, even if you really dislike your life, you should keep on living if that increases net aggregate utility. But I'm not an utilitarian, and it's hard to argue that one should be a miserable slave just because the world would be better with you in it. Note that one can also devote oneself to others, and even suffer when doing it, and yet think that such a thing is worth doing out of supererogatory considerations.

Or maybe living is good in itself, and one has to change one's way of seeing one's life to adapt it to the requirement that you must go on living. I confess I would like to have an argument for that, but I currently don't, so while I would want to say that you should change your perception and preferences to accommodate living, I cannot rationally endorse it. Yet, at least.

Crucially, unlike with vanilla ice cream, suicide is irreversible. Which is why one would be justified in stopping someone trying to commit suicide out of a whim. The justification here is hypothetical consent, the same that is used to do surgery on someone not conscious that needs said surgery to stay alive: Had you calmly thought about the situation, you would consent to that. How do we know? Because most people who have been stopped from committing suicide are glad that it happened, because they didn't really want to die.

This vision radically contrasts with popular views of suicide, where it is something terrible, regardless of the life the person was living. One _must live_, whatever it happens, even if that means incarcerating that person in a mental institution until they want to live. Suicide is seen as a cowardly act that must simply not be allowed to happen.

**(II) Assorted comments**

What follows is a collection of key paragraphs from the book, with some commentary of my own.

> _But people do not exist as individual units separate from human relationships and groups. A great deal of the cost of committing suicide faced by a person wanting to die is social and empathetic: it is resonant in the loneliness and grief that his death will cause, or at least hasten, among parents,  children, siblings, a spouse, or friends. As social creatures, we begin forming bonds at least as soon as we are born; these bonds, while often no more voluntarily chosen than our own births, are powerful motivations. Those with whom we_ _have formed social bonds…_.

To me, this seemed a bit odd: If I were considering suicide, I would be thinking on my own future, weighting present misery against future possible improvements. But the vision of suicide that is explained in the book is all about others, about groups. It is strong social bonds, in her view, what keep suicides - or perhaps, all of us? - from actually committing suicide.

> _Simple misery does not seem to reliably cause suicide, the way it would in Bryan Caplan’s naive model; rather, people_
> 
> _seem to commit suicide when they are freed from, or perhaps rather deprived of, the social bonds that were keeping them alive._

On this view, life would be a prisoner dilemma of sorts: It is because of others that we would be alive. But if we could just let go of those social bonds... then we wouldn't make other people suffer when we commit suicide, and then we would do so. (?) This doesn't seem right to me. But could it be true of other people?

In that paragraph, Perry refers to Bryan Caplan's [free disposal](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2007/10/free_disposal.html)[model of suicide](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2011/12/a_cursory_rejec.html). If you don't like living, you can just commit suicide! You can always see no to the unasked-for gift of life.

But not so fast, says Perry: Suicide is not that easy! Society is far from being a place where suicide is as trivial as undergoing surgery. She illustrates this with the interesting idea of a free disposal society: a society that doesn't view suicide as something evil, but a choice one can make. People would be taught from a young age to see their life is theirs, and that they can end if so they want. One could go to a doctor, ask for an appointment, and after some counselling and discussing, one would press a button to safely and effortlessly inject oneself with some drug that causes instant and painless death. In this society, life would really be a free disposal good. And we are far from living in that society, argues Perry. Committing suicide is hard, even if we want to, because of our biased and irrational ways. For example, if one wants to jump from a bridge, one will start to think of all the pain that it can cause, and the probability that one might not die (Higher than you think!) and thus live the rest of your days in a bed, doing nothing.

Chapter two starts by mentioning that life is something that needs justification. That there are tons of suffering and bad things out there, and that we need a good reason to keep the show going. That is: Why live?. My answer is: but the world is great, and there is not that much suffering. Why _not_ live?

Sarah Perry points that what means is not happiness or pleasure, but meaning. And I agree. But then she makes the move (throughout the book) of saying that meaning-perception is an illusion. Meaning is not really there. And thus if what matters is not even there...

> The belief that life is meaningful tends to take the form of a strong feeling rather than a reasoned conclusion; indeed, one of the functions of meaning is to shield a person from the harmful effects of reasoning by providing a value that is justified for its own sake, a foundational rock for cognition below which no whys need be answered

My reply to that: So would be the belief that suffering is bad, for all reasoning begins from premises that seem true to the reasoner, and for which no whys are provided.

> The heavy modern self has a hard task: it must do for itself what human religion and community did in the past. It must provide itself with meaning.

This is another truth. Until recently, one could seek meaning in God or the Nation, or the Community. Now, one must answer 'Why am I doing this?' and the only one answering is oneself. And perhaps some find difficulties answering the question. If there is no answer, another question arises: 'Should I continue doing this?'. And if the answer is no, then we have a candidate for a potential suicide.

Chapter three analyses suicide using Jonathan Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory: How people use the different foundations (Harm, Care, Purity, Authority, Loyalty...) to reason about suicide.

> Sacredness is necessary for the coordination of human action, for politics, for orderly human life. It is essentially a valuable illusion created cooperatively by the social unit, often over a long time at great cost, and then maintained and defended against mutations and competing sacredness structures. Sacredness secretly informs all of our judgments, even those that seem to be purely related to harm or fairness— indeed, even those that don’t seem to have a moral dimension.

This is one of the paragraphs where we can see themes that Sarah is currently talking about in ribbonfarm (rituals, community, etc). I wouldn't say that sacredness is that important, though, and that many of the rules in place that we have are down to self-interest. Not all: I do think that some altruism is needed for a modern society to work well. But that small core of other-regarding preferences (Here I talk about altruism beyond kin or reciprocal altruism) wouldn't count as sacredness. One could of course make the simile that morality is like religion, and thus moral belief are like sacred beliefs, but philosophy doesn't work like religion, and moral beliefs are subject to critique and discussion. Likewise, a belief that the Law is sacred, that one ought to comply with the Law because it is the Law doesn't seem necessary. A belief that the Law is a tool to be used for coordination, coupled with penalties for breaching the Law seem to suffice.

Chapter four

> One of the most salient features of the things that make life seem meaningful is that they frequently rely on illusion: the illusion of unchanging permanence, of a future state of happiness, of one’s ability to affect the world. It is my view that the sense of meaningfulness is itself an illusion, a cognitive phenomenon that is very adaptive for individuals and groups 
> 
> \[...\] The feeling that life is meaningful is a pre-rational sensory perception that is widely shared. However, the specific meanings that people find satisfying and convincing are disparate and often contradictory. These underlying realities should make us question whether the sense that life is meaningful—or that some specific meaning can be found in life—is a true observation, or merely an illusion. \[…\]
> 
> The things that we find to be meaningful are, in fact, miniature Experience Machines. They rely on illusion and filter the information that reaches us so that we may continue to feel that life is meaningful, or continue to search for meaning in life if it is missing. They are very useful; they help us organize our behavior, coordinate with others, and manage our emotions. In a practical sense they often make the suffering of life bearable; but, once they are recognized to be illusions, they cannot justify suffering in an abstract sense any more than pleasure can.

While one can attack meaningfulness with the spear of evolutionary theory and argue it is a bias, so we can do the same with suffering avoidance. In fact, suffering avoidance is a more \_meaningful \_candidate for an evolutionary bias of that sort than having a sense of meaningfulness. But plausibility is not a definitive argument. Evolutionary psychology is supposed to be more than the just-so stories that its opponents so much criticise.

Next in this chapter, the idea of a reverse experience machine is presented. The regular experience machine, a thought experiment first found in Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, asks the reader to imagine the existence of a machine that you can plug yourself into. If you do, you will fall in a dreamlike state where you will go on living experiencing the best life you can experience in your own terms. Within the machine, you will forget that you are in the dream. Nozick concludes that there are reasons not to plug into the machine.

The reverse experience machine is finding out we are inside the machine. Would we go out?

> The Reverse Experience Machine experiment is close to the situation we find ourselves in—if we found out we were in an Experience Machine already, would we choose to leave it for the real world? Institutions, religions, social communities, and even individual people function as Experience Machines, creating and maintaining illusions that help us feel that life is worthwhile. A meaning realist would reject the Experience Machine, but to be consistent he must also reject those aspects of life that use illusion or information filters to provide meaning. A meaning subjectivist has little ground to reject the Experience Machine. This has implications for the justification of life’s misery based on meaningfulness.

This is a section that I didn't fully get, but if my understanding is correct, then I see a bit of an issue: that it is assumed that things that are used as building blocks for meaningfulness (or [meaningness](https://meaningness.com/) ?) are really generators of a fake reality, like the experience machine. But there are differences between the experience machine and the things she mentions: You don't know where you really are while in the experience machine, and you have false beliefs about that which surrounds you. (e.g. you would be interacting with unconscious robots rather than with people, and you would be learning about the simulation, not the actual universe). With institutions and people, we do know what they are, and what they are doing, _for real_.

Another question is if one should plug or not into or out of an experience machine. It depends on how much you value knowing true beliefs, I'd say. Some people would not unplug from the machine, because they are used to living there. For some people, fake parents, fake friends, and fake life is better than being thrown into a completely unknown world. But not for everyone.

Chapter five discusses whether suicide is something impulsive or something rationally pre-planned. Most people clearly side with the first view, and Sarah gives the reader some evidence, included peer-reviewed research, to the contrary.

> One problem with this theory is that suicide does not, in fact, appear to be an impulsive act,32but generally involves a plan. And given the existing barriers to suicide, a suicide that appears “impulsive” may actually reflect the genuine rational desires of the suicide. The person who rationally prefers to die may be unfairly prevented from doing so by legal and practical barriers; he may need an “impulse” to push him over the edge and enact his rational desire.

So what is it? Suicide: fast or slow? I don't know much about the topic myself, so I outsource my opinion to psychiatrist Scott Alexander (One of my trusted authorities on a range of matters), who defends the view that suicide is [mostly impulsive](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/04/25/in-defense-of-psych-treatment-for-attempted-suicide/), and that people don't really want to do it. I even sent him a [recent literature review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263476087_Reconsidering_the_Link_Between_Impulsivity_and_Suicidal_Behavior) that argues for the rational suicide thesis, and he wasn't still convinced. You can find his reply [here](https://t.co/XQXZKnYPve).

Chapter six deals with, among other things, IQ and suicide. You'd think that IQ is negatively correlated with suicide, as most of the good things are positively correlated with IQ, and suicide argueable is a bad thing (And people with higher IQ would be more succesful, hence have less reasons to commit suicide). Sarah Perry agrees, with caveats: There is one study by Lewis Terman on a group with very high IQ ("Terman Termites", N>1500), and in that group, suicidality rates were [four times as high](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886903003829) as in the average population. The weird thing is that IQ reduce chances of suicide in a fairly predictive matter,

One thing, though, is that those studies do not attempt to engage with the literature on individual differences, and [just assume](http://prx.sagepub.com/content/100/1/108.long) that what is valid at the nation level will be valid at the individual level, and we just saw that it is not the case.

If we plot the actual variables, we see that a linear fit doesn't make much sense, and it also seems that suicide rates are first low, then they peak, and then they decrease, with IQ.

Sarah does not decompose the correlations to this level (no one else has, so it's not much of an issue), and says that high IQ societies are more complex, and thus easier to 'fall through the cracks', be alone, lose contact with social groups, and end up committing suicide. Perhaps we could try to see why the relation is the way it is. Let's go for one usual suspect: Post-communist countries, still recovering from the fall of the Soviet Union. (IQ data is from Lynn and Vanhanen (2002), suicide data is from Schmidtke (1999)

[![ee](https://nintil.com/old_assets/2016/11/30-3.png)](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/11/30/suicide-fast-and-slow-book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave-sarah-perry/ee/)

The trend remains, and if anything becomes more clear.

One other idea is to try to use data from a different year, to see if the relation is still there. This is using WHO data from 2012,

[![aa2](https://nintil.com/old_assets/2016/11/30-4.png)](https://artir.wordpress.com/2016/11/30/suicide-fast-and-slow-book-review-every-cradle-is-a-grave-sarah-perry/aa2-2/)

Now the correlation becomes much much smaller to the point of not really being there. WHO uses age-standardised suicide rates, trying to correct for the fact that suicidality changes with population age.

The countries at the top are, in order, South Korea (106), Sri Lanka (81), Lithuania (97), Suriname (89), Kazakhstan (93), and Turkmenistan (87). The dot at IQ 65 is Zimbabwe. The countries at the bottom are Kuwait (83), Jamaica (72), Azerbaijan (87), Egypt (83), Bahamas (78), and Barbados (78). The three dots at the right end of the chart are Korea, Hong Kong, and Japan.

Doesn't look very nice, so I tried to run a couple of multivariate regressions using more factors. The results are in the Appendix of this post. The correlation doesn't disappear, but the assumptions that we need for the models (like normally distributed errors) don't hold.

Even with those caveats,  it looks like we have to accept -provisionally, and with low confidence- that there is a -positive- correlation between IQ and suicide rate at a national level, that doesn't exist at an individual level, which is weird, and requires explanation. Furthermore, look at what happens if we remove the three observations to the right (red line). Then you get an increase in suicide, and then a decrease.

More could be said about this (Martin Voracek has many papers about it, and no one else seems to be doing work on this), but the IQ-suicide relationship is something that merits further investigation. Reconciling individual and aggregate-level findings is something that I haven't seen done.

At the root of my quibbles with the book is what default should we choose for judging life: Why live? or Why not live? As we lack an argument for why one ought to live  if one doesn't want to, this is important. If one looks for an answer to the first question in some sort of cosmic or universal meaning, the answer won't be there, and thus there will be no reason to live. As a Spanish saying goes, don't ask the elm for pears. Don't set impossibly high standards or you'll be disappointed.

Thus if we want to live, we live, and if we don't, rationality dictates that we should commit suicide, even though suicide in practise is difficult. This is what Gary Becker and Eric Posner argue in their 2004 paper '[Suicide: An Economic Approach](http://www.gwern.net/docs/2004-becker.pdf)'. Their model also explains why suicidal people tend to engage in risky activities (smoking or heavy drinking, for example): They have little to lose, and much to gain.

Suicide, then, is one thing we can do with our lives, and it can be a rational decision. I am not convinced that it generally is; I still think it is more of an impulsive thing. A question for future posts would be to see if it is possible to produce an argument in favour of existing vs not existing.
