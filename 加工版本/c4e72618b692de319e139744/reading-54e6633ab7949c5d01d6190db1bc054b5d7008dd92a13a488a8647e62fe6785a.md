# 1\. 制定 Full-Time Studying Schedule

---

## 正文

我的工作中有一个重要主题，就是尝试寻找更有效地 learn 的方法——我们如何才能以更少的时间和精力浪费来 gain skill 或 knowledge？   但即使 studying strategy 已经 perfectly optimized，learning 仍然需要时间。没有人能一夜之间成为 expert，而且即便只是在新的爱好、career skills 或 fields of study 中 reaching early levels of proficiency，也可能需要投入大量时间和精力。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-1.jpg)

这就引出了一个问题：你如何找到时间，去 learn 自己想学的东西？

在这篇文章中，我会分享自己寻找时间投入 learning efforts 的方法。我会讨论三种不同的情况，因为我发现有效的 strategies 在每种情况下都不同：

1.  **全职的 full-time learner。**
2.  **认真投入的 part-time learner。**
3.  **只能利用零碎时间，而且无法保持规律的人。**

## 1\. 制定 Full-Time Studying Schedule

这些建议适用于 full-time 或接近 full-time 的 students。通常，这包括在校的 students、为了 exam 而 studying full-time 的人，或正在参加 intensive training program 的人，也包括正在开展类似于[我的一些挑战](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/my-projects/)的 intensive self-directed projects 的人。

对于这些人，我强烈建议制定一份明确的 studying timetable。这是一份 schedule，会划出你准备投入 studying 的时段，从而在生活中的其他 constraints 下，maximize 你的 results。

就制定 schedule 而言（而不是实际坚持执行），这种情况可能是最简单的。如果你正在 studying full-time，就可以划出大致相当于 full-time work schedule 的时段，只不过把这些时间 allocate 给 studying。

话虽如此，还是有一些常见的陷阱：

### 1\. Studying 太多。

即使有很高的 motivation，也很少有人能每天稳定地 concentrate 超过 5 到 8 小时。每天安排 12+ 小时的 Studying schedules 通常是不切实际的——最可能的结果是，你最终会 procrastinating 和浪费时间，而不是在整个时段都 focusing。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-2.jpg)

### 2\. 忘记安排 sleep、exercise、socialization 和 stress-relief。

听我说，我能理解。为了极其重要的事情而 studying full-time 时，你很容易想压缩生活中的其他部分，以便 maximize 自己 learn 的量。当然，如果你要参加一场没有把握通过的 competitive exam，你自然希望自己保持 focused。

但如果 studying period 超过一两周，某些压缩实际上就会 counterproductive。Sleep 可能是其中影响最大的一项。Sleep 在 memory consolidation 中起着重要作用，因此，一个 student 如果为了多挤出两小时的 study，而每晚只睡六小时，实际上是在 undermining 自己的 performance。

Exercise 是另一种 cognitive booster，因此安排一点 exercise 是显而易见的选择——如果你特别忙，15 分钟的 higher-intensity activity 就足够了。我认为一定程度的 social contact 和 stress-relief 也很重要，尤其是当你的 studies 将持续数月时。

### 3\. 没有安排足够的 short breaks。

你需要多少 breaks，一部分取决于个人，一部分取决于主要的 studying activity。当我在 intensive 地完成 [MIT coursework](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/) 时，我发现，lecture 的日子比 reading 的日子需要更少的 breaks，而 problem-solving 的日子需要的最多。

穿插安排一些 10 到 15 分钟的 breaks 是明智的。留意自己何时开始感到 fatigued，或何时 focus 开始 degrading，从而确定某项 activity 需要多少 breaks。最好的 breaks 是短时间散步，或者，如果你很擅长小睡，就进行非常短的小睡——_而不是_社交媒体或娱乐。把那些留到之后。

### 4\. 没有在晚上或周末安排较大块的休息时间。

显然，这是个人偏好的问题。但我发现，确保晚上至少有一些空闲时间，而且每周休息一天，对我来说往往是 optimal 的。Studying 超过这个程度，实际上会对我的 productivity 产生 net-negative 影响。

如果你有许多其他义务，使你无法 studying full-time，这个 constraint 就可以稍微放宽。例如，如果你还做着另一份工作，或者有其他任务和家务，占据了一天中的大部分时间，那么晚上或周末可能就是你唯一能用来 study 的时间。

\_ \_ \_

归根结底，studying full-time 可能令人望而生畏，尤其是当你开展这个 project 并非出于个人兴趣，而是为了参加一场 professional advancement 所必需、又让人倍感压力的 exam。不过，如果你像对待一场 well-structured endurance event 那样对待它，让 pacing 和 recovery 都得到 optimized，你就能走上一条更有利于坚持下去的道路。

## 2\. 制定认真投入的 Part-Time Studying Schedule

Studying full-time 很艰难，但 studying part-time 可以说更难。那些既要 learn 困难的内容，又要承担工作和／或育儿责任的人，承受着更大的 strain，却只有更少的时间来 make progress。

不过，这里也有不错的 solutions，只是需要更多的 planning 和 preparation。

第一步是列出你必须履行的事务——也就是你无法 learn 的时段，例如你在办公室的时间、需要承担照护责任的时间等。先从 study schedule 中排除这些时段。

接下来，找出日程中最大块的未安排时间。对大多数人来说，这些时间会在早晨、晚上和周末。

然后，根据你准备投入的时间，选择一套你觉得最容易坚持的 consistent studying routine。例如，你可以决定早起一点，每天早晨上班前从 6:00 am study 到 9:00 am。或者，你可以决定下班后留下来，一直进行到 8:00 pm。你也可以决定晚上睡前晚一些时候 study，或者拿出周末的一天（或两天）来做这件事。

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-3.jpg)

所有 irregular time slots 都有 trade-offs。我曾尝试在这些时段中的大多数时段开展认真的 part-time learning projects，因此可以证实其中的一些困难：

-   早晨的 interruptions 较少，但有挤占 sleep 的风险，尤其是当你的 [chronotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronotype) 偏晚时。
-   下班后能与工作日保持 continuity，但你往往会更加 fatigued。
-   对许多人而言，晚一些时候可能更有 energy，但由于你可能已经为吃晚饭而休息了一阵，开始时往往更难 transition 过去。
-   周末或许最为自由，但把它们投入 studying 可能会干扰其他社交和家庭计划。

不论你如何安排，建立一套非常 consistent 的 routine 都至关重要。因此，如果你在 full-time job 之外，通过每天早晨上班前 studying 3 小时来每周 allocate 15 小时，就始终在那些时段 study。与尝试按照一份 _ad hoc_ 的 timetable 来 study 相比，这种 repetition 会让它更容易承受。

关于 exercise、sleep、stress relief 和用于休闲的 breaks，同样的建议在这里也适用，但情况可能更复杂。由于你不是 studying full-time，首要之务是确保足够的时间，而不是 maximizing 每小时的 efficiency。

因此，虽然我往往认为整天加整晚 studying 对 full-time student 而言会 counterproductive（因为在每天 12+ 小时的安排中，average productivity 会下降），但如果你 work full-time，而且只有 3 小时能用来 study，那么稳定地划出这三小时，可能比设法把这些时间 compress 到 90 分钟更有帮助。

不过，归根结底，_这一切都 constrained 于你有能力 sustain 的程度_。如果你有一份 full-time job、年幼的孩子以及其他生活责任，那么每周 15 小时的 studying goal 可能过于雄心勃勃。在这种情况下，先从自己能够稳定做到的较小投入开始，即使你认为这不足以实现自己的 goal，也比目标定得很高却无法做到更好。

## 3\. 找回零碎时间。

到目前为止，我讨论的都是比较认真的 projects。无论是 full-time 还是 part-time，你都很重视自己的 learning goal，而且 drive 很高。重要的是，你愿意在 schedule 中为 learning efforts 留出 dedicated time，让它们优先于生活中的其他事情。

不过，有时你无法做到这样的投入。你可能很想 learning 新东西，但也清楚，它不会优先于生活中许多其他必须做的事情。如果是这样，你该如何 maximize 可用于这类 learning 的时间？

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-4.jpg)

一般来说，我发现，dedicating time 比尝试以 _ad hoc_ 的方式 learn 东西更有效。即使你有很强的 intrinsic motivation 去 learn，也是如此。这也是大多数人通过 in-person classes 比通过 self-paced courses 能 learn 得更好的一个重要原因——medium of instruction 并没有很大差异，但对大多数人而言，报名参加 in-person class 会迫使他们在特定时间和地点 attend——实际上让他们转而采用前一种 strategy。

认真的 part-time strategy 与 spare-moment strategy 之间，真正发生变化的并不是投入的时间量（两者都是 part-time），而是后者要求你在一天中的适当时机，决定做一些 learning activity。

遗憾的是，learning 是 effortful 的，因此，做一些 learning activity，无论是 reading a book、watching lecture videos，还是 practicing a skill，通常都比玩电子游戏或看 Netflix 等其他选择更费劲。换句话说，即使你\*理论上\*可以每周为一个自己有强烈 motivation 去开展的 learning project 找出 5 到 10 小时，大多数时候你也不会这么做。你会转而做些更轻松的事情。

正因如此，我几乎总是更喜欢前面 dedicating time 的 strategy。当一件 higher-effort 的事情已经成为 habitual 时，坚持做它要容易得多；反之，如果你必须一次又一次地选择 engage spontaneously，就困难得多。

因此，这种 strategy 的成功关键往往在于，设法 reduce 参与 learning 所需的整体 effort cost，直到你能经常跨过 effort threshold。主要有以下两种方法：

1.  **Reduce learning friction**，也就是减少学习阻力。把 materials 准备好，随时可用。明确 define 下一步，并给自己留下一个马上就能看到的提醒。让 project 始终 available 且 easy-access。
2.  **Reduce alternative temptations**，也就是减少其他选择的诱惑。扔掉电视。从手机上 Delete social media。如果可能，Block distracting websites，甚至阻断你的 internet access。

第三种选择很常见，但我并不特别喜欢，那就是降低 learning activity 的要求，让 learning 本身变得更 appealing。Duolingo 等许多 educational games 都采用了这种 strategy。虽然这些 apps 可能有些价值，但相对于投入的时间，它们带来的 results 往往令人失望，因为你实际上把自己与那些更 effective、higher-effort 的 strategies 隔绝开了。

## 结合这些 Approaches

第四种选择是将 fixed studying schedule 与刚才讨论的 spontaneous engagement 相结合的 hybrid。对于既包含需要 deep focus 的 learning activities，也包含可以快速、以 low-effort 方式参与的 learning activities 的 projects，我用过这种方法。

例如，在 learning languages 时，我会进行 scheduled conversation practice 和 textbook study，以 master grammar 的 elements。但我也会在一天中不固定的间隙做一些 lower-effort activities，例如重看配成我正在 learning 的 language 的老电视剧，或练习我的 flashcards。

如果你 learning 的内容确实需要混合使用 high- 和 low-effort strategies，这种 hybrid approach 就可能成功。相比只靠 dedicating chunks of time，它可以帮你从 schedule 中挤出更多时间。关键是知道哪些 activities 是 high-effort、需要 dedicated chunks，并且在无法把它们塞进一天中零散的十分钟间隙时，不要感到沮丧。

\_ \_ \_

最终，你实际坚持下来的 studying schedule，永远不会与你为自己规划的完全相同。现实生活中会有意外的 interruptions、fatigue、motivation 以及其他因素，妨碍你完美地遵循 plan。

但我从未后悔认真构思一个 studying plan。对于艰难而磨人的 learning projects，schedule 和 pacing 会让沉重的负担变得仿佛可以承受。对于有趣而鼓舞人心的 learning projects，plan 则让你停止空想，开始行动。无论哪种情况，如果你从一个扎实的 plan 开始，你都更有可能 learn 到东西。

## 原文

A major theme in my work is trying to find ways to learn more effectively—how do we gain skill or knowledge with less wasted time and effort?   But even with a perfectly optimized studying strategy, learning requires time. Nobody becomes an expert overnight, and even reaching early levels of proficiency in new hobbies, career skills or fields of study can take a serious investment.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-1.jpg)

This raises a question: how do you find time to learn the things you want to?

In this essay, I’m sharing my approaches for finding time to devote to learning efforts. I’ll look at three different situations, as the strategies I’ve found successful differ for each:

1.  **The full-time learner.**
2.  **The serious, part-time learner.**
3.  **The person who can only devote spare moments, inconsistently.**

## 1\. Creating a Full-Time Studying Schedule

This advice applies to students who are full-time or nearly so. Typically, this includes students enrolled in school, studying full-time for an exam or otherwise tackling an intensive training program, including people tackling intensive self-directed projects akin to [some of my challenges](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/my-projects/).

For these people, I strongly recommend making an explicit studying timetable. This is a schedule that blocks off the hours you’re going to devote to studying to maximize your results, given the other constraints in your life.

In terms of making the schedule (not actually sticking to it) this is probably the simplest. If you’re studying full-time, you can block in a roughly full-time work schedule, but allocate your hours to studying.

That said, there are some common pitfalls:

### 1\. Studying too much.

Few people can reliably concentrate for more than 5 to 8 hours per day, even with high motivation. Studying schedules with 12+ hours are usually fantastical—the most likely result is that you’ll end up procrastinating and wasting time, not focusing for the entire period.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-2.jpg)

### 2\. Forgetting to include sleep, exercise, socialization and stress-relief.

Look, I get it. When you’re studying full-time for something extremely important, it can be tempting to cut some of the other aspects of your life down in order to maximize the amount you learn. And certainly, if you have a competitive exam that you’re not sure you’ll pass, you want to be focused.

But if the studying period is longer than a week or two, certain kinds of cuts are actually counterproductive. Sleep is probably the biggest one. Sleep plays a major role in memory consolidation, so a student who only gets six hours of sleep a night to squeeze in an extra two hours of study is actually undermining their performance.

Exercise is another cognitive booster, so including a little bit—15 minutes of higher-intensity activity is sufficient if you’re super busy—is a no-brainer. I would argue some amount of social contact and stress-relief are also important, particularly when your studies will span multi-month timescales.

### 3\. Not including enough short breaks.

The amount of breaks you need is somewhat personal, and somewhat dependent on the dominant studying activity. When I was doing intensive [MIT coursework](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/myprojects/mit-challenge-2/), I found that I needed fewer breaks on lecture days than on reading days, and problem-solving days required the most.

Inserting a number of 10 to 15 minute breaks is wise. Pay attention to when you start feeling fatigued or when your focus is degrading to figure out how many breaks you need for a given activity. The best kind of breaks are short walks or, if you’re a good napper, really short naps—_not_ social media or entertainment. Save those for after.

### 4\. Not scheduling larger chunks of time off in the evening or weekend.

This is a matter of personal preference, obviously. But I’ve found that ensuring at least some free evening time and one day off per week tends to be optimal for me. Studying more than this is actually a net-negative for my productivity.

This constraint is somewhat weaker if you have many other obligations that keep you from studying full-time. For instance, if you work another job or have other tasks and chores that keep you occupied much of the day, evenings or weekends may be your only available time to study.

\_ \_ \_

Ultimately, studying full-time can be daunting, especially if the project isn’t one taken on out of personal interest but because of a stressful exam needed for professional advancement. However, if you approach it like a well-structured endurance event, where pacing and recovery are optimized, you’ll put yourself on a much better path for sticking with it.

## 2\. Creating a Serious, Part-Time Studying Schedule

Studying full-time is tough, but studying part-time is arguably harder. Those who have to learn something difficult while also managing the responsibilities of a job and/or childcare face higher levels of strain with fewer hours to make progress.

However, there are good solutions here too, they simply require more planning and preparation.

The first step is to map out your mandatory commitments—times when you won’t be able to learn, such as time you’re in the office, caregiving responsibilities, etc. Remove these from your study schedule first.

Next, figure out when the largest chunks of unscheduled time exist in your calendar. For most people this will be mornings, evenings and weekends.

Then, choose a consistent studying routine that you feel would be the easiest to stick to, for the given time investment. For instance, you might decide to wake up a little earlier and study each morning from 6:00 am to 9:00 am before work. Or you might decide to stay after work and go until 8:00 pm. You might decide to study later in the evening before bed or dedicate one (or two) weekend days to the job.

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-3.jpg)

All irregular time slots have trade-offs. From having attempted serious part-time learning projects in most of these slots, I can attest to some of the difficulties:

-   Mornings have fewer interruptions, but they risk cutting in to sleep, particularly if you have a later [chronotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronotype).
-   After work maintains continuity with the workday, but you tend to be more fatigued.
-   Late evening can have more energy for many, but it often has a harder transition to start given you may have been taking time off for dinner.
-   Weekends are perhaps the most free, but devoting them to studying can interfere with other social and family plans.

Regardless of how you schedule it, making a super consistent routine is crucial. So, if you’re allocating 15 hours per week on top of a full-time job by studying for 3 hours every morning before work, always study at those times. The repetition will make it more tolerable than if you try to study on an _ad hoc_ timetable.

The same suggestions for exercise, sleep, stress relief and breaks for leisure also apply here, but it can be more complicated. Since you’re not studying full-time, ensuring sufficient hours is the priority rather than maximizing the efficiency of each hour.

As a result, while I tend to find studying all day and all evening to be counterproductive for a full-time student (because of the average productivity drop spread over 12+ hour days), if you work full-time and only have 3 hours to study, reliably carving out those three hours will likely do more than trying to compress those hours into 90 minutes.

Ultimately, however, _all of this is constrained by what you have the capacity to sustain_. If you have a full-time job, small kids and other life responsibilities, a 15-hour per week studying goal may be overly ambitious. In this case, starting with a smaller amount that you can reliably do, even if you think it’s insufficient for your goal, will be better than aiming high and failing at it.

## 3\. Recovering spare moments of time.

So far, I’ve looked at relatively serious projects. Whether it’s full-time or part-time, you’re serious about your learning goal and your drive is high. Importantly, you’re willing to prioritize your learning efforts over other things in your life by giving them dedicated time in your schedule.

Sometimes, however, such dedication isn’t possible. You may be really keen on learning something new, but you recognize that it isn’t going to take precedence over many other things that you have to do in your life. If so, how do you maximize your available time for this kind of learning?

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/How-to-find-time-for-learning-4.jpg)

Generally speaking, I find that dedicating time works better than trying to learn things _ad hoc_. This is true even if you have a lot of intrinsic motivation to learn. This is a big reason most people learn better from in-person classes than self-paced courses—the medium of instruction isn’t vastly different, but for most people, signing up for an in-person class forces them to attend at a particular time and place—effectively upgrading them to the prior strategy.

What shifts between the serious, part-time strategy and the spare-moment strategy isn’t really the amount of time dedicated (both are part-time), it’s that the latter requires you to make a decision to do some learning activity at opportune moments throughout the day.  

Unfortunately, learning is effortful, and so doing some learning activity, whether that’s reading a book, watching lecture videos or practicing a skill, will usually be an uphill climb compared to other options like playing video games or watching Netflix. In other words, even if you could \*in theory\* capture 5 to 10 hours per week for a learning project you’re strongly motivated to pursue, most of the time you won’t. You’ll do something easier instead.

It’s for this reason that I almost always prefer the previous strategy of dedicating time. It’s much easier to commit to something that’s higher-effort when it’s habitual than if you have to choose to engage spontaneously over and over again.

The key to success with this strategy, then, tends to be finding ways to reduce the overall effort cost of engaging in learning until you’re able to pass the effort threshold regularly. The two main ways to do this are:

1.  **Reduce learning friction**. Have your materials ready-to-go. Clearly define your next step and leave a reminder for yourself you will see immediately. Make the project always available and easy-access.
2.  **Reduce alternative temptations**. Get rid of your TV. Delete social media from your phone. Block distracting websites or even your internet access if possible.

A third option, which is quite common, but which I don’t particularly like, is to water down the learning activity so that the learning itself becomes more appealing. This is the strategy used by a lot of educational games like Duolingo and others. While there can be some value in these apps, they often produce disappointing results for the time invested, because you’re effectively cutting yourself off from the more effective, higher-effort strategies.

## Combining the Approaches

A fourth option is a hybrid between a fixed studying schedule and the spontaneous engagement just discussed. I’ve used this for projects that include both learning activities that require deep focus and ones that can be engaged in quickly with low-effort.

For instance, when learning languages, I do scheduled conversation practice and textbook study to master elements of grammar. But I also do lower-effort activities, like rewatching old television shows dubbed into the language I’m learning or working through my flashcards, at random intervals during the day.

This hybrid approach can be successful if what you’re learning genuinely involves a mixture of high- and low-effort strategies. It can help you squeeze more time out of your schedule than might be possible from dedicating chunks of time alone. The key is knowing which activities are high-effort and require dedicated chunks, and not getting frustrated when you can’t squeeze them in random ten-minute intervals throughout the day.

\_ \_ \_

Ultimately, the studying schedule you end up sticking with will never be exactly the same as the one you plan for yourself. Real life involves unexpected interruptions, fatigue, motivation and other factors that prevent perfect adherence to a plan.

But I’ve never regretted seriously thinking out a studying plan. For learning projects that are difficult grinds, the schedule and pacing make a heavy burden feel like one you can carry. For learning projects that are fun and inspiring, the plan allows you to stop daydreaming and begin taking action. Either way, you’re far more likely to learn something if you start with a solid plan.
