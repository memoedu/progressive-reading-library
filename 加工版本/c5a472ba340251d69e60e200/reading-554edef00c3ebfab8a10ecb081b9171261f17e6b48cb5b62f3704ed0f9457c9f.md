从1992年到2007年，我是一名 theoretical physicist。那是一项浪漫的活动，探索 cosmos 的 fundamental nature！我成为了一名 university professor，写了 papers 和 books，也有机会探索一些人类曾经 discovered 的最美妙的 ideas。与此同时，我也渐渐着迷于人类用来 _make_ knowledge 的 social systems。像 university 或 peer review 这样的 Systems，都是极为深刻的 inventions：我们的 discovery system 本身就是人类伟大的 inventions 之一！但我相信，一种 radically better discovery system 一定是可能的。我对这个 idea 越来越痴迷，最终放弃 tenure，转而投身于它[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn1)。我的第一个大 project 是写一本 book，帮助 develop 和 spread 支撑现代 open science movement 的 fundamental ideas。

这次转变让我很挣扎。我失去了 professional status 和深深认同的 creative identity，更不用说薪水了。虽然我凭直觉觉得自己在追求至关重要的事情，却花了多年工夫，才能清楚解释它为什么重要，甚至对自己也是如此。而在整个过程中，我始终不确定自己的 creative identity 是什么。我还是 physicist 吗？不是了——虽然我有时仍然使用 physics 的 techniques，但那已不再是我的 primary identity。我是 scientist 吗？虽然它仍是我工作方式中至关重要的一部分，却也不是 primary。我还曾尝试认同其他 identities，包括 open science activist、metascientist、philosopher of science，甚至 economist。但虽然我运用了它们各自的 practices，却没有哪一种让我觉得合适。

我常常为此深感苦恼。Identity _很重要_。要做好工作，你需要清楚知道怎样才能 effective，需要有 valuable work 的 models。通常，你会从自己所属的 communities 中获得这些。作为 theoretical physicist，我曾属于一个出了名地自豪的 community，大家有着共同的 story：我们的工作为什么重要，什么是好工作，以及我们心目中的英雄有哪些。一旦离开这样的 community，你就会失去这一切。当你正在追求一个 nascent creative project，而又没有与之相关的强烈 identity 时，尤其如此。即便如此，哪怕没有这样的 identity，我仍坚信 open science 很重要，继续追随自己的 creative hunch。

那段时期，最让我挣扎的 identity 是 _writer_。有时，别人会把我介绍为 science writer、science journalist、science communicator 或 non-fiction writer。所有这些称呼都让我觉得奇怪。虽然我写了[许多 essays，后来还写了几本 books](https://michaelnotebook.com/projects.html)，但我无法把自己看作一个“writer”，更不用说属于那些 genres 中的任何一种了。如果我是 writer，那也属于某种奇怪而未知的 genre。我偶尔会遇到极富创造力的人，他们私下告诉我，自己也曾为 identity 经历类似的挣扎：“我不太确定自己是谁，或者算什么。也许是 writer？Artist？Philosopher？某种 explorer 或 scientist？我不知道该怎么解释。”他们的朋友会告诉我，这些人“很难概括”，或者“独一无二”。这让我感到安慰：至少，在寻找 identity 的挣扎中，我并不孤单。

“我是 writer 吗？”这个问题一直困扰着我。有时，我会想一些带着玩笑意味的变体。Plato 是 writer 吗？Charles Darwin 呢？Albert Einstein 呢？毕竟，他们的 creative output 有很大一部分就是纸页上的 words。难道这不使他们成为 writers 吗？我们凭直觉就知道，这种说法很荒谬。显然，这些人不是 writers——他们分别是 philosopher、naturalist 和 physicist。Einstein 是在“doing physics”，而不是“writing”。某种意义上，这回答了那个问题。然而，我怀疑其中还有我未曾看见的深意。

在这篇 essay 中，我们会认真对待这些带着玩笑意味的问题。我们会考察许多不同的“writers”究竟在做什么——从 Darwin 和 Einstein，到 John McPhee、Jane Austen 和 Ted Chiang 这样更常被视为 writers 的人。哪些地方是 similar 的？哪些地方是 different 的？除了 writing，还有什么 activities 滋养着他们的工作？通过探索这些问题，我们将更好地理解新的 creative identities 如何 emerge，而它们往往来自 near-amateurs 的工作。我们会看到，forging 这样的 creative identities，常常要求人们离开已有的 communities，并在一段时间里变得连自己也无法辨认。虽然这听起来显而易见，但我花了将近二十年，才真正理解它，并 construct 一个强有力的 replacement identity。从外面看见答案是一回事，从里面找到答案则完全是另一回事。

## “How Buildings Learn”（建筑如何学习）

作为第一个 example，让我们考察 Stewart Brand 那本精彩而又极不寻常的 book“How Buildings Learn”。从封面上，你就能对这本 book 有所了解：封面展示了 New Orleans 两座并排的 buildings。这些 buildings 在1857年刚 built 时是 identical 的，但到了1993年，两座都已经 changed，彼此变得很不一样：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/HBL.jpg)

这是一本不寻常的 book。它不是常规的 non-fiction，也不是 journalism 或 photo essay，尽管这些形式的 elements 它都有。它也不属于任何其他常规的 form。更确切地说，它对标题所暗示的 question 展开了深入 exploration：buildings 究竟是如何随时间 change 的？它以一份范围广泛的 field report 为基础，collecting 了许多 buildings 如何 change 的 examples，并常常配有令人印象深刻的 photos。Brand 从这些 examples 中 extracts 出一种 conceptual armory——用来描述 buildings 如何 change 的 recurring patterns 的 terminology，以及针对最重要的 patterns of change 的 simple heuristic models。这套 armory 并非以 top-down 的方式 imposed，也并非源自某种 abstract philosophy，而是从对 individual examples 的深入思考中 arises 的[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn2)。reader 也会获得运用这些 terms 来 evaluating buildings 的经验。一座 building 有多 adaptable？一座 building 在哪些方面是 adaptable 的？它又在哪些方面 resist change？这些 questions 可以 applied 于一座 building 的 design 的每一个 element——从 materials、layout、walls、ceilings，到 electrical 和其他 fittings。你也开始理解，是什么使一些 buildings change well，另一些却 change poorly，以及这对 human psychology 和 built environment 有何 impact。

让我只举一个过于简短的 example。Brand 满怀喜爱地长篇讨论了 MIT 著名的 Building 20：这是一座“temporary”building，built 于 World War II 期间，用来容纳 radar researchers，但最后却使用了几十年。它是一座 three-story wooden building，“campus 上唯一一座你可以用 saw 来 cut \[从而 modify\] 的 building”；它的 electrical and plumbing services 处处 exposed，因此很容易 re-direct 和 augment。Brand 写道：“从一间 office 到另一间 office，从一个 lab 到另一个 lab，甚至从一个 wing 到另一个 wing，Recabling 在很大程度上都可以 do-it-yourself。occupants 并不把这视为负担，而是把它看作一项好处。”

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/bld20.jpg)

他引用了这座 building 的 occupants 对自己喜欢它哪些方面的描述：“能够 personalize 你的 space，并根据各种 purposes 来 shape 它。如果你不喜欢一面 wall，用胳膊肘把它捅穿就行。”（Jonathan Allan）“如果你想在 floor 上 bore a hole，以获得一点额外的 vertical space，那就去做。不必请示。这是有史以来 built 得最好的 experimental building。”（Albert Hill）“你永远不必担心损害这个 environment 的 architectural 或 artistic value。”（Morris Halle）

这只是一小段 excerpt，但你已经能明白大意。这就是 Brand 用来 extracts 他的 conceptual armory 的 raw material，而你也开始能看出上文隐含的一些 heuristic models 和 categories：哪些 properties 使 modify 一面 wall 或一块 ceiling 变得容易？又比如一个 practical observation：当人们不担心 artistic value 时，就更愿意 modify 一座 building；这种 tension 贯穿全书，并有许多 implications。他通过许多这样的 examples 来 develops 自己的 conceptual armory，并常常讨论 edge cases 和 exceptions，其中既有 real 的，也有 apparent 的。结果是，你会 internalize Brand 对 buildings 如何 learn 的看法。它永久地改变了我看待 buildings 的方式[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn3)。

“How Buildings Learn”出自一个最初以 amateur 的身份接近这个 subject 的人。我这样说，是在给予很高的赞誉。Brand 从一个 fundamental question——buildings 如何 learn——出发，而 architects 通常并不认真对待它。但他极为认真，drilling down，最终得出了不同于传统 architects community 的 viewpoint。然而，虽然他似乎是以 amateur 的身份开始这个 project 的，但他的工作之深入意味着，完成后的 project 绝不是 amateur 的作品。不过，他也没有成为任何现存 community 中的 expert。相反，他在 developing 一种新的 expertise，它与 architects community 之间存在一种可能富有成效的 tension。这种 tension 贯穿全书：Brand 指出了传统 architectural ideals 如何以种种方式让 buildings 变得 inflexible，并且 unresponsive to unanticipated needs。只举一个 example：许多 skyscrapers 有 glass curtain-wall facades，这使它们 architecturally striking，却也使 modify 变得 expensive 且 impractical。

如果说 Brand 在 implicitly、而有时也 explicitly 地 criticises architects，那么他们如何回应 Brand 呢？他们是否已经把这种 understanding integrated 到自己的 design 方式之中？我不知道。我怀疑这才刚刚开始；如果是这样，这本 book 仍然为某位出色的 architect 提供了机会，把这些 ideas 带入已有的 communities。

在“How Buildings Learn”中，Brand 是以 writer 的身份工作的吗？其中有 photo journalism、field reporting、architectural criticism、travel writing 等等 elements。但它们全都是 subsidiary，而非 primary form。由于他的 question 并非 founded 于任何已有的 field，他没有一整套 standard existing practice 可以借鉴[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn4)。相反，Brand 是一个极其在意回答某个特定 question 的人，为此，他 deploys 自己能找到的一切可能有帮助的 tool。photo journalism、travel writing 等 Practices，只不过是寻找更深刻 answers 的旅程的一部分。因此，Brand 并不是在任何公认的 genre 中以 writer 的身份工作。相反，他正在 developing 一种 unique written form，来回应自己面对的 problems。

将“How Buildings Learn”与成熟 genres 中的作品作 contrasting，也能说明这一点。看看 John McPhee，他为 basketballer Bill Bradley、art curator Thomas Hoving、nuclear weapons designer Ted Taylor 这样的人物 writes 精彩的 long form profiles。在每一个 case 中，McPhee 都向我们展示那个人生命中一段精心选取的切面：他们如何思考和感受，如何看待世界，又如何置身其中。McPhee 的目标不是为“buildings 如何 learn？”这样的 fundamental questions develop 深刻的 answers。虽然他的 profiles 常常包含对 fundamental questions 的 insight，但这不是 primary purpose，因此它们的 developed 程度远不及 Brand 的 answer 那么深入。（不过，McPhee 的作品也可以被视为一个宏大的 collective authorial project 的一部分，旨在回答这个 fundamental question：“human being 有哪些 varieties？”）虽然 McPhee 是我最喜欢的 writers 之一，但我自己的 creative drive 似乎更像 Brand，更倾向于那些此前很少受到关注的 fundamental questions。在这样的追求中，McPhee-style profiles 可能是有用的 tools，却不是 primary point。而且，由于这样的 questions 常常位于已有 fields 之外或其 border 上，addressing 它们往往需要运用 tools 的 novel combinations，从而催生一种 unique written form。

## "On the Origin of Species"（《物种起源》）

Darwin 的 "On the Origin of Species" 是另一本像 "How Buildings Learn" 那样，对一个 fundamental question 进行 deep exploration 的书：biological world 的 immense variety 是如何 arise 的？但它也 illustrates 了一些重要的新 patterns 和 problems。例如：在 Origin 的许多篇幅中，Darwin 都 writes beautifully，holding 和 developing 读者的兴趣。而在 Origin 的许多篇幅中，他又显得枯燥、冗长，甚至就是无聊。为什么一个出色的 prose stylist 会如此频繁地陷入乏味？

这并非偶然。Darwin 的 primary goal 并不像一般 non-fiction author 那样，是用 beautiful prose、gripping stories 和 compelling narrative arc 来 holding 读者的兴趣。他的 goal 而是 convincingly answer 他的 fundamental question：biological world 中的 variety 是如何 arise 的？Darwin 在做得到时会 writes beautifully and interestingly，但这些 goals 始终 subsidiary 于在他的 fundamental question 上 making progress。

例如，Origin 中有_大量_关于 pigeons 的内容。这些是 high quality evidence 和 argument，但即便是喜爱 pigeons 的人，也可能觉得乏味！更广泛地说：Darwin 常常需要以一长串 facts 的形式，为某个 assertion 提供 evidence。于是，我们读到的就是一长串 facts。他最好的 arguments 往往很长、很 detailed，并涉及 hedging、special cases 和 identification of weaknesses。于是，这些也都被写了进去。

这里的 tension 在于，interesting 的东西并不总是 true；true 的东西也并不总是 interesting。而这不仅 applies 于 truth，也 applies 于 convincing evidence 和 good explanations。Darwin prioritizes truth、evidence 和 explanation，而这 strongly shapes 了他的 writing。

像 Brand 一样，Darwin 似乎决心使用任何能帮助他 answer 自己的 question 的 engagement with the world 形式。Darwin 是一位非凡的 explorer、细微 details 的 observer、taxonomist、comparative anatomist，也是与遍布远方的 naturalists network 通信的 correspondent——他一定是位非凡的 letter writer——还是来自世界各地的信息碎片的 collector 和 arranger、synthesist，以及跨越许多 layers of abstraction 的 theorizer。他与许多 communities of practice 有着 deep connected 的关系，也愿意使用它们的 written forms。

这些不同的 forms 常常被 arranged 成一种 "long zoom"，这是我从 writer Steven Berlin Johnson 那里借来的 term。Darwin 会列出长长的 detailed observations 清单，将自己的 work 与他的 naturalists network 的 work combining 起来。然后 zoom out，来 explain 那份清单。然后再次 zoom out，来 connect 到其他 explanations。然后再一次 zoom out，直到最终我们看见整个 biosphere 中的 interrelationships。在如此多不同的 levels of detail 上 seeing 和 integrating，既 zooming out 又 zooming in，一切都服务于他的 overall project，这令人目眩，也非同寻常。

因此，与 "How Buildings Learn" 一样，Origin 是一本独特的书，deploying 许多不同 types 的 writing，以及 engaging the world 的 ways，只要能帮助 answer 它的 fundamental question，就无所不用。但与 Brand 不同，Darwin 的 main contribution 是 identify 一个 single principle，并为之 make the case：即 evolution by natural selection。这本书既是关于该 principle 是 correct 的 argument，也是关于其对 biology 所产生的 consequences 的 discussion。Darwin realizes，他的 principle 将 remake biology[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn5)，并努力将其 integrate 到最好的 biological thinking 中；Brand 对 architecture 则没有这样做。在这个过程中，这本书成为了未来 biology work 的 prototype，甚至超出 biology，延伸到 human and animal psychology 等 domains。正如 Darwin 所 realized 的那样，他已经 discovered 了一种 fundamental new type of explanation。

Brand 和 Darwin 都在 writing to discover[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn6)。但他们采用的 ways 不同。Darwin 是在已经 understood 了 evolution by natural selection 的 principle 之后，才决定 write Origin 的[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn7)。也就是说，基于此前数十年的 work 和 creative output，他不仅有自己的 question，也对自己的 answer 抱有信心。因此，Darwin 在 sharpening 和 developing 一个他已经相信的 idea；他也在_途中_ discovers 许多令人惊讶的 implications 和 applications。相比之下，Brand 似乎是从一个 question 开始，通过 writing process 才 discovered 他的 answer 的 basic shape。Brand 像一个启程远航的水手，决心无论发生什么，都要讲述这段 story 的精彩之处。Darwin 则像一个已经远航归来的水手——我是打个比方，尽管这在字面上也确实如此——他见过足够多的奇观，因而想讲述自己所 discovered 的东西的 story。当然，他期待 writing 也能成为一种载体，让他在讲述中 discover 更多。Brand 更接近 writing-to-explore；Darwin 更接近 writing-with-a-goal，尽管两者各自都包含对方的 elements。

writing 的 possible goals 之间的这种 distinction 让我着迷。在自己的 work 中，我在这条分界的两边都实践过。Writing-to-explore 更难，因为你并不总能确定自己会 discover 值得讲述的东西。你从一种 hunch 开始，而它往往只能慢慢变成 certainty。而 Darwin 在一开始一定就已经接近 certainty——他的 central result 源于更早的 activities，其中最著名的是 Voyage of the Beagle，而这些 activities 都各自有其 independent purpose。

我从“我是一个 writer 吗？”这个 question 开始，也提出了“Darwin 是一个 writer 吗？”这样带着玩味的 questions。当然，我们知道 Darwin 不是一个 writer；他在 "doing biology"，走出去，对世界 making detailed observations、collecting data，等等。正是这些让他成为了 biologist。那些 activities 与 writing 之间存在很大差别。问题在于：其实并没有一条 firm boundary。正如 Brand 和 McPhee 的 examples 所 illustrate 的，每个 writer 都 observes 世界；许多人相当 systematically 地这样做；许多人会非常深入地 draw on 现有 specialist communities 的 practices。一般 writer 的 activities 与 Darwin 这样的 specialist 的 activities 之间，并没有 firm boundary。事实上，后者是从前者中 emerge 的。Darwin 似乎并不认为自己是一个 biologist，这一点颇能说明问题。虽然这个 term 在他的时代已经有人使用，但他似乎认为自己是一个 naturalist，是从 amateur English naturalists 的伟大 tradition 中 emerging 出来的。我现在不打算 resolve 这个问题。更有帮助的是 observe，保持这种 tension，并继续 exploring 不同 creative people 的 practices。不过，我们还会回到这个问题。

## "On the electrodynamics of moving bodies"（论 moving bodies 的 electrodynamics）

这是 Einstein 关于 special relativity 的第一篇 paper，发表于 1905，表明 time 和 space 与它们看起来的样子 radically different：它们是 mutable 和 interchangeable，其方式令普通人的 intuition 感到震惊。这也使 length 和 simultaneity 等事物不同于我们本能中的假定。Fitzgerald、Lorentz 和 Poincare 更早的 work 已部分 anticipated 这些 conclusions。但 Einstein 的 work 给出了更有力、更 fundamental 的 explanation。他 transformed 了我们关于 space 和 time 的 notions。

Einstein 是 writer 吗？这似乎是个荒唐的想法。他所做的显然比任何常规 writing 类型都要 specialized 得多。他的 paper 发表于 journal _Annalen der Physik_，他刚刚 submitted 了 physics 的 PhD thesis——他显然是 physicist。

不过，如果考虑 Einstein 的先驱，情况就不那么清楚了。看看古希腊 philosopher Empedocles：他在公元前 5 世纪 developed 了一种 theory，其中 universe 的 fundamental constituents 是 fire、air、water 和 earth。与 Einstein 一样，他帮助人们 understand 我们世界的 fundamental constituents，并 influenced 了从 Aristotle 到 Boyle 的后世 thinkers，又通过他们 influenced 了 Einstein 等更晚近的 scientists[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn8)。但 Empedocles 并没有在 scientific paper 中 propose 他的 theory。事实上，他用 hexameter verse 写下了自己的主要 work，这与 Homer 使用的是同一种 meter。Lucretius 也是如此，他在那部著名的 work 中 arguing，cosmos 是由在 empty space 中 moving 的 atoms 构成的。Parmenides 也是如此，他 writing 的是 time、change 和 reality 的 nature。science 和 philosophy 的 history 中，一些最重要的 works 居然采用了 poetry 的形式！也许 _Nature_ 和 _The Physical Review_ 应该试一试。

我有时会想，这些早期 thinkers 是如何 self-identified 的。Empedocles 是否曾主要把自己视为 poet，只是对 physical world 有着异乎寻常的浓厚兴趣？Lucretius 或 Parmenides 呢？这似乎完全有可能！

这似乎是个幽默的轶事，但它的意义更深。随着我们 develop 新的 understanding 和 explaining 世界的手段，我们也 develop 出新的 literary forms。但那些 understanding 和 explaining 的手段只会缓慢 emerge，这些 forms 也一样。Empedocles 以那样的方式写作，并不愚蠢。相反，他是在使用自己熟悉的 medium 来 develop 自己的 understanding。但他也在 modifying 和 developing 这个 medium，以 support 新种类的 thought，以及这些 thought 的新 representations。从这个角度看，writing 是一种 mutable、extensible 的 tool，可以用来逐渐 expand 我们理解世界的方式。它与 physical world 中相应的 activities 一同 evolves——observing 和 intervening 的方式，以及理解事物的方式。physics 和 biology 这样的 expertise 领域不会以 fully formed 的状态 emerge。相反，它们始于一些 activities 和 written record，与其他日常活动几乎无法 distinguish。只有经过非常漫长的时间，distinct 的 body of knowledge 和与世界 engaging 的方式才会逐渐 arise。因此，Writing 可以作为一种 medium，用来 develop 新的 understanding 方式。

我们可以从这个角度看前面的 examples。"How Buildings Learn" 是一位极富才华且全心投入的 amateur 的 work。人们很容易将他与从零开始的 Empedocles 相比。但 Brand 可借鉴的 models of work 比 Empedocles 多得多。或许更好的比较对象是 Galileo：他从 modest-but-real foundations 出发，为整个 subjects laid the foundation。或者，回到 introduction 中的一个 example，也可以与 Plato 相比，他所处的时代，philosophy 才刚开始从 common thought 中 differentiate。Plato 既是 writer，也是 philosopher——一种 proto-philosopher。相比之下，Einstein builds on 一个经过数百年 development、极其深厚的 community of practice。事实上，它深厚到不仅大多数其他人无法那样 write，甚至大多数人都无法那样 read[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn9)。但情况并非一直如此。不难看出，Einstein 是 Galileo 这类人物的 intellectual descendant，再往前追溯，则是 Empedocles 的 intellectual descendant。Darwin 的 "The Origin of Species" 是一个 intermediate case，它从 communities of practice 的 network 中 emerging，但那些 communities 尚未达到 1905 年 physics 的深度。

因此，Brand、Darwin 和 Einstein 展示了 written forms、understanding 的类型与 expert community 共同 develop 的不同 stages——从 amateur exploration 到深厚的 community of practice。但它们最初都是人类共同交流的一部分，只是随着时间推移才逐渐 specialize。

我前面提到，Darwin 意识到，biology 中几乎每个 question 都需要根据 natural selection 来 rethought。同样，Einstein 对 space 和 time 的 understanding 的改变，remakes 了我们对 cosmos 的 understanding 中既 universal 又 fundamental 的东西。其程度如此之深，以至于这样的想法似乎颇为可信：数十亿 light years 之外的 aliens[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn10) 也会将 space 和 time 体验为 fundamental；或许也会经历类似的历程，先 discovering 关于 space 和 time 的 Descartes-Galileo-Newton mathematical description，再通过 discovering special and general relativity[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn11) 来 improve 它。

Einstein 的 paper 有一组 takeaways，即可以在其他 contexts 中精确 reused 的 precise mathematical relationships——例如，Lorentz transformations[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn12) 就是一组 mathematical equations，describing 了不同 reference frames 中的 space 和 time measurements 如何 related。又如 Einstein 后来一篇关于 relativity 的 paper 中，或许是所有 equation 中最著名的那个：E = mc^2。它们相当于这些 papers 的一种 interface 或 API：它们 express 了 exact conclusions，你可以在别处 reuse，甚至不必参照 paper 关于它们为何 correct 的 argument。而且，由于 space 和 time 如此 fundamental，这些 results _确实_在各处被 reused，从 engineering 到 chemistry，再到 navigation 和 science fiction。简而言之，这种 understanding 向外 propagates，影响着我们对世界的整个 understanding。这确实是一个不可思议的事实：这些 expressions——纸上的弯弯曲曲的符号！——竟能如此 precisely 地 express 对世界如此强大的 understanding。Darwin 的书也提供了这样的 principle，虽然稍欠 precise，但 Darwin 对它的 articulated 如此有力，又在 multiple contexts 中 applied，以至于它也很容易在别处被 reused。Brand 的 conceptual armory 还要稍欠 precise，但仍然足够有用，也同样可以被 reused。这三者都是人类对世界的 understanding 的胜利，也是我们在 multiple contexts 中 express 和 apply 这种 understanding 的能力的胜利[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn13)。

## Stories（故事）

我在这篇 essay 开头问，像 Einstein 和 Darwin 这样的人是否可以被视为 writers。让我们把这个问题倒过来，与那些最通常被视为 writers 的人作 contrast——也就是 "Hamlet"、"Pride and Prejudice" 或 "Stoner" 这类 fictional stories 的 authors。严格说来，要回答这篇 essay 的问题，我们并不需要这样做。但这会帮助我们更好地 understand writing 能做什么，以及 creative identities 的 space 可以是什么样的。乍看之下，这类 stories 似乎在做与我们之前的 examples 很不一样的事：它们 evoke alternate worlds，以及 experiencing the world 的其他方式[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn14)。philosophers 告诉我们，我们无法知道成为一只蝙蝠是什么感觉；但雄心勃勃的 novelists 并不因此退缩，他们或许希望让我们 experience 成为 Hamlet、Miss Elizabeth Bennet、William Stoner，甚至更加陌生的他者是什么感觉。

有时，一位 writer 会承担格外艰巨的挑战。Nabokov 在 "Lolita" 中带我们进入 pedophile Humbert Humbert 的 mind。虽然 Humbert Humbert 令人恶心、厌恶，Nabokov 却让这个 story 如此 compelling，使我们无法移开目光。我们身处这个怪物的 mind 之中，以 first person 来 experiencing，甚至比在 "Hamlet"、"Pride and Prejudice" 或 "Stoner" 中还要贴近。更令人佩服的是：Nabokov 并没有（至少大部分时候没有）通过 "true crime" sensationalism 来使它 compelling。Zadie Smith 的 comment 很贴切：“读 Lolita 时，仿佛书页在燃烧……我不知道一本书竟然能做到这样。”

我们那些 non-story examples——Brand、Darwin 和 Einstein——并不主要是为了给予我们这样的 experience of being。相反，他们 improve 我们对世界的 descriptive understanding。当然，"Hamlet" _et al_ 这样的 stories 也会 improve 我们的 descriptive understanding——fiction 有时是比 psychologists 所做的更好的 psychology，也是比 political scientists 所做的更好的 political science——但这并不是 principal point。Hamlet 没有 API。principal point 是 experience 本身[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn15)。

我们一直在讨论 story-as-experience。但请让我 describe 这一目标的两种引人注目的 variations[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn16)。其中一种，体现在我最喜欢的 short stories 之一、Ted Chiang 那篇令人惊叹的 "Story of Your Life" 中（下面有剧透！）。表面上，这是一个女人的 story，她：坠入爱河，随后离婚；那段结合带来了一个女儿；女儿后来去世；而且——这是其中最明显的 science fictional element——她帮助 humanity 学会与一个 aliens 种族 communicate。

这个 story 的 conceit 是，aliens experience time 的方式与 humans 截然不同。它们 simultaneously experience all times，因此，从 human 的角度看来，它们似乎不仅在 experiencing 过去和现在，也将未来 experiencing 为一种不可避免的展开，一件已经发生的事。此外，Chiang 勾勒出一种方式，让这种 experience of time 能够从 aliens 传给与它们 interacting 的 human beings。我们的 narrator 逐渐 internalizes 这种 altered experience of the world。这是 fiction，你需要 suspend disbelief，但它足够 compelling，能够作为 story 成立。

第一次读 "Story of Your Life" 时，我觉得 narrator 的 voice 很奇怪。她显得 detached，几乎 emotionless。并非完全 unfeeling——她也会 wry、ironic 或 humorous。但她身上有一种疏离感。当可怕的事情发生——例如女儿去世——这种疏离变得格外强烈，仿佛不近人情。我无法理解。随后：一次 radical perspective shift，一次 epiphany，所有 dissonant moments 突然各就其位：原来，这就是她 experience 这种 state of temporal consciousness 时的 _感受_。此前读来奇怪的一切，如今都有了意义：她的 dispassion，那种 wry irony，所有的一切。最奇怪的是，我在自己身上也感到了这种陌生的 state of consciousness 的一丝微弱迹象。这是我一生中最令人天旋地转、迷失方向的 experiences 之一。此后，我 reread 这个 story 很多次，每次都会有同样的感觉，虽然再也没有第一次那样强烈、那样突然：仿佛开始滑入一种能看见自己整个人生的观看方式，开始 understanding，will 和 power to choose 并不 distinct from fate 和 predetermination。

伟大的 stories——事实上，任何类型的伟大 writing——都会为我们 create 一个强大的新 context，让我们在其中 think[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn17)。但有时，在极其罕见的情况下，它们或许会 create 新的 states of consciousness，这些新的 states 会 alter 我们是谁，哪怕只是短暂而不完整地。显然，"Story of Your Life" 并没有赋予我看见未来的能力。但它是一次极其鲜明的 experience，也提醒我，humanity 可以有截然不同的 ways of being，而 alien other 或许还有更加深刻不同的 ways of being[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn18)。

另一个引人注目的 example 是 Christian Bible。我曾反复琢磨它究竟在做什么。这是一部由多位 authors 跨越几个世纪创作的 collection，因此，人们可能会预期，它的 purpose 不那么清晰。我能找到的最佳简短回答是：Bible 在为一个 people 提供 identity[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn19)。它在说：这就是你们如何成为今天的你们。这就是你们在自己的 history 中做过的事，你们的高潮与低谷，你们面向未来的 potential，以及你们能做什么。这里还有关于真正成功意味着什么的严格 standards：作为 individual，作为 family，作为 community，作为 culture。它在提供一个 model，说明如何在 cosmos 中存在。

Bible 作为 writing 的 effective 程度令人惊讶。它的 authors 写作的年代远早于 Nabokov、Williams、Austen 或 Shakespeare，也无法受益于后来的 authors 所能利用的 literary invention。然而，其中许多 stories 都有巨大的力量。尤其是 Crucifixion 的 story，是有史以来讲述过的最有力量的 stories 之一：Son of God 来到世间，如此爱你——是的，就是你——以至于他接受了极其痛苦而不公正的 death，为的是 save 你，使你免受自己的 sins 的 consequences。这是一个非凡到足以 transformative 地影响 individual 和 cultural identity 的 story！这种力量因一再被 retold 而 amplified。从 Leonardo 到 Andrew Lloyd Webber、Dante、Martin Luther King, Jr.，再到 Monty Python，Creators 都曾围绕 Biblical stories 写出 riffs。因此，Bible 拥有有史以来最好、最多样的 fan fiction；事实上，modern culture 很大一部分都是从 Bible 中 extrusion 出来的。如果说 Chiang 能短暂地 transform 一位 reader 的 consciousness，那么 Bible 则已经持久地 transformed 了我们 culture 的 consciousness。

我们一直在讨论 story forms。此外，还有许多引人注目的 adjacent forms。我最喜欢用来 write 的 forms 之一，是我称为 [discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html) 的东西。一些 examples 包括：这条 [Twitter thread](https://x.com/michael_nielsen/status/1132063254849527808)，它 explaining 一个具备 quantum mechanics 的 basic knowledge 的人如何能够逐步 invent quantum teleportation；还有[这篇 essay](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/)，它 explaining 一个人如何能够逐步 invent Bitcoin protocol。（更多 examples 见[这里](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/df.html)）。总体 idea 是，discovery fiction 提供一个 plausible story，讲述 reader 本可以如何 discovered 像 Bitcoin protocol 这样的 result。通常，它会有一条由小 questions 和 ideas、false starts 和 backtracking 构成的 arc，通过 incremental steps 最终 leading to 那个 result。理想情况下，这些东西都不应凭空出现：它们都应该是 simple、almost-obvious steps，是那种能让 reader 心想“哦，对，我很可能也会试试这个，也许不是我的第一选择，但稍微想一想之后就会”的东西。

对于 reader 来说，这种 experience 是逐渐从内部 understand 那个 result。起初，这样重大的 results 往往显得令人望而生畏，几乎 incomprehensible。然后，一点一点地，insight 开始浮现。你面对 creative choices 及其 consequences；你追究 false starts，并 discover 为什么更好的 choices 能 work。这会 create 一种 consciousness，让你知道 invent 像 teleportation 或 Bitcoin 这样的东西是什么感觉。你不只是 understand 那个 result，你还 experience 并 understand 它 _为什么_ 是那样。Discovery fiction 不需要 historically accurate——事实上，history 往往要 random、confusing 得多。相反，它是一种 fictional non-fiction，像 story 一样旨在提供一种 experience，但这是一种 discovery 的 experience。

我对 discovery fiction 有许多充满憧憬的 ideas。其中，我已经为“How to discover space 和 time 并非如其所呈现的那样”（关于 discovering relativity）、“如何 discover 一个 law of nature”、“如何 discover 一个 mathematical theorem”，甚至“如何 discover science”记下了一些 notes。像“How to discover space 和 time 并非如其所呈现的那样”这样的作品，其 aspiration 不只是让 reader 知道 Einstein 所知道的东西。而是让他们开始像 Einstein 那样看世界，在某种微小的程度上 transform 他们的 consciousness。

另一种强大的 story-adjacent form，被 Robert Caro 用在他那部广受赞誉的 US President Lyndon Johnson 的 biography 中。正如 [Tanner Greer 所 pointed out 的](https://x.com/Scholars_Stage/status/1817915094829068537)，Caro 的书以一种在 history 和 biography 中不常见的方式，incorporates 了 fictional storytelling 的强大 techniques。这些 genres 通常遵循一种 convention：writer 不能 report subject 在 thinking 或 feeling 什么，除非 subject 曾明确说出来——也许是在 interview、memoir 或 diary 中，或者对其他 reliable source 说过[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn20)。相比之下，fictional stories 的一个巨大 advantage 是，你可以用 non-fiction 目前[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn21)做不到的方式 describe characters 的 experience——他们在 thinking 什么，在 feeling 什么，甚至 evoke 他们自己也可能 unconscious of 的部分 being。这些都是 fiction 的强大 advantages[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn22)。

Caro 使用一种巧妙的 technique，来 combine fiction 和 non-fiction 的 benefits。在讲述 Johnson 的 story 时，Caro amasses 大量 detailed factual information。但他偶尔会中断 factual narrative，来 speculate Johnson 当时在 thinking 或 feeling 什么。这些内容被清楚地 marked 为 speculation，但 factual portrait 如此 compelling，Caro 对 Johnson 的 understanding 又如此 acute，以至于你会发现自己相信了 Caro 的 speculation：你被带入 Johnson 的 experience 之中。因此，你既获得了 truth 的 force、veracity 和 surprise，也获得了 fiction 的 transformative experience。

与 Brand 和 Darwin 一样，Caro 展现了 combining 多种 forms of engagement with the world 的力量。而像 discovery fiction 和 Caro 的 speculative interiority 这样的 techniques，则表明 fiction 的 techniques 如何能够用于 conventional fiction 之外的 purposes。

story 是什么？这并不那么显而易见。我们都认得 stories。但我发现，很难向自己 explain 一个 story 是什么，更不用说一个 good story 了。我很喜欢 Douglas Adams 的那句俏皮话：story 是“以巧妙的 order 排列的 80,000 个 words”。这既是一个好笑话，也是一个引人注目的 insight。但我怀疑，Adams 这样说，部分是为了回避 story 是什么这个问题。不过，我喜欢反复琢磨的一个 idea 是：story 是一个 premise，它让你强烈地想知道接下来会发生什么，并且会一直持续，直到这种感觉得到了满足[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn23)。

好的 story 与好的 explanation 表面上有许多共同之处。但它们非常不同。某个东西可以是好的 story，却是差的 explanation；也可以是好的 explanation，却是差的 story。我们在 Darwin 身上看到了这一点：他愿意 sacrifice 好的 story，以换取对世界更深入的 understanding。清楚把握这一 distinction，可以避免许多 confusion（偶尔也能避免痛苦）。这一直是 social media age 的一个挑战：good-but-false stories 往往比那些构不成好 stories 的好 explanations 更容易 propagate。

Story 的 origin 几乎肯定深藏于我们的 evolutionary past。这很奇怪，因为据我们所知，chimps 和其他 animals 并不 tell stories。但我们很难不相信，它的根源十分深远。也许正因如此，我们才如此经常地 confuse story with reality！我很喜欢 Muriel Rukeyser 的这句话：“universe 是由 stories 构成的，而不是 atoms。”从 human experience 的意义上说，这是 true：我们的 minds 是 narrativized。但在我们之外，也存在一个 unself，一个超出我们 selves 的 cosmos；它无法通过单一线索被 narrativized，从而被 human consciousness 把握。cosmos 超越了 narrative。但一个伟大的 story 可以成为一种在 real 或 imagined worlds 中进行 selecting 的方式，evoke 更广阔的 cosmos 的某些面向，在 self 与 unself 之间搭起桥梁。

## 是什么滋养了 writing？

在他的 memoir《On Writing》中，writer Stephen King 讲述了自己如何为正在写的任何一部 novel 设定每天 writing 2,000 words 的 goal。正如他指出的那样，按这个速度，即使经过 multiple drafts，你每年也能 write 一本篇幅可观的 book。

这对 King 行得通。他住在 Maine 一个安静的地方。他很少旅行。他此前已经 internalized 了数量惊人的 story patterns 和 ideas。他也继续喂养自己的 story 恶魔：上午 writing，下午则以 reading 作为补充。他已有的 understanding 与 reading 结合起来，generate 足够多的 ideas，支撑每天 2,000 word 的 goal。

需要注意的是，这种 approach 是为 King 喜欢 tell 的特定 story types 量身定制的。

我提起这件事，是因为 King 每天 2,000 word-per-day 的 goal 一直令我十分着迷。King 把自己的 argument 讲得很好，而且充满确信。有时我会觉得自己很失败，因为我无法始终如一地做到同样的事，哪怕把 goal 定得更低一些（比如每天 600 words）。对我所做的这种 creative work 而言，这_显然_很荒唐。但这也说明了对 identity 的 confusion 会造成怎样的问题。如果你正琢磨着“我是 writer 吗？”，又看到一个令人信服的 argument，说你应该能够做到某件事，而你却做不到，你便会疑惑：是不是我做的方法不对？当然，King 和我的 creative goals 截然不同。但很长一段时间里，我都难以足够有说服力地 articulate 这些差异，以摆脱“为什么不能每天写 2,000 words”的困扰[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn24)。在这一节里，我要弥补这一点。

这是 Stewart Brand，身处他 writing《How Buildings Learn》时使用的 shipping container 中：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/shipping.jpg)

他正在箱子里面 thinking。这个人对自己的 creative work 是_认真的_。这个 space 的一切都传达着同一个信息：这个 project 很重要。这是一个让他借以 think 的 space，帮助他 organize 自己的 thinking，并让他在情感上与 project 相连。它是 adaptable 的，能够随着他所做的事情 evolve；但同时又是 organized 的，很可能 reflecting 并 supporting 这本 book 的 organization。我经常和其他 creative researchers 一起谈论这张图片，其中许多人都觉得它很鼓舞人心。

如果说 Brand 为他的 book 注入了巨大的 creative force，那么 Darwin 的投入就更加非同寻常。《The Origin of Species》是一段非凡人生的顶峰；他以巨大的力量度过了这一生，而这一切——回过头来看！——都是在为 writing《Origin》做准备。Darwin 以为期 5-year、环游世界的 Voyage of the Beagle _开始_了这一切。他经历了 disease、seasickness、有记录以来规模最大的 earthquakes 之一、一次险些发生的 shipwreck，以及许多其他灾难。但自始至终，他都是一位出色的 naturalist，不断 exploring、observing，并将 detailed accounts 寄回 England。Darwin 对 biological world 中惊人的 diversity，对环绕着我们的那些“endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful”，有着历史上几乎无人能够比拟的深切确信。结束 voyage 回来之后，他花了多年时间 studying，与世界各地的 naturalists 通信，并完成了另外几本可称为小经典的 books。他与 natural world 在情感、智识和实践上都保持着深刻的交流。从 Einstein 的 letters[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn25) 中，你也能获得类似的感受：那是与 cosmos 背后的 order 的另一种深刻交流。有时，读他的文字会让我热泪盈眶。

Writers 有时会谈论自己的“research process”。这个 term 很有用，却远远不够宽广。Ben Franklin 为其著名 autobiography 所做的“research process”，就是度过他不可思议的一生。为了他的 book《Seven Pillars of Wisdom》，T. E. Lawrence 在 World War I 期间帮助促成了反抗 Ottoman Empire 的 Arab Revolt。这可真是一番 research process。Einstein 不仅将自己人生中的许多经历带入了那篇关于 special relativity 的 paper，也带入了数百年来人类最杰出的 thought 和 experiment。对 Brand 和 Darwin，也可以作出类似的评价。只要一个人的人生足够丰富，无论他是否选择 write，他总会拥有足以写出一本非凡 book 的 material[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn26)。对于任何 creative person，更好的问题是：你想 pursue 哪些 activities？那会 create 哪些 kinds of understanding？通过更深入、更有力、更富想象力的 exploring，你能走多远？又有哪些 communities 提供了 powerful tools，让你可以用来 deepen 自己的 understanding？

这一切也有令人难过的另一面。许多 self-identify 为 writers 的人，都抱怨自己的日常工作。要是能辞掉那些烦人的工作就好了！那样他们就能加快速度，作为 writers 大展身手！终于，他们鼓足勇气辞了职。但令人惊讶的是，这往往反而使他们的 writing 枯竭。他们没有理解，那些“blocking their writing”的日常 activities，对于 generating material 至关重要。Writing 并非凭空而来。对于任何 writes 的人——实际上，对于任何以任何方式 creates 的人——它都来自其他某种 source。你需要一些 activities，generate 你的 writing 所依托的 insight。有时那是其他 books，Stephen King 显然就是如此。有时那是对世界的深入 investigation，正如 Brand 或 Darwin 的例子。有时那是帮助建立一个国家，正如 Ben Franklin 的情况。而有时，他们借鉴的是极为深厚而成熟的 communities of expert practice，Einstein 就是如此。再说一次，问题是：哪些 activities 会 generate 你所寻求的 types of understanding？又如何能在 write 的同时 deepen 这些 activities，形成一个 loop，让你的 writing practice 与你进入世界的方式 co-evolves，从而 generate 更多、更深入的 insight？

在《On Writing》中，King criticises Harper Lee 只 writing 了一本 book[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn27)——《To Kill a Mockingbird》。King 表达了对这本 book 的赞赏，但也问道：她把所有时间都花在什么事情上了？为什么没有 write 更多？当然，答案是 Lee generated insight 的方式与 King 截然不同。她更接近 Ben Franklin 或 T. E. Lawrence：她花了几十年生活在 Jim Crow Alabama（并 closely observing 那里）。而且，尽管 King 是一位优秀的 writer，他却没有 written 出任何能与《To Kill a Mockingbird》相媲美的作品[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn28)。Lee 的 process 或许很慢。但它 generated 了对世界的非凡 understanding，以及一本同样非凡的 book。

让我回到开头的问题：“我是 writer 吗？”答案比我最初领会到的要微妙得多。Brand、Darwin 和 Einstein 在某种程度上都是 writers——他们都在用 words 作为 understanding 的 medium[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn29)。从实用角度看，一个有用的问题是：你首要期望的 creative output 是什么？如果你的 emotional investment 主要在于 writing 一本 book，那么你把自己 classify 为 writer 就是合理的。从这个意义上说，我认为 Brand 在 wrote《How Buildings Learn》时，是在以 writer 的身份工作。但 Darwin 的主要投入在于 exploring natural world，以及参与相关 communities。Darwin 是一位同时也 wrote 的 explorer 和 researcher。我对此很有共鸣。当 Richard Feynman 说出下面这段话时，我也很有共鸣：“我是个 explorer，明白吗？我对一切都会好奇，我想 investigate 各种各样的东西。”对我而言，仅仅成为一个 writer 太过孤立。Exploring 和 writing 并不处于 tension 之中；相反，它们相互 support、相互 reinforce。

从这个角度看，围绕“我是 writer 吗？”的 confusion 就消解了，不再重要。这就像是在问：“我是 speaker 吗？”嗯，是的，如果你指的是我用 language 来 develop 自己的 understanding。之所以会产生这种 confusion，只是因为 writing 既是一种 identity，也是一种 understanding 的 medium，而这种 medium 可以用来 support 其他截然不同的 identities。对于那些 pursuing 不寻常的 creative work、却没有相应 expert identity 的人来说，“writer”因此很容易成为一个有吸引力的 fallback identity。这虽然自然，却也可能 misleading，甚至 damaging——如果它暗示了错误的工作 models 的话。从这个角度说，这篇 essay 恰好讨论 writing，完全是一种偶然。我不是 writer。我是一位同时也 writes 的 explorer 和 researcher。更准确地说：我会对 under-explored fundamental questions 产生兴趣，然后用任何看起来最 powerful 的 tools 去 pursues 它们，writing 也是其中之一。对其他人而言，这仍不是一种容易 legible 的 identity；它没法印在 bumper sticker 上[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn30)。但它 captures 了我的 creative drive 的很大一部分。

我没能更早就有力地向自己 articulating 清楚这一点，为此我觉得自己很傻。但当一个人身处 well-defined communities 之外时，要让自己对自身变得 legible，是需要时间的[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn31)。现在，我可以自信地回答开头提出的 personal identity 问题了。在某种程度上，我是 scientist、physicist、interface designer、writer，也拥有许多其他 identities。但这些都是 subsidiary，都服务于我作为 creative explorer 和 researcher 的 primary identity。这也包括我身上的 scientist——但我所说的 scientist，在意义上更接近 Darwin 或 Brand，而不是某个 professionalized subfield 中的现代 specialist。对我而言，Brand 或 Darwin 这样的 prototypical model，远胜于 Stephen King 这样的人。Writing 只是 express 和 develop 我的 understanding 的一种载体。但它是我的仆人，而非主人，也不是 primary identity。这一切听起来像个很容易得出的 conclusion，但对我而言，却是通过 experience 艰难获得的。许多其他 creative workers 也走过了同样曲折的道路。一个更好的问题是：为了服务于我的 primary identity，我可以成为哪一种 writer？

## 尾声：我是哪一种 writer？

摆脱了把 "writer" 当作 primary identity 的桎梏之后，问这样一个问题就很有趣了：为了服务于 creative exploration 和 research，我喜欢做什么样的 writing？就我而言，我喜欢 writing to discover——让 writing 与对世界的 exploration 彼此对话。我喜欢把 writing 当作 think 和 understand 的场所，当作一个 powerful context，不仅供 reader 在其中 think，更为 writer 提供一个更加 powerful 的 context 来 think。这样的 writing 是一个容纳缓慢生长的勇气、胆识和 imagination 的场所。我过去曾希望自己能 write quickly。但如果你的 writing 正在提供一个 powerful context 来 think，那么，只要这个 process 还在 rapidly generating 额外的 insight，你就应该希望它尽可能长久地持续下去。

在 writing to discover 中，有一个问题：你会在最终的 text 中把 discovery 隐藏多少。Background notes 和 first drafts 通常围绕 discovery 的 process 展开。但在后来的 drafts 中，如果你愿意，就可以把 raw exploration 转化为看似直接揭示出来的 insight，把 construction 的路标隐藏起来。所有 writers 都会在某种程度上隐藏 thought[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn32)，既出于实际需要——没有人能记录自己的全部 thinking——也出于对 reader 的体贴，省略自己最不有趣的 thinking。但隐藏多少，取决于选择和意愿。做这些隐藏工作往往需要大量额外劳动。在这篇 essay 中，我做了一点 elision，但也保留了许多 construction 的路标，因此，对作为 reader 的你和作为 author 的我来说，这篇 essay 本身都是一次 discovery 的 experience。

我喜欢通过 writing 来 drill down on fundamental questions，这些 questions 属于 nascent 或尚未存在的 fields。这个 pattern 是我关于 [open science](https://www.amazon.com/Reinventing-Discovery-New-Networked-Science/dp/0691160198)、[metascience](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html)、[tools for thought](https://numinous.productions/ttft/)、[the mnemonic medium](https://quantum.country/) 等主题的 books 或 book-length essays 的基础。但我对此也保持警惕。它很容易变成 dilettantism。我经常遇到一些人，他们告诉我，他们也想像我一样，exploring 各种各样的 fundamental questions。但要做到这一点，你就需要找到真正 new 且 useful 的 insights，而且不仅对你自己如此，（理想情况下）也应对全人类如此。这是一个严苛的 standard。而且，虽然我说的是 "drill down on fundamental questions"，但往往并没有一个 well-formed starting question，只有一种直觉，觉得有_某种东西_值得 exploring。因此，你必须忍受 creative fog、困惑与无知，找到值得 drilling down on 的 questions，而在这一切过程中，你都不知道最终能否找到值得这番努力的 insights！

我喜欢把 writing 当作 making a unique contribution 的一种方式。我曾与一位知名 scientist 交谈，他在竞争极其激烈的 CRISPR 领域工作。他说（大意是）："当然，我们 scientists 做这些工作，很大一部分原因是竞赛带来的兴奋感。"他津津乐道地讲述自己如何比另一个或几个 lab 早一天或几天得到某个 result，从而 "击败" 对方；又如何以同样微小的差距输掉这场 "竞赛"。我很惊讶。我宁愿做能让我 making a unique contribution 的事，而不是某种 easily substitutable 的事。

在上述各方面，我在 Brand 和 Darwin，以及许多其他我敬佩的 creative workers 身上，都看到了许多 similar patterns。我不知道他们自己是否这样看。但从外部看，我是这样理解的。也许更不寻常的是，我喜欢写 discovery fiction，让自己和别人获得一些 experience，体会从内部 discover 一组 ideas 是什么感觉。我喜欢 experience 和 description 之间这种界限的模糊，喜欢我们能够借此领会 discovery，领会一种发生了变化的 state of consciousness。更一般地说：我认为，在 fiction 之外，story 被低估了。我喜欢寻找新的方式来 bridge experience and description，既让人获得对 the other 的 experience，也给予他们关于 the other 的 powerful descriptions。这种 fusion 远比两者单独存在时 powerful。我也喜欢写 possibility，尝试在 reader（以及我自己）心中唤起一种感觉：有新的 worlds 等待 explore。

有一件事我不做：我不会 write beautifully。Poets 从这一追求中找到意义，借助 texture 和 resonance，evoke 出比看似可能的还要更多的东西。许多其他 writers 也是如此，尤其是 literary fiction 的 writers。许多人和 Flaubert 一样相信，"prose 中的 good sentence 应当像 poetry 中的 good line 一样，unchangeable，同样 rhythmic，同样 sonorous"。我知道这会改善 experience，但我首先忠于的，是 plainly expressed 的 understanding。Writing true sentences 就已经够难了。话虽如此，正如我的朋友 Fawaz Al Matrouk 向我强调的，对世界 sharply distilled understanding 往往_确实_蕴含着巨大的 beauty。这是一种我深深认同的、对 beauty 的追求。

另一件我兴趣不大的事是：为了 educate 或 reach a wide audience 而 writing。当人们觉得我写的东西在这些方面 useful 时，我很高兴，也常常心怀感激。但这不是一种 primary emotional drive。当我依靠这些追求来推动自己时，就会感到疲惫而兴味索然。即使是我（与 Ike Chuang 合著）的 quantum computing textbook，虽然许多人认为它具有 educational 性质，但写它主要也是为了向我自己 explain 这个 field，并在可能的时候，在 creatively 的意义上稍稍向前推进。我很高兴这项工作对他人有所帮助。但这只是 motivation 中的一小部分，而不是 primary 的部分。

Writing 是一种神奇的 technology，能够 support exploration and discovery，让 human understanding 得以进行某种 gradient ascent。你一遍又一遍地做出微小的 improvements。也许是丢掉一个 word 或 phrase，换成更好的；也许是整本 book。据说 Robert Louis Stevenson 曾把 "Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde" 的 first draft 扔进火里。你 write down 自己的困惑、不适、exceptions 和疯狂的 ideas。然后，你慢慢地 agglomerate improvement。你可以连续多年不断 improving。我不相信，不使用 external medium[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn33)，你就能 think well；你需要某种东西，帮助你 improve 那些对不借助外力的头脑而言过于 complex 的 thoughts。这可能是一场艰难的跋涉，充满了 understanding 上那些往往极其微小的 improvements。但它们会积少成多，在巅峰时刻带来 transformative 的效果。正如 _New Yorker_ 的 editor William Shawn 对 John McPhee 所说："该花多久，就花多久。"而对于我们这些正在 developing 新的 creative identities 的人，writing 是我们拥有的最 powerful 的仆人之一。

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

[Astera Institute](https://astera.org/)

March 25, 2026

I was a theoretical physicist from 1992 to 2007. It was a romantic activity, exploring the fundamental nature of the cosmos! I became a university professor, wrote papers and books, and got to explore some of the most beautiful ideas ever discovered. In parallel, I also developed a fascination with the social systems humanity uses to _make_ knowledge. Systems like the university or peer review are immensely deep inventions: our discovery system is itself one of the great inventions of humanity! But I believed a radically better discovery system must be possible. I became obsessed with this idea, eventually giving up tenure to work on it[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn1). My first big project was to write a book helping develop and spread the fundamental ideas underlying the modern open science movement.

I struggled with this transition. I lost my professional status and deeply-held creative identity, not to mention my salary. While I instinctively felt I was pursuing something crucial, it took years of work before I could clearly explain why it mattered, even to myself. And throughout I was uncertain what my creative identity was. Was I still a physicist? No – while I sometimes still used the techniques of physics, it was no longer my primary identity. Was I a scientist? While it was still a crucial part of how I worked, it wasn't primary either. Other identities I flirted with included open science activist, metascientist, philosopher of science, even economist. But while I used practices from all, none felt right.

I often felt great distress about this. Identity _matters_. To do good work, you need a strong sense of how to be effective, models for valuable work. Most often, you acquire these from the communities you belong to. As a theoretical physicist I'd been part of a notoriously proud community, with a shared story about why our work mattered, what good work is, and a pantheon of heroes. When you exit such a community, you lose all that. This is especially true when you're pursuing a nascent creative project without a strong associated identity. Still, even without such an identity, I held onto the belief that open science mattered, and continued pursuing my creative hunch.

The identity I struggled with most during that period was _writer_. I'd sometimes be introduced as a science writer, science journalist, science communicator, or non-fiction writer. All seemed strange to me. While I wrote [many essays and eventually several books](https://michaelnotebook.com/projects.html), I couldn't see myself as a "writer", certainly not in any of those genres. If I was a writer, it was in some strange unknown genre. I occasionally met highly creative people who confided similar struggles over identity: "I'm not quite sure who or what I am. A writer, maybe? An artist? A philosopher? Some kind of explorer or scientist? I'm not sure how to explain". Their friends would tell me those people were "hard to sum up" or "one of a kind". There was comfort in this: at least I wasn't alone in my struggle for identity.

The question "am I a writer?" continued to bother me. I would sometimes consider playful variants. Was Plato a writer? What about Charles Darwin? Albert Einstein? After all, much of their creative output was words on a page. Didn't that make them writers? Instinctively we know this is silly. Obviously these people are not writers – they are a philosopher, naturalist, and physicist. Einstein was "doing physics", not "writing". In some sense that answered the question. However, I suspected there were hidden depths I wasn't seeing.

In this essay, we'll take these playful questions seriously. We'll examine what many different "writers" are really doing – ranging from Darwin and Einstein to people more conventionally considered writers, like John McPhee, Jane Austen, and Ted Chiang. What is similar? What is different? What activities beyond writing feed their work? By exploring these questions we'll better understand how new creative identities emerge, often from the work of near-amateurs. We'll see that forging such creative identities often requires leaving existing communities, and becoming illegible even to oneself for a time. While this sounds obvious, it took me almost twenty years to really understand, and to construct a strong replacement identity. It's one thing to see the answer from the outside, quite another to find it from the inside.

## "How Buildings Learn"

As a first example, let's examine Stewart Brand's wonderful and very unusual book "How Buildings Learn". You get a sense of the book from the cover, which shows two buildings side by side in New Orleans. The buildings are identical when first built in 1857, but by 1993 both have changed and become very different from one another:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/HBL.jpg)

It's an unusual book. It's not conventional non-fiction, nor journalism, nor a photo essay, though it contains elements of all. Nor is it any other conventional form. It is, rather, a deep exploration of the question implied by the title: how _do_ buildings change over time? It draws on a wide-ranging field report, collecting many examples of how buildings change, often illustrated with striking photos. From these examples, Brand extracts a kind of conceptual armory – terminology for recurring patterns in how buildings change, and simple heuristic models for the most important patterns of change. This armory is not imposed top-down or from some abstract philosophy, but arises from deep consideration of individual examples[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn2). The reader also acquires experience evaluating buildings in these terms. How adaptable is a building? In what ways is a building adaptable? In what ways does it resist change? These questions can be applied to every element of a building's design – from the materials to the layout to the walls to the ceilings to the electrical and other fittings. And you start to understand what makes some buildings change well and others poorly, and what the impact is on human psychology and the built environment.

Let me give just one too-brief example. Brand has a lengthy, loving discussion of MIT's famous Building 20, a "temporary" building built to host radar researchers during World War II, though ultimately used for decades. It was a three-story wooden building, "The only building on campus you can cut \[and thus modify\] with a saw", a building which exposed the electrical and plumbing services throughout, making them easy to re-direct and augment. Brand: "Recabling from office to office, lab to lab, or even wing to wing is largely a matter of do-it-yourself. Rather than a burden, the occupants consider this a benefit."

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/bld20.jpg)

He quotes reports from occupants of the building about what they like about it: "The ability to personalize your space and shape it to various purposes. If you don't like a wall, just stick your elbow through it." (Jonathan Allan) "If you want to bore a hole in the floor to get a little extra vertical space, you do it. You don't ask. It's the best experimental building ever built." (Albert Hill) "One never needs to worry about injuring the architectural or artistic value of the environment." (Morris Halle)

This is a tiny excerpt, but you get the idea. This is the raw material from which Brand extracts his conceptual armory, and you can start to see some of the heuristic models and categories implicit above: what properties make it easy to modify a wall or ceiling? Or the practical observation that people are more willing to modify a building when they're not worried about the artistic value; this is a tension that recurs throughout, with many implications. He develops his conceptual armory through many such examples, often with discussion of edge cases and exceptions, both real and apparent. The result is that you internalize Brand's view of how buildings learn. It permanently changed the way I see buildings[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn3).

"How Buildings Learn" is the work of someone initially approaching the subject as an amateur. I mean this as a high compliment. Brand begins with a fundamental question – how buildings learn – that is not usually taken seriously by architects. And he takes it tremendously seriously, drilling down to arrive at a viewpoint different from that of the traditional community of architects. But if he seems to have begun the project as an amateur, his depth of work means the finished project is certainly not the work of an amateur. But neither had he become an expert in any extant community either. Rather, he was developing a new kind of expertise, one in a potentially productive tension with the community of architects. This tension expresses itself through the book, as Brand points out many ways traditional architectural ideals make buildings inflexible and unresponsive to unanticipated needs. As just one example: many skyscrapers have glass curtain-wall facades, which make them architecturally striking, but also expensive and impractical to modify.

If Brand implicitly and sometimes explicitly criticises architects, how have they responded to Brand? Have they integrated this understanding into how they design? I do not know. I suspect it has only barely begun, in which case the book still presents an opportunity for some brilliant architect to bring these ideas into existing communities.

Is Brand working as a writer in "How Buildings Learn"? There are elements of photo journalism, field reporting, architectural criticism, travel writing, and more. But all are subsidiary, not the primary form. Because his question isn't founded in any existing field, he doesn't have a body of standard existing practice to draw on[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn4). Rather, Brand is a person who cares a tremendous amount about answering a particular question, and to that end deploys every tool he can find that might help. Practices like photo journalism, travel writing and so on are simply part of a journey to find deeper answers. So Brand is not working as a writer in any recognized genre. Rather he is developing a unique written form, in response to the problems he was facing.

This point can also be made by contrasting "How Buildings Learn" with work in established genres. Consider John McPhee, who writes marvellous long form profiles of people like the basketballer Bill Bradley, the art curator Thomas Hoving, and the nuclear weapons designer Ted Taylor. In each case, McPhee shows us a well-chosen slice of the life of that person: how they think and feel, how they see and move through the world. McPhee is not aiming to develop deep answers to fundamental questions like "how do buildings learn?" While his profiles often contain insight on fundamental questions, it's not the primary purpose, and so they're not developed in nearly as much depth as Brand's answer. (McPhee's work may, however, be viewed as part of the grand collective authorial project to answer the fundamental question: "What are the varieties of human being?") While McPhee is one of my favourite writers, my own creative drive seems more like Brand, more toward fundamental questions which have received little prior attention. In such a pursuit, McPhee-style profiles may be useful tools, but are not the primary point. And because such questions are often outside or on the border of existing fields, addressing them often demands the use of novel combinations of tools, giving rise to a unique written form.

## "On the Origin of Species"

Darwin's "On the Origin of Species" is another book that, like "How Buildings Learn", is a deep exploration of a fundamental question: how does the immense variety of the biological world arise? But it also illustrates important new patterns and problems. For instance: for much of the Origin, Darwin writes beautifully, holding and developing the reader's interest. And for much of the Origin he is tedious, long-winded, even just plain boring. Why does a good prose stylist so often slip into tedium?

It's not an accident. Darwin's primary goal is not that of a general non-fiction author, holding the reader's interest with beautiful prose, gripping stories, and a compelling narrative arc. Rather, his goal is to convincingly answer his fundamental question: how does the variety in the biological world arise? Darwin writes beautifully and interestingly when he can, but those goals are kept subsidiary to making progress on his fundamental question.

For example, there is a _lot_ about pigeons in the Origin. It's high quality evidence and argument, but even someone who loved pigeons might find it tedious! More broadly: often Darwin needs evidence for some assertion in the form of a long list of facts. And so long lists of facts we get. Often his best arguments are long and detailed and involve hedging and special cases and identification of weaknesses. And so they go in too.

The tension here is that what is interesting is not always true; what is true is not always interesting. And this applies not only to the truth, but also to convincing evidence and to good explanations. Darwin prioritizes truth, evidence, and explanation, and that strongly shapes his writing.

Like Brand, Darwin seems determined to use any form of engagement with the world which will help him answer his question. Darwin is a remarkable explorer, observer of tiny details, taxonomist, comparative anatomist, correspondent with a far-flung network of naturalists – he must have been an extraordinary letter writer – collector and arranger of tidbits of information from all over the world, synthesist, and theorizer over many layers of abstraction. He is deeply connected to many communities of practice, and willing to use their written forms.

These different forms are often arranged in a kind of "long zoom", a term I've borrowed from the writer Steven Berlin Johnson. Darwin will make long lists of detailed observations, combining his own work with that of his network of naturalists. And then zoom out to explain that list. And then zoom out again to connect to other explanations. And then zoom out again, until eventually we see interrelationships in the biosphere whole. It is dizzying and extraordinary, this seeing and integrating at so many different levels of detail, zooming both out and in, all in service of his overall project.

And so, like "How Buildings Learn", the Origin is a unique book, deploying many different types of writing and ways of engaging the world, anything to help answer its fundamental question. But unlike Brand, Darwin's main contribution is to identify and make the case for a single principle: that of evolution by natural selection. The book is both an argument the principle is correct and a discussion of the consequences for biology. Darwin realizes his principle will remake biology[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn5), and works hard to integrate it into the best biological thinking, in a way Brand does not for architecture. In so doing, the book becomes a prototype for future work in biology and even beyond, in domains such as human and animal psychology. As Darwin realized, he'd discovered a fundamental new type of explanation.

Both Brand and Darwin are writing to discover[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn6). But they're doing it in different ways. Darwin only decided to write the Origin after he'd already understood the principle of evolution by natural selection[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn7). That is, he had not only his question, but also confidence in his answer, building on decades of prior work and creative output. And so Darwin is sharpening and developing an idea he already believes; he also, _en route_, discovers many surprising implications and applications. By contrast, Brand seems to have begun with a question, and discovered the basic shape of his answer through the writing process. Brand is a sailor, setting out on a voyage, determined to tell the highlights of the story, no matter what happens. Darwin is a sailor who has gone on a voyage – I mean figuratively, though it was also literally true – and seen wonders enough to want to tell the story of what he has discovered. Of course, he expects that writing will also be a vehicle to discover still more in the telling. Brand is closer to writing-to-explore; Darwin is closer to writing-with-a-goal, although each has elements of the other.

I'm fascinated by this distinction in possible goals for writing. In my own work I've operated on both sides of the divide. Writing-to-explore is more difficult, since you're not always sure you will discover things worth the telling. You begin with a hunch, and it often only slowly turns into certainty. Whereas Darwin must have had near certainty at the outset – his central result having arisen out of earlier activities, most famously the Voyage of the Beagle, which had their own independent purpose.

I began with the question "Am I a writer?", and playful questions like "Was Darwin a writer?" Of course, we know Darwin wasn't a writer; he was "doing biology", going out and making detailed observations of the world, collecting data, and so on. That's what made him a biologist. There's a big difference between those activities and writing. The trouble is: there isn't really a firm boundary. As the Brand and McPhee examples illustrate, every writer observes the world; many do so quite systematically; many will draw very deeply on the practices of existing specialist communities. There is no firm boundary between the activities of a general writer and those of a specialist like Darwin. In fact, the latter emerge out of the former. It's telling that Darwin doesn't seem to have thought of himself as a biologist. While the term was used in his time, he seems to have thought of himself as a naturalist, emerging out of the great tradition of amateur English naturalists. I won't try to resolve this now. It's more helpful to observe, and hold the tension, and just continue exploring the practices of different creative people. But we'll come back to it.

## "On the electrodynamics of moving bodies"

This was Einstein's first paper on special relativity, published in 1905, showing that time and space are radically different from how they appear: they are mutable and interchangeable in a way shocking to ordinary human intuition. This also makes things like length and simultaneity different than we instinctively assume. These conclusions were partly anticipated in earlier work by Fitzgerald, Lorentz, and Poincare. But Einstein's work gives a more forceful and fundamental explanation. He transformed our notions of space and time.

Is Einstein a writer? It seems a ridiculous notion. He's obviously doing something much more specialized than any conventional type of writing. His paper was published in the journal _Annalen der Physik_, he had just submitted his PhD thesis in physics – he was obviously a physicist.

Still, if you consider Einstein's progenitors, the situation is less clear. Consider the ancient Greek philosopher Empedocles, who in the 5th century BCE developed a theory in which the fundamental constituents of the universe are fire, air, water, and earth. Like Einstein, he helped understand the fundamental constituents of our world, and influenced later thinkers from Aristotle to Boyle, and through them more recent scientists like Einstein[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn8). But Empedocles didn't propose his theory in a scientific paper. In fact, he wrote his major work in hexameter verse, the same meter used by Homer. So too did Lucretius, with his famous work arguing that the cosmos is made of atoms moving through empty space. And so too Parmenides, writing on the nature of time and change and reality. Some of the most important works in the history of science and philosophy were in the form of poetry! Perhaps _Nature_ and _The Physical Review_ should give it a go.

I've sometimes wondered how these early thinkers self-identified. Did Empedocles ever think of himself primarily as a poet, with an unusually strong interest in the physical world? What about Lucretius or Parmenides? It seems distinctly possible!

This seems like a humorous anecdote, but it's deeper. As we develop new means of understanding and explaining the world, we develop new literary forms. But those means of understanding and explaining emerge only slowly, as do the forms. Empedocles wasn't a fool to write as he did. Rather, he was using the medium he knew in order to develop his understanding. But he was also modifying and developing the medium to support new kinds of thought and new representations for that thought. Viewed this way, writing is a mutable, extensible tool that can be used to gradually expand the ways we make sense of the world. It evolves in concert with corresponding activities in the physical world – ways of observing and intervening, ways of making sense. Areas of expertise like physics and biology don't emerge fully formed. Rather they begin with activities and a written record scarcely distinguishable from other everyday activities. It's only over a very long time that a distinct body of knowledge and ways of engaging with the world gradually arise. Writing can thus be used as a medium to develop new ways of understanding.

We can look at our earlier examples through this lens. "How Buildings Learn" is the work of an extremely talented and devoted amateur. It's tempting to compare to Empedocles, starting from scratch. But Brand has many more models of work available to him than Empedocles. An even better comparison is perhaps Galileo, who laid the foundation for entire subjects, starting from modest-but-real foundations. Or, to return to an example from the introduction, to Plato, from a time when philosophy was beginning to differentiate from common thought. Plato was both writer and philosopher – a kind of proto-philosopher. Einstein, by contrast, builds on an immensely deep community of practice, with centuries of development. Indeed, so deep that not only can most other people not write like that, most can't even read like that[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn9). But it wasn't always that way. Einstein is recognizably an intellectual descendant of people like Galileo, and still further back of Empedocles. Darwin's "The Origin of Species" is an intermediate case, emerging out of a network of communities of practice, but those communities did not yet have the depth of physics in 1905.

Brand, Darwin, and Einstein thus illustrate different stages in how written forms, types of understanding, and expert community develop together – from amateur exploration to deep community of practice. But all begin as part of the common conversation of humanity, and only gradually specialize over time.

I noted earlier Darwin's realization that almost every question in biology needs to be rethought in the light of natural selection. Similarly, Einstein's changed understanding of space and time remakes something both universal and fundamental in our understanding of the cosmos. So much so that it seems plausible that aliens billions of light years away[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn10) will also experience space and time as fundamental; and perhaps go through a similar arc of discovering the Descartes-Galileo-Newton mathematical description of space and time, and then improve upon it by discovering special and general relativity[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn11).

Einstein's paper has a set of takeaways, precise mathematical relationships that can be reused exactly in other contexts – the Lorentz transformations[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn12), for instance, are a set of mathematical equations describing how space and time measurements are related in different reference frames. Or, from a later paper by Einstein on relativity, perhaps the most famous equation of all: E = mc^2. These work as a kind of interface or API to these papers: they express exact conclusions, which you can reuse elsewhere, even without reference to the paper's argument for why they're correct. And because space and time are so fundamental, these results _are_ reused everywhere, from engineering to chemistry to navigation to science fiction. In short, this understanding propagates out to affect our entire understanding of the world. It is a truly incredible fact that these expressions – squiggles on paper! – can express so precisely such a powerful understanding of the world. Darwin's book also offers such a principle, albeit somewhat less precise, but so forcefully articulated and applied in multiple contexts by Darwin that it too can easily be reused elsewhere. Brand's conceptual armory is somewhat less precise still, but still useful enough that it too can be reused. All three are triumphs of human understanding of the world, and of our ability to express and apply that understanding in multiple contexts[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn13).

## Stories

I began this essay by asking if people like Einstein and Darwin could be considered writers. Let's invert that question, and contrast with the people most conventionally considered writers – the authors of fictional stories like "Hamlet" or "Pride and Prejudice" or "Stoner". We don't strictly need this to answer the question of the essay. But it will help us better understand what writing is capable of, what the space of creative identities can be. At first glance, such stories seem to do something quite different from our earlier examples: they evoke alternate worlds, and other ways of experiencing the world[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn14). The philosophers tell us we cannot know what it is like to be a bat; undeterred, ambitious novelists may aspire to let us experience what it is like to be Hamlet, Miss Elizabeth Bennet, William Stoner, or even stranger others.

Sometimes, a writer takes on an especially great challenge. Nabokov takes us inside the mind of a pedophile, Humbert Humbert, in "Lolita". While Humbert Humbert is gross and repellent, Nabokov makes the story so compelling we cannot look away. We are inside the monster's mind, experiencing in first person, closer even than in "Hamlet", "Pride and Prejudice", or "Stoner". Even more impressive: Nabokov does not (for the most part) make it compelling through "true crime" sensationalism. Zadie Smith's comment is apt: "Lolita seemed like the page was on fire… I didn't know a book could do that."

Our non-story examples, Brand, Darwin, and Einstein, are not primarily about giving us such an experience of being. Rather, they improve our descriptive understanding of the world. Of course, stories like "Hamlet" _et al_ also improve our descriptive understanding – fiction is sometimes better psychology than the psychologists, and better political science than the political scientists – but it's not the principal point. There is no API for Hamlet. The principal point is the experience itself[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn15).

We've been discussing story-as-experience. But let me describe two striking variations on this goal[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn16). One is illustrated by one of my favourite short stories, Ted Chiang's astonishing "Story of Your Life" (spoilers ahead!). It is ostensibly the story of a woman who: falls in love and then divorces; has a daughter as fruit of that union; whose daughter later dies; and – the most obviously science fictional element – who helps humanity learn to communicate with a race of aliens.

The conceit of the story is that the aliens experience time very differently than humans. They experience all times simultaneously, so from a human point of view they seem to be experiencing not only the past and present, but also the future as a kind of inevitable unfolding, something that has already happened. Furthermore, Chiang sketches a way this experience of time can be passed from the aliens to the human beings interacting with them. Our narrator gradually internalizes this altered experience of the world. It's fiction and you need to suspend disbelief, but it's compelling enough to work as story.

As I read "Story of Your Life" for the first time I found the voice of the narrator strange. She seemed detached, almost emotionless. Not entirely unfeeling – she could be wry, or ironic, or humorous. But there was a distance to her. When terrible events occurred – like the death of her daughter – that distance became acute, a seeming inhumanity. I couldn't make sense of it. Then: a radical perspective shift, an epiphany, as all the dissonant moments suddenly fell into place: this is what it was _like_ for her to experience this state of temporal consciousness. Everything that had hitherto read as strange now made sense, her dispassion, the wry irony, everything. Strangest of all, I felt some tiny hint of this unfamiliar state of consciousness in myself. It was one of the most wildly vertiginous and disorienting experiences of my life. I have reread the story many times since, and each time feel that same sense, though never as strong nor as sudden as the first time, that sense of starting to slip into a way of seeing my life entire, of understanding that will and the power to choose is not distinct from fate and predetermination.

Great stories – indeed, great writing of any type – create a powerful new context for us to think in[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn17). But sometimes, very rarely, they may create new states of consciousness, new states which alter who we are, however momentarily and incompletely. Obviously, "Story of Your Life" didn't give me the ability to see the future. But it was an extremely vivid experience and reminder that there are profoundly different ways of being for humanity, and perhaps even more profound for the alien other[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn18).

Another striking example is the Christian Bible. I've puzzled a lot about what it's doing. It's a collection created by multiple authors across centuries, so one might expect less clarity of purpose. The best brief answer I can find: the Bible is providing an identity for a people[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn19). It is saying: here is how you came to be. Here is what you have done through your history, your highs and your lows, your potential for the future, what you are capable of. And here are demanding standards for what it means to be truly successful: individually, as a family, as a community, as a culture. It is providing a model for how to be in the cosmos.

It is surprising how effective the Bible is as writing. Its authors wrote long before Nabokov or Williams or Austen or Shakespeare, and without the benefit of the literary invention available to the later authors. Yet many of the stories have great power. The story of the Crucifixion, in particular, is one of the most powerful stories ever told: the Son of God came into the world and loved you – yes you – so much that he accepted an excruciating and unjust death, in order to save you from the consequences of your sins. That's a story so extraordinary as to be transformative of individual and cultural identity! That force has been amplified by being retold so often. Creators from Leonardo to Andrew Lloyd Webber to Dante to Martin Luther King, Jr. to Monty Python have all written riffs on Biblical stories. The Bible thus has the best and most varied fan fiction of all time; indeed, much of modern culture is extrusion from the Bible. If Chiang can briefly transform the consciousness of a reader, the Bible has durably transformed the consciousness of our culture.

We've been discussing story forms. There are also many striking adjacent forms. One of my favorite forms to write in is what I call [discovery fiction](https://michaelnotebook.com/df/index.html). Some examples include this [Twitter thread](https://x.com/michael_nielsen/status/1132063254849527808) explaining how a person with a basic knowledge of quantum mechanics could come to invent quantum teleportation, and [this essay](https://michaelnielsen.org/ddi/how-the-bitcoin-protocol-actually-works/) explaining how a person could come to invent the Bitcoin protocol. (More examples [here](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/df.html)). The general idea is that discovery fiction provides a plausible story of how the reader could have discovered a result such as the Bitcoin protocol. Typically, there's an arc of small questions and ideas, false starts and backtracking, incremental steps eventually leading to the result. Ideally, none of these things should come out of thin air: they should all be simple, almost-obvious steps, the kind of thing where the reader can think "oh, yeah, I might well have tried that, maybe not as my first choice, but after a little thought".

The experience for the reader is of gradually coming to understand the result from the inside. At first such grand results often seem daunting and near incomprehensible. And then, bit by bit, insight dawns. You face creative choices and their consequences; you chase down false starts and discover why better choices work. This creates a consciousness of what it is like to invent something like teleportation or Bitcoin. You don't merely understand the result, you experience and understand _why_ it is that way. Discovery fiction does not need to be historically accurate – indeed, history is often much more random and confusing. It's instead a kind of fictional non-fiction, aiming like a story to provide an experience, but an experience of discovery.

I have many aspirational ideas for discovery fiction. Among others, I've got notes toward "How to discover that space and time are not as they appear" (on discovering relativity), "How to discover a law of nature", "How to discover a mathematical theorem", and even "How to discover science". The aspiration of a piece like "How to discover that space and time are not as they appear" is for the reader not merely to know what Einstein knew. But rather to start to see the world as Einstein saw it, to in some small measure transform their consciousness.

Another powerful story-adjacent form is used by Robert Caro in his acclaimed biography of US President Lyndon Johnson. As [Tanner Greer has pointed out](https://x.com/Scholars_Stage/status/1817915094829068537), Caro's book incorporates powerful techniques of fictional storytelling in a manner unusual in history and biography. Those genres usually adhere to the convention that the writer cannot report what a subject was thinking or feeling, unless the subject has explicitly said it – perhaps in an interview or memoir or diary, or to some other reliable source[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn20). By contrast, a great advantage of fictional stories is that you can describe the experience of the characters in ways non-fiction currently[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn21) cannot – what they're thinking, what they're feeling, even evoke parts of their being they may themselves be unconscious of. These are powerful advantages for fiction[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn22).

Caro uses a clever technique to combine the benefits of fiction and non-fiction. In telling Johnson's story, Caro amasses a great deal of detailed factual information. But he occasionally breaks his factual narrative to speculate on what Johnson was thinking or feeling. It is clearly marked as speculation, but the factual portrait is so compelling, and Caro's understanding of Johnson so acute, that you find yourself believing Caro's speculation: you are taken inside Johnson's experience. And so you get the force and veracity and surprise of truth, but also the transformative experience of fiction.

Like Brand and Darwin, Caro illustrates the power in combining multiple forms of engagement with the world. And techniques like discovery fiction and Caro's speculative interiority show how the techniques of fiction can be useful for purposes beyond conventional fiction.

What is story? It's not so obvious. We all recognize stories. But I've found it hard to explain to myself what a story is, much less a good story. I enjoy Douglas Adams' quip that a story is "80,000 words in a cunning order". It's both a good joke and a striking insight. But I suspect Adams said it partly to dodge the question of what a story is. But an idea I enjoy playing with is that a story is a premise that makes you strongly desire to know what happens next, and which continues until that feeling is satisfied[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn23).

Good story and good explanation superficially have much in common. But they are very different. Something can be a good story and a bad explanation; or a good explanation and a bad story. We saw this in Darwin, with his willingness to sacrifice good story for a deeper understanding of the world. Keeping clear on the distinction can prevent much confusion (and, occasionally, misery). It's been an ongoing challenge of the social media age, where good-but-false stories often propagate more easily than good explanations which make bad stories.

Story's origin seems almost certainly to lie deep within our evolutionary past. It's strange, because chimps and other animals do not, as far as we know, tell stories. But it's hard to not believe the roots lie deep. Perhaps that's why we so often confuse story with reality! I love Muriel Rukeyser's line: "The universe is made up of stories, not atoms". This is true, in the sense of human experience: our minds are narrativized. But there is also an unself out there, a cosmos beyond our selves, which can't be narrativized through a single line to be held by human consciousness. The cosmos is beyond narrative. But a great story can be a way of selecting through real or imagined worlds, to evoke something of the broader cosmos, a bridging of self and unself.

## What feeds the writing?

In his memoir "On Writing" the writer Stephen King tells of how he sets himself a goal of writing 2,000 words each day, for whatever novel he has in progress. As he points out, at that pace, even with multiple drafts, you can write a sizeable book each year.

This works for King. He lives in a quiet part of Maine. He rarely travels. He has previously internalized a tremendous number of story patterns and ideas. And he continues to feed his story demon, writing in the morning, and complementing it by reading in the afternoon. His prior understanding and his reading combine to generate enough ideas to feed a 2,000 word per day goal.

The caveat is that this approach is tailored to the particular types of story King likes to tell.

I mention this because King's 2,000 word-per-day goal has exerted a strong fascination over me. King presents his argument well and with conviction. And I've sometimes felt that I'm failing because I can't consistently do the same, even for a more modest goal (say, 600 words per day). This is _obviously_ ridiculous for the kind of creative work I do. But it's also an example of the problems confusion about identity can cause. If you are wondering "Am I a writer?" and see a compelling argument you should be able to do something, and you can't, you wonder: am I just doing this wrong? Of course, King and I have radically different creative goals. But for a long time I struggled to articulate those sufficiently compellingly to escape the grip of why-not-2,000-words-per-day[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn24). In this section, I remedy that.

This is Stewart Brand, inside the shipping container in which he wrote "How Buildings Learn":

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/assets/shipping.jpg)

He is thinking inside the box. This is a man taking his creative work _seriously_. Everything about this space conveys the message: this project matters. It is a space for him to think with, to help him organize his thinking, and to connect him emotionally to his project. It is adaptable, and able to evolve in response to what he is doing; but at the same time also organized, likely reflecting and supporting the organization of the book. I've often talked this image over with other creative researchers, many of whom find it inspiring.

If Brand brought great creative force to his book, Darwin's commitment was even more extraordinary. "The Origin of Species" was the summit of a remarkable life, lived with tremendous force that – in retrospect! – was preparation for writing the Origin. Darwin _began_ that with the 5-year Voyage of the Beagle, travelling around the entire world. He endured disease, seasickness, one of the largest earthquakes ever recorded, a near-shipwreck, and many other disasters. But through it all he was a consummate naturalist, exploring and observing and sending detailed accounts back to England. Darwin understood, with a conviction almost no-one else in history could match, the tremendous diversity in the biological world, the "endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful" that surround us. After returning from his voyage, he spent years studying, in correspondence with naturalists all over the world, and completing several other books which are minor classics. He was in deep emotional and intellectual and practical communion with the natural world. You get a similar sense from Einstein's letters[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn25), another deep communion with the order underlying the cosmos. At times, reading him brings tears to my eyes.

Writers sometimes talk about their "research process". The term is useful, but far too limited. Ben Franklin's "research process" for his renowned autobiography was to live his incredible life. For his book "Seven Pillars of Wisdom", T. E. Lawrence helped enable the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire, during World War I. That's quite a research process. Einstein not only brought much of his life to his paper on special relativity, he also brought centuries of the best human thought and experiment. Similar remarks may be made about Brand and Darwin. A person with a sufficiently rich life will always have material for an incredible book, whether they choose to write or not[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn26). A better question for any creative person is: what activities do you want to pursue? What kinds of understanding will that create? How far can you go, exploring more deeply, more forcefully, more imaginatively? And what communities offer powerful tools that you can use to deepen your understanding?

There is a sad counterpart to all this. Many people who self-identify as writers bemoan their day jobs. If only they could quit those pesky jobs! That would speed them up and let them flourish as writers! Finally after screwing up their courage they quit their job. Surprisingly often, this causes their writing to dry up. They didn't understand that the everyday activities "blocking their writing" were crucial to generating material. Writing doesn't come from nothing. For anyone who writes – indeed, anyone who creates in any way – it comes out of some other source. You need activities that generate the insight your writing is founded in. Sometimes that's other books, as it apparently is for Stephen King. Sometimes it's a penetrating investigation of the world, as in the Brand or Darwin examples. Sometimes it's to help found a country, as in Ben Franklin's. And sometimes they draw on immensely deep and established communities of expert practice, as in Einstein's case. Again, the question is: what activities will generate the types of understanding you seek? And how can you deepen those activities as you write, a loop in which your writing practice co-evolves with how you enter the world, to generate more and deeper insight?

In "On Writing", King criticises Harper Lee for writing only one book[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn27), "To Kill a Mockingbird". King expresses admiration for the book, but also asks: what was she spending all her time doing? Why didn't she write more? The answer, of course, is that the way Lee generated insight was very different from King's. It was much closer to Ben Franklin or T. E. Lawrence's: she spent decades living in (and closely observing) Jim Crow Alabama. And while King is a fine writer, he hasn't written anything to match "To Kill a Mockingbird"[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn28). Lee's process might be slow. But it generated a remarkable understanding of the world, and an equally remarkable book.

Let me return to my opening question: "Am I a writer?" The answer is far more subtle than I initially apprehended. Brand, Darwin, and Einstein are all in some measure writers – they were all using words as a medium for understanding[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn29). For practical purposes, a useful question is: what's your primary intended creative output? If your emotional investment is mostly in writing a book, then you may reasonably classify yourself as a writer. In that sense, I believe Brand was working as a writer when he wrote "How Buildings Learn". But Darwin's main investment was in exploring the natural world, and participating in related communities. Darwin was an explorer and researcher who also wrote. I empathize a lot with that. I also empathize with Richard Feynman when he says: "I'm an explorer, okay? I get curious about everything, and I want to investigate all kinds of stuff." Being a writer alone is, for me, too isolating. Exploring and writing are not in tension; rather they support and reinforce each other.

In this point of view, the confusion over "Am I a writer?" dissolves into unimportance. It's like asking: "Am I a speaker?" Well, yes, in the sense that I use language to develop my understanding. The confusion only arises because writing is both an identity and also a medium for understanding, a medium which can be used to support other very different identities. For people pursuing unusual creative work without an associated expert identity, "writer" is thus tempting as a fallback identity. While natural, this can also be misleading, even damaging, if it suggests the wrong models for working. In this view, it's an accident that this essay is about writing at all. I'm not a writer. I'm an explorer and researcher who also writes. More precisely: I'm someone who gets interested in under-explored fundamental questions, and then pursues them with whatever tools seem most powerful, writing among them. That's still not an easily legible identity to other people; it's not going on a bumper sticker[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn30). But it captures much of my creative drive.

I feel foolish for not forcefully articulating this to myself much earlier. But it takes time to become legible to oneself when outside well-defined communities[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn31). Now I can confidently answer the personal identity question I posed at the beginning. I am in some measure a scientist, a physicist, an interface designer, a writer, and many more identities. But all are subsidiary and in service to my primary identity as a creative explorer and researcher. This includes the scientist in me – but by scientist I mean in a sense closer to Darwin or Brand, rather than a modern specialist in some professionalized subfield. For me a prototypical model like Brand or Darwin is much better than someone like Stephen King. Writing is merely one vehicle to express and develop my understanding. But it's my servant, not a master or primary identity. This all sounds like an easy conclusion, but for me it was hard won through experience. And many other creative workers travel a similarly twisting path. A better question is: what kind of writer can I be in service of my primary identity?

## Coda: what kind of writer am I?

Having freed myself from the tyranny of "writer" as a primary identity, it's fun to ask: what kind of writing do I like to do, in service of creative exploration and research? For myself, I love writing to discover – writing and exploration of the world in dialog with each other. I love writing as a place to think and understand, as a powerful context not only for the reader to think in, but as an even more powerful context for the writer to think in. It's writing as a place for slow courage, for chutzpah and imagination. I used to aspire to write quickly. But if your writing is providing a powerful context to think in, then you should want that process to continue for as long as it's rapidly generating additional insight.

In writing to discover, there is a question of how much you hide the discovery in the eventual text. Background notes and first drafts are usually about the process of discovery. But in later drafts you can, if you choose, convert raw exploration into what seems like revealed insight, hiding the signposts to construction. All writers hide thought to some extent[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn32), both as a practical matter – no-one can capture all their thinking – and as a courtesy to the reader, eliding their least interesting thinking. But how much is hidden is a matter of choice and will. Doing that hiding is often a lot of extra work. In this essay I've done a little elision, but I've also left many signposts to construction in, so the essay is itself an experience of discovery, for you the reader and for me the author.

I love writing to drill down on fundamental questions in nascent or yet-to-exist fields. This pattern underlies my books or book-length essays on [open science](https://www.amazon.com/Reinventing-Discovery-New-Networked-Science/dp/0691160198), [metascience](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html), [tools for thought](https://numinous.productions/ttft/), [the mnemonic medium](https://quantum.country/), and others. But I am wary of this too. It can easily turn to dilettantism. I often meet people who tell me they'd like to do what I do, exploring a wide range of fundamental questions. But to do so you need to find insights which are genuinely new and useful not merely to you, but (ideally) to humanity. That's a demanding standard. And while I say "drill down on fundamental questions" often there is no well-formed starting question, merely a hunch there is _something_ worth exploring. So you must endure creative fog and confusion and ignorance, to find questions worth drilling down on, all without knowing whether you will eventually find insights worth the effort!

I love writing as a way of making a unique contribution. I talked once to a well-known scientist working in the ultra-competitive world of CRISPR. He remarked that (roughly) "of course, much of the reason we scientists do the work is the thrill of the race", remarking fondly on instances where he had "beaten" another lab or labs to a result by a day or a few days, or lost the "race" by an equally thin margin. I was astonished. I'd far rather work on something where I will be making a unique contribution, not something easily substitutable.

In all the above, I see many similar patterns in Brand and Darwin, and in many other creative workers I admire. I do not know if that's how they see it. But it's how I see it, from the outside. Perhaps more unusually, I love to write discovery fiction, to give myself and others some experience of what it is like to discover a set of ideas from the inside. I love that blurring of experience and description, the way we can come to appreciate the discovery, a changed state of consciousness. More generally: I believe story is underrated outside fiction. I love finding new ways to bridge experience and description, giving people both an experience of the other, and also powerful descriptions of the other. The fusion is far more powerful than either alone. And I also love to write about possibility, to attempt to evoke in the reader (and myself) a sense of new worlds to explore.

Something I do not do: I do not write beautifully. Poets find meaning in this aspiration, using texture and resonance to evoke more than it seems ought to be possible. And many other writers do too, especially writers of literary fiction. Many believe with Flaubert that a "good sentence in prose should be like a good line in poetry, unchangeable, as rhythmic, as sonorous". I know it improves the experience, but my first fidelity is rather to understanding plainly expressed. Writing true sentences is hard enough. With that said, as my friend Fawaz Al Matrouk has emphasized to me, there often _is_ enormous beauty in sharply distilled understanding of the world. That is an aspiration toward beauty that I feel deeply.

Another thing I have only a little interest in: writing to educate or to reach a wide audience. I'm happy and often grateful when people find what I write useful in these ways. But it's not a primary emotional drive. When I lean on these aspirations I find myself exhausted and uninterested. Even my textbook (with Ike Chuang) on quantum computing, which many think of as educational, was mostly written to explain the field to myself and, when possible, to push a little creatively beyond. I'm delighted this work has been helpful to others. But it was only a small part of the motivation, not primary.

Writing is a miraculous technology to support exploration and discovery, enabling a kind of gradient ascent in human understanding. You make tiny improvements, over and over. It may be a word or phrase you throw out and replace with a better; it may be an entire book. Robert Louis Stevenson is said to have thrown the first draft of "Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde" into the fire. You write down your confusions, your discomforts, your exceptions, your crazy ideas. And slowly, you agglomerate improvement. You can keep improving for years. I do not believe you can think well without using an external medium[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fn33), something to help you improve thoughts too complex for your unaided mind. This can be a trudge, all those often tiny improvements in understanding. But it adds up and at peak moments is transformative. As the _New Yorker_ editor William Shawn said to John McPhee: "It takes as long as it takes." And for those of us developing new creative identities, writing is one of the most powerful servants we have.

A spectacular example of the power of reusing such squiggles on a page – albeit, an example too long for the main text – is the Los Alamos Primer, a short document created at the beginning of the Manhattan Project, and distributed to new arrivals to the project. It collects up many facts about neutrons and fission, and a sequence of simple qualitative and quantitative models of how the physics of fission works. By relating these models to one another, it makes a strong case that an atomic bomb is plausible, would be highly destructive, and identifies much of what needs to be done to build it, although some crucial problems were yet to be solved. Relativity plays only a small (and largely implicit) role, though popular accounts tend to emphasize the connection between E = mc^2 and the bomb. Still, the document is in large part a compilation of results from prior papers, explaining how they interlock together. The Primer and other associated technical documents are astounding objects. To a surprising extent humanity _wrote_ its way to the atomic bomb. Of course, this built on earlier experiments – the discovery of the neutron and of fission, for instance – but it was still a large theoretical leap to a bomb. The US government bet billions of dollars on words and symbols on paper. We take it for granted that it worked, but it's remarkable, and was not obvious at the time. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, Admiral William Leahy, told President Truman that the atomic bomb project was "the biggest fool thing we have ever done. The bomb will never go off, and I speak as an expert in explosives." And yet we all know how it turns out: the bomb destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and transformed the world.[↩︎](https://michaelnotebook.com/dci/index.html#fnref13)
