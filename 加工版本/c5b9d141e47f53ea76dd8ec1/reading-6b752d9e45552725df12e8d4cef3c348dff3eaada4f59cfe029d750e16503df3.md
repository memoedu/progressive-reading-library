## 正文

communication styles 的根本 divide，是 _synchronous_ 与 _asynchronous_ communication 之间的 divide。Synchronous communication 是指你说了些什么之后，就不做其他事情，直到对方回应你说的话，就像打电话时那样。Asynchronous communication 是指你发出一条 message 后，就把它放在一边，去做其他事情，直到收到 response；这时，你再 process 这个 response，并在未来某个不确定的时间 reply。大多数人就是这样使用 email 的。

问题在于，很少有 emails 是 _真正_ asynchronous 的，也就是说，sender 并非 _完全_ 不在意 response 的 timing。大多数时候，他们都有某种 timeframe，希望在那之前收到 response。因此，把所有 asynchronous messages 都放在同一个地方是行不通的：你需要一个地方放 fast-turnaround asynchronous messages，另一个地方放 slow 的那些。（通常在实际使用中，“fast” media 会在有 message 时 interrupt 你，而“slow” media 不会。）

以下是我分别用来处理这些情况的工具：

**Slow 且 asynchronous：**Email 和 Facebook messages（不过，我更希望完全不用后者）。

**Fast 且 asynchronous：**Text 和 Google Hangouts messages。

**Synchronous（同步）：**Hangouts、phone 和 Skype。

你的选择可能略有不同。问题在于，我默认以为，每个人对这些工具的 use patterns 都与我大致相同。例如，以前我经常使用 instant messaging 时，我是以 synchronously 的方式使用它的：我会对某个人说些什么，等对方 reply，然后立即开始 responding。但我的一些朋友把它当作 fast asynchronous tool 来用，因此，他们有时会隔很久才 replied 我说的话，因为他们正在 multitasking。我会感到沮丧，因为我以为他们很没礼貌，但其实，他们只是在按照一种与我不同的 norm 使用 IM。

同样，有时人们会期待 email 或 Facebook messages 对我来说是一种 fast-async medium。我不希望别人能通过 email 或 Facebook 迅速联系到我，因为那样一来，我就得把 phone 设置为每当收到新的 email 或 Facebook message 都提醒我，而这会非常令人分心。但我相信，对于那些习惯更频繁地查看 email 的人来说，我每天只在晚上 respond 一次，会让他们感到沮丧。（事实上，有时我太担心错过紧急的 email，结果还是忍不住一天查看不止一次。这通常不是个好主意。）

还有一些较小的 mismatches，也可能带来麻烦。我已经把 phone 设置为在我睡觉时自动 silence，因此，我有时会在深夜给别人打电话或发 text，结果把他们吵醒。我很少知道，在再次询问之前，我应该等多久才能收到 email 或 text response。当别人留下 voicemail 时，我通常会阅读 Google Voice transcript，因此，有时我会漏掉一些细节，让他们感到恼火。

如果你不 document 自己如何 communicate，那么，当你的 usage patterns 与别人的不 match 时，你就会让他们感到沮丧。人们将不得不尝试一堆不同的 media，并努力记住你如何使用其中每一种，才能猜出你是怎么做的。与其让大家经历这些，不如在某个地方写下来，再把它的 link 放在例如你的 email signature 中，这样可能更省事。

我打算在[我的联系页面](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/)上 document 自己的做法，不过，我也正在进一步 optimizing 这些做法。我很想听听其他人如何处理这个问题。你如何确保自己能够顺畅地 handle 所有 incoming messages，同时又不被它们不断分心？

## 原文

The fundamental divide in communication styles is the divide between _synchronous_ and _asynchronous_ communication. Synchronous communication is when you say something, and then you don’t do anything else until the other person responds to what you say, like in a phone call. Asynchronous communication is when you fire off a message and then you forget about it and go do other things until you receive a response, at which point you process the response and reply at some indeterminate point in the future. That’s how most people use email.

The problem is that very few emails are _truly_ asynchronous in the sense that the sender doesn’t care _at all_ about the timing of the response. Most of the time they have some sort of timeframe by which they’d like a response. So it doesn’t work to put all your asynchronous messages in the same place: you need one place for fast-turnaround asynchronous messages, and one place for slow ones. (Usually in practice “fast” media interrupt you when you have a message and “slow” media don’t.)

Here’s what I use for each of those things:

**Slow and asynchronous:** Email and Facebook messages (although I’d prefer not to use the latter at all).

**Fast and asynchronous:** Text and Google Hangouts messages.

**Synchronous:** Hangouts, phone, and Skype.

Yours are probably a bit different. The problem is that by default I assume that everyone has roughly the same use patterns for these that I do. For instance, back when I used instant messaging a lot, I used it synchronously: I would say something so someone, wait for them to reply, and then immediately start responding. But some of my friends were using it as a fast asynchronous tool, so they would sometimes leave long gaps before they replied to what I said because they were multitasking. I would get frustrated because I thought they were being rude, but they were just using IM with a different norm than I was.

Similarly, sometimes people expect email or Facebook messages to be a fast-async medium for me. I don’t want to be quickly reachable by email or Facebook, because then I’d have to set my phone to tell me whenever I get a new email or Facebook message, and that would be incredibly distracting. But I’m sure for people who have a norm of being on their email more frequently, it’s frustrating that I only respond once a day in the evening. (In fact, sometimes I get worried enough about missing an urgent email that I break down and check more than once a day. This is usually a bad idea.)

And there are other smaller mismatches that can be bad too. I’ve set my phone up to silence itself automatically when I sleep, so sometimes I call or text people late at night and it wakes them up. I rarely know how long I should wait for an email or text response before checking in. When people leave a voicemail, I usually read the Google Voice transcript, so sometimes I miss details and they get annoyed.

If you don’t document how you communicate, you’re going to frustrate people when your usage patterns don’t match theirs. People will have to guess how you operate by trying a bunch of different media and trying to remember how you used each one. Rather than making people go through this, it might be easier to write it up somewhere and link to it in e.g. your email signature.

I’m planning to document mine on [my contact page](https://www.benkuhn.net/contact/), but I’m also in the process of optimizing them further. I’d be interested to hear how other people approach this problem. How do you make sure that you handle all your incoming messages smoothly without being constantly distracted by them?
