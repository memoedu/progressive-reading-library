# Mini Blog Post 1: The value of experiments — Neel Nanda

---

## 正文

一种对改善我的生活方式极其有用的办法，是对自己 run experiments。这里说的“experiment”，是指明确改变自己生活方式中的某个方面，按这个计划坚持一段时间，然后明确 track 过程如何，以及我从中学到了什么。比如，连续两周每天写一篇简短的博客文章，看看不那么像个 perfectionist 是什么感觉！或者，为了看看 coworking 能否让我更 productive，注册一个 focusmate.com 账号，试上几次。又或者，在接下来的一个月里，确保自己每个星期天早上都去健身房，并记录星期天下午的感受，看看 exercise 能否 improve 我的 mental health。

这里的 underlying model 是，我可以过的生活有_很多_种。我目前遵循着某一种特定的路径、方法和日常习惯。而且我觉得现在这样相当不错！但它显然还远非 optimal——我之所以能看出来，是因为我的生活现在有很多方面都比一年前更好了，而且这是一个 high-dimensional space，_如此_复杂，我根本不可能已经找到了 perfect point。Naively，我可能会试着仅靠认真思考哪些地方可以变得更好来改善现状，我也觉得这可以是个很棒的方法！但这个世界上经常有让我意外的事，事情也经常不像我预期的那样。所以，真正_动手_做点什么、尝试新事物，以及 gathering data，是发现我所遗漏之事的一种非常有力的办法。

而 experiments 尤其有意思，因为 upside risk 是 _high_ 的，而 downside risk（只要你做事合理）是 low 的。因为你真正获得的是 information。如果我尝试 10 家没去过的餐厅，其中 9 家都让我讨厌，那就是吃了 9 顿难吃的饭。但既然我已经知道剩下那家餐厅非常棒，就可以再去吃 100 次！这就是 _exploring_ 而非 exploiting 的基本思路。而且我觉得，尤其是在你年轻的时候（我猜读到这里的绝大多数人都是如此），exploring 的 value _massively_ outweighs 做某件 sub-optimal 的事所带来的 risk。

说实话，上面这些听起来都挺显而易见的——experiments 和尝试新事物是有用的，我大概本来就可以直接从这个 assertion 说起。只要做一个粗略的 Fermi Estimate 就能看出，一个例如能让我提高 1% productive 程度或多 1% 快乐的 experiment，就是对时间_好得不可思议_的利用。

然而，我其实并不怎么 run experiments……这正是我目前正在努力改变、而且已经有所进展的事！（这件事本身也是个很有趣的 run experiments 的对象 ;) ）不过，我在这方面还有很大的改善空间

我的内心感受大致是这样：并不是我明确考虑了 experiments，然后决定不去 run 它们。而是 running 一个 experiment 并不让我觉得是在做 _default action_。它从来不会让我觉得紧迫，也从来不像是我的 highest priority，即便这个念头确实冒进了我的脑海。我想，这是因为 experiments 的 benefits 全都在于 getting information，以及未来的 long-run benefits。而我的 intuitions 实在太过 focused 于 short-term、novelty，以及那些让人觉得兴奋的事。它们忽略了这样一个事实：这是在 long-term 改善我的生活的一种非常有力的办法。

我找到的主要应对办法是：把 experiments 看成_令人兴奋_、_新鲜_的事。再建立一些 systems，让自己定期想一想这件事。我目前每周留出 2 hours，快速浏览生活中的问题清单，并且有一套用于 designing 新 experiments 的 algorithm，这显著 increased 了我 run 的数量！Social accountability，也就是和朋友及教练聊一聊，对此也非常有帮助。它让我更容易坚持下来，也让我觉得这更接近一种 default action。

## 原文

An incredibly useful way to improve the way that I’m living my life is to run experiments on myself. Where by “experiment”, I mean to explicitly change something about how I live my life, follow this plan for some period of time, and then explicitly track how it went, and what I learned from this. For example, writing a mini blog post every day for two weeks, to see what it’s like to be less of a perfectionist! Or, to see if coworking could make me more productive, sign up for a focusmate.com account and try a few sessions. Or see if exercise can improve my mental health, by ensuring I go to the gym every Sunday morning for the next month, and recording how I feel on Sunday afternoon.

The underlying model here, is that there are a _lot_ of ways I can be living my life. I am currently following a particular path, approach and routine. And I think this is a pretty good one! But it’s clearly far from optimal - I can tell because there are a bunch of ways my life is better now than it was a year ago, and it’s _such_ a high-dimensional space that there’s no way I’ve actually found the perfect point. Naively, I might try to improve things by just thinking hard about what could be better, and I think this can be a great approach! But there are often things that surprise me about the world, and things are often not as I expect them. So actually _doing_ something, trying new things, and gathering data, is a really powerful way of finding the things that I’m missing.

And experiments are particularly interesting, because the upside risk is _high_ and the downside risk (if you’re sensible about things) is low. Because what you’re really getting is information. If I try 10 new restaurants, and hate 9 of them, that’s 9 bad meals. But I can go to the one awesome restaurant 100 times, now that I know it’s great! This is the general idea of _exploring_, rather than exploiting. And I think especially when you’re young (as I assume most everyone reading this is), the value of exploring _massively_ outweighs the risk of doing something sub-optimal.

Honestly, the above all feels pretty obvious - experiments and trying new things are useful, and I could probably have just jumped in with that assertion. Just doing a rough Fermi Estimate suggests that an experiment that eg makes me 1% more productive or 1% happier is an _insanely_ good use of time.

And yet, I don’t really run experiments… And this is something I’m currently trying to change, and making progress on! (and a very fun things to run experiments with ;) ) But something where I have a lot of room to improve

The internal experience is approximately: it’s not that I explicitly consider experiments, and decide not to run them. It’s that running an experiment doesn’t feel like the _default action_. It never feels urgent, it never feels like my highest priority, even if the idea does pop into my head. I think it’s that the benefits of experiments are all about getting information, and future long-run benefits. And my intuitions are far too focused on the short-term, on novelty, and the things that feel exciting. And they neglect the fact that this is a really powerful way to improve my life in the long-term.

The main way I’ve found of dealing with this: Framing experiments as _exciting_, as something _novel_. And setting up systems so I think about this regularly. I currently have 2 hours a week to skim through the list of problems in my life, and an algorithm for designing new experiments, and this has notably increased the amount that I run! Social accountability, talking to friends and coaches, is also really helpful for this. It makes it stick better, and feel closer to a default action.
