# 开发者薪酬呈双峰分布吗？｜Is dev compensation bimodal?

---

## 正文

自 [Google et al. wage-suppressing no-hire agreement](https://danluu.com/google-wage-fixing/) 终结以来，Developer compensation 已经 skyrocketed，以至于 compensation 能够 rivals，甚至可能 exceeds，law、consulting 等向来 remunerative 的 fields 的 compensation。在 software 领域，一家 high-paying tech company 的“senior” dev salary 是 $350k/yr，其中“senior”可以指“刚离开学校三年的人”，而被认为是 high performing engineer 的人拿到 seven figures 也并不少见。

这些 fields 有着 sharply bimodal income distributions。programmers 也会面临同样的命运吗？让我们看看能找到什么 data。首先，让我们看看[来自 National Association for Law Placement 的 data](http://www.nalp.org/uploads/0812Research.pdf)，它展示了 legal salaries 是在什么时候变成 bimodal 的。

### 1991 年的 Lawyers

Median salary 是 $40k, with the numbers slowly trickling off until about $90k。根据 BLS，$90k in 1991 is worth $按 2016 dollars 计为 160k。这是相当丰厚的 starting salary。

### 2000 年的 Lawyers

![2000 年的 First-year lawyer salaries。$50k median; bimodal with peaks at $40k 和 $125k](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/law-2000.webp)

到 2000 年，distribution 已经变成 bimodal。lower peak 按 nominal（non-inflation-adjusted）口径来看大致不变，这使得它按 real（inflation-adjusted）口径来看 substantially lower，而在大约 $125k, with almost everyone coming in under $130k 处还有一个 upper peak。$130k in 2000 is $按 2016 dollars 计为 180k。左边的 peak 已经从大约 $30k in 1991 dollars to roughly $移到了按 2000 dollars 计的 40k；这两个数值都 translate 为按 2016 dollars 计大约 $55k。处于 right mode 的人境况更好了，而处于 left mode 的人境况大致不变。

我就不再用更多 graphs 来反复说明这一点了，不过，如果你看看较新的 data，就会发现两个 modes 之间的 middle area 已经 hollowed out，使这个 field 内部的 inequality 程度 increasing。作为一种 profession，lawyers 受到了 automation 的沉重打击，而按 real terms 来看，今天的 95%-ile offers 实际上并不比 2000 年时更好。但由于 bimodal distribution，50%-ile 甚至 75%-ile offers 的境况都更差了。

### 2015 年的 Programmers

lawyers 就说到这里！programmers 又如何呢？遗憾的是，很难获得有关这方面的优质 data。从轶闻来看，我确实觉得我们在走同一条路。遗憾的是，几乎所有可用的 public data sources，比如 H1B data，都有 salary numbers，却没有 total compensation numbers。由于 upper end 的 compensation 中 bonus 和 stock 所占的比例 disproportionately 高，我能找到的大多数 data sets 都没有 capture 实际发生的情况。

一个值得注意的例外，是 Dan Zhang 和 Jesse Collins 记录的 new grad compensation data：

![2016 年的 First-year programmer compensation。Compensation 的 ranges 从 $50k to $到 250k](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/cs-2015.webp)

这里的 range 确实很 wide，尽管严格来说它是 bimodal，但中间并没有像 law 和 business 中那样巨大的鸿沟。请注意，这些 data 主要来自 bachelors grads，还有少量 master's grads。这里没有包括 PhD numbers，而这些 numbers 有时会高得多。

你知道更好（larger）的 data source 吗？这里的 data 来自 2015 年“Hackathon Hackers” Facebook group 成员提供的大约 100 个 data points。Dan 和 Jesse 也有 2014 年的 data，不过，如果能获得涵盖更长时间段的 data，以及单纯数量更多的 data，就更好了。另外，这些 data 很明显是 biased towards the high end 的——如果你看看所有 experience levels 的 programmers 的 national averages，就会发现，其 average 远低于这个 data set 中 new grads 的 average。这里的 data 与我们争取人才时我听到的 numbers 相 match，但“在 Microsoft negotiating offers 的人”这个 population 也并不 representative。

如果我们有更 representative 的 data，那么除了这里已有的 data，我们可能还会看到更多处于 $40k to $60k range 的 data points，这会让 data 看起来是 bimodal 的。也有可能，我们会看到更多处于 $40k to $60k range 的 points，更多处于 $70k to $80k range 的 points，以及一些处于 $90k+ range 的 points，等等，而我们看到的会是 smooth drop-off，而不是两个 distinct modes。

暂且放下我们掌握的这点贫乏的 data，看看实际情况，programmer compensation“应该”是 bimodal 的吗？大多数其他具有 bimodal compensation 的 fields，其 compensation structure 与我们在 programming 中看到的非常不同。例如，顶尖的 law 和 consulting firms 具有 up-or-out structure，实质上是一种 tournament，它会 distorts compensation，也确实使 compensation 最终变成 bimodal 看起来更有可能。此外，competitive firms 向所有 1st year employees pay 相同的 rate，它们通过 matching 那些看起来 pay 最高的 firms 来 determine 这个 rate。例如，今年，[Cravath 宣布将向 first-year associates pay $180k，许多其他 firms 随后效仿](http://abovethelaw.com/2016/06/breaking-ny-to-180k-cravath-raises-associate-base-salaries/2/)。与大多数 high-end firms 一样，Cravath 的 salary schedule 完全 based on experience：

-   0 years：$180k
-   1 year：$190k
-   2 years：$210k
-   3 years：$235k
-   4 years：$260k
-   5 years：$280k
-   6 years：$300k
-   7 years：$315k

在 software 领域，compensation 往往是以 case-by-case basis 来确定的，这使得我们看到像 law 中那样的 sharp peak 的可能性小得多。如果一定要我猜，我会说，虽然 programmer compensation 的 dispersion 正在 increasing，但它并不是 bimodal，不过我确实没有合适的 data set 来 conclusively 作出任何判断。如果你手头有更好的 data，请指给我看。

### 附录 B：为什么 programmers 的 paid 这么高？

既然我们手头既有 programmer 的 compensation，也有 lawyer 的 compensation，就来 examine 一下这个问题。Programming 的 pays 如此之高，以至于显得有些荒谬。如果看看 compensation 相近的其他 careers，就会发现有多种 factors 充当 barriers 或 disincentives to entry。

如果看看 law，你得赢得 prestige lottery，进入一所 top school，而这将花费数十万美元（虽然有可能获得 full scholarship，但 top schools 中获得 full scholarships 的 students 所占 fraction 相对较小）。接着，你得赢得 grades lottery，拿到足够好的 grades，才能进入一家 top firm。然后，你还得不断 winning tournaments，避免被踢出去，而这要求你牺牲任何像样的个人生活。Consulting、investment banking 等也类似。Compensation 似乎与 level of sacrifice 成 proportional 关系（例如，investment bankers 的 paid 更高，但 work hours 甚至比 lawyers 更长；private equity 在 hours 和 compensation 上介于 investment and banking 与 law 之间，等等）。

从牺牲的角度来看，Medicine 似乎稍好一些，因为有一个 cartel 在 limits 进入这个 field 的人数，但 medical school 与 residency 的 combination，与 Facebook 和 Google 等地方的大多数 jobs 相比，仍然残酷得惊人。

Programming 也没有 licensing body 来 limiting programmers 的 number，也没有同样的 prestige filter，要求你必须上 top school 才能得到 well paying job。当然，确实有许多 startups 基本上只从 MIT、Stanford、CMU 和其他几所 prestigious schools hire，而且每当我查看 startups 时，都会看到如下这样的 job ads（下面这则来自一家曾长期在 Slate Star Codex 上投放广告的 company）：

> 我们的 team 有 14 人，其中包括 6 位 MIT alumni、3 位 ex-Googlers、1 位 Wharton MBA、1 位 MIT Master in CS、1 位 CMU CS alum，以及 1 位 "20 under 20" Thiel fellow。Candidates 经常说，我们是他们见过的 strongest team。
> 
> 我们并不适合所有人。我们是一家 enterprise SaaS company，你妈妈可能永远都不会听说我们。我们每周 6 天都非常努力地工作，因为我们相信 mobile 的未来，而且我们想赢。

痴迷于 Prestige 的地方确实存在。但是，在 programming 中，根据 markers of prestige 来 measuring 人，似乎是 Silicon Valley startup 的特点，而不是 top-paying companies 的特点。Big companies [的 pay 比 startups 高得多](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/)，却远没有那么经常根据 prestige 来 filter people out。你不仅不需要从合适的 school 获得合适的 degree，甚至也不需要拥有合适种类的 degree，或者任何 degree。虽然没有 degree 的情况越来越少见，我仍然会遇到没有 experience 的 new hires，他们要么没有 degree，要么拥有 unrelated field（如 sociology 或 philosophy）的 degree。

既然没有其他 similarly remunerative fields 所具有的这些 barriers to entry，programmers 怎么还能获得如此高的 paid？一种 possibility 是，我们存在 programmers 的 shortage。如果是这样，你会预期有更多 programmers 进入这个 field，从而 bringing down compensation。CS enrollments 最近一直处于 record levels，因此这种情况可能已经在发生。另一种 possibility 是，programming 在某种意义上 uniquely hard，但我觉得这不太可信。Programming 看起来并不 inherently harder 于 electrical engineering 或 chemical engineering，而且过去十年间，它显然也没有变得 much harder；但在这段时期，programming 从 compensation 与大多数 engineering fields 相近，变成了 paying 高得多。上次我与一家 EE company 就 offers 进行 negotiating 时，他们对我说，他们的 VPs 挣得还不如我多，而我所在的 software company 与其 peers 相比，pays 还相对较低。我们没有理由认为，不会有一股人员从 engineering fields 流向 programming，直到 compensation 达到 balanced。

另一种 possibility 是，U.S. immigration laws 充当 protectionist barrier，来 prop up programmer compensation。这似乎不可能持续下去（为什么就不该有真正 valuable 的 non-U.S. companies 呢），但目前看来，这确实在一定程度上属实。我在 Google 时，有一件事让我印象深刻：他们在 Washington 或 Colorado 给你的 pay，与在 Silicon Valley 给你的大致相同，但在 London 给你的 pay 却低得多。每当出现这类讨论时，人们总会提起那个所谓的“事实”：SV salaries 实际上并没有听起来那么好，因为 cost of living 太高；但 companies 不仅会在 Seattle match SV offers，在 Pittsburgh 等地方也会 match。我对其原因最合理的猜测是，Midwest 的人可以可信地威胁说要搬到 SV，在那里任何一家 company 找份 job，而 London 的人却做不到[2](https://danluu.com/bimodal-compensation/#fn:M)。虽然我们似乎不太可能放宽现行 immigration restrictions，但这些 immigration restrictions 已经并且仍在促使那些本来会在 U.S. founded companies 的人，到别处去 found companies。考虑到 U.S. 对 found startups 的人并没有 monopoly，而我们又竭尽所能把想在这里 found startups 的人挡在门外，那么，最终在 U.S. 之外 founded 的 Facebooks 和 Googles，以 U.S. 内部 companies 同样的方式 compete for programmers，似乎是不可避免的。

最近我经常听到的另一种 theory 是，large companies 的 programmers 获得很高的 paid，是因为 [Kremer's O-ring model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-ring_theory_of_economic_development) 所 described 的 phenomenon。这个 model assumes，productivity 是 multiplicative。如果你的 co-workers 更好，你就会更 productive，并 produce 更多 value。如果是这样，你就会预期出现某种 assortive matching，最终形成 pay 更高的 high-skill firms，以及 pay 更低的 low-skill firms。这个 model 有一种直觉上的吸引力，但它无法 explain，为什么 programming compensation 的 dispersion 比（例如）electrical engineering compensation 更高。随着 open source 的 prevalence，与大多数 fields 相比，utilize 自己 firm 之外 productive people 的 work 要容易得多。这个 model 对 programming 的适用程度理应低于大多数 engineering fields，但 compensation 的 dispersion 却更高。

由于类似的原因，另一种相关 theory 也不可能正确：high-paid software engineers 格外 elite，是精英中的精英，只因为他们 productive，所以 paid 更高。如果看看 BLS 所说的 US programmers 有多少（on the order of 几百万），以及 high-paying tech companies 在 U.S. employ 了多少 engineers（on the order of 二三十万），那么 high-paying software compnaies 根本不可能只由 top 1% 构成。即使他们的 filters 是 perfect（而不是人们普遍认为的彻头彻尾的笑话），他们也不可能比 90%-ile 更好。实际上，更有可能的是，一家 high-paying tech company 的 median programmer 只比 50%-ile 高一点。

我最常听到的 theory 是，“software 正在吞噬世界”。这个 theory 是这样说的：programmers 当然会获得很高的 paid，而且会继续获得很高的 paid，因为 software 很 important，而且只会越来越 important。尽管这是我听过最常被提出的 theory，但如果 compare 其他 fields，这似乎毫无道理。对于 90 年代末的 microprocessor design 和 fiber optics，你同样可以这样说。这两个 fields 如今都比 90 年代更 important，不仅对 processing power 和 bandwidth 的 demand 比以往任何时候都多，对 software 的 demand 实际上也 dependent on 它们。然而，optics engineering job market 至今仍未从 dot com crash 中 recovered，而 microprocessor design engineer market 在 recovering 之后，给 experienced PhDs 的 pays 仍然低于 Facebook 的 CS new grad。

此外，如果看看实际上是什么 driven up programmer pay，任何依赖 market conditions、the economy at large、programming 的 impact 等某种 inherent property 来支持 high programmer pay 的 argument，似乎都不可能正确。FB 拒绝参与 [Google/Apple 那项后来基本上扩展到 industry wide 的 wage fixing agreement](https://danluu.com/google-wage-fixing/)，这意味着 FB 当时在 outpaying 其他 major tech companies。wage-fixing agreement 被解除后，其他 companies 为了避免人员既流失到 FB，又流失到彼此那里，“不得不”接近 matching FB compensation。当它们这样做时，FB 又不断提高 compensation 的标准，于是 compensation 不断变好。\[2022 年更新\] 这一点在 benefits 和 pay structure 的 changes 中体现得最清楚：FB 作出 change，Google 就立即跟进，其他 companies 则稍后采纳这一 change；例如，FB removed vesting cliffs 后，Google 在几周内也做了同样的事，随后这一 change 逐渐传遍整个 industry。确实有些 companies 给 programmers 的 paying 与 FB 一样好，甚至更好，例如 Netflix 和各种 finance companies；但 major tech companies 往往不会 match 那些地方的 offers，因为它们规模太小，无法 hire away 足够多的 programmers 来构成威胁。然而 FB 规模很大，hires 的人数足以让 Google 在意；Google 会 matches FB，而两者加在一起，规模又足以让其他 major tech companies 在意。

由于 compensation increases 的 mechanism 一直带有 arbitrary 性（FB 本可以不存在，或者完全掌控 FB 的人 Zuckerberg 可以决定采用不同的 compensation policy），因此 programmer pay 能达到如今这样的水平，也是相当 arbitrary 的。

总之，high programmer pay 对我来说似乎仍是个谜。我很想听到一种有说服力的 theory，解释为什么 programming 的 pay“应该”高于其他 similar fields，或者为什么它的 pay 应该与那些 barriers to entry 高得多的 fields 一样多。

### 更新

Eric Roberts 已经观察到，[在 downturn 之后，CS enrollments 需要很长时间才能 recover，导致拥有 CS degrees 的人数相对于 demand 存在很大的 deficit](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/CSCapacity.pdf)。

![downturn 与 enrollment 的 recovery 之间存在超过十年的 lag](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/cs-degrees.webp)

2001 年的 bubble bursting caused 了 CS enrollment 的 severe drop。CS enrollment 直到 2014 年才再次达到此前的 peak，而且如果你对 graph 进行 fit，并参照这些 peaks 进行 extrapolate，enrollments 又过了一两年才达到 historical trend。即使我们没有任何 data，发现存在五年的 delay 也并不令人意外。在四年 graduate 的人中（而不是五年或更久），大多数人在大二中后期之后都不会再 change 他们的 major，因此，仅这一点就已经带来了两到三年的 delay。而且，在 downturn 之后，recover 需要一些时间，因此我们会预期至少还要再过两到三年。Roberts 提出的论证是，额外的 latency 来自其他一些 factors，包括人们担心，即使情况看起来还不错，jobs 也很快会被 outsourced，以及 colleges 的 slow response。

[Dan Wang](https://danwang.co/why-so-few-computer-science-majors/) 指出，根据 SO survey，3/4 的 developers 拥有 BS degree（或更高学位）。如果没有 CS degree 就很难在 statistically 的意义上获得 high-paying job，而且 2001 年 downturn 的余波延续了十多年，那么这就可能 explain 为什么 programmer compensation 如此 high。当然，我们大多数人都认识 industry 中没有 degree 的人，但没有 credential 似乎更难找到 entry-level position。

这对未来意味着什么，目前还不清楚。即使缺少拥有适当 credential 的 candidates 是 programmer compensation 的一个 major driver，过去几年创纪录的 CS enrollments 对未来 compensation 意味着什么，也仍然不清楚。创纪录的 enrollments 可能意味着，我们应该预期 compensation 回落到其他需要 similar skills 的 fields 所呈现的 levels，比如 electrical engineering。也有可能，enrollment 继续比 demand 落后十年，而创纪录的 enrollments 只是在跟上十年前的 demand；如果是这样，我们或许会预期 elevated compensation 会 persist（只要其他 factors，比如在 U.S. 以外 hiring，不会对情况产生过大 influence）。由于存在如此大的 latency，另一种可能是，enrollment 已经或将会 overshoot demand，而我们应该预期 compensaton programmer compensation 会 decline。甚至 Roberts 的 paper 能否作为当前 high comp 的 explanation，也并不清楚，因为 Roberts 同样发现，80 年代存在巨大的 capacity crunch；虽然一些 programmers 当时的 paid 水平 very well，但 paid 水平达到“very well”的 programmers 的 fraction，似乎远小于今天。仅 Google 就 employs 30k 名 engineers。如果其中 20k 是 U.S. 的 programmers，并且采用 U.S. 有 3 million 名 programmers 的 estimate，那么仅 Google 就 employs 了 U.S. programmers 的 0.6%。如果再加上其他以 pay competitively 著称的大公司（Amazon、Facebook 等），那就占 U.S. 全体 programmers 的一个 significant fraction；我认为，这与 80 年代的情况相当不同。

这篇文章收到的最常见回应是，我们理应预期 programmers 是 well-paid 的，因为 software 无处不在，而且未来的 software 至少会和现在一样多。完全相同的 line of reasoning 也可以 apply 于 electrical engineering；它比 software 更 fundamental，因为 software requires hardware，然而 electrical engineering comp 与 programmer comp 却不在同一档次。没有 microprocessors，Highly paid programmers 就无法完成工作，而且 processors 的 sold 数量比以往任何时候都多，但 Intel 和 Qualcomm 等公司的“senior”人员的 comp packages，甚至还不到 Google 或 Facebook 的一半。对于从事 water and sewage systems 工作的人，你也可以提出类似的 argument，但这些人的 compensation 似乎同样不在 programmers 的 range 内。任何形如“X 的 price 是 high 的，因为 X 很 important”的 argument，都 implicitly assumes 存在某种 force 在 constraining X 的 supply。“X 很 important”或“我们需要大量 X”这样的 claim，只讲了事情的一半。“X 很 important”或“X 很 hard”之类的 claims 还存在另一个问题：对于 pay 低得多的 industries，这些 statements 似乎也同样 true。如果你对 programmers 为何 well paid 的 explanation，对于任何“classical” engineering discipline 都同样 true，那么你就需要某种 explanation，说明为什么那些其他 fields 不应该获得同样好的 pay。

我听到的第二常见评论是，programmers 当然是 well paid 的，software companies 的价值那么高，这使得这一结果不可避免。但一家公司是 profitable 的，并不意味着 workers 实际获得良好 compensated 就是不可避免的。提出这种 argument 的一个人给我发来了[这份按每位 employee 的盈利衡量的 most profitable companies 名单](http://www.businessinsider.com/apple-facebook-alphabet-most-profitable-companies-per-employee-2017-12)的链接。名单中有一些 pay 相当好的 software companies，比如 Alphabet（Google）和 Facebook，但我们也看到了 Qualcomm、Cisco、TSMC 等 hardware companies（也可以说包括如今已经 acquired ARM 的 SoftBank）；它们的 pay 甚至不如那些不 turn a profit、或者 barely make money 且未来也没有途径变得 wildly profitable 的 software companies。此外，名单上这些 software companies 的 compensation 与它们的 profit per employee 并没有很强的 related 关系。

举一个我熟悉的具体例子，因为我是在 Madison 长大的：Epic Systems 的 execs 建立了一家 generated 了大量 wealth 的公司，其 founder 的 estimated net worth 达到 \$3.6 billion，远高于 tech 领域除最成功的 founders 之外的其他 founders。但 Epic 的 line engineers 的 paid 水平，significantly less than 那些与 SV 争夺 talent 的 tech companies 中的 engineers，甚至低于那些从未赚过钱的 tech companies 中的 engineers。同样赚到相近金额的一些 software companies，究竟有什么特点，使它们没有把自己 generate 的 wealth 几乎全部输送到顶层？对此的典型回答是 cost of living，但正如我们已经看到的，这个回答在此处比平常更说不通，因为 Google 在 Epic 所在的同一座城市设有办公室，而对于 typical dev，Google 的 pay 远超 Epic 的 double。如果存在某种简单的 cost of living adjustment，你会预期 Google 在 Madison 的 pay 低于 Toronto 或 London，但实际似乎恰好相反。这并不是 Madison 特有的情况——仅举一例，你可以在 Austin 找到不少成功的 software companies，它们的 pay 大约只有同城 Amazon 和 Facebook 的一半；这些公司的 upper management 收入颇丰，line engineers 也过着不错的生活，但远远比不上他们转到 Amazon 或 Facebook 这样的公司后能获得的收入。

所有这些 theories 的共同之处在于，它们也 apply 于其他 fields，因此，按目前的表述，它们不可能是 programmers 比这些其他 fields 的人获得更好 paid 的 reason。有人可以 argue，programming 具有其中许多因素的 unique combination，或者应该预期其中某个 reasons 对 programming 的 apply 程度远强于任何其他 field，但我还没有见到谁提出这样的论证。相反，人们只是给出“programming 真的很 hard”这样显然站不住脚的 statements（在这场讨论中，只有当它确实是现存最 hardest 的 field，并且比其他 engineering fields 要 hard 得多时，这才是 valid 的 reason）。

## 原文

Developer compensation has skyrocketed since the demise of the [Google et al. wage-suppressing no-hire agreement](https://danluu.com/google-wage-fixing/), to the point where compensation rivals and maybe even exceeds compensation in traditionally remunerative fields like law, consulting, etc. In software, "senior" dev salary at a high-paying tech company is $350k/yr, where "senior" can mean "someone three years of out school" and it's not uncommon for someone who's considered a high performing engineer to make seven figures.

The fields have sharply bimodal income distributions. Are programmers in for the same fate? Let's see what data we can find. First, let's look at [data from the National Association for Law Placement](http://www.nalp.org/uploads/0812Research.pdf), which shows when legal salaries become bimodal.

### Lawyers in 1991

![First-year lawyer salaries in 1991. $40k median, trailing off with the upper end just under $90k](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/law-1991.webp)

Median salary is $40k, with the numbers slowly trickling off until about $90k. According to the BLS $90k in 1991 is worth $160k in 2016 dollars. That's a pretty generous starting salary.

### Lawyers in 2000

![First-year lawyer salaries in 2000. $50k median; bimodal with peaks at $40k and $125k](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/law-2000.webp)

By 2000, the distribution had become bimodal. The lower peak is about the same in nominal (non-inflation-adjusted) terms, putting it substantially lower in real (inflation-adjusted) terms, and there's an upper peak at around $125k, with almost everyone coming in under $130k. $130k in 2000 is $180k in 2016 dollars. The peak on the left has moved from roughly $30k in 1991 dollars to roughly $40k in 2000 dollars; both of those translate to roughly $55k in 2016 dollars. People in the right mode are doing better, while people in the left mode are doing about the same.

I won't belabor the point with more graphs, but if you look at more recent data, the middle area between the two modes has hollowed out, increasing the level of inequality within the field. As a profession, lawyers have gotten hit hard by automation, and in real terms, 95%-ile offers today aren't really better than they were in 2000. But 50%-ile and even 75%-ile offers are worse off due to the bimodal distribution.

### Programmers in 2015

Enough about lawyers! What about programmers? Unfortunately, it's hard to get good data on this. Anecdotally, it sure seems to me like we're going down the same road. Unfortunately, almost all of the public data sources that are available, like H1B data, have salary numbers and not total compensation numbers. Since compensation at the the upper end is disproportionately bonus and stock, most data sets I can find don't capture what's going on.

One notable exception is the new grad compensation data recorded by Dan Zhang and Jesse Collins:

![First-year programmer compensation in 2016. Compensation ranges from $50k to $250k](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/cs-2015.webp)

There's certainly a wide range here, and while it's technically bimodal, there isn't a huge gulf in the middle like you see in law and business. Note that this data is mostly bachelors grads with a few master's grads. PhD numbers, which sometimes go much higher, aren't included.

Do you know of a better (larger) source of data? This is from about 100 data points, members of the "Hackathon Hackers" Facebook group, in 2015. Dan and Jesse also have data from 2014, but it would be nice to get data over a wider timeframe and just plain more data. Also, this data is pretty clearly biased towards the high end — if you look at national averages for programmers at all levels of experience, the average comes in much lower than the average for new grads in this data set. The data here match the numbers I hear when we compete for people, but the population of "people negotiating offers at Microsoft" also isn't representative.

If we had more representative data it's possible that we'd see a lot more data points in the $40k to $60k range along with the data we have here, which would make the data look bimodal. It's also possible that we'd see a lot more points in the $40k to $60k range, many more in the $70k to $80k range, some more in the $90k+ range, etc., and we'd see a smooth drop-off instead of two distinct modes.

Stepping back from the meager data we have and looking at the circumstances, "should" programmer compensation be bimodal? Most other fields that have bimodal compensation have a very different compensation structure than we see in programming. For example, top law and consulting firms have an up-or-out structure, which is effectively a tournament, which distorts compensation and certainly makes it seem more likely that compensation is likely to end up being bimodal. Additionally, competitive firms pay the same rate to all 1st year employees, which they determine by matching whoever appears to be paying the most. For example, this year, [Cravath announced that it would pay first-year associates $180k, and many other firms followed suit](http://abovethelaw.com/2016/06/breaking-ny-to-180k-cravath-raises-associate-base-salaries/2/). Like most high-end firms, Cravath has a salary schedule that's entirely based on experience:

-   0 years: $180k
-   1 year: $190k
-   2 years: $210k
-   3 years: $235k
-   4 years: $260k
-   5 years: $280k
-   6 years: $300k
-   7 years: $315k

In software, compensation tends to be on a case-by-case basis, which makes it much less likely that we'll see a sharp peak the way we do in law. If I had to guess, I'd say that while the dispersion in programmer compensation is increasing, it's not bimodal, but I don't really have the right data set to conclusively say anything. Please point me to any data you have that's better.

### Appendix B: why are programmers well paid?

Since we have both programmer and lawyer compensation handy, let's examine that. Programming pays so well that it seems a bit absurd. If you look at other careers with similar compensation, there are multiple factors that act as barriers or disincentives to entry.

If you look at law, you have to win the prestige lottery and get into a top school, which will cost hundreds of thousands of dollars (while it's possible to get a full scholarship, a relatively small fraction of students at top schools are on full scholarships). Then you have to win the grades lottery and get good enough grades to get into a top firm. And then you have to continue winning tournaments to avoid getting kicked out, which requires sacrificing any semblance of a personal life. Consulting, investment banking, etc., are similar. Compensation appears to be proportional to the level of sacrifice (e.g., investment bankers are paid better, but work even longer hours than lawyers, private equity is somewhere between investment and banking and law in hours and compensation, etc.).

Medicine seems to be a bit better from the sacrifice standpoint because there's a cartel which limits entry into the field, but the combination of medical school and residency is still incredibly brutal compared to most jobs at places like Facebook and Google.

Programming also doesn't have a licensing body limiting the number of programmers, nor is there the same prestige filter where you have to go to a top school to get a well paying job. Sure, there are a lot of startups who basically only hire from MIT, Stanford, CMU, and a few other prestigious schools, and I see job ads like the following whenever I look at startups (the following is from a company that was advertising on Slate Star Codex for quite a long time):

> Our team of 14 includes 6 MIT alumni, 3 ex-Googlers, 1 Wharton MBA, 1 MIT Master in CS, 1 CMU CS alum, and 1 "20 under 20" Thiel fellow. Candidates often remark we're the strongest team they've ever seen.
> 
> We’re not for everyone. We’re an enterprise SaaS company your mom will probably never hear of. We work really hard 6 days a week because we believe in the future of mobile and we want to win.

Prestige obsessed places exist. But, in programming, measuring people by markers of prestige seems to be a Silicon Valley startup thing and not a top-paying companies thing. Big companies, [which pay a lot better than startups](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/), don't filter people out by prestige nearly as often. Not only do you not need the right degree from the right school, you also don't need to have the right kind of degree, or any degree at all. Although it's getting rarer to not have a degree, I still meet new hires with no experience and either no degree or a degree in an unrelated field (like sociology or philosophy).

How is it possible that programmers are paid so well without these other barriers to entry that similarly remunerative fields have? One possibility is that we have a shortage of programmers. If that's the case, you'd expect more programmers to enter the field, bringing down compensation. CS enrollments have been at record levels recently, so this may already be happening. Another possibility is that programming is uniquely hard in some way, but that seems implausible to me. Programming doesn't seem inherently harder than electrical engineering or chemical engineering and it certainly hasn't gotten much harder over the past decade, but during that timeframe, programming has gone from having similar compensation to most engineering fields to paying much better. The last time I was negotiating with a EE company about offers, they remarked to me that their VPs don't make as much as I do, and I work at a software company that pays relatively poorly compared to its peers. There's no reason to believe that we won't see a flow of people from engineering fields into programming until compensation is balanced.

Another possibility is that U.S. immigration laws act as a protectionist barrier to prop up programmer compensation. It seems impossible for this to last (why shouldn't there by really valuable non-U.S. companies), but it does appear to be somewhat true for now. When I was at Google, one thing that was remarkable to me was that they'd pay you approximately the same thing in Washington or Colorado as they do Silicon Valley, but they'd pay you much less in London. Whenever one of these discussions comes up, people always bring up the "fact" that SV salaries aren't really as good as they sound because the cost of living is so high, but companies will not only match SV offers in Seattle, they'll match them in places like Pittsburgh. My best guess for why this happens is that someone in the Midwest can credibly threaten to move to SV and take a job at any company there, whereas someone in London can't[2](https://danluu.com/bimodal-compensation/#fn:M). While we seem unlikely to loosen current immigration restrictions, our immigration restrictions have caused and continue to cause people who would otherwise have founded companies in the U.S. to found companies elsewhere. Given that the U.S. doesn't have a monopoly on people who found startups and that we do our best to keep people who want to found startups here out, it seems inevitable that there will eventually be Facebooks and Googles founded outside of the U.S. who compete for programmers the same way companies compete inside the U.S.

Another theory that I've heard a lot lately is that programmers at large companies get paid a lot because of the phenomenon described in [Kremer's O-ring model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-ring_theory_of_economic_development). This model assumes that productivity is multiplicative. If your co-workers are better, you're more productive and produce more value. If that's the case, you expect a kind of assortive matching where you end up with high-skill firms that pay better, and low-skill firms that pay worse. This model has a kind of intuitive appeal to it, but it can't explain why programming compensation has higher dispersion than (for example) electrical engineering compensation. With the prevalence of open source, it's much easier to utilize the work of productive people outside your firm than in most fields. This model should be less true of programming than in most engineering fields, but the dispersion in compensation is higher.

A related theory that can't be correct for similar reasons is that high-paid software engineers are extra elite, the best of the best, and are simply paid more because they're productive. If you look at how many programmers the BLS says exist in the US (on the order of a few million) and how many engineers high-paying tech companies employ in the U.S. (on the order of a couple or a few hundred thousand), high-paying software compnaies literally can't consist of the top 1%. Even if their filters were perfect (as opposed to the complete joke that they're widely regarded to be), they couldn't be better than 90%-ile. Realistically, it's more likely that the median programmer at a high-paying tech company is a bit above 50%-ile.

The most common theory I've heard is that "software is eating the world". The theory goes: of course programmers get paid a lot and will continue to get paid a lot because software is important and only becoming more important. Despite being the most commonly stated theory I've heard, this seems nonsensical if you compare other fields. You could've said this about microprocessor design in the late 90s as well as fiber optics. Those fields are both more important today than they were in the 90s, not only is there more demand for processing power and bandwidth than ever before, demand for software is actually dependent on those. And yet, the optics engineering job market still hasn't recovered from the dot com crash and the microprocessor design engineer market, after recovering, still pays experienced PhDs less than a CS new grad at Facebook.

Furthermore, any argument for high programmer pay that relies on some inherent property of market conditions, the economy at large, the impact of programming, etc., seems like it cannot be correct if you look at what's actually driven up programmer pay. FB declined to participate in [Google/Apple wage fixing agreement that became basically industry wide](https://danluu.com/google-wage-fixing/), which mean that FB was outpaying other major tech companies. When the wage-fixing agreement was lifted, other companies "had to" come close to matching FB compensation to avoid losing people both to FB and to each other. When they did that, FB kept raising the bar on compensation and compensation kept getting better. \[2022 update\] This can most clearly be seen with changes to benefits and pay structure, where FB would make a change, Google would follow suit immediately, and other companies would pick up the change later, as when FB removed vesting cliffs and Google did the same within weeks and the change trickled out across the industry. There are companies that were paying programmers as well or better than FB, like Netflix and a variety of finance companies, but major tech companies tended to not match offers from those places because they were too small to hire away enough programmers to be concerning, but FB is large and hires enough to be a concern to Google, which matches FB and combined, are large enough to be a concern to other major tech companies.

Because the mechanism for compensation increases has been arbitrary (FB could not exist, or the person who's in total control of FB, Zuckerberg, could decide on different compensation policy), it's quite arbitrary that programmer pay is as good as it is.

In conclusion, high programmer pay seems like a mystery to me and would love to hear a compelling theory for why programming "should" pay more than other similar fields, or why it should pay as much as fields that have much higher barriers to entry.

### Update

Eric Roberts has observed that [it takes a long time for CS enrollments to recover after a downturn, leading to a large deficit in the number of people with CS degrees vs. demand](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/CSCapacity.pdf).

![More than a one decade lag between downturn and recovery in enrollment](https://danluu.com/images/bimodal-compensation/cs-degrees.webp)

The 2001 bubble bursting caused a severe drop in CS enrollment. CS enrollment didn't hit its previous peak again until 2014, and if you fit the graph and extrapolate against the peaks, it took another year or two for enrollments to hit the historical trend. If we didn't have any data, it wouldn't be surprising to find that there's a five year delay. Of the people who graduate in four years (as opposed to five or more), most aren't going to change their major after mid or late sophmore year, so that's already two to three years of delay right there. And after a downturn, it takes some time to recover, so we'd expect at least another two to three years. Roberts makes a case that the additional latency came from a number of other factors including the fear that even though things looked ok, jobs would be outsourced soon and a slow response by colleges.

[Dan Wang](https://danwang.co/why-so-few-computer-science-majors/) has noted that, according to the SO survey, 3/4 of developers have a BS degree (or higher). If it's statistically "hard" to get a high-paying job without a CS degree and there's over a decade hangover from the 2001 downturn, that could explain why programmer compensation is so high. Of course, most of us know people in the industry without a degree, but it seems to be harder to find an entry-level position without a credential.

It's not clear what this means for the future. Even if the lack of candidates with the appropriate credential is a major driver in programmer compensation, it's unclear what the record CS enrollments over the past few years means for future compensation. It's possible that record enrollments mean that we should expect compensation to come back down to the levels we see in other fields that require similar skills, like electrical engineering. It's also possible that enrollment continues to lag behind demand by a decade and that record enrollments are just keeping pace with demand from a decade ago, in which case we might expect elevated compensation to persist (as long as other factors, like hiring outside of the U.S., don't influence things too much). Since there's so much latency, another possibility is that enrollment has or will overshoot demand and we should expect compensaton programmer compensation to decline. And it's not even clear that the Roberts paper makes sense as an explanation for high current comp because Roberts also found a huge capacity crunch in the 80s and, while some programmers were paid very well, the fraction of programmers who were paid "very well" seems to have been much smaller than it is today. Google alone employs 30k engineers. If 20k are programmers in the U.S. and the estimate that there are 3 million programmers in the U.S., Google alone employs 0.6% of programmers in the U.S. If you add in the other large companies that are known to pay competitively (Amazon, Facebook, etc.), that's a significant fraction of all programmers in the U.S., which I believe is quite different from the situation in the 80s.

The most common response I've gotten to this post is that we should expect programmers to be well-paid because software is everywhere and there will be at least as much software in the future. This exact same line of reasoning could apply to electrical engineering, which is more fundamental than software, in that software requires hardware, and yet electrical engineering comp isn't in the same league as programmer comp. Highly paid programmers couldn't get their work done without microprocessors, and there are more processors sold than ever before, but the comp packages for a "senior" person at places like Intel and Qualcomm aren't even within a factor of two as at Google or Facebook. You could also make a similar argument for people who work on water and sewage systems, but those folks also don't seem compensation that's in the same range as programmers either. Any argument of the form, "the price for X is high because X is important" implicitly assumes that there's some force constraining the supply of X. The claim that "X is important" or "we need a lot of X" is missing half the story. Another problem with claims like "X is important" or "X is hard" is that these statements don't seem any less true of industries that pay much less. If your explanation of why programmers are well paid is just as true of any "classical" engineering discipline, you need some explanation of why those other fields shouldn't be as well paid.

The second most common comment that I hear is that, of course programmers are well paid, software companies are worth so much, which makes it inevitable. But there's nothing inevitable about workers actually being well compensated because a company is profitable. Someone who made this argument sent me a link to [this list of the most profitable companies per employee](http://www.businessinsider.com/apple-facebook-alphabet-most-profitable-companies-per-employee-2017-12). The list has some software companies that pay quite well, like Alphabet (Google) and Facebook, but we also see hardware companies like Qualcomm, Cisco, TSMC (and arguably SoftBank now that they've acquired ARM) that don't even pay as well software companies that don't turn a profit or that barely make money and have no path to being wildly profitable in the future. Moreover, the compensation at the software companies that are listed isn't very strongly related to their profit per employee.

To take a specific example that I'm familiar with because I grew up in Madison, the execs at Epic Systems have built a company that's generated so much wealth that its founder has an estimated net worth of $3.6 billion, which is much more than all but the most successful founders in tech. But line engineers at Epic are paid significantly less than engineers at tech companies that compete with SV for talent, even tech companies that have never made any money. What is it about some software companies that make a similar amount of money that prevents them from funneling virtually all of the wealth they generate up to the top? The typical answer to this cost of living, but as we've seen, that makes even less sense than usual in this case since Google has an office in the same city as Epic, and Google pays well over double what Epic does for typical dev. If there were some kind of simple cost of living adjustment, you'd expect Google to pay less in Madison than in Toronto or London, but it seems to be the other way around. This isn't unique to Madison — just for example, you can find a number of successful software companies in Austin that pay roughly half what Amazon and Facebook pay in the same city, where upper management does very well for themselves and line engineers make a fine living, but nowhere near as much as they'd make if they moved to a company like Amazon or Facebook.

The thing all of these theories have in common is that they apply to other fields as well, so they cannot be, as stated, the reason programmers are better paid than people in these other fields. Someone could argue that programming has a unique combination of many of these or that one these reasons should be expected to apply much more strongly than to any other field, but I haven't seen anyone make that case. Instead, people just make obviously bogus statements like "programming is really hard" (which is only valid as a reason, in this discussion, if literally the hardest field in existence and much harder than other engineering fields).
