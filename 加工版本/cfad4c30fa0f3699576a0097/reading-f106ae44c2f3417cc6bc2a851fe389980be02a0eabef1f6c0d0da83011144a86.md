# Funding for longer：提供更长期的资助

---

## 正文

> Max Plank，他研究 thermodynamics，而他花了 20 年才得出他的 conclusions，即，即 matter 是，是 quantized。(Donald [Braben](https://ideamachinespodcast.com/donald-braben))

Max Planck 大约在 1879 年获得 PhD，直到 ~1900 年，他才 published 那些 postulating energy 的 [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_postulate) 的关键 papers，并用它来 explain black body radiation（一些背景可参见[这里](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1252829754203312128)）。1879-1900 这段间隔大概就是 Braben 所说的那 20 年。有意思的是，要找到 Planck 在此之前 published 的任何东西都很难。他的 1900 年 papers 并未 reference 他更早的 work。wikipedia 上 Planck 的 bibliography 从 1900 年开始。得找上一阵子才能找到这份 [bibliography](https://artfiles.org/netlib.org/bibnet/authors/p/planck-max.pdf)，从中我们可以发现（第 140 页及以后），他当时确实在 publishing papers，其中还包括一本书，但那些 papers 似乎并未受到特别高的评价，其中大部分 work 甚至没有被译成英语 \[不像比如 Einstein 的 Annus Mirabilis papers，或 Planck 自己在 1900 年发表的 Nobel-winning papers\]，因此，认为它们相对而言是 inconsequential，或许是一个合理的 assumption。这倒符合 Braben 的观点：直到他的 1900 年 paper，Planck 才成为一位 stellar academic，确实花了 20 年才 achieve 一个 outstanding result。倘若 Planck 是根据他在 1900 年以前的 results 来接受 judged，他可能早就被踢出这个 system 了。可他真的会吗？

> 多亏 Helmholtz 的大力支持——他当时被广泛称为“Reich Chancellor of Science”，而 Planck 的 originality 显然给他留下了深刻印象——他在 1888 年获任 University of Berlin 的 professor of theoretical physics，当时年仅 30 岁，仍然年轻得令人惊讶。他一直留在那里，直到将近半个世纪后退休。([Braben](https://ipfs.io/ipfs/bafykbzacedvhurmspj7f7yjasy3p4cvmfr4gzcphd5yvpwi6zabsulhs4fym2?filename=Donald%20W.%20Braben%20-%20Promoting%20the%20Planck%20Club_%20How%20defiant%20youth%2C%20irreverent%20researchers%20and%20liberated%20universities%20can%20foster%20prosperity%20indefinitely-Wiley%20%282014%29.pdf))

Braben 将 Planck 的故事讲述为一个人追求 truth、却无人能够知道这会把他引向何处的历程（而且现代 grant system 本来无法 support 这样的历程）；我看到的却是另一种故事：身处现代的 Planck 完全能够顺利开展自己的 research，或者直接 working on black body radiation 的 problem，或者为其他事情获得 funding（比如他在 1900 年之前的 chemistry work），然后仍在业余时间 working on 他真正想 working on 的事情；这虽然是 suboptimal，却仍然会 lead to 同样的 result。Planck 并没有花 20 年 working on 同一个 problem，而是碰巧花了 20 年才找到一个后来碰巧极其 relevant 的 problem；与此同时，在此前的那段时间里，他知道自己可以做什么，也完全可以向 peer review panel explain 这一点。

## Funding for longer：提供更长期的资助

_Funding people_ 这种 approach 似乎往往与 _funding for longer_ 这一想法相联系。HHMI 和 [NIH director's award](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/) 获得 funded 的时间，比通常的 R01-funded NIH investigator 更长。

但是，选择你预期会 successful 的人这一 philosophy，与长期 funding 那个人的 philosophy 之间，并没有 necessary connection。你可以 fund people，给他们一年时间随便做什么。或者，你可以 fund 一个 project 长达 20 年，而不要求 interim reports。

事实上，[Braben's](https://ipfs.io/ipfs/bafykbzaceagsnks3nwmq7fe7vlg6deb25digxro4tj45l7e6zc7kxaj7sguuy?filename=Braben%20D.W.%20%20-%20Scientific%20Freedom_%20The%20Elixir%20of%20Civilization%20%282008%29.pdf) 自己的 funding scheme——Venture Research——似乎并未特别着重于长期 funding，有些 projects 持续了 3 年，另一些则持续了 10 年。对于 HHMI/NIH 的 cases，确实获得它们的 researchers 更加 successful，但在上一篇文章中，我已经 showed，要 disentangle 这究竟是因为 researchers 本来就很出色，还是因为 award empowers 他们做出出色的事情，并非易事。而且，即使我们知道原因是后者，也仍然不知道，究竟是 award 的 amount，还是 longer term funding 的 prospect 在这里起作用。

Funding for shorter 有它的 advantages：必要时，它允许 funders 更迅速地向新的 proposals redeploy funding。但与此同时，它本身也可能 incentivize 一种不同的 work，而非在更长期的 funding setups 下原本会开展的 work。假设 Dr. X 承诺要做 Very Important Science，但他需要 20 年才能做到，而且在这 20 年结束之前，这一切看起来都不会特别令人兴奋；等到最后，一切才会说得通，获得的 results 也一定会为他赢得 Nobel Prize。你会 fund Dr. X 吗？也许不会，尤其是如果 Dr. X 坚持说，在这 20 年里根本不能对他进行 evaluated，这就会引起一些怀疑。他是在说，他的 research plans 并不 legible，而且也没有任何 intermediate steps 可以用来 assess final product 的 quality。这是极不寻常的：如果 Dr. X 说他想 prove relativity 是假新闻，而且打算通过 building 一个新的 measurement device，再做一些 measurements，并使这些 measurements 的 interpretation contradicts relativity，那么这个 device 的 construction 就可以定期 reported。Prototypes 可以展示，unpromising avenues 也可以在 periodic reports 中着重指出。如果他只是想做 theory，那么仍然可以在 milestone reviews 中 presented intermediate results 和 failed approaches。

Human Genome Project 花了超过[十年](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project)，但 produced 了[各种](https://www.genome.gov/news/news-release/the-Human-Genome-Project-turns-the-big-3-0) intermediate results。Apollo 从最初的 feasibility studies 到 Apollo 11，花了略[少于十年](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1vN5cHkyPLRmjE2BSEElKuzyAm9p8jFU-2gEnlcLAikg/edit#gid=0)的时间，但这个 project 在此后[不到两年](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I_SA-1)就已经在 demoing rockets。ARPA projects 可以只持续三年（[Azoulay](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/699933) 等，2019）。这些只是用来说明一条 general rule 的 illustrative examples：我想不到任何 projects 的 examples，是花费了很长时间，却无法在其 progressed 的过程中接受 assessed 的。

在我看来，这 suggests，longer funding 的 benefits（那些确实需要很多时间才能 completed 的 Projects）可以通过一连串 shorter term milestoned grants 来 achieved。这还有一个 advantage：research performers（无论是在 academic 还是 industrial context 中）可以随着 project 的 advances 接受 assessed，而他们的 funds 则可以随着每年对 needs 的 reassessed，被 partially 或 completely cut、renewed，或 increased。可以想象一种 model：即使采用这种 milestoned approach，researcher 仍可能不会 propose 某项 work，因为第一个 reasonable milestone 能否在所分配的时间内 delivered，是 uncertain 的。于是，funding length 的 problem 就变成了 milestone spacing 的 problem：也许，第一个 milestone 应该设在最初 funding 后的一年、两年或三年，这取决于 domain。

对于具体的 projects，这个 problem 或许是 solvable 的，但对于 funding people，它也是 solvable 的吗？HHMI 将自己的 funding milestone threshold 设在成为 HHMI investigator 后的[七](https://www.hhmi.org/programs/biomedical-research/investigator-program/review)年。HHMI 通常不会在 Investigator 进入 program 一年后就 cut 其 funding，但 DARPA 对一个 project 却很可能这样做。这是因为 DARPA 会 impose 必须 met 的具体 milestones，而 HHMI 会进行一种宽泛的“展示你的 work”的 assessment，只要求事先提交一份宽泛的 research program proposal，并不 detail 什么事情会在什么时候完成。这对于 fund science 来说听起来是合理的（不进行持续不断的 assessing），但一定得是七年吗？对于具体细节，我们能说得更多吗？

## Empirical Evidence（实证证据）？

关于发放期限为 1,2, 10 年的 grants，或者在 milestones 得到满足或出现一些 progress 时提供 rolling grants，究竟哪一种比其他方式更好，实际上并没有多少 evidence。

Manso（[2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01688.x)），以及通过实验开展研究的 Ederer & Manso（[2012](http://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/manso/em.pdf)），在 managerial context 中讨论 fostering innovation 时表示，_optimal innovation‐motivating incentive scheme 对 early failure exhibits substantial tolerance（甚至是 reward），并对 long‐term success 提供 reward。此外，对 long‐term compensation plan 的 commitment、job security，以及针对 performance 的 timely feedback，对于 motivate innovation 都是 essential_，并用一个 formal model 展示了这一点；这个 model 本质上是对我前述评论的 restatement，只不过用上了 math。这个 model 不允许存在 partial disclosure（milestones）的可能性；也就是说，它认为，对 researcher 或 manager 的 judged 将 solely 依据 final output 的 value，而不是像 real world setting 中那样，依据关于 process 的 interim reports 或 ongoing results。他认为，对于 exploratory research，不能仅仅为一个在开始时 uncertain 或 totally unknown 的 outcome 付费，这一点是对的，但这并不意味着不能通过其他方式对 agent 进行 assessed。即便是在 funding 某个 obscure math problem 的 solution，而一年后 Dr. X 只有围绕 problem of interest 的一堆 excursions，却没有 solution，做过类似事情的 peers 也能判断 Dr. X 是否在做正确的事。也许，他们甚至会把他 failing 得不够多视为一个 red flag！

当然，这 assumes 可以对完成 project 所需 total amount of time 的 uncertainty 设定一些 bounds。Dr. X 可能会说，对这个 problem 进行 thorough exploration 需要 5 年，那么在这种情况下，2.5 年后进行一次 midterm assessment 可能是 ideal 的。

从这个例子中 Dr. X 的角度看，假设他自己大致知道需要多长时间，以及自己将以什么方式被 assessed，那么他就没有不做自己想做之事的 incentive：他会想要 tackle hard problem Y，并提出一系列 tactics Z，预计耗时 W。

Dr. X 可能被 deterred 的情况，是其他人完全无法理解他在做什么。他说——我准备围绕 Y 这个 topic 做一些 research，每年需要这么多钱

——好，这可以安排

——但我不知道自己会找到什么

——嗯，好吧，毕竟是 blue skies research 嘛

——也不知道什么时候能完成

——没问题，我们可以给你安排 rolling grant，只会进行一些 interim evaluations

——~但只有上帝能评判我，而我是无神论者，所以其实谁都不能~，我很可能直到完成时才有东西可供展示，所以你们很可能无法公平地了解我的 progress

——怎么会呢？

——我只会四处摸索，也许会在第 1 年，也许在第 10 年，找到一个有趣的 problem 来 solve，谁知道呢

——呃……但我们可以看看你是怎么摸索的，判断你的摸索方式是否正确，这种事以前也有人做过，我们本来就预期你会摸索，我们在 funding blue skies research，也知道它是怎么回事！

——嗯，看来你也许是对的

你能看出问题所在。即便是 blue skies research，也有正在做的事情，可以展示给 external reviewer，说明是否正在取得 progress。Exploratory research 有它自己的 structure，只要这一点得到 acknowledged，而不是受到 penalized 就行了\[不能仅仅因为第 1 年 PI 能展示的只有一份 literature review、一个 disconfirmed 某个 hypothesis 的 failed experiment，以及一堆读过的书，就认为其 performance 很差；这可能一直就是其 plan\]

NSF 就这个问题[询问了](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Janice_Ballou/publication/241762144_National_Science_Foundation_Report_on_Efficiency_of_Grant_Size_and_Duration_Principal_Investigator_FY_2001_Grant_Award_Survey_and_Institutional_Survey_Princeton_NJ_Mathematica_Policy_Research_Inc/links/5422c0df0cf26120b7a4709b/National-Science-Foundation-Report-on-Efficiency-of-Grant-Size-and-Duration-Principal-Investigator-FY-2001-Grant-Award-Survey-and-Institutional-Survey-Princeton-NJ-Mathematica-Policy-Research-Inc.pdf)其 funded PIs。通常 NSF grant 的期限是 3 年，而 PIs 希望平均达到 5 年，更接近 NSF \[也许他们只是看着 NIH，希望向它看齐\]。96% 的受访 PIs 认为，这种 increase 应当会提高 pursue high-risk ideas 的能力；这可以成为对该 hypothesis 的一次有趣的 test，看看他们是否正确。其中一位这样说道：

> 正如我之前提到的，我对 NSF 的主要顾虑是 grants 的 funding 存在 3 年 cap。这个 3 年 cap 不足以让一个 student 完成 project。即便 project 得到 renewed，renewal 也往往无法及时到位，无法 support 在原 project 上工作的 graduate student 的 stipend。因此，student 不得不做 TA、寻找 scholarship，或者干脆从其他 grants 借钱，才能获得完成工作并毕业所需的钱。所以，我非常赞成将 grants 延长至 4 年，而不是 3 年。

在这里我们看到，从 empirically 的角度说，设定 duration 的一个 key variable，是 project 实际上通常需要的 expected time，正如前面 theorized 的那样。当被问到\[Table IV.3\]更喜欢以下哪一项：更多钱、期限更长的 awards，还是每年更多的 awards，平均有 40% 的 Pis 选择更多钱，平均有 24% 选择期限更长的 awards。

## Conclusion（结论）

可供考察的东西并不多！这里唯一能得出的 conclusion 是，撇开不断进行 assessments 的 cost 不谈\[cost 越高，frequency 就应越低\]，assess progress 越容易\[以及 researcher 越能相信 reviewers 会公平对待自己\]，它们的 frequency 就应越高。在一个 extreme 情况下，当 funding 的是 defined projects 时，甚至可以进行 monthly assessments。

这样的 system（其中实际上没有人拥有 unconditional funding）会把 Planck 排除在外吗？（即 [Planck test](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/)）。我不这么认为；Planck 本来可以先申请一些早期 grants，做 obscure chemistry research，完成这些工作，然后逐步申请更接近他最终所做之事的 grants。他的情况并不是一个无人能够 judge 的 illegible research program，而是一系列 progressive steps，最终让他找到了一个有趣的 problem，以及一个 solution。当然，总可以说，他可能无法获得那些 grants，但当年他也同样可能无法获得 professorship。现在的问题是 applicants 非常多，因此，在钱的总额 fixed、挑选世界各地 Plancks 的手段又 imperfect 的情况下，任何 system 最终都会让一些人得不到 funded。

确实可以为期限更长的 grants 提出一个理由：花在申请它们上的时间更少！但在钱的总额 fixed 的情况下，期限更长的 grants 的代价，是 grants 的数量更少。反过来，这可能 reduce funding success rates，从而一开始就需要撰写更多 grants！如果这能因下面的原因而提高每份 grant 的 quality，那么也许可以接受：

想象一下，你需要 build 一个新的、更 cheaper 的 methylation array，然后用它 develop 新的 [methylation clocks](https://nintil.com/epigenetic-clocks/)。每件事可能都要花 5 年。但这个新 method 并不特别令人印象深刻，它只是以更低成本做同样的事；key advance 在于利用这种 cost reduction 大幅 expand training set，再将其用于后面那个新的 clock idea，而后者才是可能获得大量 citations 的东西。所以，你可以做这件事，也可以做一个耗时 5 年、没有那么好，但你确信能在这段时间内完成的 project。要做那个很酷的 project，你需要先获得一项为期五年的 grant，然后再申请另一项。你可能拿不到第二项，结果手中只剩下在 colleagues 看来并不出彩的工作。考虑到这一点，你可能决定 pursue 那个能在 5 年内展示出令人印象深刻的 results 的 project。我认为，这并不是支持普遍实行 longer term funding 的 argument，而是支持不要僵化规定 funding terms 的 argument。在 ideal scenario 中，researcher 会事先 discloses 整个 research project，并获得第一部分的 funding，然后根据那个更 cheaper 的 methylation array 接受 evaluated；如果完成交付，就保证再获得接下来 5 年的 funding。这可能是一种尚且缺失的 grant modality：research program grant，即提出一个 >5 年的 research project，并将其 broken down 为 intermediate deliverables。这有助于 fund 那些对于不了解 overall project 的 idea 的 peers 而言难以 evaluate、但可以由一开始就了解 original idea 的 panel 来 evaluated 的 projects。这可能是 HHMI 真正的独门诀窍的一部分：通过从整体上考虑其 Investigators 的 careers，而不是将其视为一连串 unrelated grant applications、每次交给不同的人 reviewed，他们就能促成这些需要 longer term funding 的 projects。

本文开头的考量 assume，有人了解所讨论的 research program；如果并非如此（而这正是大多数试图接续 grants 的 scientists 面对的现实），那么我们就处于“在 interim 难以 assess”的 projects 类别中，这有利于采用 longer term assessments。但请注意，这不是由于 project 的某个 intrinsic feature，而是因为当前 funding arrangement 的设置方式，不允许在 assessment 中利用对 researcher 整个 program 的了解（除非你是 HHMI，或负责颁发 NIH Director Pioneer's Award 及类似奖项的 committee）。

假设我们是 HHMI，正在考虑将 7 年的 funding period 改为 10 年、5 年或 4 年，同时仍为 first term review period 保留非常宽容且 risk-tolerant 的安排。我们会怎么做？在 general case 中，并没有 solid evidence 能帮助我们作出任何一方的决定；HHMI 过去发放期限为 5 [年](https://www.hhmi.org/news/hhmi-bets-big-on-19-new-investigators)的 grants，后来改成了 7 年，[理由是](https://www.hhmi.org/news/hhmi-investigator-program-opens-national-competition)“这些 scientists 中有很多人正在尝试做的事——open up 新的 areas of research——非常困难”。这是我们能找到的唯一 justification；也许他们采用了这样的 rule of thumb：“如果 NIH 给 5 年，而我们想要 fund 更有趣的东西，那么再加 2 年似乎差不多。”也可能他们有一个非常 detailed model，但我对此表示怀疑。

我想知道是否还有更多 evidence 能在这里派上用场，所以如果你有什么材料，请发给我！

## 原文

> Max Plank, who looked at thermodynamics, and it took him 20 years to reach his conclusions, that, that matter was, was quantized. (Donald [Braben](https://ideamachinespodcast.com/donald-braben))

Max Planck got his PhD around 1879, and it wouldn't be until ~1900 when he published the key papers postulating the [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_postulate) of energy, which he used to explain black body radiation (See [here](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1252829754203312128) for some context). The 1879-1900 gap are probably those 20 years that Braben has in mind. It's interestingly hard to find anything published by Planck prior to this. His 1900 papers don't reference his earlier work. The wikipedia bibliography for Planck starts in 1900. One has to search for a while for this [bibliography](https://artfiles.org/netlib.org/bibnet/authors/p/planck-max.pdf) where we can find (pg. 140 and onwards) that he was indeed publishing papers, including a book, but it seems those papers were not particularly well regarded, most of that work has not even been translated to English \[Unlike say, Einstein's Annus Mirabilis papers, or Planck's own 1900 Nobel-winning papers\] and it may be a reasonable assumption that they were relatively inconsequential. This would fit with Braben's point: Planck wasn't a stellar academic until his 1900 paper, indeed taking 20 years to achieve an outstanding result. Had Planck been judged on his pre-1900 results he might have been kicked out of the system. Or would he?

> thanks to strong backing from Helmholtz—then widely dubbed as the “Reich Chancellor of Science”—on whom Planck’s originality had clearly made a strong impression, he was appointed professor of theoretical physics at the University of Berlin in 1888 at the still remarkably young age of 30. He stayed there until he retired almost half a century later. ([Braben](https://ipfs.io/ipfs/bafykbzacedvhurmspj7f7yjasy3p4cvmfr4gzcphd5yvpwi6zabsulhs4fym2?filename=Donald%20W.%20Braben%20-%20Promoting%20the%20Planck%20Club_%20How%20defiant%20youth%2C%20irreverent%20researchers%20and%20liberated%20universities%20can%20foster%20prosperity%20indefinitely-Wiley%20%282014%29.pdf))

Whereas Braben frames the story of Planck as the quest of a man for truth that no one could know where it led him to (And that the modern grant system would not have been able to support), I see an alternate story where modern Planck would have been able to do his research just fine, either working on the problem of black body radiation directly, or getting funding for something else (Like his pre-1900 chemistry work) and then working on what he actually wanted to work on anyway on the side, which suboptimal as it is, would have still lead to the same result. Planck did not spend 20 years working on one problem, rather so it happened to take him 20 years to find a problem that would happen to be extremely relevant; at the same time, in the period leading up to that he knew what he could be doing, and could have explained so to a peer review panel.

## Funding for longer

The _Funding people_ approach tends to, seemingly, be associated with the idea of _funding for longer_. The HHMI and [NIH director's award](https://nintil.com/hhmi-and-nih/) get funded for longer than the usual R01-funded NIH investigator.

But there is no necessary connection between the philosophy of choosing someone that you expect will be successful and that of funding that person for long. You could fund people, for a year to do whatever. Or you could fund a project for 20 years without asking for interim reports.

Indeed, [Braben's](https://ipfs.io/ipfs/bafykbzaceagsnks3nwmq7fe7vlg6deb25digxro4tj45l7e6zc7kxaj7sguuy?filename=Braben%20D.W.%20%20-%20Scientific%20Freedom_%20The%20Elixir%20of%20Civilization%20%282008%29.pdf) own funding scheme, Venture Research did not seem particularly focused on funding for long, some projects ran for 3 years, others for 10. For the HHMI/NIH cases, the researchers that do get it are more successful, but in my previous post I showed that it's not trivial to disentangle if this is because the researchers are great to begin with or because the award empowers them to do great things. Moreover, even if we knew that it's the latter, we wouldn't know if it is the amount of the award or the prospect of longer term funding that is doing the work here.

Funding for shorter has its advantages: it allows funders to more rapidly redeploy funding to new proposals if needed. But at the same time it can by its nature incentivize a different kind of work that would otherwise be done under longer funding setups. Say Dr. X promises to do Very Important Science, but that it will take him 20 years to do so, and moreover that it will all not seem very exciting up until the end of those 20 years, when it will all make sense and the results obtained will surely earn him the Nobel Prize. Would you fund Dr. X? Maybe not, perhaps if Dr. X insists that he can't be evaluated at all during those 20 years, which induces some suspicion. He's saying that his research plans are not legible, and moreover that there are no intermediate steps that can be used to assess the quality of the final product. This is highly unusual: If Dr. X says he wants to prove that relativity is fake news and that he intends to do so by building a new measurement device and then doing some measurements, the interpretation of which contradicts relativity, the construction of the device can be reported periodically. Prototypes can be shown, and unpromising avenues can be highlighted in periodic reports. If all he wants to do is to do theory, then still intermediate results, and failed approaches can be presented during milestone reviews.

The Human Genome Project took over a [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project) but produced all [sorts](https://www.genome.gov/news/news-release/the-Human-Genome-Project-turns-the-big-3-0) of intermediate results. Apollo took somewhat [less than a decade](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1vN5cHkyPLRmjE2BSEElKuzyAm9p8jFU-2gEnlcLAikg/edit#gid=0) from the initial feasibility studies to Apollo 11 but the project was already demoing rockets [less than two years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I_SA-1) after. ARPA projects can run for as little as three years ([Azoulay](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/699933) et al., 2019) These are just illustrative examples of a general rule: There are no examples that come to mind of projects that took a long time but that could not be assessed as they progressed.

To me this suggests that the benefits of longer funding (Projects that do need a lot of time to be completed) can be achieved with a string of shorter term milestoned grants. This also has the advantage that research performers (Be it in an academic or industrial context) can be assessed as the project advances, and their funds can be cut partially, completely, renewed, or increased as needs are reassessed each year. It's possible to imagine a model where even with this milestoned approach, a researcher may not propose some piece of work because it's uncertain whether the first reasonable milestone can be delivered within the time allocated. The problem of funding length then becomes the problem of milestone spacing: Maybe the first milestone should be a year or two or three after initial funding, something that is domain dependent.

This problem may be solvable for concrete projects, but is it solvable for funding people? HHMI has set their own funding milestone threshold [seven](https://www.hhmi.org/programs/biomedical-research/investigator-program/review) years after becoming an HHMI investigator. HHMI will generally not cut an Investigator a year into the program, but DARPA very well might do so with a project. This is because DARPA will impose specific milestones to be met whereas HHMI will do a broad "Show your work" assessment with just a broad research program proposal upfront that doesn't detail what will be done by when. This sounds reasonable to fund science (Not assessing constantly), but does it have to be seven years? Can we say more about the specifics?

## Empirical Evidence?

There isn't really much evidence on whether giving out grants for 1,2, 10 years, or rolling grants if milestones are met or some progress is showed are better than any others.

Manso ([2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01688.x)), and experimentally Ederer & Manso ([2012](http://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/manso/em.pdf)) in the context of fostering innovation in a managerial context, say that _the optimal innovation‐motivating incentive scheme exhibits substantial tolerance (or even reward) for early failure and reward for long‐term success. Moreover, commitment to a long‐term compensation plan, job security, and timely feedback on performance are essential to motivate innovation_, and shows this using a formal model which is essentially a restatement of my remarks above, but with math. The model does not allow for the possibility of partial disclosure (milestones); that is it considers that a researcher or manager will be judged on the value of the final output solely and not on interim reports on the process, or ongoing results, as would be the case in a real world setting. He is right that for exploratory research one can't just pay for an outcome that is uncertain or totally unknown at the start, but that doesn't mean that the agent can't be assessed through other means. Even when funding the solution to some obscure math problem, and after a year Dr. X only has a bunch of excursions around the problem of interest but not a solution, peers that have done something similar could tell if Dr. X is doing the right thing. Maybe they could even see it as a red flag that he is not failing enough!

Of course, this assumes that one can put some bounds over the uncertainty of the total amount of time required to finish the project. Dr. X may say that 5 years are required for a thorough exploration of the problem, so in that case a midterm assessment after 2.5 years may be ideal.

From the point of view of Dr. X in this example, assuming that privately he knows more or less how long it will take, and in which kind of way he'll be assessed then he does not have an incentive not to do what he wants to do: He'll want to tackle hard problem Y proposing a series of tactics Z to take an estimated time W.

The situation where Dr. X may be deterred is when what he's up to is completely illegible to others. He says —I'm going to do some research around the topic of Y and I need this much money per year

—Ok, can work with that

—But I don't know what I'll find

—Mmmkay, blue skies research and all

—Or when will I finish

—Yeah, we can set you up with a rolling grant, we'll just have some interim evaluations

—~But only God can judge me, and I'm an atheist so no one can really~, I probably won't have anything to show until completion so you probably won't get a fair view of my progress

—How come?

—I'll just be fumbling around and maybe I'll find an interesting problem to solve on year 1 or on year 10, who knows

—Umm... But we can examine your fumbling and see if you are fumbling in the right way, this is something people have done before, we expect you to fumble, we are funding blue skies research and we know how it works!

—Yeah, you may be right after all

You can see the problem. Even on blue skies research there's something that is being done and can be shown to an external reviewer to show that progress is being made or not. Exploratory research has a structure of its own, and as long as that is acknowledged and not penalized \[Just because on year 1 all a PI has to show is a literature review, a failed experiment that disconfirmed a hypothesis, and a bunch of read books doesn't mean their performance is bad, that may have been their plan all along\]

The NSF [asked](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Janice_Ballou/publication/241762144_National_Science_Foundation_Report_on_Efficiency_of_Grant_Size_and_Duration_Principal_Investigator_FY_2001_Grant_Award_Survey_and_Institutional_Survey_Princeton_NJ_Mathematica_Policy_Research_Inc/links/5422c0df0cf26120b7a4709b/National-Science-Foundation-Report-on-Efficiency-of-Grant-Size-and-Duration-Principal-Investigator-FY-2001-Grant-Award-Survey-and-Institutional-Survey-Princeton-NJ-Mathematica-Policy-Research-Inc.pdf) their funded PIs about the question. The usual NSF grant is for 3 years and PIs would like an average of 5, closer to the NSF \[perhaps they are just looking at the NIH and going for that\]. 96% of surveyed PIs think that this increase should lead to an increase ability to pursue high-risk ideas which would make for an interesting test of the hypothesis to see if they are right. Here's what one of them says:

> My main concern with NSF is the 3 year cap on funding for grants as I mentioned before This 3 year cap is not enough to have a student finish a project. Even if the project is renewed, then the renewal often does not come in time to support a graduate student’s stipend working on the original project. As a result, the student has to TA or find a scholarship or just borrow from other grants for obtaining money to finish and graduate. Consequently, I would be very much in favor of extending grants to 4 years rather than 3.

Here we see that a key variable to set duration is, empirically, the expected time a project as a matter of fact tends to take, as theorized. When asked \[Table IV.3\] whether they prefer one of: more money, longer awards, or more awards per year, on average 40% of Pis chooses more money, with 24% on average choosing the longer awards.

## Conclusion

There wasn't much to look at! The only thing that can conclude here is that setting aside the cost of doing constant assessments \[the costlier, the less frequent\], their frequency should increase the easier it is to assess progress \[and the more trust the researcher can put in that the reviewers will be fair\]. In one extreme, when funding defined projects, one could even do monthly assessments.

Would such a system (Where effectively no one has unconditional funding) have excluded Planck? (The [Planck test](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/)). I don't think so; Planck would have applied for some early grants to do obscure chemistry research, done that, then progressively apply for grants that get closer to what he ended up doing. His was not a case of an illegible research program that no one could judge, it was a series of progressive steps where he ended up finding an interesting problem, and a solution. It can always be said that he may have failed to secure those grants but the same is true of failing to secure a professorship back then, the problem now is that there are lots of applicants, so given a fixed pot of money and imperfect means of picking up the Plancks of the world, any system is going to end up leaving some unfunded.

There is a case that could be made for grants that run for longer: Less time applying for them! But longer grants, for a fixed pot of money, come at the cost of fewer grants. In turn, that could reduce funding success rates, requiring for more grants to be written in the first place! This may be okay if this causes the quality of each grant to increase because of the following:

Imagine you need to build a new cheaper methylation array that you will then use to develop new [methylation clocks](https://nintil.com/epigenetic-clocks/). Doing each thing may take 5 years. But the new method is not particularly impressive, it just does the same thing but cheaper, the key advance is using that cost reduction to vastly expand the training set that is then used for the latter new clock idea, which is the one that may get tons of citations. So you could do that or you could do a project that runs for 5 years that is less good but that you are sure can be done in that time. To do the cool project you need to get one grant for five years, and then apply for another one. You might not get the second one, leaving you with work that doesn't look impressive to your colleagues. In view of this you may decide to pursue the project that can show impressive results in 5 years. I see this not as an argument for generalized longer term funding, but for not being rigid with funding terms. In the ideal scenario, the researcher discloses the entire research project upfront and would secure funding for the first part, then be evaluated on the cheaper methylation array and then be guaranteed the next 5 years if that's delivered. This may be a missing grant modality: A research program grant, where a >5 years research project is proposed, broken down into intermediate deliverables. This helps fund projects that are hard to evaluate by peers that are not aware of the overall project's idea, but that can be evaluated by a panel that was initially informed of the original idea. This may be part of the actual secret sauce of HHMI, by considering their Investigators' careers broadly rather than as a string of unrelated grant applications, reviewed by different people each time, they can enable these projects that require longer term funding.

The considerations at the start of this article assume that someone is aware of the research program in question; if that's not the case (And this is the reality for most scientists trying to string together grants) then we are in the "hard to assess in the interim" category of projects which favors longer term assessments. But note that this is not due to a intrinsic feature of the project, but due to the fact that the way the current funding arrangement is set up does not allow for knowledge of a researcher's whole program to be used for their assessment (Unless you are the HHMI or the committee that awards the NIH Director Pioneer's Award or similar).

Suppose that we were the HHMI and were considering a change of the 7 year funding period for 10 or 5 or 4, again with a very generous and risk-tolerant first term review period. What would we do? There is no solid evidence there to decide either way in the general case; HHMI used to give grants for 5 [years](https://www.hhmi.org/news/hhmi-bets-big-on-19-new-investigators) and then they switched to 7 on the grounds [that](https://www.hhmi.org/news/hhmi-investigator-program-opens-national-competition) "what many of these scientists are trying to do – open up new areas of research – is very difficult". That's the only justification that we can find; perhaps they applied a rule of thumb like "If NIH does 5 and we are trying to fund more interesting stuff then 2 more years seems about right". Or maybe they had a very detailed model but I doubt it.

I'd like to know if there's more evidence that could be useful here, so if you have something please send it my way!
