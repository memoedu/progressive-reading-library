# 那些 breakthroughs 和 great scientists 在哪里

---

## 正文

在 science policy 界，一个经常听到的 term 是“high-risk, high-reward”活动；也就是 funding 那些单独来看很可能 fail、但也有潜力带来真正 radical、breakthrough discoveries 的 initiatives、projects 或 researchers。不过，“low-risk, high-reward”又如何呢？那岂不是很美好？

Stripe Press 又一本编辑精美的大部头，_Scientific Freedom: the elixir of civilization_，既是 Donald Braben 为这类 scientific opportunities 的存在提出的 theoretical case，也是他亲自运营 funding program——Venture Research (VR)——的经历记录，证明作者并不只是在对我们空谈。我不会细说 VR 是如何诞生的，而会着重介绍 theory，并讨论几个他们 funded 的 projects 的 examples。

这本书本身符合那句老话：大多数书都应该写成博文；虽然颇有见地，但其中许多内容本可以大幅压缩，唯独详细介绍各位 Venture Researchers (VRs) 的 projects 的最后一章除外。因此，如果你还没有读过这本书，可以先读这份 [RAND report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR506.html)，再看 Braben 本人的[这些 slides](https://dg.dk/filer/20_aars_jubilaeum/Donald_Braben.pdf)，就能了解大意，~除非你非得强迫自己收齐 Stripe Press 所有又大又漂亮的书~。

那么，这本书说了些什么？

1.  真正从事 groundbreaking research 的 researchers 数量很少，就像一个俱乐部。
    1.  一个历史上的 example 是作者称为 Plank Club 的那群各不相同的 scientists（举几个名字：Planck、Einstein、Brenner、Bohr 或 McClintock）
    2.  Braben 认为，20th century 真正 innovative 的 researchers 大约有 300 位，其他人所做的都只是 incremental work
2.  这种 groundbreaking research 需要特定的 environment 才能开展：让 researchers 在很长一段时间内（multi-year），不受任何 mandate 限制，享有按自己意愿 research 的 freedom，同时几乎无需承担 accountability
    1.  例如，Peter Higgs 认为，自己在目前的 system 下会是 [unhireable](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2013/dec/06/peter-higgs-boson-academic-system) 的；[Wilczek](https://www.livemint.com/science/news/could-einstein-get-published-today-11601014633853.html) 和 [Philip Ball](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2016/feb/12/einstein-gravitational-waves-physics) 认为，Einstein 也可能如此；[David Deutsch](https://twitter.com/patrickc/status/1309584692643950592) 对自己的看法也是这样。
    2.  书中还给出了另外几个 examples，molecular biology 的 pioneer Sydney Brenner 在他的 [Nobel Prize address](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/brenner-lecture.pdf) 中说过：_这样的 longterm research 如今已经无法进行了，因为所有人都只想要 assured short term results，没有人愿意 gamble。Innovation 只来自对 unknown 的 assault。_
3.  随着现代 post-WWII research system 的兴起（或者是 post 1970s？），这种 environment 已经消失了；这一 system 的特征是 research enterprise 的 massification，以及 publish-or-perish culture
    1.  或者，用作者的话来说，目前的 system 无法通过“Planck test”：它会 reject Max Planck 等人。
4.  这导致了自 1970s 以来 scientific progress 的 slowdown
5.  这种局面简直就是把万亿美元的钞票丢在 science 的人行道上，因为只需向一个假想的 Planck Club V2 中少数精心挑选的 members 提供 open-ended grant，就可以 restore pre-1970s conditions，让 knowledge 的泉流再次喷涌而出
6.  Venture Research (VR) 的经历是一项 practical demonstration，表明事实确实如此：一个仅有少量 resources 的 private funder，也能 achieve outsized knowledge returns；甚至可能还有 financial returns。

Venture Research 本身作为一项 funding initiative，面向的是 successful researchers，或正在 proposing research、且这些 research 有潜力 altering 他们所在的 field、甚至彻底 creating 新 fields 的 researchers。因此，我们在本书的 Chapter 7 中会发现，有些 VRs 在开始时就已经获得 Nobel prizes，或者是像 Edsger Dijkstra 那样广受赞誉的 professors。在决定是否 fund 这些 VRs 时，他们会被问到这样一些问题：

-   如果你有 infinite resources 和 freedom，你会做哪些现在没有做的事？
-   所 proposed 的 research 是否可能 radically change perceptions？
-   你的 research 是否可能被 recommended for a Nobel prize？

这些问题可不太像 NIH R01 grantees 会被问到的问题！

Braben 不喜欢 peer review（我的看法也一样），但既然需要一种 mechanism，在 competing proposals 之间 allocate funding，他就必须作出选择，而他选择了 Braben-review。除了一些手续以外，他似乎拥有充分的 discretion 来 allocate 这些 grants，这类似于 Tyler Cowen 在 Emergent Ventures grants 中扮演的角色。这种做法可能奏效的原因，或许在于他缺乏 deep domain expertise（他深入了解的基本上只有自己的 field，而这有利于 avoid consensus thinking），并且没有 conflict of interest（他不是 potential recipient；即使 grantee successful，他也不会因此 lose relative status，不像一个同时也是待考察 researcher 的 competitor 的 peer）。当然，这种 mechanism 也可能 introduce false positives；一个可能的 case 是 Stan Clough 和 Tony Horsewill 在 1989-1992 进行的 research project（p. 171），Braben 到 2007 年时仍称其为“physics 的 development 中，即使算不上 revolution，也是一座 significant milestone”。只需指出，十多年过去，他们的 work 仍然相对不为人知，除了这篇 [blog](https://sciencetimedistance.blog/)，我也没能找到多少相关内容（对于 basic research，人们总可以说，它得到认可需要时间，但这毕竟已经过了好几年）。不过，也可以说，一个 field 越是 stagnant——而 [fundamental physics](https://nintil.com/new-physics) 无疑就是如此——就越应该有兴趣寻找那些狂野古怪的 science works，希望让这个 field 的 balance 不再偏向 exploiting 贫瘠的 research grounds，而转向 exploring alternatives；正如我在上面的链接中提到的，我也把 Quantized Inertia 和各种 warp drive proposals 归入这一类；physics community 普遍反对这两者，认为它们显然方向错误。不过，它们是例外，书中最后一章介绍的大多数 proposals 似乎都取得了一些进展。

\[1\]. Braben 说，其中大多数都是 successful 的，但要真正相信这一点，还需要一项 impartial、careful assessment

VR 是否属于“low-risk, high-reward”research，取决于 comparison group 是什么，以及怎样才算 success[1](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/#sidenote-1) 或 fail。比如说，拿 Y Combinator 来看，他们承担了多少 risk？粗略 [Eyeballing](https://medium.com/datadriveninvestor/a-fifth-of-yc-startups-fail-data-analysis-1f909d0b4a64) 一下，早期 cohorts 的 failure rate 可能是 50%。这是 high risk 吗？还是 Low risk？无论如何，VR model 确实表明，funding 那些其他所有人都 rejected 的 proposals（Braben 明确认可，充当 funder of last resort 是他们明确追求的目标），总体而言可以带来 successful research。

至于 science 整体的 decline，这本书中的 quantitative evidence 很少，anecdote 却很丰富。认为 science 正处于 crisis 的想法时隐时现；早在 1830 年，Charles [Babbage](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4024987?seq=1) 就写过同样的问题，认为当时存在 decline。Assessing Babbage 是否正确以及正确到什么程度，或者 Braben 是否正确以及正确到什么程度（如果正确，究竟是什么 changes 导致 science 先 decline，再 revival，然后又 decline），是一项复杂的工作，我个人对此很感兴趣，也曾希望在这里学到一些新东西，但并没有。

全书穿插着一些有趣的讨论，涉及若干花了二十多年才完成的 substantial discoveries 的具体 cases，其中之一是 [Perutz's](https://www2.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/achievements/lmb-nobel-prizes/1962-john-kendrew-max-perutz/) 对 hemoglobin 的 structure 的 determination。花了这么长时间的 Achievements，让人不禁追问原因：只是在一个 direction 上缓慢地苦干吗？还是有很多 false starts？如果看这个[具体的](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5571795/) case，原因似乎在于缺少好的 tooling，而这些 tooling 必须随着时间逐步 built 起来（用于 protein crystallography）。

这会是一件以后值得做的有趣事情：看看那些耗时很长的 projects，弄清楚原因（以及它们是否本可以少花一些时间）

## 那些 breakthroughs 和 great scientists 在哪里

读这本书时，我注意到，虽然我听说过 Planck Club 的大多数 members（抱歉，Charles H Townes），但除了 Dijkstra，我从未听说过任何 VRs（即便那次，award 也不是授予他本人，而是授予他的朋友兼 student Netty van Gasteren）。

因为 VR 没有产生像 Einstein 那样广为人知的名字，就说它 failed，并不公平；但我认不出其中任何名字这一事实，让我想到，这或许与你不知道大多数较新的 Nobel Prizewinners 是谁，或无法立即回答“[Darwin 在世的 successor 是谁](https://nintil.com/darwin-level-insights/)”的原因相同。

借用 William Gibson 的说法，答案是：great ideas 和 breakthroughs 就在这里，只不过它们是 _evenly_ distributed 的。过去由 single researcher 就能迈出的 large、easily recognizable steps（例如 Einstein 的 relativity，当然也应给予 Poincaré、Lorentz 等人应有的 credit），如今已被 salami-sliced 到越来越多的 researchers 之间；就连 [Higgs Boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson#Naming)，在某种意义上也只是 Englert–Brout–Higgs–Guralnik–Hagen–Kibble boson 的 shorthand，而不是 Peter Higgs 的 sole theorising。

Scientific freedom 之所以是 necessary，与 economic rights（start a business、以 arbitrary ways 对其进行 organize 的 right）之所以是 necessary，原因相同：因为 incumbents 并不总能明显看出什么会 successful，所以 challengers 必须能够 circumvent 他们。这是程度问题：当前 scientific funding 的格局也为 fringe science 留有空间：[Mike McCulloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantized_inertia) 获得了 DARPA 的 funding，直到不久前，[Harold White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_G._White) 仍受雇于 NASA，[Salvatore Cezar Pais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvatore_Pais) 则受雇于 US Navy。我们对他人 thoughts 的 Judgment 往往借助 heuristics：如果某件事在我们听来显然是 false，我们就懒得花大量时间尝试理解它，因为我们预计，所花的时间无法抵偿它是 right 的微小 expected probability。[想想 homeopathy](https://nintil.com/on-the-express-acceptance-and-rejection-of-beliefs/)。

另一方面，另一端（mission-oriented research、industrial research 或 [FROs](https://www.dayoneproject.org/post/focused-research-organizations-to-accelerate-science-technology-and-medicine)）也同样是 necessary。Science 不能只是 making discoveries，这些 discoveries 也应该得到妥善利用，而且常见的情况可能是，basic research 已经完成，却没有人出面收集其中的零散成果，拼凑成一项新的 invention。也可能是一开始就需要 engineering work，才能使那项 basic research 得以开展。

在这一语境下，Braben 的意思应当理解为：我们在前者上做得太少，少到即使只是 marginal increases，也仍然可能是 impactful。我不知道他是否进一步主张，所有 science 都应该是 free。

## Planck Club（普朗克俱乐部）

这本书认为，在全部 scientists 中，只有极少数对 opening up 新的 fields 和 challenging dogmas 起着重要作用。应该如何 identified 他们？从书中来看，一个明确的方法是：如果某人获得了 Nobel 或某种类似的 field-specific high prestige award，就将其视为这个 club 的一员。然后，你可以给他们终身优厚的 salary，并免除他们任何明确的 duties。不过，这样做究竟会带来什么，还不完全清楚，因为确实，从理论上说，这可以让那些已经 proven 自己 genius 的 Planck Club members 继续 pursue 其他 projects，而不必解释原因，而且至少有一位 VRs 符合这一 pattern，即 Nobelist Dudley Herschbach。但并不是每位 scientist 在获得 Nobel Prize 后都会做 interesting research，甚至并不是每个人还会做 research。众所周知，Peter Higgs 在获得 2013 年 Nobel 后，[没有 published 任何东西](https://www.ph.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/images/Peter%20Ware%20Higgs%20CV_190610.pdf)。他很可能正在慢慢酝酿一些惊人的 results，或者要求他做到这一点（更多 publications）本就不公平（他已经 91 岁了）；但也可能是 Nobel status（谁会解雇他？）以及随之 reduced 的 think 和 publish 的 pressure，意味着 reduced 的 output，或者像这里的情况一样，output 为 null。

这一 argument 的 general form，就是针对 scientific freedom 的 standard critique：如果没有人要求 scientists 保持 accountable，他们就会变得懒惰。归根结底，这是一个 empirical question，我预计它会得出大致这样的 results：是的，对许多、甚至可能对大多数 scientists 来说，确实如此。但对 Planck Club members 并非如此。真正的 genius 是 internally motivated 的，只要仍有 resources 和 faculties，他们就会继续工作。至少从我阅读 [Genius: The Natural History of Creativity](https://www.amazon.com/Genius-Creativity-Problems-Behavioural-Sciences/dp/0521485088) 的感受来看，似乎是这样。

但是，将 funding 仅限于那些已经 successful 的人，就无法通过 Planck test。Einstein 在 patent office 工作时，我们怎样才能发现他？Braben 也意识到了这一点，因此 VR 也向较年轻的 researchers awarded grants，依据是他们对 science 的热情，以及 Braben 自己的 individual judgement。他们采用的 process 有以下一些细节：

这看起来仍然有些 arbitrary，归根结底，核心 criterion 是“Braben 是否印象深刻？”。谁能够、谁不能够扮演这个角色？例如，对于 funding scientists，你可以提出以下想法：

-   从 Nobelists 中 Hire 你的 reviewers（你的“Brabens”，而不是 peer reviewers）。甚至可以找 3 位，只要其中一位 approves 这项 proposal，就继续推进，或者
-   随机挑选的退休 professors，或者
-   曾跨越 field lines 进行 published 的 researchers，或者
-   其 discoveries 直到几十年后才 proven useful 的 researchers，或者
-   在某些 standardized tests 中以某种方式 score 的人，或者
-   总体上有意思的人

你也可以合理地混合搭配。比如，找一位 Nobel Prize in Chemistry 获得者、一位同时也是 UFO conspiracy theorist 的 particle physicist、一位对 distributed systems 做 research 的 PhD student，再加上你最喜欢的 blogger。让他们所有人 review 每项 proposal，并通过某种 mechanism 决定哪些继续、哪些不继续。这样（作为一个 team）会更好吗？还是让这群人分别工作、每个人都拥有 final authority 会更好？

或者，我们也可以换个问题：谁不应该 awarding 这些 grants？也许是在同一个 field 工作的人（前提是，他们会过于深陷当前 paradigm），但仍可以保留他们来提供 advice。那些对相关 field 至少没有一定程度的理解、也没有足够好奇心按需进一步学习的人，同样会被 excluded。

## 让 Science 再次伟大！

我曾在推文中谈过 _[efficient nepotism](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1261387662352510978)_ 这个想法：你可以不经过任何 formal process，就做出影响他人的 decisions（比如 hiring 或 funding），利用自己对这个 individual 的 personal tacit knowledge 来 make the decision，绕过繁琐的 red tape 和那些了解更少的人的意见：靠的是 HR-person，而不是 HR-department。Science 过去就是这样，而 Braben 正在提议回到这种 allocating human and financial capital 的模式。

这本书为至少再次试行一种 Venture Research-type 的 funding mechanism 提出了有力的论证，也给我们留下了几个有趣的问题：

1.  如何挑选负责 funding 的 individual 或 team？一路向上都采用 efficient nepotism 是最好的办法吗？
2.  是否应该对某些应该或不应该获得 funding 的 fields 设置 built-in bias？
3.  作为 future accomplishment 的 predictors，youth 和 prestige 如何共同发挥作用？
4.  我们应该如何看待 funding individuals 与 funding teams 的区别？

## 原文

In the science policy world a term often heard is "high-risk, high-reward" activities; funding initiatives, projects, or researchers that individually are likely to fail but that also hold potential for truly radical, breakthrough discoveries. But what about "low-risk, high-reward", wouldn't that be nice?

One more of Stripe Press' beautifully edited tomes, _Scientific Freedom: the elixir of civilization_, is Donald Braben's theoretical case for the existence of such scientific opportunities as well an account of his own experience running a funding program—Venture Research (VR)— that proves that the author is not merely cheap-talking us. I won't go into the details of how VR came to be, focusing instead on the theory and discussing a few examples of projects they funded.

The book itself fits the adage that most books should be blogposts; while insightful, a lot of the content could have substantially compressed, with the exception of the final chapter that details individual Venture Researchers' (VRs) projects. Hence if you have not read the book you can get an idea by reading this [RAND report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR506.html) followed by [these slides](https://dg.dk/filer/20_aars_jubilaeum/Donald_Braben.pdf) from Braben himself and you'll get the gist of it ~unless you need to compulsively collect all of Stripe Press' big beautiful books~.

So what does the book say?

1.  The number of researchers that are doing truly groundbreaking research is small, akin to a club.
    1.  An historical example is the varied set of scientists the author dubs the Plank Club (Planck, Einstein, Brenner, Bohr, or McClintock to name some)
    2.  Braben puts the number at around 300 truly innovative researchers in the 20th century, with everyone else doing just incremental work
2.  This groundbreaking research requires a specific environment to enable it, affording researchers freedom to research as they wish without any mandate, for a prolonged (multi-year) period of time, with little accountability
    1.  For example Peter Higgs thinks he would be [unhireable](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2013/dec/06/peter-higgs-boson-academic-system) under the current system, [Wilczek](https://www.livemint.com/science/news/could-einstein-get-published-today-11601014633853.html) and [Philip Ball](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2016/feb/12/einstein-gravitational-waves-physics) consider that perhaps Einstein would be as well, and so does [David Deutsch](https://twitter.com/patrickc/status/1309584692643950592) about himself.
    2.  In the book itself a few more examples are given, Sydney Brenner, a pioneer in molecular biology remarked on his [Nobel Prize address](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/brenner-lecture.pdf): _Such longterm research could not be done today, when everybody is intent only on assured short term results and nobody is willing to gamble. Innovation comes only from the assault on the unknown._
3.  Such environment has been lost with the rise of the modern post-WWII research system (Or post 1970s?), characterized by the massification of the research enterprise, and the publish-or-perish culture
    1.  Or, as the author would put it, the current system would fail the "Planck test": it would reject Max Planck et al.
4.  That has led to a slowdown in scientific progress since the 1970s
5.  This scenario leaves literal trillion-dollar bills on the sidewalks of science, because with just an open-ended grant for a handful of handpicked members of a hypothetical Planck Club V2 one could restore the pre-1970s conditions and allow the stream of knowledge to gush forth once again
6.  The experience of Venture Research (VR) is a practical demonstration that this is indeed true: A private funder with a small amount of resources can achieve outsized knowledge returns; perhaps even financial returns as well.

Venture Research itself as a funding initiative was targeted at successful researchers, or researchers that were proposing research with the potential of altering the field they were working on, or even creating new fields altogether. Hence in Chapter 7 of the book we find that some of the VRs had Nobel prizes to begin with, or were widely praised professors like Edsger Dijkstra. Some of the questions that were asked to VRs when deciding whether to fund them:

-   If you had infinite resources and freedom what would you do that you are not doing now?
-   Might the proposed research radically change perceptions?
-   Might your research be recommended for a Nobel prize?

These are not quite the same questions that NIH R01 grantees get!

Braben doesn't like peer review (And I'm of the same view), but given that there is a need for a mechanism to allocate funding among competing proposals, he had to choose something, and he chose Braben-review. Some formalities aside, he seems to have had ample discretion to allocate the grants, in a way similar to the role played by Tyler Cowen in the Emergent Ventures grants. The reason this might work could be that due to his lack of deep domain expertise (He knows in depth just about his own field, and that's good to avoid consensus thinking) and lack of conflict of interest (He is not a potential recipient, nor he stands to lose relative status if the grantee is successful, unlike a peer who is also a competitor of the researcher under consideration). To be sure, this mechanism can introduce false positives as well; a potential case might be Stan Clough and Tony Horsewill's 1989-1992 research project (p. 171) that Braben as of 2007 points to as a "significant milestone if not a revolution in the development of physics". Suffice to say, their work remains relatively unknown over a decade after and I couldn't find much about it other than this [blog](https://sciencetimedistance.blog/) (With basic research one can always say that it will take time for it to be acknowledged, but it has been a few years already). Nonetheless it can be argued that the more stagnant a field is—and [fundamental physics](https://nintil.com/new-physics) definitely is—the more interest there should be in finding wild weird works of science, in the hope of tilting the balance of the field away from exploiting arid research grounds and towards exploring alternatives; it is in this category where I also put Quantized Inertia and the various warp drive proposals as I mention in the link above; both are generally opposed by the physics community as clearly wrongheaded. They are the exception though, most of the proposals described in the final chapter of the book seem to have gone somewhere.

\[1\]. Braben says most of it was successful, but one'd need an impartial careful assessment to truly believe that

Whether VR is "low-risk, high-reward" research depends on what comparison group is, and what would count as success[1](https://nintil.com/review-scientific-freedom/#sidenote-1) or fail. If you take, say, Y Combinator, how much risk are they taking? [Eyeballing](https://medium.com/datadriveninvestor/a-fifth-of-yc-startups-fail-data-analysis-1f909d0b4a64) it, the failure rate of the earlier cohorts may be 50%. Is that high risk? Low risk? In any case, the VR model does show that funding proposals that everyone else had rejected (Braben explicitly acknowledges that playing the role of funder of last resort is something they explicitly aimed for) in general can lead in successful research.

Regarding the decline of science in general, the book is scant in quantitative evidence and rich in anecdote. The belief that science is in crisis comes and goes; Charles [Babbage](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4024987?seq=1) wrote back in 1830 on this same question thinking back then there was a decline. Assessing whether Babbage was right and to what extent, or if Braben is right and to what extent (And if so, what exactly were the changes that led to science to go into decline, then revival, and then decline again) is a complex enterprise that personally interests me and I was hoping to learn something new here but I did not.

Throughout the book there are some interesting discussions of specific cases of substantial discoveries that took over two decades to accomplish, one of them [Perutz's](https://www2.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/achievements/lmb-nobel-prizes/1962-john-kendrew-max-perutz/) determination of the structure of hemoglobin. Achievements that take that long makes one wonder why; was it just slow toil in one direction? Lots of false starts? If you take this [particular](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5571795/) case, it seems to have been lack of good tooling, which had to be built over time (for protein crystallography).

That would be an interesting thing to do in the future: Look at projects that took a long time and figure out why (And if they could have taken less time)

## Where are the breakthroughs and great scientists

As I was reading through the book, I noted that while I had heard of most of the members of the Planck Club (Sorry, Charles H Townes) I had heard of none of the VRs with the exception of Dijkstra (And even then the award was not for the man himself, but for his friend and student Netty van Gasteren).

It would be unfair to say VR failed because it didn't produce a name as recognizable as Einstein's, but the fact that I couldn't recognize any of the names prompted me think that a reason why that might be is the same reason you don't know who most of the newer Nobel Prizewinners are, or why you can't think of an immediate answer to "[Who is the living successor of Darwin](https://nintil.com/darwin-level-insights/)".

The answer is that the great ideas and breakthroughs are here, it's just that they are _evenly_ distributed, to paraphrase William Gibson. What used to be large, easily recognizable steps a single researcher could take (As in Einstein's relativity, with due credit to Poincaré, Lorentz, and others), now has been salami-sliced among an increasing number of researchers; even the [Higgs Boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson#Naming) is, in some sense, a shorthand for the Englert–Brout–Higgs–Guralnik–Hagen–Kibble boson, not Peter Higgs's sole theorising.

Scientific freedom is necessary for the same reason economic rights (the right to start a business, organize it in arbitrary ways) are necessary: because what is going to be successful is not always obvious to the incumbents, and so the challengers have to be able to circumvent them. This is a matter of degree: The current landscape of scientific funding also has space for fringe science: [Mike McCulloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantized_inertia) got funding from DARPA, until recently [Harold White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_G._White) was employed at NASA, [Salvatore Cezar Pais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvatore_Pais) is employed by the US Navy. Judgment of thoughts not our own often comes via heuristics, where if something sounds obviously false to us we don't bother spending a lot of time trying to grapple with it, as we expect the time spent won't make up for a tiny expected probability of it being right. [Think of homeopathy](https://nintil.com/on-the-express-acceptance-and-rejection-of-beliefs/).

On the other hand, the other end of the spectrum (mission-oriented research, industrial research, or [FROs](https://www.dayoneproject.org/post/focused-research-organizations-to-accelerate-science-technology-and-medicine)) is necessary as well. Science can't just be making discoveries, those discoveries should also be put to good use and it can often be the case that the basic research has been done but no one has stepped in to collect bits of it and cobble together a new invention. Or it can also be that engineering work is required to enable that basic research to begin with.

In this context, Braben is to be understood as saying that we are doing too little of the former, so little that even marginal increases can still be impactful. I don't know if he goes as far as saying that all science should be free.

## The Planck Club

The book suggests that only a handful of the total number of scientists mattering for opening up new fields and challenging dogmas. How should they be identified? From the book, a clear way is considering someone part of the club if they have won a Nobel or some similar field-specific high prestige award. You could them give them a nice salary for life and free them from any explicit duties. It's not fully clear what one will get out of this though, because yes in theory this can enable those Planck Club members that have already proven their genius to pursue then other projects without having to explain why, and at least one of the VRs follows this pattern, Nobelist Dudley Herschbach. But not every scientist post-Nobel Prize does interesting research, or research at all. Peter Higgs famously has [not published anything](https://www.ph.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/images/Peter%20Ware%20Higgs%20CV_190610.pdf) after his 2013 Nobel. It could well be that he is slow-cooking some stunning results, or that it is unfair to demand that (more publications) of him (He's 91 years old), but it could also be that Nobel status (Who would fire him?) and the associated reduced pressure to think and publish means reduced, or in this case null, output.

A general form of this argument would be the standard critique against scientific freedom: That scientists will grow lazy without someone keeping them accountable. Ultimately it is an empirical question, and one that I expect will yield results along the lines of: Yes, for many, perhaps most scientists this is true. But not for Planck Club members. A true genius is internally motivated and will continue to work as long as they have the resources and faculties to do so. Or so seems to be the case from my reading of [Genius: The Natural History of Creativity](https://www.amazon.com/Genius-Creativity-Problems-Behavioural-Sciences/dp/0521485088).

But limiting funding to those already successful would fail the Planck test. How could we have spotted Einstein while he was at the patent office? Braben is also aware of this and VR awarded grants to younger researchers as well, going off their enthusiasm for the science and Braben's own individual judgement. Some details of the process they used:

Maybe someone who works in the same field (On the premise that they will be too steeped in the current paradigm), but they could be retained for advice. Someone who does not have at least a level of understanding of the relevant field and is not curious enough to learn more as needed would be excluded as well.

## Make Science Great Again!

I've tweeted about the idea of _[efficient nepotism](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1261387662352510978)_, this is when you can make decisions that affect people (hiring or funding say) without any formal process, using your personal tacit knowledge about the individual to make the decision, bypassing cumbersome red tape and the opinions of those who know less: The HR-person instead of the HR-department. Science used to be like this and Braben is proposing a return to this mode of allocating human and financial capital.

The book makes a good case for at least a second trial at a Venture Research-type of funding mechanism, and leaves us with a few interesting questions:

1.  How to pick the individual or team that will do the funding? Is it efficient nepotism all the way up the best way?
2.  Should there be a built-in bias towards certain fields that should or should not get funding?
3.  How do youth and prestige play together as predictors of future accomplishment?
4.  How should we think about funding individuals vs funding teams?
