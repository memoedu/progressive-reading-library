# Effective altruism and quantitative estimates

---

## 正文

前一阵子（因为我写博客比较慢），Zach Groff 在 Effective Altruism Forum 上[尝试](http://www.effective-altruism.com/ea/k6/collective_action_and_individual_impact_part_ii/)对 participating in collective action 的 impact 作出 quantitative estimate。

那里的评论很好地 criticizing 了 Zach 的那个 specific quantitative estimate，但我想提出一个更广泛的观点——我实际上很难想象，在这里会有什么 quantitative cost-benefit argument 能让我觉得很有说服力。问题在于，causality 太难 assess，以至于你能做的最好的事情，就是通过牵强的 counterfactuals 得到非常 wide bounds（就像 Carl Shulman 的观点：即使 Black Lives Matter protests 在十年内 eliminated 了每一起 police shooting death，也只能 save ~10k lives，而且这还是在 assuming 一个 implausibly large effect 的情况下。）但在许多情况下，你无法用这种方式得到 useful bounds，因此最后只能猜测，cost-effectiveness estimate 大概落在“每个人都应该整天做这件事”和“总比被尖棍戳眼睛强”之间的某个位置。

在这些情况下，effective altruist 倾向于进行 quantitative analysis，即使这种 analysis 是 highly speculative，也照做不误，这恐怕是只见树木，不见森林。我不想看 Zach 试着把一堆 large benefits 与 small probabilities 进行 multiply，最后得到一个很大的结果，却带着更大的 error bars；相比之下，我更希望他（以及其他 EAers）专注于能否 robustly show 出 _some_ effect。我会请他们从另一个方向入手：有什么 evidence 表明 mass collective action 到底是 works 的？好，Tea Party/rainy day study——除此之外呢？niche issues 又如何？有什么理由认为，open-borders protesters 不会像 Zach 提到的所有其他抗议者那样被人遗忘？counterfactual 是什么（没有 collective action 的 movements 表现如何）？

也许，等我们回答了那些问题，就可以更深入地探讨 quantitative estimation problem。但首先，我们需要处理 basics，并对 collective action 如何 works 获得透彻的 qualitative understanding。

## 原文

A while ago (because I’m slow at blogging) Zach Groff [attempted](http://www.effective-altruism.com/ea/k6/collective_action_and_individual_impact_part_ii/) a quantitative estimate of the impact of participating in collective action on the Effective Altruism Forum.

The comments there did a good job of criticizing Zach’s specific quantitative estimate, but I want to make a broader point—I’m actually having a hard time imagining a quantitative cost-benefit argument that I would find very convincing here. The problem is that the causality is so hard to assess that the best thing you can do is get really wide bounds via cheesy counterfactuals (a la Carl Shulman’s point that even if the Black Lives Matter protests eliminated every police shooting death for a decade, this would only save ~10k lives, and that’s assuming an implausibly large effect.) But in many cases, you can’t get useful bounds this way, and so you’re left guessing that the cost-effectiveness estimate falls somewhere between “everyone should do this all day” and “better than a poke in the eye with a sharp stick.”

Under these circumstances, the effective altruist tendency towards quantitative analysis, even when highly speculative, probably misses the forest for the trees. Rather than see Zach try to multiply a bunch of large benefits by small probabilities and end up with something big but with even bigger error bars, I’d prefer if he (and other EAers) would focus on being able to robustly show _some_ effect. I’d ask them to start from the other direction: what’s the evidence that mass collective action works at all? OK, the Tea Party/rainy day study—what else? What about niche issues? Is there any reason to think that open-borders protesters will be less forgotten than all the other ones Zach mentions? What’s the counterfactual (how well do movements without collective action do)?

Maybe once we’ve answered those questions we could get more into the quantitative estimation problem. But first we need to address the basics and get a thorough qualitative understanding of how collective action works.
