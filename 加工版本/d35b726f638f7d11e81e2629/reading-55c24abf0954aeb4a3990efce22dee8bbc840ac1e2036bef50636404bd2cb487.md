## 应付 academic hoops

借用 [Robin Hanson](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2008/09/politics-isnt-a.html) 的精神来说：“大学不是为了学习东西；大学是为了 [costly signalling](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costly_signalling_theory)。”

当然，如果你能 minimize 花在 costly signalling 上的时间，也能学到不少东西。而我在这方面做得相当不错：这学期的 grades 都达到了目标，但更重要的是，我克服了自己的 perfectionism。例如：

-   我更留意自己在 problem sets 上花了多长时间，偶尔也会放弃那些过于繁琐的 problems。

-   我意识到 Harvard 的 distribution requirements 就是个笑话（后面再详细说——我感觉自己快要忍不住吐槽了），所以我进行了 optimized，让自己的 general education course 尽可能 minimally attention-draining。

-   我开始考虑利用 advanced placement 提前跳过一个或更多学期的可能性，以防我认定，到了这个阶段，大学的主要好处就是 signalling。

（说明：我对大学其实没那么愤世嫉俗！除了是一场盛大的 signal-fest，它确实也提供了很多实际价值。）

## 学习东西

我之所以把这一项与学业上的成功分开（而不是把两者都归在“上课”下面），是因为我觉得自己在学习东西这方面做得明显更差。在 Harvard 的头两年，我能感觉到自己在 math 和 computer science 方面变得更加 technically proficient：我在 solving problems、figuring out algorithms、getting mathematical intuition 等方面都有了明显进步。这个过程似乎已经放慢了——也许我正在遇到 diminishing returns？

另一方面，我在寻找好的 humanities 或 social sciences classes 方面也一直没有进展。来到 Harvard 之前，我已经学过[一套相当扎实的 humanities curriculum](http://commschool.org/)，而我最初接触大学 humanities courses，是通过 General Education program——所以我上的 classes 大多 superficial，挤满了并不真正想来上课的学生，总体上令人失望。

回头想想，我觉得根据 Gen Eds 来 extrapolate，认为自己无法从_任何_ humanities class 中获得多少收获，是不对的。但现在我只剩三个学期了，这意味着我实在没有时间再漫无目的地寻找好的 courses。[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/review/#fn:1) 我不确定有什么好办法：尤其是，什么 courses 算“好”，似乎会因各人过去的经历、思维方式等而大相径庭。所以，核实别人的推荐需要花不少工夫。我打算在今后的学期里更努力地做这件事，但对能否成功并没有把握。

在 social science 方面，我觉得自己更有头绪；我知道至少有两门 courses 很可能让我有所收获（advanced microeconomics 和 econometrics）。不过，我仍在留意其他可能让我学到不少东西的 courses。

我正在与 [Cognito Mentoring](http://cognitomentoring.org/) 的 Jonah 和 Vipul 讨论 course choice。和他们交谈时，我还想到，如果 humanities 和 social science 目前真的并不是 universities 的 comparative advantage——与阅读 blogs、书籍等相比，lectures 并没有提供多少 value added——那么，也许我应该专注于那些大学 classes 肯定_能够_提供 value-add 的东西，也就是 procedural knowledge。（例如，我认为对我而言可能是 high-value 的 procedural subjects，包括 public speaking、improv theater、drawing、hardware 和 creative writing。）对于什么会更有价值，我还没有明确的想法——这方面我还需要再想一想。

## 写作

我决定这学期增加写作量，甚至设立了一个每周 blogging 两次的 [Beeminder](http://beeminder.com/) goal（现在大概已经很明显了，我在 finals 期间停了下来——我还有 30 页其他材料要写！）。我的 goals 是 spread effective altruist ideas，更善于想出 new non-trivial interesting ideas，以及 improve 我的 writing。

在 meme-spreading 方面，我相当成功。我在 Harvard Crimson 上写了一篇关于 effective altruism 的 [op-ed](http://www.thecrimson.com/article/2013/10/8/philosophy-is-awesome/)，我一个朋友在另一所大学的 philosophy professor 还提到了它。另外，我怀疑（虽然没有确凿证据），把 EA-related entries 发到 Facebook 上，是让我的一些朋友开始对 effective altruism 感兴趣的一个 significant factor。

在想出 new、non-trivial、interesting ideas 方面，我做得没那么好。我经常能做到三项中的两项，但我不认为这学期的任何一篇 posts 同时满足了三项。最接近的是 [A Critique of Effective Altruism](http://lesswrong.com/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/)，但我仍会把那篇 post 的大部分内容归为 derivative——只有 synthesis 是 novel，或许还有“pretending to actually try”这个说法的 coining，它引发了[一些](http://meteuphoric.wordpress.com/2013/12/22/pretend-to-really-really-try/)[讨论](http://nothingismere.com/2013/12/23/in-defense-of-actually-doing-stuff/)。（当然，它在其他 metrics 上是有用的，下面我会详细说。）

在 improving 我的 writing 方面，我基本上失败了。诚然，这是我三个 goals 中最不重要的一个，但我仍以为多写至少会有所帮助。然而，看来要让这个办法奏效，我得写得多得多。如果下学期我想在这个 goal 上加倍投入，我会更专注于 editing、reading，以及在 format 和 subject matter 上大胆尝试。不过，我觉得这不值得投入那么多时间；我的 writing 已经够用了。

大量写作也给我带来了一些意料之外的好处。它是 status 和 social reinforcement 的极佳来源。_A Critique of Effective Altruism_ 引发了一些关于 effective altruism 的有益[讨论](http://lesswrong.com/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/)（在 Less Wrong 以及其他地方）。而且，它让我能够不虚伪地鼓励朋友们多写一些，我也喜欢读朋友们写的东西。

今后，我希望更经常写一些 non-effective-altruism subjects；我更希望拥有更多样的 readership，也不想成为一个“single-issue person”（如果我还不算的话）。另外，我对“mini-posts”的 experiment（每周写更多、更短的 posts）初步看来似乎是个好主意，但我后来一直没有继续；我想对它以及其他 formats 做更多 experiment。

总体而言，我担心自己对写作用处的 assessment 存在 biased——写作是 public 的，而且有 fast feedback，所以它提供的 social reinforcement 多得 disproportionately。例如，回头看来，那篇对 effective altruism 的 critique 显然不仅受哪些 criticisms 最 damning 的影响，也受我预期哪些 criticisms 会引起别人共鸣（也就是获得最多 social reinforcement）的影响（甚至是后者取代了前者？）。我仍在寻找一个好办法；备选做法包括不再发到 Facebook、降低发布 frequency，或者干脆把写的东西留给自己看；我还不确定自己更倾向于哪一种。

## 共同运营 Harvard Effective Altruism

我和另一位 co-president John 的一项主要计划，是让 [Harvard Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/)（原名 Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy）从几个人聚在一起的小团体变成一个真正的 Student Organization。我们举办了新设的 Philanthropy Fellowship program 的第一轮，吸引了一批人申请参加一系列出色的演讲活动，并在学期末开展 fundraiser。这场 [fundraiser](http://harvardea.org/fundraisers/winter2013/) 目前正在进行，而且在吸引 new members 的兴趣（enrollment 大约增长了 500%）和提高我们在校园里的知名度方面取得了很好的成效。

为下学期的 fellowship 做准备这件事，我做得就没那么成功了。寒假期间，我得抓紧一些，把演讲嘉宾安排好，不过我觉得这不会是什么大问题。

接下来，我的主要任务是让这个团体为第一次真正的 leadership turnover 做好准备。为此，我会争取尽快卸任 co-president，好让我们开始 succession。其次，我希望下学期我们做的不只是 Philanthropy Fellowship：现在已有足够多积极投入的 members，我们也可以开始做 side projects 了。

## 交朋友（以及影响他人？）

我对自己的朋友圈相当满意，所以以前没怎么考虑过扩大它。不过，最近我决定把这件事列为优先事项，因为我意识到，相比大学，在外面的世界里，结识许多出色的人其实是_最难_做到的事情之一。这学期我在这方面取得了很大进展，但主要是靠[室友的努力](http://andrewliu.me/)，而不是我自己的。

我在维系和深化现有关系方面的尝试，也比前几个学期更成功，主要是因为我克服了自己那种荒唐的抗拒心理：没有什么由头，也可以问别人要不要一起待一会儿。不过，随着学期推进，我越来越忙（而且_想联系上 Harvard 的学生简直是不可能的_），在主动找那些平时不会见到的人相处这件事上，我做得越来越差。

在我看来，这两方面都值得投入，都是 high-return 的领域。我怀疑自己找不到多少像 Andrew 每周聚餐那样多样而有趣的其他群体，但如果能找到，那会很有价值。另一方面，在改善自己维系现有友谊的方式上，我看到了相当明确的 low-hanging fruit，希望下学期能把它们摘下来。

## 自我提升

这学期也是我第一次有机会把一些 [CFAR](http://rationality.org/) 的内容付诸实践。正如我预料的那样，收获相当大。尤其值得一提的是：

-   我重新安排了使用 email 和 Facebook 的方式，这对我一天能完成多少工作产生了出乎意料的大影响。
-   我 designed 了一套 to-do system，并开始使用它和 Beeminder，因此对各项事情的掌控好了很多。
-   我开始更多地做让自己不舒服的事，包括更积极地宣传 HEA，以及更多地向别人发送 cold-emailing 邮件（这两件事带来的收获最大）。

以后我大概会再多写一些这些事。当然，有些尝试并不那么顺利：用 CFAR 的 jargon 来说，下学期我尤其想在 offline habit training 和 againstness units 上多下些功夫。（对于没参加过 CFAR workshop 的读者，我把这些 jargon 留在这里，是给自己做个提示——等我能解释它们对我有什么用时，再解释它们是什么，免得把事情弄得太傻。）

## 维护 Harvard Class

与我这学期的大多数计划不同，maintaining [Harvard Class](http://harvard-class.com/) 几乎彻底失败了。学期开始时，Billy 和我已经快完成一次很棒的 rewrite；三个月后，我们基本上没有再往前走。我唯一做的工作，就是维持 data 的 up-to-date 所必需的最低限度工作（甚至连这点也只是勉强做到）。与此同时，_另一个_ course catalog 似乎已经超越了我们，正在重新赢回 users（尽管它们的_劣势显而易见_）。眼下，我不太有动力继续做下去。我觉得自己现在有 higher-return projects，而且如果要 coding，我宁愿别再做更多的 web-dev。话虽如此，我也许会在寒假或暑假重新捡起来，既是为了给这件事一个交代，也是因为它会成为一份不错的 portfolio piece。

## 决定自己这辈子要做什么

和任何一个有自尊心的大学生一样，我也正在反躬自问，试图弄清自己的人生使命，处于这样一个[过程](http://www.benkuhn.net/plans-i)中。写下之前那些计划后，我开始和 [80,000 Hours](http://80000hours.org/) 的 Paul Christiano 一起做一项 career advice case study，但现在暂时搁置了，要等我看看自己在 [GiveWell](http://www.givewell.org/) 的 winternship 进展如何。我也开始制定暑假和明年的计划，不过都还处于很早的阶段。总体而言，我觉得这项计划不需要调整。

## 放松

虽然这学期我确实（令人意外地）有机会放松，但最近我意识到，自己越来越不擅长放松了。我尝试做的大多数事情（玩各种 video games、浏览 Internet、看电影），最后都没让我觉得特别愉快。我 hypothesize，自己在 [confusing 我的 “wanting” 和 “liking” neural circuitry](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive_salience)，如果在放松活动中更留意自己是否真的享受这些活动，我就会更清楚怎样有效地放松。与此同时，我现在知道，“想做 X 来放松”并不怎么能说明 X 真的让人放松，所以我应该多尝试不同的事情，并留意它们的效果。

## Jumping through academic hoops

To channel the spirit of [Robin Hanson](http://www.overcomingbias.com/2008/09/politics-isnt-a.html), “college is not about learning things; college is about [costly signalling](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costly_signalling_theory).”

Of course, if you can minimize the amount of time you spend on costly signalling, you can get a good amount of learning done, too. And in this department I’m doing quite well: my semester grades are all on target, but more importantly, I was able to overcome my perfectionism. For instance:

-   I paid more attention to how long I was spending on problem sets, and occasionally punted on problems that were too tedious.

-   I realized that Harvard’s distribution requirements were a joke (about which more later—I’m feeling a rant coming on), so I optimized for making my general education course minimally attention-draining.

-   I began considering the possibility of using advanced placement to skip ahead one or more semesters, in case I decide that the main benefit of college at this point is signalling.

(Note: I’m not actually that cynical about college! In addition to being a massive signal-fest, it does provide a lot of actual value.)

## Learning things

The reason that I split this off from succeeding academically (rather than filing them both under “taking classes”) is that I feel I’m doing significantly worse at learning things. For the first two years of Harvard, I could feel myself becoming more technically proficient at math and computer science: I got noticeably better at solving problems, figuring out algorithms, getting mathematical intuition, etc. This process seems to have slowed—perhaps I’m hitting diminishing returns?

On the other hand, I haven’t been able to make progress in finding good humanities or social sciences classes, either. I came to Harvard having already taken [a fairly strong humanities curriculum](http://commschool.org/), while my first experience with college humanities courses was through the General Education program—hence the classes I took were for the most part superficial and full of students who didn’t really want to be there, and generally disappointing.

On reflection, I think it was wrong to extrapolate from Gen Eds that I wouldn’t be able to get very much out of _any_ humanities class. But I now only have three semesters left, which means I don’t really have time to flail around looking for good courses.[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/review/#fn:1) I’m not sure of a good solution to this: in particular, it seems like what courses are “good” varies widely from person to person, based on previous experience, how their brain works, etc. So it takes a fair amount of work to verify people’s recommendations. I plan to try harder at this in future semesters, but I’m not confident of success.

On the social science side, I think I have a better handle on things; I know of at least two courses that I’ll very likely get things out of (advanced microeconomics and econometrics). However, I’m still on the lookout for other courses that might teach me noticeable amounts.

I’m in the process of talking to Jonah and Vipul of [Cognito Mentoring](http://cognitomentoring.org/) about course choice. Another idea that came to me while talking to them is that, if it’s really the case that humanities and social science just aren’t universities’ comparative advantage currently—lectures just don’t give very much value added compared to reading blogs, books, etc.—then maybe I should be focusing on things where college classes definitely _are_ a value-add, namely, procedural knowledge. (For example, procedural subjects I think are plausibly high-value for me include public speaking, improv theater, drawing, hardware, and creative writing.) I don’t have strong thoughts yet what would be more valuable—I have some more thinking to do here.

## Writing

I decided to step up my writing activity this semester, even going so far as to set up a [Beeminder](http://beeminder.com/) goal for blogging twice a week (as is probably obvious by now, I stopped during finals—I had 30 pages of other material to write!). My goals were to spread effective altruist ideas, get better at coming up with new non-trivial interesting ideas, and improve my writing.

At meme-spreading I was quite successful. I wrote an [op-ed](http://www.thecrimson.com/article/2013/10/8/philosophy-is-awesome/) on effective altruism in the Harvard Crimson that got mentioned by the philosophy professor of one of my friends at a different university. Additionally, I suspect (though I don’t have hard evidence) that posting my EA-related entries on Facebook was a significant factor in getting some of my friends interested in effective altruism.

At coming up with new, non-trivial, interesting ideas, I did less well. I managed two out of three fairly often, but I wouldn’t count any of my posts this semester as all three. I came closest with [A Critique of Effective Altruism](http://lesswrong.com/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/), but I’d still categorize most of that post as derivative—only the synthesis was novel, and perhaps the coining of the phrase “pretending to actually try”, which has seen [some](http://meteuphoric.wordpress.com/2013/12/22/pretend-to-really-really-try/) [discussion](http://nothingismere.com/2013/12/23/in-defense-of-actually-doing-stuff/). (Of course, it was useful on other metrics, as I’ll go into below.)

At improving my writing, I mostly failed. Granted, this was the least important of my three goals, but I still thought that writing more would at least somewhat help. However, it seems I’d have to write a lot more for that to work. If I wanted to double down on this goal next semester, I would focus on more editing, reading, and being adventurous with format and subject matter. However, I don’t think that’s worth the time investment; my writing does the job already.

I also got a few unforseen benefits by writing a lot. It’s a great source of status and social reinforcement. _A Critique of Effective Altruism_ started some good [conversation](http://lesswrong.com/lw/j8n/a_critique_of_effective_altruism/) about effective altruism (on Less Wrong and elsewhere). And it allows me to non-hypocritically encourage my friends to write more, and I enjoy reading my friends’ writing.

Going forward, I’d like to write about non-effective-altruism subjects more frequently; I would prefer to have a more diverse readership, and would rather not become a “single-issue person” (to the extent that I’m not already). Also, my experiment with “mini-posts” (writing more and shorter posts for the week) seemed tentatively like a good idea, but I never followed up; I’d like to experiment more with that and other formats.

Overall, I worry that I’m biased in my assessment of the usefulness of writing—it’s public and has fast feedback, so it provides disproportionately much social reinforcement. For instance, in hindsight, the critique of effective altruism was clearly influenced by which criticisms I anticipated would resonate with others (i.e. would get the most social reinforcement), in addition to (instead of?) which criticisms were the most damning. I’m still looking for a good solution to this; candidates include stopping posting to Facebook, or posting at a reduced frequency, or keeping my writing to myself altogether; I’m not sure which I favor yet.

## Co-running Harvard Effective Altruism

A major plan for me and my co-president John was to take [Harvard Effective Altruism](http://harvardea.org/) (formerly Harvard High-Impact Philanthropy) from a few people hanging out to a Real Student Organization. We ran the first iteration of our new Philanthropy Fellowship program, in which we got a bunch of people to apply to attend a series of awesome speakers and do a fundraiser at the end of the semester. The [fundraiser](http://harvardea.org/fundraisers/winter2013/) is now in progress, and succeeded admirably in getting new members interested (enrollment is up something like 500%) and raising our profile on campus.

I was less successful in preparing for next semester’s iteration of the fellowship. I’m going to have to scramble a bit to get our speakers lined up over winter break, though I don’t think that’ll be a large issue.

My main mission for the future is to prepare the group for our first real leadership turnover. To that end, I’m going to try to step down as co-president ASAP so we can start the succession. Secondarily, I want us to do more than just the Philanthropy Fellowship next semester: we now have enough engaged members that we can start doing side projects as well.

## Making friends (and influencing people?)

I’m pretty content with my social group, so previously I didn’t put much thought into expanding it. However, I recently decided to make this a prioriy because I realized that getting to know lots of awesome people is actually one of the things that’s _hardest_ in the outside world relative to college. I made a lot of progress on that this semester, but mostly through my [roommate’s doing](http://andrewliu.me/), not my own.

My attempts to maintain/build existing relationships were also more successful than in previous semesters, largely because I got over my ridiculous aversion to asking people if they wanted to hang out without having an excuse. However, as the semester progressed, because I was getting busier (and because it is _absolutely impossible to get ahold of Harvard students_), I got worse at proactively spending time with people I wouldn’t otherwise see.

These both strike me as high-return areas to work on. I suspect I won’t be able to find very many other communities as varied and interesting as Andrew’s weekly dinners, but doing so would be quite valuable if I could. On the other hand, I see pretty clear low-hanging fruit for improving the ways that I maintain my existing friendships, which I hope to pick over the coming semester.

## Self-improving

This semester was also my first chance to put some [CFAR](http://rationality.org/) material into practice. As I predicted, the benefits were pretty large. Of particular interest:

-   I rearranged how I use email and Facebook, which had a surprisingly large effect on the amount of work I’m able to get done in a day.
-   I designed and started using a to-do system and Beeminder, and am vastly more on top of things as a result.
-   I started doing more things that made me uncomfortable, including advertising HEA more aggressively and cold-emailing people more (these were the two biggest wins).

I’ll probably write more about these at some point. Of course, some of my attempts didn’t go so well: in CFAR jargon, I’d particularly like to work more on the offline habit training and againstness units this coming semester. (For those who haven’t been to a CFAR workshop, I’m leaving this jargon here as a handle to myself—I’ll explain what these are once I can explain how they’re useful to me, to avoid making things too silly.)

## Relaxing

Although I did (surprisingly) get to relax this semester, I realized recently that I’ve gotten a lot worse at it. Most of the things I tried to do (playing various video games, browsing the Internet, watching a movie) ended up not being particularly enjoyable. I hypothesize that I’m [confusing my “wanting” and “liking” neural circuitry](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive_salience), and if I’m more mindful during relaxation activities of whether I actually enjoy them, I’ll have a better sense of how to relax effectively. In the mean time, I now know that “wanting to do X to relax” isn’t particularly indicative of X being relaxing, so I should try more different things and pay attention to how they work.
