# Investment（投资）

---

## 正文

\[本文是我[另一篇文章](https://nintil.com/2015/06/24/mazzucato-y-el-iphone-ii-el-mito-del-estado-emprendedor/)的译文\]

正如我们[在此前的一篇文章中](https://nintil.com/2015/08/19/mazzucato-and-the-iphone-i/)所看到的，Mariana Mazzucato 用来 validate 她的 arguments 的主要 examples 之一，就是 iPhone 的 case。让我们记住，Mazzucato 的 thesis 是，大多数 modern technology 都源于 directed governmental research efforts，不仅涉及 basic science，还涉及 technological commercialisation、direct investment、loans 等。这些 policies 构成了她所谓的 The Entrepreneurial State。我们必须明确区分这个 concept 与另一个更合理的 concept，即仅仅 invests 于 basic science 以 correct market failures 的 State。后者通常为 economic mainstream 所接受，而 Mazzucato 并不属于这一 mainstream，因为她恰恰在 arguing against 关于 State 在 innovation 中的 role 的根深蒂固的 conceptions。

在本文中，我将 criticise 她的 arguments，并 question 为 defend 它们而提出的 evidence。与此前的文章一样，她自己的 bibliography 就足以让我们 criticise 她自己的 points。我们将从 study 她在这一章中为 support 每一段而 cites 的每一条 bibliographical reference 开始，并在必要之处以额外的 references 加以补充。

这篇 critique 仅限于书中相关的一章，在这里就是讨论 iPhone example 的第五章。文章中提到的一些内容不在讨论范围内：一般意义上的 computers 和 semiconductors 的 case，以及 GPS，因为它们值得单独讨论。Mazzucato 也说它们大多来自 State，因此我不会把她的 arguments criticise 两遍。

这一章开篇解释说，Apple 的历史和 Jobsian 的“求知若饥，虚心若愚”philosophy 都被某种程度地神化了，因为按照 Mazzucato 的说法，

> Individual genius、对 design 的重视、对玩乐的热爱以及愚拙，无疑都是重要的 characteristics。但如果没有 computer 和 Internet revolutions 背后的巨额 public investment，这些 attributes 或许只会带来一件新玩具的 invention，而不是像 iPad 和 iPhone 这样改变了人们工作和交流方式的 cutting-edge revolutionary products。$$...$$ 尽管这些 products 的 beautiful design 和 slick integration 得益于 Jobs 及其庞大团队的 genius，但 iPod、iPhone 和 iPad 中几乎每一种 state-of-the-art technology，都是 government 和 military 的 research efforts 与 funding support 所取得、却经常被忽视和无视的 achievement。

接下来是关于 Apple 的 R&D expenditures 的 discussion。书中提到，它在 quantity 上一直 growing（自 1999 年以来 multiplied by 25，在过去 5 年中则 multiplied by more than 4），但并未保持为 sales revenue 的 constant percentage：按照 Mazzucato 书中的说法，随着时间推移，Apple 的 R&D spending 占 sales revenues 的 fraction 越来越小。不过，我们可以在前一个链接所指向的 graph 中看到，这一 tendency 似乎已经 reverted。这位 economist 对此给出了两种可能的 interpretations：第一，也许 Apple 对 funds 的 uses 非常 efficiently。第二，按照她引用 analyst Horace Schmidt 的说法，也是她认为更 plausibly 的 interpretation，Apple 实际上并不是一家 develops new technologies 的 company，而更像是一家 integrates new technologies 的 company。在 Schmidt 的 analysis 之上，Mazzucato 又补充道：“technologies，在许多情况下由 State finances”，借此将这一节与下一节联系起来；下一节将详细说明 State 为 Apple 做过什么。

这些 interventions 有三种 types：

1.  对 Apple 的 initial funding 和 growth 进行 Direct investment
2.  Access 源于 state research programmes、military initiatives，或由 public research institutions 开展的 technologies
3.  在困难时期 support Apple 的 Fiscal、commercial 和/或 technological policy。

## Investment（投资）

关于第一点，Mazzucato 说，Apple 有许多 private investors：Don Valentine、Arthur Rock、Venrock、Mike Markkula……但它在 1980 年 IPO 之前，也收到了 Continental Illinois Venture Corp 的资金。按照 Mazzucato 的说法，后者是一家“Small Business Investment Company (SBIC)，由 Small Business Administration authorized，以 invest 于 small businesses”。对于 investors 的名单，这位 economist cites 了 Rao and Scaruffi 2011；对于 state investment data，则 cites 了 Slater 1983 和 Audretsch 1995。我未能查阅这些 sources 中的任何一个，所以将使用其他 sources。已知 Markkula invested 了 250,000$ in 1977 and in Moritz 2009, we find that in 1978, Apple managed as much as 517,500$ 的 investments，而这一切都发生在 CIVC 进入之前。

按照 Lazonick and Mazzucato 2013 的说法，这家 CIVC 是 Continental Illinois Bank 的一个分支，曾获得 US Government 的 guaranteed loans，并于 1978 年向 Apple invested 了 500,000$。在 San Francisco Federal Reserve 的[这份 report 中](http://www.frbsf.org/community-development/files/Bennett_SBICs.pdf)，我们可以看到，SBIC 是一种 private venture capital fund，可以有——但不一定有——某种形式的 State backing。[它们也享有 lessened regulatory requirements](https://www.sba.gov/content/faqs)。[当时的 Sources](http://articles.latimes.com/1990-09-25/business/fi-1303_1_small-businesses/2) confirm 了 CIVC 的 investment，但对 SBIC concept 似乎持悲观态度。

考虑到 SBICs 的运作方式，对此更 plausible 的 interpretation 是，这并不是 Entrepreneurial State 采取的 action，而是一群 private investors（即 Continental Illinois Bank 的那些人）设法通过 SBIC programme 获得 state benefits 的 action。[我并不是第一个](http://somerandomthoughts-mythoughts.blogspot.com.es/2014/04/the-entrepreneurial-state-is-apple.html)得出这一 conclusion 的人。考虑到 private investors 的存在，以及 Apple 当时的 sales revenue 和 valuation，State 为某个 private entity 的 loans 提供 guarantees、再由该 entity 向 Apple invested 这件事的 importance，就被放回了它的历史背景之中，其 importance 也就不再那么绝对。也就是说，在 CIVC 参与之前，Apple 就已经在迅猛前进；根据我们掌握的情况，最 reasonable 的 assumption 是，即使没有 government involvement，它也会继续如此。因此，你当然可以说，Apple 在最初阶段得到了 government support，但不能说这种 support 是 crucial 的，也不能说没有它就不会有 Apple。

## Technology（技术）

按照 Mazzucato 的说法，哪些 technologies 使 iPhone、iPod 和 Ipad 与 market 中的其他产品有所区别，或者使它们能够 work？

作为 central technologies 的有 microprocessors、RAM、hard drive storage、LCD screens、Li-pol 和 Li-ion batteries、基于 Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) 的 digital signal processing (DSP)、the internet、HTTP 和 HTML protocols，以及 cellular networks。

作为让这些 devices 略显独特的 additional technologies：GPS、click wheel navigation、multitouch screens，以及一个 speech based artificial intelligence assistant：SIRI。

## Hard Drives (GMR)（硬盘）

Mazzucato 从 hard drives 说起；按照她的说法，它们使 iPod 得以取代 Sony 的 Walkman 和 Discman。这些新的 hard drives 之所以成为可能，要归功于一项使 Albert Fert 和 Peter Grünberg 在 2007 年获得 Physics Nobel Prize 的 discovery：Giant Magnetoresistence (GMR)。这项 discovery 于 1988 年 parallel 发生。也就是说，Fert 和 Grünberg 都 independently discovered 了 GMR。

这里的问题是，hard drives 的 history 并不以 GMR 的 discovery 为起点或终点。最早 apply GMR 的 disks 是[在 1997 年](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_hard_disk_drives) commercialised 的，而且第一款 iPod 中的 Toshiba disk 确实[使用了 GMR heads](http://www.funkygoods.com/schwarzschild/2013_06/MK5002MAL_MK1002.pdf)。但从 discovery 到 manufacturing 的这一步并非 trivial。一方面，Fert 和 Grünberg discovered 了 GMR effect，但随后 [IBM Research](http://www.research.ibm.com/research/gmr.html) 早在 1991 年就接过了这项 research，并将其 applies 于 hard drives；从那里到 [Toshiba](http://www.toshiba-tdmt.com.tw/english/products/article02.aspx?ccid=19)，又是另一步，可能是因为 IBM 和 Hitachi 向 Toshiba 出售了 technology，如前一个链接所述。就 discovery 本身而言，其 structure 与其他 discoveries 所遵循的相似：它们从已有的 scientific results 出发，将其推进到下一个层次。GMR 是在数千名 scientists 历经十年努力之后才被 discovered 的，并不是一个突然冒出来的巧妙念头。(Grünberg et al. 1989, Fert 2007)

Mazzucato 试图通过 McCray 2009a 论证，GMR 和 hard drives 的 history 不仅说明了 State 在 research 中的 role（大多数 economists 早已这样说），也说明了它在将 scientific ideas convert 为 manufactured、commercially viable products 所需的工作中的 role。她还说，即便促成 GMR 的 discovery 的最重要 scientific development 是 European，US government 在 commercialising 这项 technology 方面也发挥了重要 role；她的论据是，Grünberg 的 lab 与 Argonne National Laboratory 存在 affiliated 关系，而且他获得了 Department of Energy grants。此后，companies developed 了这项 technology。这些 evidence 似乎并不 support 这样一种看法：State 帮助完成了从 science 到 product 的 transition。如前所见，发挥这一 role 的是 IBM。

据 McCray 所说，magnetoresistence 这一 field 的 origin 可以 traced back 到 William "Lord Kelvin" Thomson 在 1857 年的 research，而对 electron spin 的现代 understanding（它也是 GMR 的 base）可以 traced back 到 Pauli、Dirac 和 Goudsmit 在 1920 年的工作。接下来，Bell Labs 和 IBM 的 scientists experimented with 多种 techniques 来 produce artificial materials，developing 了 molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) 等 technologies，以便进一步 build nanostructures。据 McCray 所说，MBE-based apparatuses 在 Grünberg 和 Fert 的 research 中是 crucial 的。

关于 State 在 commercialization 中的 role——这正是 Mazzucato 引用 McCray 的原因——他说，GMR 最先被 applied 于 weak magnetic field detection（由 IBM Research 的 electrical engineer James M. Daughton 完成，他后来在 1989 年 founded 了自己的 company，Nonvolatile Electronics。high temperature superconductivity 也是在 IBM Research 于 1986 年被 discovered 的）

McCray 证实了我们前面所说的：完成从 research 到 commercial development 这一 crucial 步骤的是 IBM：

> Companies 急于将 spintronics 的 nascent form apply 于 broad er 且更 lucrative 的 markets。在 GMR 的 discovery 之后，位于 California 的 San Jose 附近的 IBM’s Almaden laboratory 中的 scientists 寻找其他将其 apply 于 commercial products 的方式。与位于 New York 的 Yorktown Heights、长期开展 semiconductor logic devices 的 research 的 IBM’s Thomas J. Watson Research Center 不同，历史更久的 Almaden lab 传统上 focused on magnetic information-storage technologies。
> 
> $$...$$
> 
> Fert 和 Grünberg 用更 precise 但更慢的 molecular beam epitaxy technique built 他们的 samples，而 Parkin 的 \[IBM researcher\] group 则 adopted 了 sputter-deposition equipment。Almaden group 的 focus 并不是 basic science 本身，而是 making 能够 readily manufactured 的 devices。Parkin 使用更快、更便宜的 sputtering technique，对 IBM 而言是合理的，因为这家 company 在以 industrial scale 进行 fabricating sputter deposited magnetic-storage media 方面有丰富经验。正如一位观察 Parkin 的 research 的人士后来回忆的那样，这位 British scientist 及其同事在 made and characterized 超过 30,000 种不同的 multilayer combinations 时，“简直把 underlying GMR discovery 中的 \[脏话已删\] 都 engineered 出来了”
> 
> $$...$$
> 
> IBM 最终利用 Parkin 及其同事的 spintronics research，来 redesign 和 improve 该 company 的 hard disk drives 中的一个 basic element。The Wall Street Journal 在 November 1997 以头版报道披露了这家 company 的 innovation。基于 Almaden group 对 GMR 的 exploitation，IBM 的新 drives 配备了 exquisitely sensitive magnetic-read heads。这些 redesigned read heads 能够 store 比 competitors’ equipment 多八倍的 data，同时 size 仍然更小，为随后 computer memory 的爆发式增长创造了条件，而这又帮助音乐爱好者得以在 iPods 和类似 handheld gadgets 上 store 数 gigabytes 的音乐及其他 files。IBM 将其 GMR-based technology licensed 给其他 companies，几年之内，几乎每个 computer hard drive 都 included 一个基于 IBM 的 innovation 的 read head。Seagate Technology 等 Firms，以及一度的 IBM，都获得了巨额 profits。IBM 对 Fert 和 Grünberg 的 basic discovery 的 rapid commercialisation，将 spintronics 带入了一个每年价值数十亿美元的 market。尽管这些无处不在的 tools 并未被明确 marketed 为 nanotechnology，IBM researchers 仍可以自豪地说，由于他们的 research，“每个人的 desktop 上都有 \[一个\] spintronics device。”

当 products 已经进入 market 之后，State 才开始对它们 invest：

> 随着 exploiting GMR effect 的 products 出现在 market 上，更多 scientists 开始在后来被称为 spintronics 的领域开展 research，来自 military laboratories 和 funding agencies 的 science managers 开始注意到它。与此同时，主要报纸将 spinbased electronics 描述为一种潜在的新 industry paradigm。Journalists 的语调迎合了一种盛行的倾向：将新的 electronics technology 炒作为该 industry 的“下一个重大突破”，借此 predict Moore’s Law 的终结。

DARPA 在 1992 年 invested 于 GMR，

> 关注 nascent GMR-based spintronics 的人之一是 Stuart Wolf。Wolf 在 Rutgers University 接受了 physicist 的训练，于 1969 年获得 doctorate，最初 researched metals 中的 low-temperature superconductivity。44 在 Case Western Reserve University 待了三年之后，Wolf 成为 Naval Research Laboratory 的 staff scientist，他在那里关于 superconductors 的工作对若干 defence applications 具有吸引力。在 1993 年，Wolf 接受了 Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency 的 program manager 这一新职位。当然，DARPA 对 supporting computer 和 semiconductor applications 的 research 并不陌生；几十年来，它一直 funded molecular electronics、integrated-circuit design 和 supercomputing 等 areas 的重大 cutting-edge programs。
> 
> 在 1992 年末，作为向新的 geopolitical environment transition 的一部分，Department of Defense initiated 了 Technology Reinvestment Project (TRP)。由 DARPA Managed 的 TRP，其 purpose 是在 commercial 和 military sectors 之间 build stronger links，并帮助 United States 获得预期的 "peace dividend" 中更大的 share。TRP 出现时，IBM 和 Bell Labs 等老牌 companies 也正在削减对 basic research 的 support。在该 program 于 1996 年结束之前，TRP 向数十个 dual-use technology projects directed 了超过 \$800 million（commercial firms 还提供了 additional funds）。
> 
> TRP portfolio 中一项规模不大的努力，是试图 stimulate 基于 GMR phenomenon 的 commercial innovations。在 1995 年，凭借 DARPA 最初提供的 \$5 million 和 matching industry funds，Wolf initiated 了 GMR Consortium Project。在这个 program 中，Honeywell 等 large companies 和 Nonvolatile Electronics 等较新的 firms，与 Wolf 在 Naval Research Laboratory 的前同事 partnered，来 develop 一种新的 computer memory

Wolf 是如何让 State 接受他的 GMR Initiative 的？他把一个 40 pound 的 satellite memory 往 DARPA 的 director 桌上一放，说自己用一个 fifty cent chip 就能做到同样的事。这并不是 DARPA 的高瞻远瞩之举，他们只是看到了一个对自己想法的成功充满信心的人。

> Wolf 从一个 satellite system 中取出一个旧 memory component，把它带到 DARPA 办公室，以此推销自己的 program。“我把它往 director 的桌上一放，”Wolf 回忆道。“它重 forty pounds，cost 是 a quarter of a million dollars。我说：‘我要用一个 fifty-cent chip 来 replace 它。’”Wolf 规模不大的 program 很快 expanded 为 DARPA 最初称作 Magnetic Materials and Devices Project 的项目。Wolf 对这个名称不满意，提出了一个新名字——SPin TRansport electrONICS——并将其 shortened 为“SPINTRONICS”。DARPA 最终提供了约 \$100 million 来 support Wolf 的 SPINTRONICS program。在其六年的 lifetime 中，四家 companies（Honey well、Motorola、IBM 和 Nonvolatile Electronics）参与其中。

这个 project 本身不是 basic science，而是 applied engineering，尤其 application 于 Defence。

> 需要注意的是，该 program 的 primary focus 是 applied physics 和 engineering，而不是 fundamental research。通过他的 SPINTRONICS initiative，Wolf 帮助促成了 commercial 和 defence interests 之间的 partnership，这一 outcome 与 TRP 的 goals 很契合。

文章其余关于 SPINTRONICS 和 SPINS programmes 的部分，基本上讲的是 Wolf 和其他 scientists 如何接连向 DARPA 提出 programmes，并获得 financed。在这个 case 中，按照 McCray 的说法，假定这种 financing 是 effective 的，似乎是 plausible 的：

> 整个 1990s 期间 publications 和 patents 的 increase 的 general pattern，与 DARPA funding 的 growth 及新的 magnetic data-storage technologies 的 development coincided。尽管不能假定 direct causality，但可以合理地 conclude：Wolf 及其同事帮助争取到的 funding，在 fostering spintronics re - search community 的 growth 方面发挥了 not-insignificant role。

最后，他确实说到

> spintronics 这一 case，以及总体上对 nanotechnology 的 federal support，都是 sociologist Fred Block 所称的“hidden developmental state”的启发性 examples，U.S. government 正是通过它 supported 了 private sector 对 new technologies 的 commercialisation

但读完整篇文章后，我们可以 infer，它主要指 technologies 的 research 方面（Scientific support），而不是将 research conversion 为 saleable artefacts。别忘了，最早的 IBM disks 在 SPINTRONICS 甚至还没有开始之前，就已经在 sold 了。

在 2001，DARPA 停止了对这个 sector 的 financing，但这并没有使 researchers 停止 investigating：他们接着向 companies 寻求 funds，而且得到了这些 funds。

最后，这篇文章还 criticises 了 linear model of innovation（公平地说，Mazzucato 在书中的另一章也这样做了），也就是认为从 scientific discoveries 到它们的 application 存在一条 causal arrow，仅此而已。

> 在 first order of approximation 下，spintronics 的 case 似乎为传统的 linear model 提供了支持，这个 model 将 science 视为 technological applications 的 prime mover。在 2007 Nobel committee 的 members 看来，一项意料之外的 laboratory discovery inspired 了 IBM 的 industrial research 以及对这个 phenomenon 的 successful exploitation，随后便出现了数十亿台 computers 和 iPods。当然，完整的故事要 complex 得多，揭示了 basic science、instrumentation、federal policy、industrial research 与 commercial goals 之间的 interplay。人们不禁会 conclude，那个“simple”的 linear model，只要 examined 得足够仔细，就绝不是那么回事

最后，前一篇文章的概述性、也更易读的版本可以在 McCray 2009b 中找到。

## Click-wheels（点击滚轮）

Click-wheels 本身并不是 iPhone 的 components，而是 iPod 的。有人可以 argue，没有 iPod 就不会有 iPhone，因此 click-wheels 对后者而言是 necessary 的。无论如何，我们在本节关注的是 analyse 其 underlying technology。

Mazzucato 首先说，click-wheel benefited from capacitive technology，这项 technology 是由 E.A. Johnson invented 的；他在 1960 发表了自己关于这个主题的最初 studies，当时他正在 Royal Radar Establishment 工作，那是一个致力于 defence technology research 的 British government agency。至于 touchscreens，一项重要的 development 是 Bent Stumpe 和 Frank Beck 在 1973 的工作，我们稍后会讨论；另一项是 Samuel Hurst 的工作，他刚离开 Oak Ridge National Laboratory、前往 University of Kentucky 任教两年时，便 invented 了 tactile resistive screens，而当他返回 Oak Ridge 后，又在 1971 创办了一家 company 来 commercialise 这项 technology，并在 1983 producing 了第一个 operating version。

这里 cited 的文章基本上是 Buxton 2012 和 Brown et al. n.d.。

在 Brown et al. 中，我们可以发现，Hurst invented resistive touchscreens 的原因，并不是他在 Oak Ridge 进行着相关 research，而只是他在 reading 某些 charts 时遇到了一些麻烦，这项任务即使有两名学生帮忙，也需要几个月才能完成：

> 他从 Oak Ridge National Laboratory 休假，到 University of Kentucky 任教两年，在那里，他需要 read 一大摞 strip chart data。两名 graduate students 要完成这项任务，大约需要两个月。他开始思考一种 read 这些 charts 的方法，在这个过程中，"Elograph"（electronic graphics）coordinate measuring system 和 Elographics 这家 company 诞生了。University of Kentucky Research Foundation 为 Elograph applied for 并获授了一项 patent。该 Foundation 向 Elographics granted 了一项 exclusive license。

他的 company 起步时采用了很有 Silicon Valley 风格的 model：在车库里，resources 很少：

> 在 1971，返回 Oak Ridge 后，Dr. Hurst 召集了九位来自不同 areas of expertise 的朋友，创办一家 company，以 refine、manufacture 并 sell 这个新 product。此时，Elographics 确实是作为一家地下室企业起步的。所有工作都在三个不同的地下室里完成；一个制作 sensors，另一个制作 electronics，还有一个制作 cabinets。在 February of 1972 搬到 Four Oaks Center 之前，办公室设在制作 sensors 的那所住宅里。这个 product 的 parts 仍然是在夜间和周末于地下室里 produced，然后运到 Four Oaks，在那里 assembled 并 shipped。

government 是否必须 acquire 大量这样的 screens，才能使它们 viable？看来并非如此：

> Ted Wilmart 是最早的 sales representatives 之一，他以每 unit \$8000 的 price，向 Georgia 的 Dalton 一家地毯 company sold 了超过 60 台 Elographs，该 company 用这个 product 来 measure 地毯中的 contaminated particle sizes。
> 
> 从 March, 1971 到 December, 1971 期间，25 units 的 Model E-100 Elographs 被 built，Elographics 也开始了一场规模很小的 sales campaign。这带来了几笔面向 end users 的 sales。第一份购买一台 Elographics Model E-100 measuring system 的 purchase order 于 August 15, 1971 接下，约定于 November 30, 1971 向 Massachusetts Institute of Technology delivery，金额为 $995.00。这 lead-time 可真够长的！

关于 E.A. Johnson，我不质疑 Mazzucato 的叙述。至于 Stumpe 和 Beck，看来他的 screen 是受前者在一家 TV 工厂工作的启发，而且这个 screen 似乎并非长期 investigation 的结果：

> 在 1972 年初，在 CERN 工作的 Danish engineer——Bent Stumpe（他在 1960s 年初曾在一家 television 工厂工作，见旁边的照片）——受 Frank Beck 所托，为一个 _intelligent_ system build hardware，这个 system 只需三个 console units，就能 replace 所有那些 conventional buttons、switches 等。Frank Beck 当时负责 SPS（Super Proton Synchrotron）control room 的 central control hub。在 March, 1972，仅仅几天之内，Stumpe 就提出了一项 proposal，要 build 一个带有 fixed number 的 programmable buttons 的 touch screen、一个用作 computer-controlled pointing device 的 tracker ball（类似于 mouse 的东西），以及一个 programmable knob。最早的 touchscreens 于 1973 installed，并一直保持 in operation，直到 2008。（[computers 的历史](http://history-computer.com/index.html)）

最后一个细节是，在 E.A. Johnson 的 device 之前，就已经存在带有 touchscreen 的 device：musical instrument [Electronic Sackbut](http://www.hughlecaine.com/en/sackbut.html)，只不过这里的 technology 不是用来在带有 images 的 panel 上 select options，而是用来 generate sounds。

## Multi-touch screens（多点触控屏幕）

如果说现代 smartphones 以什么而闻名，那就是 gesture controlled multi-touch screens。Mazzucato 说，这项 technology 是由 University of Delaware 的 Wayne Westerman 和 John Elias invented 的（根据 Westerman 1999）。Westerman 是 John Elias 指导的 PhD student，后者在该 university studied neuromorphic systems，这是 post-doctoral joint programme NSF-CIA/DCI 的一部分。他的 research 由他们创办的 company Fingerworks commercialised，他们也开始 selling 自己的第一个 product——iGesture Numpad。这家 company 在 2005 被 Apple acquired。

但 Elias 和 Westerman 的 device 诞生于 serendipity：

> Prof. John Elias 和我在 1996 开始了我们的 Multitouch research，它是 [neuromorphic systems](http://web.archive.org/web/20100614153128/http://www.ece.udel.edu/%7Eelias/neuromorphicSystems/index.html "http://www.ece.udel.edu/~elias/neuromorphicSystems/index.html")experiments 的一个 serendipitous outgrowth。我们感兴趣的是 training 这些 neuromorphs，让它们 memorize multi-point paths，例如 fingers 掠过一片 skin 时被 sensed 的 patterns。由于 market 上没有 multi-touch tablets 来 capture 这样的 patterns，我们很快就‘偏离了原来的方向’，开始 building 自己的 tablets，运用的正是我们在 designed silicon neuromorphs 时所用的 analog VLSI 和 capacitive circuitry expertise。

而第一个 prototype，则由他们所在 department 的负责人 financed，

> 特别感谢前 ECE Dept. Chair Neal Gallagher 从 department 经费中 funding 了我们的第一个 prototype。
> 
> UD 的 Multitouch research 也得到了来自 National Science Foundation 和 CIA/DCI Post-Doctoral Fellow Program 的 grants 的 supported。

然而，Multi-touch technology 的历史要早得多。回到我们前面 cited 的 Buxton，可以读到，他将第一个 multi-touch screen 的 invention 归于 Bell Labs 的 Bob Boie，尽管此前就已经有了相当多的工作：早在 1982，就已经存在一个由 University of Toronto 的 Nimish Mehta designed 的 multi-touch system（不过它并不是 screen）。因此，不能说 Westerman（以及与 NSF 和 CIA/DCI programme 的 relation）对 multi-touch 的 invention 是 crucial 的。但 gestures 又如何呢？

根据 Buxton，也不是这样：Westerman 的 thesis cites 了一些已经提及 gestures 的 works，例如 digital artist Myron Krueger 的 VideoPlace（[视频](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dmmxVA5xhuo)）。

再一次，即使我们只是阅读 Mazzucato 自己使用的那些 sources，现实也比她的叙述更 complex。

## SIRI（语音助手）

SIRI 始于 DARPA 向 Stanford Research Institute 提出的一项请求（SRI，一家 private nonprofit。它获得大量 public funding）。他们希望它牵头一个 project，develop 一种 "virtual office assistant"，以 assist military personnel。这个 project 最初名为 CALO，即 Cognitive Assistant that Learns and Organizes，并汇集了来自 US 各地的 20 所 universities。后来，SRI launches 一家 startup，并 commercialises SIRI，后者最终在 2010 年被 Apple acquired。

这里我并不质疑 Mazzucato 的 interpretation，但我要指出，确实存在 separately developed 的类似 technology：Microsoft 的 Cortana，以及 Google 的 Google Now。此外，[人们其实并不怎么使用 SIRI](http://bgr.com/2013/10/23/apple-siri-usage-ios-7-survey/)。没有 SIRI，iPhone 基本上仍会是同样的 device。而没有 DARPA，也会出现 SIRI surrogates。

## LCD screens（液晶屏幕）

据 Mazzucato 所说，LCD screens 的关键 development 发生在 70 年代，当时 thin film transistor (TFT) 在 Peter Brody 的 direction 下于 Westinghouse 被 created。据 Hart and Borrus 1992 所说，这几乎完全由 US Army financed。Westinghouse executives 在某个时候 ended 了这项 investigation，而 Brody 尝试请求其他 companies 来 finance 他的 research。据 Florida and Browdy 1991 所说，他询问了 Xerox、3M、IBM、DEC 和 Compaq，但没有一家愿意向他 buy screens，因为他们怀疑，相对于他的 Japanese competitors，他能否以 reasonable cost 来 manufacture 它们。1988 年，在获得 DARPA 的一份 7.8 million dollar contract 后，他 sets up Magnascreen，以 develop TFT-LCD technology。Florida 和 Browdy 将这段 LCD 经历视为 American innovation system 中的一个 problem：big corporations 和 venture capitalists 都没有 develop 这个 product，尽管他们曾使 semiconductor 和 personal computer industries 成为可能（这会与 Mazzucato 关于 semiconductors 和 computers 的其他说法相矛盾，但那不是本文的主题）。

为了 retain TFT-LCD 方面的 leadership，并将 manufacturing 留在国内，主要 manufacturers launch 了一个 consortium，即 ADMARC，获得了 National Institute of Standards and Technology 的 support 和 funds、针对 Japanese 的 antidumping tariffs，以及与许多 civilian 和 military public agencies 签订的、用于 buy 这些 screens 的 contracts。

我对这段经历的解读基本上是：一群 companies successfully lobbies US government，以 resist 更 superior 的 Japanese technology。既然有 better 且 cheaper 的 alternative，reject investing in TFT-LCD 就并非 irrational。也许适合它的时机要晚些才会到来，而这个 project 本应通过 long term research channels（research institutes 和 universities）来 carried out。

现在让我们读一读 Mazzucato 所 cited 的 evidence。首先是 Hart and Borrus 1992。

> 在 television displays 中，CRTs 的主要 alternatives 是 liquid crystal displays (LCDs)。**LCDs 最初是在 Westinghouse 位于 Pittsburgh 的 laboratories，以及位于 New Jersey 的 Princeton 的 David Sarnoff Research Center 中 developed 的**（后者当时由 RCA owned）。Sarnoff lab 的 George Heilmeier 和 Richard Williams 于 1963 年首次提出了 liquid crystals 可以用于 displays 的想法。**Westinghouse 的 Jim Fergason 于 1964 年开始探索他自己的 LCDs approach**。Fergason 于 1970 年离开 Westinghouse，found 了一家名为 Liquid Xtal 的新 company，后来它因 obtaining patents 的延误而 failed。同样在 Westinghouse 的 Peter Brody pioneered 了 LCDs 的 “active matrix” addressing。Brody 于 1979 年离开 Westinghouse，两年后 founded 了自己的 firm，即 Panelvision；它于 1985 年被 sold 给 Litton Industries。

被用来 support 该书 affirmation 的这段话，确实符合所给出的 explanation：

看起来，screens 经历了 relatively independent development，但迈向 LCD-TFT 的那一步完全由 Army financed。然而，那项 technology 是 Bernard J. Lechner 在 Radio Corporation of America invented 的（Kawamoto 2012），并于 1968 年由他的 team 在一个 2x18 matrix 中 implemented。Brody 和他的同事们于 1974 年加入其中，最后在 1988 年，Sharp produces 了一块 14 inch full colour screen，使 industry 相信 LCD-TFT technology 是有未来的。

Brody 实际上 founded 了两家 companies。在 Magnavision 之前，他 founded 了 [Panelvision](https://books.google.es/books?id=yC4EAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA43&lpg=PA43&dq=Panelvision+Corporation&source=bl&ots=CuoBn2IHw7&sig=NCkY8micVdBcY-66MOwwSxNpQ6M&hl=es&sa=X&ei=_7GJVbrwENefyASF2pPoCg&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=Panelvision%20Corporation&f=false)，而且那家是 successful 的。[他似乎在那里开始研究](https://books.google.es/books?id=sAEAAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA86&lpg=PA86&dq=%22panelvision+corp%22+pittsburg&source=bl&ots=UiPbIx3g_F&sig=L0ydNcXRDNw32INZdrKN96jQffI&hl=es&sa=X&ei=TryJVZCnOcH--AHU9YGgBA&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=%22panelvision%20corp%22%20pittsburg&f=false)后来会为 Magnavision implement 的 technology，但后者没有 success，因为他需要 high manufacturing volumes 才能使其 profitable。

在这一系列 interventions 之后，时至今日，[US 仍未能在](http://www.displaysearch.com/cps/rde/xchg/displaysearch/hs.xsl/130409_smartphone_tablet_pc_demand_drive_small_medium_flat_panel_display_revenue.asp) LCD screen manufacturing 上 lead，但我们却拥有越来越好、越来越便宜、各种不同 sizes 的 screen。如果从 mercantilist point of view 来看世界，这无疑是 US 及其所谓 mercantilist policy（以及它的 Entrepreneurial State）的一次 defeat。但如果从全世界 consumers 的角度来看，他们却赢了。Mazzucato criticises investors 和 American companies 回避与 Japanese 竞争，并认为这是它们的 failure。我则会 criticise 她将此视为一个问题。

再补充一点：如果 tariffs 之类的措施取得了成功，Mazzucato 是否会给 US Entrepreneurial State 记上一功，反过来说 Japanese 不可能 develop LCD screens 呢？

## Batteries（电池）

下一个例子是 lithium batteries。根据 Henderson 2004 和 OSTI 2009，John B. Goodenough 是在这个 secor 开辟道路的 researcher，他在八十年代末获得了 Department of Energy 和 National Science Foundation 的 funding。后来，University of Texas 的 scientific developments 在 1991 年由 Sony 进行 commercialised。在 2005 年的一次 NIST conference 上，Ralph J. Brodd identified 了与我们在 LCD panels 的案例中看到的类似的 problems，并将其归咎于 corporate and investor short termism。Brodd 说，federal government 通过各种 agencies 和 rogrammes 帮助 small companies，以 strenghten 它们的 productive capacity。

让我们读一读。在 Henderson 2004 和 OSTI 2009 中，我们发现，Goodenough 在前往 University of Texas 之前，就已在 Oxford University 开始 developing 他的 technology，尽管他确实是在前者那里 improved 了自己的 designs。然而，他的国际声誉确实来自他在 Oxford 的工作：lithium-cobalt oxide batteries 的 invention。总体而言，[batteries 的 history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery#History) 展现了通常的 pattern：许多 development centers，分布在世界许多地方。

Brodd 2005 confirms 了我们关于 Goodenough 和 Oxford 的说法。至于针对 small businesses 的 state support，它确实存在，但除了为某些 niches（defence 和 healthcare）develop batteries 之外，似乎没有起到多大帮助。我们或许会问，这个 programme 的 cost 是多少。

在这张[Reuters chart](http://graphics.thomsonreuters.com/310/JP_LITON0310.gif) 中，我们可以看到如今是谁在 manufactures Li-ion batteries，仍然是日本人，再加上韩国人。与 LCD screens 的案例一样：Batteries 正在被 manufactured 和 improved。它们在哪里被 manufactured，重要吗？

有人可能会论证，Sony 的 battery 是由日本的 Entrepreneurial State 所 fostered 的 outcome。根据 [Sony](http://www.sony.net/SonyInfo/CorporateInfo/History/SonyHistory/2-13.html#block3)，他们的 battery 的 history 就是如此。尝试论证这一点，就留给读者作为 exercise。

## 速度与激情：这个 Fast Fourier Transform

Mazzucato 写道：

> Office of Science and Technology Policy (2006, 8) 还 documented 了 State support 在 digital signal processing (DSP) technology 中的 role；这项 technology 是随着 1980s 期间 fast Fourier transform (FDT) algorithm 的 application 方面的 scientific advancements 而出现的。这种新的 signal processing approach enabled 了 sound 的 real-time processing（例如在 two-way phone call 期间），也 enabled 了 large audio or multimedia files 的 real-time processing，后者能够 improve 它们的 playback 的 quality。

这份 report 说，FFT 是在 1965 年 revolutionizes signal processing 的。经过查找，我发现她指的是 Cooley 和 Tukey 对 Gauss 的用于 calculate FFT 的 algorithm 的 rediscovery（Heideman 19849，比 Gauss 早一个世纪。请注意，自 Gauss 以来，FFT 已经取得了一些 advancements，而 Cooley 和 Tukey 仅仅 represented 了这条 chain 中的一步。通过[这篇 paper](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cme324/classics/cooley-tukey.pdf) 来 Rediscovering Gauss——没错，它确实由 Army Research Office 提供 financed——如果你想以此论证 ARO 在这项 discovery 中的 role，似乎并没有那么重要，因为 authors 当时为 Princeton University 工作（Cooley 同时也是 IBM employee，而 Tukey 是 Princeton professor）。一项 mathematical advance 似乎不需要数量庞大的 resources，而且 Princeton 和 IBM 无论当时还是现在，都是 financially sound entities，当时它们正在 supporting Tukey 和 Cooley 所做的 research。如果说了这些之后，有人仍然想论证这一事件是由于 ARO funding，那么就更有理由说，我们拥有这个 algorithm 要感谢 Princeton 和 IBM；沿用同样的理由，这个 algorithm 就是一场 private sector triumph。不过，这就深入到了 causality 的 concepts，我们会在以后的文章中讨论，因此，如果上面的 reasoning 没有说服你，我希望在我解释了这里所采用的 implicit view of causality 之后，你会觉得它更 plausible。

## DRAM（动态随机存取存储器）

在这个例子中，chart 将我们引向 1960-70，以及 VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) technology 的 development。它指向 IBM 和 DARPA 的 basic science funding。Mazzucato 没有花多少篇幅谈论此事，我也不会。你可以在这里阅读 DRAM 的 history，看看它获得了多少 state financing（其中甚至没有提到）。在 Itoh (2008) 中，我同样找不到 state influence。我们看到的是 IBM、Intel 和 Hitachi。根据她提供的 evidence 和我找到的 additional evidence，断言 DRAM 是 Entrepreneurial State 的 creation 似乎并不 plausible。可以主张 VLSI 是 Entrepreneurial State 的 creation，并由此主张它对 DRAM 存在 indirect influence。但这属于 semiconductors 的 category；我会在另一篇文章中，将它们与这里没有讨论的那些问题一起讨论（这些问题对 iPhone 而言并不那么 central）。

## Cellular technology（蜂窝技术）

chart 中说 US Army supported 了这项 technology，并引用 Breakthough Report (2010) 来支持这一 affirmation。report 说：

> cellular communication 的早期 foundation 在于 radiotelephony capabilities，后者在整个 20th century 中，在 U.S. military 的 support 下 progressively advanced。

但它完全没有为此提供依据，也没有展开说明这一点。在一本书中，使用如此薄弱的 evidence 来 affirm 这样有争议的说法，未免有些草率。让我们宽容一些，考察可能找到的其他 evidence。让我们不局限于 Mazzucato。cellular communication 从何而来？

在 Gosh (ch. 1,2010) 中，我们没有发现 cellular technology 的 technological development 中存在 state influence——它确实存在于 GSM protocol 中，但那是后来的事，而且肯定不是来自 US Army。会不会是 General Dynamics 在 1985 为 US Army developed 的 [Mobile Subscriber Equipment](http://thesoldiersnetwork.com/army-innovations-building-the-first-cellular-network/) (MSE)？根据 Ghosh，1983 时 Chicago 已经有了 commercial cellular services，Japan 在 1979 就有了，而 Bell Labs 在 1960-70 就已充实了这一 concept。因此，这似乎值得怀疑。

这里再次留给读者一个 exercise：尝试找出这项 technology 的 development 与 State 之间的 link。

## Protectionism（保护主义）

Mazzucatian 的第三个论点是，American State protected 了 Apple 的 intellectual property，并努力为它打开 international market。发生 global conflicts 时，它求助的是 government。她说，Apple 在八十年代曾难以 accessing Japanese market，而它之所以能够 enter 这一 market，是因为 US government support。此外，Apple 还 receives 一系列 sical benefits、R&D incentives，以及 State 通过 public schools 对其 products 的 purchase。

但即使接受所有这些说法，我们仍须 quantify 它们，才能看出它们对 Apple，或对整个 society，是否真的 useful。Intellectual property 似乎并不被 manufacture iPhone clones 的 companies 放在心上，而且它可能 more detrimental than beneficial。Apple 手持 patents，与全世界展开 patent wars，这对促成 technological progress 真的 useful 吗？值得怀疑（Boldrin & Levine 2002）。

另一方面，即使这个论点是正确的（也就是说，事情确实如 Mazzucato 所说的那样发生，而且确实帮助了 Apple），我们对它最应该做的，也还是 criticise 它，并要求结束 corporate welfare；即便仅从 efficiency 的角度看，它似乎也没有多少 beneficial 之处，至少在 Mazzucato 向我们提供 solid evidence 之前如此。

一个相关的问题是，整个问题恰恰由 Mazzucato 似乎赞同、或至少并不反对的那类 policies 所 generated。也许，tariffs 和 nascent industry protection 等 mercantilist policies 能够在那个 sector foster 一个 national industry。也可能只是 decrease overall welfare，这取决于它们是否成功；而它们通常并不成功，因为 Park Chung-hee 这样的人并不多见。即便如此，如果采取 global perspective，我们应该关心的也不是 national industry，而是 global consumer welfare。因此，一项 technology 在哪里 developed 并不那么重要，重要的是它确实 developed 了，并且可以在全世界 exported 和 sold。

## Conclusion（结论）

书中这一章这样结尾：

> 总之，在这样一个国家，‘找到你热爱的事情’，并在做这件事时保持‘愚拙’，要容易得多：State 在其中承担着关键而严肃的职责，接手 high-risk technologies 的 development，进行早期、large 和 high-risk investments，然后 sustaining 它们，直到 later-stage private actors 能够出来‘玩耍和享受乐趣’。因此，尽管 ‘free market’ pundits 继续警告 government ‘picking winners’ 的 danger，我们仍可以说，US government 的各种 policies 奠定了 foundation，为 Apple 提供了 tools，使它成为 twenty-first century 迄今最 dynamic 的 high-tech industries 之一中的 major industry player。没有 US government 频繁的 targeted investment 和 intervention，大多数潜在的 ‘Apples’ 很可能都会在争夺 computing 和 communications age 主导地位的 global race 中成为输家。这家 company 将 complex technologies integrating 成 user-friendly 且 attractive 的 devices，并辅以 powerful software mediums，其 organizational success 不应被 marginalized；然而，无可争辩的是，Apple 的大多数最佳 technologies 之所以 exist，是因为此前由 State driven 的 collective 和 cumulative efforts；这些 efforts 是在面对 uncertainty 时作出的，而且往往即使不是以 national security 的名义，也是以 economic competitiveness 的名义。

基本上，最后这一段是错误的。那些有助于 iPhone 的 science 领域的 State interventions，既不是 crucial，也不是 entrepreneurial，其数量也没有 Mazzucato 试图展示的那么多。

让我们再次追问，是什么使 iPhone 成为 iPhone。Mazzucato 原本可以尝试说 State invented 了 mobile phone，或者 smartphone，但她选择了 iPhone（因为她以为这会引起争议，而它确实引起了争议）。但事实是，我们在这里谈论的大多数事情，对于回答这个问题都多少有些 irrelevant。如果拿一部 iPhone 与任何其他 high end smartphone 相比（尤其是 iPhone released 时的那些），我们不会在 screen、internal memory 或 GPS 上找到 difference。大多数 smartphones 都有这些。

使 iPhone 成为 iPhone，使它成为一款将 Apple 带到如今地位的 product 的，是 design、它的 iOS operating system、correct technological integration，以及 careful quality assurance process。所有这些 factors，本质上都是 Apple 自身的 internal factors。没有这些东西，我们会拥有一部 smartphone，但它不会是 iPhone。正是这些赋予 Apple comparative advantage，构成其巨额 revenues 的 base。Mazzucato 在另一章讨论了 State 是否应得 Apple 的部分 benefits——我们会在另一篇文章中谈到这一点。对此，可以提出三重 criticism：第一，就是本文提出的 criticism。State 的 role 并不是 Mazzucato 所说的那样。第二，即使 Mazzucato 是对的，相比较而言，那些 extra profits 也不应 attributed 于 state reserach，而应 attributed 于 internal company factors。第三，Mazzucato 用来论证 Apple 欠 State 什么的 ethical premises 是 dubious 的（任何 recommendation 都 implies 一个 ought）。

无论是一般意义上的 Technology，还是作为 particular case 的 iPhone，背后都是一个 complex story。谈论谁 invented 了这个、谁 discovered 了那个，通常会掩盖许多 nuances，而我们在研究 technology 的 history 时不应忽略它们。当然，声称我们应将那部开启 smarthphone revolution 的 phone 归功于 Entrepreneurial State，在 available evidence 中并没有 basis。State 只是又一个 actor，就像 IBM、Bell Labs、Sony、Goodenough、Brody 或 Lechner 一样。

Science 和 Technologies 中有推动它们一步步前进的英雄，而不是没有他们就不可能 progress 的神。我们摒弃 Great Man Theory of Innovation 是对的；按照这种 theory，我们应将 discoveries 归功于只有他们才能作出这些 discoveries 的 individual geniuses。但出于同样的理由，我们也应该摒弃 Great State Theory。

## 原文

\[This post is a translation of [this other post](https://nintil.com/2015/06/24/mazzucato-y-el-iphone-ii-el-mito-del-estado-emprendedor/) of mine\]

As we saw [in a previous post](https://nintil.com/2015/08/19/mazzucato-and-the-iphone-i/), one of the main examples used by Mariana Mazzucato to validate her arguments is the case of the iPhone. Let us remember, Mazzucato's thesis is that most of modern technology is due to directed governmental research efforts, not only in basic science, but also in technological commercialisation, direct investment, loans, etc. These kinds of policies make up what she calls The Entrepreneurial State. We must make clear the difference between this concept from another, more reasonable one, that is of a State that merely invests in basic science to correct market failures. Such thing is what is usually accepted in the economic mainstream, where Mazzucato is not, given she is precisely arguing against entrenched conceptions of the role of the State in innovation.

In the present article, I shall criticise her arguments, and question the evidence put forward to defend them. Like in previous articles, her own bibliography allows us to criticise her own points. We shall begin from a study of every single bibliographical reference she cites in the chapter to support every single one of her paragraphs, and complete this with additional references wherever it is necessary.

This critique limits itself to the relevant chapter of the book, in this case to chapter five, where the iPhone example is discussed. Some elements that are mentioned in the article are excluded: the case of computers and semiconductors in general, and GPS, as these deserve to be discussed separately. Mazzucato also says these also come mostly from the State, so I'm not going to criticise her arguments twice.

The chapter begins explaining that Apple's history, and the Jobsian "stay hungry, stay foolish" philosophy, are somewhat mystified, as according to Mazzucato

> Individual genius, attention to design, a love for play, and foolishness were no doubt important characteristics. But without the massive amount of public investment behind the computer and Internet revolutions, such attributes might have led only to the invention of a new toy – not to cutting-edge revolutionary products like the iPad and iPhone which have changed the way that people work and communicate. \[...\] While the products owe their beautiful design and slick integration to the genius of Jobs and his large team, nearly every state-of-the-art technology found in the iPod, iPhone and iPad is an often overlooked and ignored achievement of the research efforts and funding support of the government and military.

A discussion of Apple's R&D expenditures follow. It is mentioned that is has been growing in quantity (it multiplied by 25 since 1999, and by more than 4 in the last 5 years), it has not represented a constant percentage of sales revenue: As time passes, Apple's R&D spending represents an ever shrinking fraction of sales revenues, according to Mazzucato's book. We can see that this tendency seems to have reverted in the graph linked in the previous link, however. The economist gives two possible interpretations to this: One, that maybe Apple uses her funds very efficiently. Or second, more plausibly according to her, citing analyst Horace Schmidt, that Apple is not really a company that develops new technologies, but is more of a company that integrates new technologies. To Schmidt's analysis, Mazzucato adds: "technologies, in many cases finances by the State", using this to link his section to the one that follows, where what the State has done for Apple is detailed.

These interventions are of three types:

1.  Direct investment in the initial funding and growth of Apple
2.  Access to technologies resulting from state research programmes, military initiatives, or carried out by public research institutions
3.  Fiscal, commercial, and/or technological policy to support Apple in hard times.

## Investment

With respect to point one, Mazzucato says that Apple had many private investors: Don Valentine, Arthur Rock, Venrock, Mike Markkula... but that it also received money before its IPO in 1980 from Continental Illinois Venture Corp, which was, according to Mazzucato, a "Small Business Investment Company (SBIC), authorized by the Small Business Administration to invest in small businesses". For the list of investors, the economist cites Rao and Scaruffi 2011 and for the state investment data, Slater 1983 and Audretsch 1995. I wasn't able to access any of these sources, so I will use others. It is known that Markkula invested 250,000$ in 1977 and in Moritz 2009, we find that in 1978, Apple managed as much as 517,500$ in investments, all of that being before CIVC's entry.

This CIVC, say Lazonick and Mazzucato 2013, would be an arm of Continental Illinois Bank who had gotten guaranteed loans from the US Government, and that it had invested 500,000$ in Apple in 1978. [In this report](http://www.frbsf.org/community-development/files/Bennett_SBICs.pdf) from the San Francisco Federal Reserve, we can see that a SBIC is a private venture capital fund, that can have -but that does not necessarily have- some sort of State backing. T[hey also have lessened regulatory requirements](https://www.sba.gov/content/faqs). [Sources from that time](http://articles.latimes.com/1990-09-25/business/fi-1303_1_small-businesses/2) confirm CIVC's investment, but seem pessimistic about the SBIC concept.

A more plausible interpretation of this, given how SBICs work, is that this was not an action taken by an Entrepreneurial State, but that of a group of private investors who managed to get state benefits through the SBIC programme (the people from the Continental Illinois Bank). [I am not the first](http://somerandomthoughts-mythoughts.blogspot.com.es/2014/04/the-entrepreneurial-state-is-apple.html) in reaching this conclusion. Taking into account the existence of private investors, and the sales revenue and valuation of Apple back then, the importance of State guarantees for the loans to a private entity that in turn invested in Apple is framed into its historical context, and has its importance relativized. That is, Apple was steaming ahead before the involvement of the CIVC and given what we know, it is most reasonable to assume that it would have continued to do so hadn't there been government involvement. Therefore, you can certainly say that Apple had government support in its initial stages, but not that it was crucial, or that we would have had no Apple without it.

## Technology

What technologies, according to Mazzucato, are the ones that set the iPhone, iPod, and Ipad apart from others in the market, or that allow them to work?

As central technologies, microprocessors, RAM, hard drive storage, LCD screens, Li-pol and Li-ion batteries, digital signal processing (DSP) based in the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the internet, HTTP and HTML protocols and cellular networks.

As additional technologies that give these devices a touch of distinction: GPS, click wheel navigation, multitouch screens, and an speech based artificial intelligence assistant: SIRI.

## Hard Drives (GMR)

Mazzucato begins with hard drives, that according to her enabled the iPod to dethrone Sony's Walkman and Discman. These new hard drives were made possible thanks to the discovery that earned Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg the Physics Nobel Prize in 2007: Giant Magnetoresistence (GMR). The discovery was parallel, in 1988. Both Fert and Grünberg discovered GMR independently, that is.

The problem here is that the history of hard drives doesn't begin or end with the discovery of GMR. The first disks that apply GMR were commercialised [in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_hard_disk_drives), and certainly the Toshiba disk that t[he first iPod had used GMR heads](http://www.funkygoods.com/schwarzschild/2013_06/MK5002MAL_MK1002.pdf). But the step from discovery to manufacturing wasn't trivial. Fert and Grünberg discovered on one side the GMR effect, but then [IBM Research](http://www.research.ibm.com/research/gmr.html) takes that research and applies it to hard drives as early as 1991, and from there to [Toshiba](http://www.toshiba-tdmt.com.tw/english/products/article02.aspx?ccid=19) it was another step, probably due to the sale of IBM and Hitachi technology to Toshiba, as mentioned in the previous link. For the case of the discovery itself, the structure is similar to what other discoveries follow: They begin from existing scientific results and take them to the next level. GMR was discovered after the decade long effort by thousands of scientists, it wasn't just a happy idea that popped into existence. (Grünberg et al. 1989, Fert 2007)

Mazzucato tries to argue, via McCray 2009a, that the history of GMR and hard drives illustrates the role of the State not only in research (Most economists were already saying this), but also in the work necessary to convert scientific ideas into manufactured, commercially viable products. She also says that, even when the most important scientific development leading to the discovery of GMR was European, the US government also played an important role in commercialising the technology, arguing that Grünberg's lab was affiliated with Argonne National Laboratory, and that he received Department of Energy grants. From there, companies developed the technology. This evidence does not seem to support the idea that the State helped in the transition from science to product. As we saw before, it was IBM who played that role.

According to McCray, the origin of the field of magnetoresistence can be traced back to the 1857 research of William "Lord Kelvin" Thomson, and that the modern understanding of the electron spin (also base of GMR) can be traced back to the 1920 work of Pauli, Dirac, and Goudsmit. Next, scientists in Bell Labs and IBM experimented with diverse techniques to produce artificial materials, developing technologies such as molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), to be able to, in turn, build nanostructures. MBE-based apparatuses were crucial in Grünberg and Fert's research, according to McCray.

On the role of the State in commercialization, which is why Mazzucato was citing McCray, he says that GMR was first applied to weak magnetic field detection (by James M. Daughton, electrical engineer from IBM Research, who later founded his own company, Nonvolatile Electronics, in 1989. It was in IBM Research where high temperature superconductivity was also discovered, in 1986)

McCray confirms what we were saying: it was IBM the one who made the crucial step of going from research to commercial development:

> Companies were eager to apply the nascent form of spintronics to broad er and more lucrative markets. Following the discovery of GMR, scientists at IBM’s Almaden laboratory near San Jose, California searched for other ways to apply it to commercial products. Unlike IBM’s Thomas J. Watson Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York, which had a long history of research on semiconductor logic devices, the older Almaden lab had traditionally focused on magnetic information-storage technologies.
> 
> \[...\]
> 
> Unlike Fert and Grünberg, who built their samples with the more precise but slower molecular beam epitaxy technique, Parkin’s \[IBM researcher\] group adopted sputter-deposition equipment. The focus of the Almaden group wasn’t on basic science per se, but on making devices that could be readily manufactured. Parkin’s use of the quicker and cheaper sputtering technique made sense for IBM, a company with extensive experience in fabricating sputter deposited magnetic-storage media on an industrial scale. As one observer of Parkin’s research later recalled, the British scientist and his colleagues “simply engineered the \[expletive deleted\]” out of the underlying GMR discovery as they made and characterized over 30,000 different multilayer combinations
> 
> \[...\]
> 
> IBM eventually used the spintronics research of Parkin and his colleagues to redesign and improve a basic element in the company’s hard disk drives. The Wall Street Journal revealed the company’s innovation with a front-page story in November 1997. Based on the Almaden group’s exploitation of GMR, IBM’s new drives featured exquisitely sensitive magnetic-read heads. Able to store eight times more data than competitors’ equipment while remaining smaller in size, the redesigned read heads set the stage for a subsequent explosion in computer memory that, in turn, helped make it possible for music lovers to store gigabytes of music and other files on iPods and similar handheld gadgets. IBM licensed its GMR-based technology to other companies and, with in a few years, practically every computer hard drive included a read head based on IBM’s innovation. Firms like Seagate Technology and, for awhile, IBM reaped tremendous profits. IBM’s rapid commercialisation of Fert and Grünberg’s basic discovery introduced spintronics into a market worth billions of dollars annually. And while these ubiquitous tools were not explicitly marketed as nanotechnology, IBM researchers could boast that, be cause of their research, “everybody has \[a\] spintronics device on their desktop.”

Once products were already in the market, the State began to invest in them:

> As products exploiting the GMR effect appeared on the market and more scientists began to do research in what would become known as spintronics, science managers from military laboratories and funding agencies began to take notice. At the same time, major newspapers presented spinbased electronics as a potential new industry paradigm. Journalists struck a tone that comported with a prevailing tendency to predict the end of Moore’s Law by hyping the new electronics technology as the “next big thing” for the industry.

DARPA invested in GMR in 1992,

> One of those monitoring nascent GMR-based spintronics was Stuart Wolf. Trained as a physicist at Rutgers University, Wolf received his doctorate in 1969 and initially researched low-temperature superconductivity in metals.44 After three years at Case Western Reserve University, Wolf became a staff scientist at the Naval Research Laboratory, where his work on superconductors held interest for a number of defence applications. In 1993, Wolf accepted a new post as program manager at the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency. DARPA, of course, was no stranger to supporting research for computer and semiconductor applications; for decades, it had funded major cutting-edge programs in areas such as molecular electronics, integrated-circuit design, and supercomputing.
> 
> In late 1992, as part of the transition to the new geopolitical environment, the Department of Defense initiated the Technology Reinvestment Project (TRP). Managed by DARPA, the TRP’s purpose was to build stronger links between the commercial and military sectors and help the United States reap a greater share of the anticipated "peace dividend." The TRP also emerged at a time when established companies like IBM and Bell Labs were cutting support of basic research. The TRP directed over $800 million (with commercial firms providing additional funds) to scores of dual-use technology projects before the program ended in 1996.
> 
> One of the modest efforts in the TRP portfolio involved trying to stimulate commercial innovations based on the GMR phenomenon. In 1995, with an initial $5 million from DARPA and matching industry funds, Wolf initiated the GMR Consortium Project. In this program, large companies like Honeywell and newer firms like Nonvolatile Electronics partnered with Wolf’s former colleagues at the Naval Research Laboratory to develop a new form of computer memory

How did Wolf manage to get his GMR Initiative accepted by the State? Plopping a 40 pound satellite memory in DARPA's director's desk, and saying he could do the same with a fifty cent chip. It wasn't a DARPA visionary effort, they just saw a man confident in the success of his idea.

> Wolf sold his program by pulling an old memory component from a satellite system and taking it into the DARPA offices. “I plopped it on the director’s desk,” Wolf recalled. “It weighed forty pounds and cost a quarter of a million dollars. I said, ‘I’m going to replace this with a fifty-cent chip.’” Wolf’s modest program soon expanded into what DARPA initially called the Magnetic Materials and Devices Project. Unsatisfied with this moniker, Wolf suggested a new name—SPin TRansport electrONICS—which he shortened to “SPINTRONICS.” DARPA eventually provided some $100 million to support Wolf ’s SPINTRONICS program. During its six-year lifetime, four companies (Honey well, Motorola, IBM, and Nonvolatile Electronics) took part in it.

The project as such was not basic science, but applied engineering, with special application to Defence.

> It is important to note that applied physics and engineering, not fundamental research, was the program’s primary focus. Through his SPINTRONICS initiative, Wolf helped broker a partnership between commercial and defence interests, an outcome that meshed well with the TRP’s goals.

The rest of the article, about the SPINTRONICS and SPINS programmes are basically the story of how Wolf and other scientists proposed successive programmes to DARPA, and got financed. In this case, according to McCray, it seems plausible to assume this financing was effective:

> The general pattern of increase in publications and patents throughout the 1990s coincided with the growth of DARPA funding and the development of new magnetic data-storage technologies. While direct causality cannot be assumed, it is reasonable to conclude that the funding Wolf and his colleagues helped make available played a not-insignificant role in fostering the growth of the spintronics re - search community.

Finally, he does say that

> The case of spintronics and federal support for nanotechnology in general are suggestive examples of what sociologist Fred Block calls the “hidden developmental state” through which the U.S. government has supported the private sector’s commercialisation of new technologies

But from the reading of the full article, what we can infer is that it refers mostly to the research side of the technologies (Scientific support), not the conversion of research into saleable artefacts. Let us remember that the first IBM disks were being sold before SPINTRONICS even began.

IN 2001, DARPA stopped financing this sector, but that didn't cause researchers to stop investigating: they went on to ask companies for funds, and they got them.

Finally, the article also criticises (as Mazzucato also does in another chapter of the book, to be fair) the linear model of innovation, the idea that there is a causal arrow from scientific discoveries and the application of them, and that's it.

> To a first order of approximation, the case of spintronics appears to lend credence to the traditional linear model, which posits science as a prime mover for technological applications. As members of the 2007 Nobel committee saw it, an unexpected laboratory discovery inspired IBM’s industrial research and successful exploitation of the phenomenon and consequently billions of computers and iPods followed. The full story, of course, was much more complex, revealing the interplay among basic science, instrumentation, federal policy, industrial research, and commercial goals. One cannot help but conclude that the “simple” linear model, when examined closely enough, is anything but

## Click-wheels

Click-wheels are not components of the iPhone as such, but of the iPod. One could argue that without iPod there wouldn't have been no iPhone and therefore click-wheels were necessary for the latter. In any case, our interest in this section is to analyse the underlying technology.

Mazzucato begins by saying that the click-wheel benefited from capacitive technology, invented by E.A. Johnson, who published his first studies on the topic in 1960, while he was working in the Royal Radar Establishment, a British government agency dedicated to defence technology research. With respect to touchscreens, an important development was Bent Stumpe and Frank Beck's 1973 work which we will discuss later on, and Samuel Hurst's, who invented tactile resistive screens just after he left Oak Ridge National Laboratory to teach for two years at the University of Kentucky,and when he came back to Oak Ridge, he launched a company in 1971 to commercialise the technology, producing the first operating version in 1983.

The articles cited here are basically Buxton 2012 and Brown et al. n.d.

In Brown et al. we can find that the reason Hurst invented resistive touchscreens wasn't that he was carrying out related research at Oak Ridge, but that he just had some trouble reading certain charts, task that even with the help of two students, would take months:

> He was on leave from the Oak Ridge National Laboratory to teach at the University of Kentucky for two years, where he was faced with a need to read a huge stack of strip chart data. It would have taken two graduate students approximately two months to do the task. He started thinking of a way to read the charts, and during the process, the "Elograph" (electronic graphics) coordinate measuring system and Elographics the company were born. The University of Kentucky Research Foundation applied for and was granted a patent on the Elograph. The Foundation granted an exclusive license to Elographics.

His company began with a very Silicon Valley style model, in a garage and with scant resources:

> In 1971, after returning to Oak Ridge, Dr. Hurst gathered nine friends from various areas of expertise to start a company to refine, manufacture and sell this new product. At this point Elographics truly began as a basement business. All work was done from three different basements; sensors in one, electronics in another, and cabinets in still another. The office was located in the home where the sensors were being built before moving to Four Oaks Center in February of 1972. The parts of the product were still being produced in basements at night and on weekends and brought to Four Oaks where they were assembled and shipped.

Was it necessary for the government to acquire huge amounts of these screens to make them viable? It seems not:

> Ted Wilmart, one of the first sales representatives, sold over 60 Elographs at a price of $8000 per unit to a carpet company in Dalton, Georgia, who used the product to measure contaminated particle sizes in carpet.
> 
> During the time from March, 1971 until December, 1971, 25 units of Model E-100 Elographs were built, and Elographics started a very minor sales campaign. Out of this there were a few sales to end users. The first purchase order for one Elographics Model E-100 measuring system was taken on August 15, 1971 for delivery on November 30, 1971 to Massachusetts Institute of Technology for $995.00. Talk about extended lead-time!

On E.A. Johnson, I don't question Mazzucato's account. For Stumpe and Beck, apparently his screen was inspired by the work of the former in a TV factory, and this screen doesn't seem to be the result of a long investigation:

> In the early 1972, the Danish engineer (who had an experience from a television factory in the early 1960s), working in CERN—Bent Stumpe (see the nearby photo), was asked by Frank Beck, who was in charge of the central control hub in the SPS (Super Proton Synchrotron) control room, to build the hardware for an _intelligent_ system which, in just three console units, would replace all those conventional buttons, switches, etc. In just a few days, in March, 1972, Stumpe came up with a proposal to build a touch screen with a fixed number of programmable buttons, a tracker ball to be used as computer-controlled pointing device (something like mouse) and a programmable knob. The first touchscreens were installed in 1973 and remained in operation until 2008. ([History of computers](http://history-computer.com/index.html))

A final detail is that a device with a touchscreen existed before E.A. Johnson's: the musical instrument [Electronic Sackbut](http://www.hughlecaine.com/en/sackbut.html), only that here technology wasn't used to select options in a panel with images, but to generate sounds.

## Multi-touch screens

If modern smartphones are known for something, that would be gesture controlled multi-touch screens. Mazzucato says this technology was invented by Wayne Westerman and John Elias, from the University of Delaware (according to Westerman 1999). Westerman was a PhD student under John Elias, who studied neuromorphic systems in the university, as part of the post-doctoral joint programme NSF-CIA/DCI. His research were commercialised by a company they founded, Fingerworks, and they began selling their first product, the iGesture Numpad. This company was acquired by Apple in 2005.

But Elias and Westerman's device was born out of serendipity:

> Prof. John Elias and I began our Multitouch research in 1996 as a serendipitous outgrowth of [neuromorphic systems](http://web.archive.org/web/20100614153128/http://www.ece.udel.edu/%7Eelias/neuromorphicSystems/index.html "http://www.ece.udel.edu/~elias/neuromorphicSystems/index.html")experiments.  We were interested in training the neuromorphs to memorize multi-point paths, like the patterns sensed by a patch of skin when fingers skim across.  With no multi-touch tablets on the market to capture such patterns, we soon got ‘side-tracked’ building our own, drawing on the same analog VLSI and capacitive circuitry expertise with which we designed silicon neuromorphs.

And the first prototype, financed by the head of the department they worked in,

> Special thanks to former ECE Dept. Chair Neal Gallagher for funding our first prototype from departmental coffers.
> 
> Multitouch research at UD was also supported by grants from the National Science Foundation and the CIA/DCI Post-Doctoral Fellow Program.

Multi-touch technology, however, comes from long before. Coming back to Buxton, whom we cited earlier, one can read there that he attributes the invention of the first multi-touch screen to Bob Boie, from Bell Labs, even though there was quite a lot of earlier work already there: as early as 1982 there existed a multi-touch system designed by Nimish Mehta from the University of Toronto (it wasn't a screen, however). So it cannot be said that Westerman (And the relation with NSF and CIA/DCI programme) was crucial in the invention of multi-touch. But what about gestures?

According to Buxton, not the case either: Westerman's thesis cites works where gestures are already mentioned, such as Myron Krueger's VideoPlace, a digital artist ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dmmxVA5xhuo)).

Once again, reality is more complex than Mazzucato's account, even if we are just reading her same sources.

## SIRI

Here I don't question Mazzucato's interpretation, but I will point out that similar technology, developed separately, exists: Cortana from Microsoft, and Google Now, from Google. ## LCD screens

According to Mazzucato, the key development for LCD screens happens in the 70s, when the thin film transistor (TFT) is created in Westinghouse under Peter Brody's direction. This was almost completely financed by the US Army according to Hart and Borrus 1992. Westinghouse executives ended the investigation at some point, and Brody tried to ask other companies to finance his research. He asked Xerox, 3M, IBM, DEC and Compaq, and none wanted to buy screens from him because his they doubted he would be able to manufacture them at a reasonable cost in relative to his Japanese competitors, according to Florida and Browdy 1991. In 1988, after securing a 7.8 million dollar contract from DARPA, he sets up Magnascreen to develop the TFT-LCD technology. Florida and Browdy see the LCD episode as a problem in the American innovation system: both big corporations and venture capitalists didn't develop the product, even though they had made possible the semiconductor and personal computer industries (Which would contradict other things Mazzucato says about semiconductors and computers, but that is not the present article's topic).

To retain leadership in TFT-LCD and keep manufacturing in the country, the main manufacturers launch a consortium, ADMARC, with support and funds from the National Institute of Standards and Technology,  antidumping tariffs against the Japanese, and also contracts with many public agencies, both civilian and military, to buy these screens.

My lecture of this episode is basically that a group of companies successfully lobbies the US government to resist the superior Japanese technology. Having a better and cheaper alternative, it is not irrational to reject investing in TFT-LCD. Maybe the time for that would come later, and the project should have been carried out by long term research channels (research institutes and universities).

Let's now read the evidence cited by Mazzucato. First, Hart and Borrus 1992.

> The primary alternatives to CRTs for television displays are liquid crystal displays (LCDs). **LCDs were developed initially in the Pittsburgh laboratories of Westinghouse and at the David Sarnoff Research Center** (then owned by RCA) in Princeton, New Jersey. George Heilmeier and Richard Williams of the Sarnoff lab first put forward the idea that liquid crystals could be used for displays in 1963. **Jim Fergason at Westinghouse began to pursue his own approach to LCDs in 1964**. Fergason left Westinghouse in 1970 to found a new company called Liquid Xtal, which later failed due to delays in obtaining patents. Peter Brody, also at Westinghouse, pioneered “active matrix” addressing of LCDs. Brody left Westinghouse in 1979 and two years later founded his own firm, Panelvision, which was sold to Litton Industries in 1985.

The paragraph taken to support the book's affirmation does fit within the explanation given:

> In 1972-77, Davies managed the laboratory at Westinghouse which was working on active matrix flat panels. Peter Brody — the originator of the idea of active matrix — was one of Davies’ engineers. The lab had about 25 people funded mostly by money from the U.S. Army. Almost no Westinghouse funds were used. Army Electronics Command wanted electroluminescent (EL) displays because of their utility for night-time viewing. LCD displays were not considered appropriate for military applications. In addition, Westinghouse engineers in the Western Electric Tube Division in Elmira, New York, blocked commercial development of flat panel displays within the company. The money from the government was too little in the wrong place to develop active matrix TFT-LCDs.

It seems that screens had a relatively independent development, but that the step to LCD-TFT was totally financed by the Army. However, that technology was invented in Radio Corporation of America by Bernard J. Lechner (Kawamoto 2012) and implemented by his team in 1968, in a 2x18 matrix. Brody and his colleagues join the fray in 1974, and finally in 1988, Sharp produces a 14 inch full colour screen, that convinces the industry that there is future in the LCD-TFT technology.

Brody actually founded two companies. Before Magnavision he founded [Panelvision](https://books.google.es/books?id=yC4EAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA43&lpg=PA43&dq=Panelvision+Corporation&source=bl&ots=CuoBn2IHw7&sig=NCkY8micVdBcY-66MOwwSxNpQ6M&hl=es&sa=X&ei=_7GJVbrwENefyASF2pPoCg&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=Panelvision%20Corporation&f=false) and that one was successful. T[here it seems he started working](https://books.google.es/books?id=sAEAAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA86&lpg=PA86&dq=%22panelvision+corp%22+pittsburg&source=bl&ots=UiPbIx3g_F&sig=L0ydNcXRDNw32INZdrKN96jQffI&hl=es&sa=X&ei=TryJVZCnOcH--AHU9YGgBA&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=%22panelvision%20corp%22%20pittsburg&f=false)in technology he would later implement for Magnavision without success, given he required high manufacturing volumes to make it profitable.

Let's now check Florida and Browdy 1991

There we find that originally, Westinghouse wasn't developing screens alone:

> Westinghouse was not alone in these early days of fiat-panel display development. RCA had large scale efforts in both thin-film technology and flatpanel displays. Other companies-including General Electric, Hughes Aircraft, Raytheon, Zenith, Burroughs, Owens-Illinois, and IBM-were also active in the field. But most of them abandoned their efforts when they failed to come up with a way to produce inexpensive, manufacturable flat-panel displays. By the early 1970s, Westinghouse had the field almost to itself.

They also indicate that their work wasn't entirely dependent on military contracts:

> After making the rounds of Westinghouse divisions, Brody got several to sign on in support of the thin-film transistor research. (In the interim, he received military contracts to keep his work going.) At Westinghouse, his biggest supporters were the consumer-electronics division and the electron-tube division. Consumer Electronics was a large and powerful organization with a long history in radios, televisions, and home appliances: The division saw flat-panel displays as away for Westinghouse to gain ground on KCA and others in the television business, where Westinghouse was losing market share.

And against Mazzucato, Brody did manage investments after leaving Westinghouse after founding Panelvision, the company the economist does not mention,

> Brody then got the attention of Wall Street venture capitalist Bruce K. Anderson of the venture-capital fund of Welsh, Carson, Anderson and Stowe. Anderson suggested that one of his major limited partners might be willing to fund the outfit. That limited partner turned out to be 3M. Even after being told about the previous turndown, Anderson still decided to proceed. In the brief interim since rejecting Brody’s earlier proposal, 3M had restructured. A new vicepresident now headed technology development, and the venture-capital firm’s proposal became his first opportunity to launch a visible new project. As a major producer of overhead projectors, 3M wanted to use active-matrix technology to make LCD overhead-projector screens. The board of directors took only three weeks to approve an investment of $1.5- million. In November 1980, the new company, called Panelvision, was launched. Panelvision bought equipment from Westinghouse’s old thin-film transistor labs. By the summer of 1981, the firm had rented a building in a Pittsburgh suburb near the Westinghouse R&D center, and begun developing a process for manufacturing active-matrix display products. Seemingly on the verge of pilot production, the company got an additional infusion of venture capital, bringing total investment to almost $4-million. \[…\]
> 
> the company became reasonably successful. Between 1979 and 1984, it raised roughly $13-million in six or seven rounds of financing from heavyweight venture capitalists such as Welsh, Carson, Anderson, and Stowe; Drexel Burnham; First Chicago’s venture arm; and several Boston-area concerns. More significant, Panelvision became the first company to bring active-matrix display screens to market. In 1984, the firm began selling experimental products and lab prototypes. They soon had 80 customers in 12 industry segments.

Now, the goal was to increase production to make the company profitable, but with the Japanese competition, that seemed difficult

> But it was impossible to break even, much less turn a profit, selling on such small scale. The company needed to develop a real manufacturing process and high-volume production capability-and this required more capital. After squabbles between the board and management over how to do this, the board hired Panelvision’s third president in three years, Tim DeSilva. Armed with a new business plan, the company aimed to raise $5-million and move into larger-volume production.

Seiko introduced a color pocket television in the United States, infringing on the original Westinghouse patents for active-matrix displays, to which Panelvision held exclusive rights. The International Trade Commission encouraged Panelvision to bring suit. The company started this process in motion, alerting Seiko of a potential lawsuit. Japan’s entry sounded the death knell for Panelvision. Investors had already been hesitant about moving from R&D into volume production. Now they thought it utterly foolish to try to compete with the Japanese on their strong suit of manufacturing efficiency. The board of directors decided to recoup its investment by putting the firm up for sale. A team from 3M evaluated the firm and recommended taking it over, but top management declined. In 1985, Panelvision was sold to Litton Industries, which wanted to use the active-matrix technology in aircraft-cockpit displays.

Panelvision ended up being bought by Litton, who instead of producing screens for the consumer market, investing in technological improvements, chose to dedicate to the defense market:

> Litton-Panelvision began to produce display products for its own defense avionics systems but never ventured into the commercial markets. And while Litton made some significant improvements, it was not in the business of advancing the technology.

It is now when Brody tries again with a new company, but it seems Body wasn't able to promise attractive prices compared to the Japaneses'

> Several major US computer makers were excited by the possibilities offered by flat-panel displays. Apple, IBM, DEC, and Compaq each indicated that they would place big orders, but shrank from becoming involved in the extremely expensive undertaking of building a factory that could produce large volumes of flat-panel displays. They believed it was not the job of computer firms to create their own supplier base, especially since they could buy flatpanel displays from the Japanese. The most receptive company was Apple, which was planning its Macintosh portable. Enthusiastic about active-matrix displays, Apple told Brody to bring back a proposal for a factory capable of producing 50,000 units a month. But Apple balked at the price tag. The company ultimately decided to buy screens for its portable Mac from a Japanese supplier.

But he went on and in 1987 he founded Magnascreen, with the objective of manufacturing screens for the defense sector

> Brody decided to rethink his strategy. The Japanese had been concentrating on small displays (10 to 14 inches across), for laptop computers. Brody decided to develop larger (20- to 40-inch) displays for use in military command-and-control systems and in corporate teleconferencing. Brody saw large displays as the key to the next big frontier-high-definition television. The idea was to create a large screen out of smaller active-matrix “tiles.”\[…\]
> 
> The new company, called Magnascreen, attracted significant funding from individual investors close to Wiesner and Leghorn-including John Sculley of Apple and a former chairman of Xerox-and from Ven-West, the venture-capital arm of Westinghouse. And confirming what I mentioned earlier, for a different case, the DARPA contract seems to be the end result of a lobbying process (and not entrepreneurial state activity)

> Despite difficulty raising money, Brody launched Magnascreen in 1988. The company bought Panelvision’s original Pittsburgh facility from Litton, and Brody rehired his old collaborator Tom Maloney. At the time, DARPA was headed by Craig Fields, who strongly supported industrial policy-the idea that government should channel money to develop technologies key to the nation’s competitiveness. Some companies, trying to launch a recovery of their LCD manufacturing sector in the US, set up ADMARC, that Mazzucato mentions, with about 1.25 million dollars from NIST:

> Last year, a group of 10 small companies-Cherry Electrical Products, Coloray Display Corporation, Electro-plasma, Magnascreen, Optical Imaging Systems, Photonics, Planar Systems, Plasmaco, Standish Industries, and Tektronix-banded together to form the Advanced Display Manufacturers of America Research Consortium (ADMARC) to develop flat-screen technology. But the problem was not research and development, but manufacturing!

> While such consortia are a move in the right direction, they are not the answer for the US display. industry. Research consortia, by their nature, focus on high-end R&D or advanced development work-so-called generic or precompetitive technology. They have not had great success in manufacturing, where US industry is weakest. Indeed, we may well see a repeat of the computer-memory-chip story.

The article, with somewhat of a mercantilist tone, ends with a recommendation for American corporations: to apply Continual Improvement (aka Kaizen). The problem wasn't technologica, says the article, it was organizational:

> US investments in manufacturing must be coupled with deep organizational and management changes. The (mainly Japanese) companies that have succeeded in active-matrix technology have applied a basic formula: continuous process improvements on the factory floor. In these companies, R&D scientists and engineers work alongside factory workers to make sure the manufacturing process works. The factory is a center for innovation, change, and constant refinement. Such perseverance has, more than any other single factor, spelled success for the Japanese in active-matrix technology. This is where we failed and continue to fail.

After this series of interventions, as of today,[the US has not managed to lead](http://www.displaysearch.com/cps/rde/xchg/displaysearch/hs.xsl/130409_smartphone_tablet_pc_demand_drive_small_medium_flat_panel_display_revenue.asp) LCD screen manufacturing, yet we have ever better and cheaper screen of many different sizes. If one looks at the world from a mercantilist point of view, it certainly is a defeat for the US and its supposed mercantilist policy (and its Entrepreneurial State). But if one sees it from the point of view of consumers all over the world, they have won. Mazzucato criticises investors and American companies for avoiding battling the Japanese, and that that's a failure on their side. I would criticise her for seeing that as a problem.

As an additional note: If  tariffs and the like had been successful, would Mazzucato score a goal for the US Entrepreneurial State, saying that it wasn't possible for Japanese to develop LCD screens instead?

## Batteries

Next example are lithium batteries. John B. Goodenough was the researcher who opened the path in that secor, receiving funding from the Department of Energy and the National Science Foundation in the late eighties, according to Henderson 2004 and OSTI 2009. Later on, scientific developments in the University of Texas were commercialised by Sony in 1991.  In a NIST conference in 2005, Ralph J. Brodd identified problems similar to those we saw in the case of LCD panels, and blames corporate and investor short termism for them. Says Brodd that the federal government helped small companies through a variety of agencies and rogrammes to strenghten their productive capacity.

Let's read. In Henderson 2004 and OSTI 2009 we find that Goodenough started developing his technology in Oxford University, before going to the University of Texas, even though it was there where he improved his designs. His international fame, however, did came from his work at Oxford: the invention of lithium-cobalt oxide batteries. In general, the [history of batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery#History) shows the usual pattern: many development centers, in many parts of the world.

Brodd 2005 confirms what we said about Goodenough and Oxford. With regards to state support of small businesses, it is there, but it doesn't seem to have helped much except to develop batteries for some niches (defence and healthcare). We may wonder what was the cost of the programme.

In this[Reuters chart](http://graphics.thomsonreuters.com/310/JP_LITON0310.gif), we can see who manufactures Li-ion batteries today, and they are still the Japanese, plus Koreans. ame as in the case of LCD screens: Batteries are being manufactured and improved. Does it matter where they are manufactured?

Some could argue that Sony's battery is an outcome fostered by Japan's Entrepreneurial State. According to [Sony](http://www.sony.net/SonyInfo/CorporateInfo/History/SonyHistory/2-13.html#block3), the history of their battery is this. Trying to argue that is left as an exercise for the reader.

## Fast and Furious: The Fast Fourier Transform

Mazzucato:

> The Office of Science and Technology Policy (2006, 8) also documented the role of State support in the digital signal processing (DSP) technology that came about following scientific advancements in the application of the fast Fourier transform (FDT) algorithm during the 1980s. This new signal processing approach enabled real-time processing of sound (such as during a two-way phone call) as well as real-time processing of large audio or multimedia files that can improve the quality of their playback.

Says the report that it was in 1965 when the FFT revolutionizes signal processing. Searching, I find that she means the rediscovery, by Cooley and Tukey, of Gauss' algorithm to calculate the FFT (Heideman 19849, a century before Gauss. Note that since Gauss, advancements had been made in FFT, and that Cooley and Tukey represented just one step in that chain. Rediscovering Gauss with[this paper](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cme324/classics/cooley-tukey.pdf), which yes, financed by the Army Research Office, doesn't seem that important if you want to argue for the role of the ARO in the discovery, given that the authors worked for Princeton University (Cooley was also an IBM employee, and Tukey a Princeton professor). A mathematical advance doesn't seem to require huge quantities of resources, and Princeton and IBM are and were financially sound entities, which were supporting whatever research Tukey and Cooley were doing at the moment. If even after this is said one still wants to argue that this event is due to ARO funding, with more reason you will have to say that we have this algorithm thanks to Princeton and IBM, and then with that same reason the algorithm is a private sector triumph. Now, this delves into concepts of causality, which we will addressed in future posts, so if you are not convinced by the above reasoning, I hope you will find it more plausible after I explain the implicit view of causality I'm taking here.

## DRAM

In this case, the chart points us to 1960-70 and the development of VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) technology. It points us to basic science funding of IBM and DARPA. Mazzucato doesn't devote much lines to talk about this and neither will I. You can read the history of DRAM here and see how much state financing it had (Which isn't even mentioned). In Itoh (2008) I cannot find state influence either. We find IBM, Intel and Hitachi. It doesn't seem plausible to affirm that DRAM was a Entrepreneurial State creation, given the evidence supplied by her and the additional evidence found by me. It could be argued that VLSI was a Entrepreneurial State creation, and via that, that there was an indirect influence in DRAM. But this belongs to the category of semiconductors, and along the points I'm not addressing here (Which aren't that central for the iPhone), I'll discuss them in a separate article.

## Cellular technology

In the chart it is said that the US Army supported this technology and the Breakthough Report (2010) is cited to support this affirmation. The report says

> The early foundation of cellular communication lies in radiotelephony capabilities progressively advanced throughout the 20th century with support from the U.S. military

But doesn't ground that in anything at all, nor it expands on that point. Using evidence this poor to affirm something as controversial in a written book is somewhat sloppy. Let's be charitable and examine other evidence we could have. Let's go beyond Mazzucato. Where does cellular communication come from?

In Gosh (ch. 1,2010) we find no state influence in technological development of cellular technology - It is present in the GSM protocol, but that came later, and certainly not from the US Army. Could it be the [Mobile Subscriber Equipment](http://thesoldiersnetwork.com/army-innovations-building-the-first-cellular-network/) (MSE) from General Dynamics, developed in 1985 for the US Army? According to Ghosh, in 1983 there were already commercial cellular services in Chicago, in 1979 in Japan, and Bell Labs had already fleshed out the concept in 1960-70. So it seems dubious.

Again, it is left as an exercise for the reader to try to find a link between the development of this technology and the State.

## Protectionism

The third Mazzucatian point was that the American State had protected Apple's intellectual property, and fought to open the international market for them. It is the government they go to when global conflicts occur. Says that Apple had problems accessing the Japanese market in the eighties, and that it was able to enter it thanks to US government support. Also, Apple receives a series of sical benefits, R&D incentives, and State purchase of their products via public schools.

But even accepting all of this, we would have to quantify it to see if it is really useful for Apple, or for society in general. Intellectual property is something companies who manufacture iPhone clones don't seem to care about, and could be more detrimental than beneficial. Apple patent wars vs the world, patents in hand, are really useful to allow technological progress? Doubtful (Boldrin & Levine 2002)

On the other side, the best that can be done with this point, even if it were correct (that is, that it happened as Mazzucato says, and that it did help Apple) is to criticise it, and ask for an end to corporate welfare, that even from a simple efficiency point of view doesn't seem much beneficial, at least until Mazzucato presents us with solid evidence.

A related point is that this whole problem is generated by the kind of policies Mazzucato seem to endorse, or at least don't oppose. Maybe mercantilist policies such as tariffs and nascent industry protection can foster a national industry in that sector. Or just decrease overall welfare, depending on its success, and they usually aren't succesful, as Park Chung-hee's don't abund. Even then, if we take a global perspective, we should care not about national industry, but about global consumer welfare, thus it doesn't matter much where a technology is developed, but that it is developed, and that it can be exported and sold all over the world.

## Conclusion

The chapter of the book ends like this

> In sum, ‘finding what you love’ and doing it while also being ‘foolish’ is much easier in a country in which the State plays the pivotal serious role of taking on the development of high-risk technologies, making the early, large and high-risk investments, and then sustaining them until such time that the later-stage private actors can appear to ‘play around and have fun’. Thus, while ‘free market’ pundits continue to warn of the danger of government ‘picking winners’, it can be said that various US government policies laid the foundation that provided Apple with the tools to become a major industry player in one of the most dynamic high-tech industries of the twenty-first century so far. Without the frequent targeted investment and intervention of the US government it is likely that most would-be ‘Apples’ would be losers in the global race to dominate the computing and communications age. The company’s organizational success in integrating complex technologies into user-friendly and attractive devices supplemented with powerful software mediums should not be marginalized, however it is indisputable that most of Apple’s best technologies exist because of the prior collective and cumulative efforts driven by the State; which were made in the face of uncertainty and often in the name of, if not national security, then economic competitiveness.

Basically this final paragraph is false. State interventions in science that were conducive to the iPhone were neither crucial, nor entrepreneurial, not as numerous as Mazzucato tries to show.

Let us ask again what makes the iPhone the iPhone. Mazzucato could have tried to say that the State invented the mobile phone, or the smartphone, but she chose the iPhone (Because the controversy whe supposed it would generate, and did generate). But the truth is, most of the things we have talked about here are somewhat irrelevant to answer this question. If we take an iPhone and any other high end smartphone (especially from the time the iPhone was released), we won't find the difference in the screen, internal memory or GPS. Most smartphones have that.

What makes the iPhone the iPhone, a product that managed to put Apple in the position it is, is design, its iOS operating system, a correct technological integration, and a careful quality assurance process. All this factors are essentially internal to Apple itself. Without these things we would have an smartphone that wouldn't be an iPhone. That is what it gave Apple its comparative advantage, the base of its huge revenues. Mazzucato addresses in other chapter -we'll come to that in other article- the question of whether the State deserves part of Apple's benefits. To this, a triple criticism can be made: First, the one presented in this article. The role of the State is not the one Mazzucato talks about. Second, those extra profits, comparatively, are not to be attributed, in the case Mazzucato were right, to state reserach, but to internal company factors. And third, the ethical premises Mazzucato uses to argue (Any recommendation implies an ought) for Apple owing something to the State are dubious.

Technology in general, and the iPhone as a particular case is a complex story. Talking of who invented this, or who discovered that usually hides many nuances that we shouldn't obviate when we study the history of technology. And of course, talking about an Entrepreneurial State to whom we owe the phone that launched the smarthphone revolution doesn't have basis in available evidence. The State was one more actor, like IBM, Bell Labs, Sony, Goodenough, Brody, or Lechner.

Science and Technologies have heroes who make it go forward step by step, not Gods without which progress is impossible. We are right to discard the Great Man Theory of Innovation, where we owe individual geniuses for discoveries only them could have made, but for the same reason we should also discard the Great State Theory.
