# Silly semantics arguments

---

## 正文

在[我正在修读的一门课程](http://christakis.med.harvard.edu/pdf/teaching/Soc190Syllabus.pdf)中——这门关于 sociology of medicine 的 Sociology 190 由 Nicholas Christakis 讲授——我们花了很多时间探讨“什么是 disease？”预期的 conclusion 似乎是：Disease 是 Socially Constructed 的；还有，我们不断 Socially Constructing 并非“Real Diseases”的 New Diseases，这难道不可怕吗，社会正在走向衰败，呜呼哀哉！我觉得这种态度很不妥，这些 debates 也相当愚蠢。据我所知，其实没有人对 facts 有异议；我们只是在争论 connotations。

几周前，Christakis 教授给我们所有人发了一份 survey，在其中列出了一些事物（cancer、short stature、large nose、homosexuality、nuclear war 等），并针对每一项问：“这是 disease 吗？”以及“这是 doctors 需要 concern 的事吗？”对于许多 conditions，学生们对这两个问题的看法大相径庭。这很好地 evidence 了我们关注错了对象。我们是否认定某种事物“是 disease”，除了它是否属于 doctors 需要 concern 的事之外，几乎没有什么 material consequences。而后一个问题容易讨论得多，因为我们可以提出具体问题（如果 doctors 了解这种 condition，patients 会有更好的 health outcomes 吗？doctors 对它的 concern 会不会对 privacy 等方面产生不良 side effects？），而不是争论背后毫无 motivation 的 definitions。

在最近一次讨论课上，我们花了很长时间争论 internet addiction 是否应该算作 disease。在整场 debate 中，我们都没能 isolate 出任何人的 concrete beliefs 之间哪怕一处 difference。我们实际上争论的 hidden question 是：“internet addiction 是否足够 serious/natural/damaging/不管什么，以至于它配得上从‘disease’这个词获得的 connotations？”问题在于，这些 connotations 因人而异，差别极大；而且无论如何，争论它们都不会有什么结果——除了知道“internet addiction afflicts 中国 10% 的 college students”[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:1)，或者“internet addiction 与那些和 drugs 等引起的 physiological addictions 的 neural activity patterns 相似的 neural activity patterns 存在 associated with 的关系”[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:2)之外，知道“internet addiction 是否是 disease”并不会让你额外获得什么。

这也不只是一个 semantics 问题。Sociology 190 不断强化这样一种观念：相关的是某事是否为“disease”，而不是它是否属于 doctors 需要 concern 的事；这会造成 confusion，还可能让学生对 medical establishment 产生毫无根据的负面看法。当然，我赞同 Christakis 教授的 thesis 的主要观点，即 doctors 是 fallible 的，因此，作为一个社会，我们应该尽量避免对太多事物进行 [medicalizing](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicalization)，因为存在 [iatrogenesis](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iatrogenesis) 的 risk。[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:3) 但当我们以“diseases”是“socially constructed”的方式思考时，人们得到的信息却是：几乎任何 newly-defined disease 都是 illegitimate 的，这实在是 harmful。我不在乎人们是否把 internet addiction 称为 disease，但当 South Korean high schooler 平均每周花 23 hours 在 gaming 上时，doctors（或者至少 health officials）最好还是对此 concerned。

这种 pattern——把 value arguments 偷偷塞进听起来像 factual 的东西里——其实相当常见。当人们争论“什么是 art？”时，他们做的也是类似的事：争论到底什么配得上被称为 art 所获得的 halo。这样的 argument 并不十分 productive。大多数其他具有强烈 connotations 或 halo effects 的词也是如此，比如“love”“freedom”等。这些都是很好的 examples，说明在什么情况下，对你正在争论的那个词进行 [taboo](http://lesswrong.com/lw/nu/taboo_your_words/)，并尝试用 lower-level terms 来 rephrase，会有所帮助。

## 原文

In [a course I’m taking](http://christakis.med.harvard.edu/pdf/teaching/Soc190Syllabus.pdf) on the sociology of medicine—Sociology 190, taught by Nicholas Christakis—we’ve spent a lot of time investigating “what is a disease?” The desired conclusion seems to be that Disease Is Socially Constructed; also, that Isn’t It Terrible How We Keep Socially Constructing New Diseases That Aren’t “Real Diseases”, Society Is Going Down The Tubes, Alas And Alack! I think this is an unfortunate attitude and these debates are pretty silly. As far as I know, nobody actually disagrees about facts; we’re just arguing about connotations.

A few weeks ago, Prof. Christakis gave us all a survey in which he listed a number of things (cancer, short stature, large nose, homosexuality, nuclear war, etc.) and asked for each “is this a disease?” and “is this a concern of doctors?” For many conditions, students’ opinions on the two questions differed wildly. This is good evidence that we’re focusing on the wrong thing. Whether or not we decide something “is a disease” has few material consequences separate from whether it’s a concern of doctors. And the latter issue is much easier to debate, because we can ask concrete questions (if doctors know about this condition, will patients have better health outcomes? could doctors’ concern for this one have bad side effects for e.g. privacy?) rather than arguing about definitions with no motivation behind them.

In a recent discussion section, we spent a long time arguing whether internet addiction should count as a disease. During the entire debate, we didn’t manage to isolate a single point of difference in anyone’s concrete beliefs. The hidden question we were actually debating was, “is internet addiction sufficiently serious/natural/damaging/whatever that it deserves the connotations it gets from the word, ‘disease’?” The problem is that those connotations vary wildly from person to person, and anyway, arguing about them doesn’t get you anywhere—you gain nothing from knowing whether “internet addiction is a disease” separately from knowing that “internet addiction afflicts 10% of college students in China”[1](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:1) or “internet addiction is associated with neural activity patterns similar to those of physiological addictions to e.g. drugs”[2](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:2).

This isn’t just a semantics problem, either. By reinforcing the idea that being a “disease”, rather than a concern of doctors, is relevant, Sociology 190 causes confusion and can give students an unwarrantedly poor opinion of the medical establishment. Now, I agree with the primary thrust of Prof. Christakis’ thesis, which is that doctors are fallible, and so as a society we should try to avoid [medicalizing](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicalization) too many things because of the risk of [iatrogenesis](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iatrogenesis).[3](https://www.benkuhn.net/silly-semantics-arguments/#fn:3) But when we think in terms of “diseases” being “socially constructed”, people come away with the message that almost any newly-defined disease is illegitimate, which is downright harmful. I don’t care whether people call internet addiction a disease, but when the average South Korean high schooler spends 23 hours a week gaming, doctors (or at least health officials) had better be concerned.

This pattern—sneaking value arguments into things that sound factual—actually comes up quite a bit. When people debate about “what is art?”, they’re doing a similar thing, arguing about what exactly deserves the halo it gets from being called art. That’s not a very productive argument to have. Similarly with most other words with strong connotations or halo effects, like “love”, “freedom”, etc. These are great examples of when it’s helpful to [taboo](http://lesswrong.com/lw/nu/taboo_your_words/) whatever word you’re arguing about and try to rephrase in lower-level terms.
