# My weekly review habit

---

## 正文

每个星期六早晨，我都会花 3-4 小时思考这一周过得怎么样，以及如何让下一周过得更好。

具体形式一直在变化，不过，举例来说，以下是我上周反思的一些事情：

-   我注意到，自己的 written output 远未达到目标。我决定这周给阅读分配更多时间，希望这样能产生更多想法。我还重新安排了 morning routine，让自己更容易在早晨开始写作。

-   我查看了 [RescueTime](https://www.rescuetime.com/rp/ben) 和 [Complice](https://complice.co/?r=6ct6ukjyqf) 中的一些 stats，了解自己把时间花在了什么事情上，以及完成了什么。我注意到，自己花在 Slack 上的时间已接近危险程度，于是决定做几项 experimental tweaks，把它降下来：

    -   我试用了 [Contexts](https://contexts.co/)，它可以替代 macOS window switcher。我将它 configured 为只 show 当前 workspace 中的 windows，希望这样能防止自己 cmd+tabbing 到 Slack，分散注意力。

    -   我决定做一个 experiment：当同事在我的 focused block of time 中途打电话过来时，不立即接听，并在纸上保留一份“todo when done focusing” list，提醒自己之后给他们回电话、查看 Slack 等。

-   我注意到，自己似乎很难从 RescueTime 和 Complice 的 time-tracking data 中获取有用信息，于是重新思考自己究竟想回答哪些问题，以及怎样才能更容易回答这些问题。

    -   我意识到，应该用 Google Calendar，而不是 RescueTime 或 Complice，来 track 自己花在会议上的时间，于是把它加入了自己的 time-tracking data sources。

    -   我还对自己使用 Complice 的方式做了几项 tweaks，让自己更容易看到感兴趣的各种 stats。

诸如此类。到 review 结束时，我已经找出了许多可以在接下来一周落实的其他 improvements。

* * *

虽然每一项 tweak 都很小，但经过一周又一周、一年又一年的 compounded，它们让我变得 effective 得多。因此，这个 weekly review 是我养成的最有用的 habit（或者说，habit-generating meta-habit）。以下是我通过 weekly reviews 做出的一些 improvements：

-   我决定 experiment 一下 time tracking，意识到它需要做到 [zero-effort](https://www.benkuhn.net/zero/) 才能成功，并认定 RescueTime 是最佳选择，让我能通过好得多的 data 了解自己_实际上_是怎样花时间的。

-   开始使用 RescueTime 后，我 eliminated 了它 flagged 的许多 distractions。结果，我[将自己的 focused time improved 了 50%](https://www.benkuhn.net/50pct/)。

-   后来，reviewing RescueTime 的 stats 也帮助我意识到，自己因 Internet 而分心的时间远比原先以为的多。我尝试了各种办法来打破自己的 Internet news habit，最终找到了 [Focus](https://heyfocus.com/)，一个 zero-effort website blocker，它大概已经为我节省了数百小时。

-   我将“冬天晚上感觉 low-energy”认定为一个 blocker，并尝试了几个 experiments，以 improve 自己晚上的 energy levels。其中一个是[一只 ultra-bright lightbulb](https://www.benkuhn.net/lux/)，效果好得惊人，让我每天重新获得了大约一小时。

-   在思考如何帮助伴侣完成她的 PhD 时，我想到了进行 [one-on-ones](https://www.benkuhn.net/11/) 的主意。她认为，这帮助她提前一年完成了 dissertation。

-   通过思考 improve 我们的 relationship 的方法，以及过去一周我们之间的 points of friction，我们两人都在这些 one-on-ones 中发起了许多有用的讨论，帮助我们更好地相互理解和 communicate。

-   我对自己的 daily routine 和 habits 做了数百项 tweaks，以确保自己能 reliably exercise、睡够觉，并 maintain high energy levels。

当然，你并不_需要_养成 weekly review habit 才能想到这类 improvement。但通过 systematically thinking it through，你会产生更多这样的 improvement。而通过 consistently 地这样做，你就能让这些小的 improvements 层层累积。

* * *

我不得不对 weekly review 的形式和时间安排进行多次 iterate，才找到一种让我能 consistently maintain 这个 habit，并 output 有用的 weekly reviews 的方式。我目前采用的形式是：

-   Review 是每个星期六早晨的第一件事。这段时间神圣不可侵犯，而且（基本上）不可挪动。早晨很重要，因为它让我拥有合适的 energy 和 mindset；周末很重要，因为这样我就不会被工作分散注意力；consistency 很重要，因为这样我就不会丢掉这个 habit。

-   我会先重读一些自己最喜欢的人生建议文章的部分内容，以此开始 review。（每周读不同的部分或文章。这有时也会让我注意到文章中之前没留意的部分，它们会引起共鸣，或激发有趣的想法。）

-   接下来，我会通过 reviewing 这一周发生的事情，把这一周重新 load 到 working memory 中。

-   根据上述内容，我会写下一份需要思考的 topics 的 list，并在思考每个 topic 时做 written notes。

-   我还有一组每周都会思考的 recurring prompts。随着它们逐渐失去新意，我会不断 tweak 它们，不过可以举几个例子：

    -   这周我在 core habits（exercise、morning routine、todo system 等）上做到了 consistent 吗？我可以怎样 tweak 它们，让它们更 consistent 或更有用？

    -   这周我做了什么错事，又该如何避免重蹈覆辙？

    -   这周我有多少时间花在了真正属于自己 [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage) 的事情上？对于其他所有事情，我怎样才能 get out of the loop？

* * *

作为附录，下面是一些关于 weekly reviews 的零散 tactical tips：

-   改变 physical environment 有助于我 context-switch 到一种不那么 focused、更 reflective 的 mindset。以前咖啡馆还开门时，我经常去家附近的咖啡馆。在家时，我会换个房间工作，播放不同的背景音乐，等等。

-   我仍然发现，自己在 weekly reviews 期间很容易分心，所以我会确保开始时关掉电脑上其他所有东西。

-   当我拥有关于“这周发生了什么”的 granular、objective data（例如 RescueTime、calendar、todo lists）时，我发现 review 这些 data 很有帮助，因为它们偶尔会让我感到意外。（参见上文关于 RescueTime 的几点。）

-   我发现，一边思考一边 taking notes 非常重要，否则我会忘记自己正在思考什么，或者分散注意力。

-   对于 note-taking，我建议使用 hierarchical bulleted lists，而不是 free-written paragraphs。Lists 更 efficient，因为你可以用 incomplete sentences 来写，并省略 transitions（依靠 bullet hierarchy 让 structure 清楚）。

    Bulleted lists 也更容易 reorder（尤其是当你使用的 app 为此提供 keyboard shortcuts 时），所以，如果你和我一样，它们会让你更 efficiently 地满足自己对所有 lists 进行 stack-ranking 的那种强迫性冲动。

## 原文

Every Saturday morning, I take 3-4 hours to think about how my week went and how I’ll make the next one better.

The format has changed over time, but for example, here’s some of what I reflected on last week:

-   I noticed I’d fallen far short of my goal for written output. I decided to allocate more time to reading this week, hoping that it would generate more ideas. And I reorganized my morning routine to make it easier to start writing in the morning.

-   I looked at some stats from [RescueTime](https://www.rescuetime.com/rp/ben) and [Complice](https://complice.co/?r=6ct6ukjyqf) about what I’d spent time on and accomplished. I noticed that my time spent on Slack was nearing dangerous levels, so I decided to make a couple experimental tweaks to get it down:

    -   I tried out [Contexts](https://contexts.co/), a replacement for the macOS window switcher, which I configured to only show windows from my current workspace—hoping that this would prevent me from cmd+tabbing over to Slack and getting distracted.

    -   I decided to run an experiment of not answering immediately when coworkers called me in the middle of a focused block of time, and keeping a paper “todo when done focusing” list to remind myself to call them back, check Slack, etc.

-   I noticed that it felt hard for me to get useful info from the time-tracking data in RescueTime and Complice, so I revisited what questions I actually wanted to answer and how I could make them easy to answer.

    -   I realized that I should be using Google Calendar, not RescueTime or Complice, to track my time spent in meetings, so I added that to my time-tracking data sources.

    -   I also made several tweaks to the way I used Complice to make it easier to see various stats I was interested in.

And so on. By the end of the review I had surfaced lots of other improvements for the coming week.

* * *

While each individual tweak is small, over the weeks and years they’ve compounded to make me a lot more effective. Because of that, this weekly review is the most useful habit (or habit-generating meta-habit) I’ve built. Here are some of the improvements I’ve made that have come out of weekly reviews:

-   I decided to experiment with time tracking, realized that it needed to be [zero-effort](https://www.benkuhn.net/zero/) to succeed, and identified RescueTime as the best option, giving me much better data on how I was _actually_ spending my time.

-   Once I started using RescueTime, I eliminated a bunch of distractions that it flagged. As a result I [improved my focused time by 50%](https://www.benkuhn.net/50pct/).

-   Later on, reviewing RescueTime stats also helped me realize I was spending much more time distracted by the Internet than I’d realized. I tried various things to break my Internet news habit and eventually found [Focus](https://heyfocus.com/), a zero-effort website blocker which has probably saved me hundreds of hours.

-   I identified “feeling low-energy on winter evenings” as a blocker and tried several experiments to improve my evening energy levels. One of them was [an ultra-bright lightbulb](https://www.benkuhn.net/lux/) which worked amazingly well, giving me back about an hour a day.

-   By thinking about how to help my partner with her PhD, I came up with the idea of doing [one-on-ones](https://www.benkuhn.net/11/), which she thinks helped her finish her dissertation a year faster.

-   By thinking about ways to improve our relationship and points of friction we’ve had over the last week, we’ve both started lots of useful discussions in those one-on-ones that have helped us understand each other and communicate better.

-   I’ve made hundreds of tweaks to my daily routine and habits to make sure I reliably exercise, sleep enough, and maintain high energy levels.

Of course, you don’t _need_ to have a weekly review habit to come up with this type of improvement. But by systematically thinking it through, you’ll generate more of them. And by doing it consistently, you’ll be able to build these small improvements on top of each other.

* * *

I’ve had to iterate a lot on the format and timing of the weekly review to get to one where I can consistently maintain the habit and output useful weekly reviews. The format I currently have is:

-   Review happens first thing every Saturday morning. This time is sacred and (largely) immovable. Morning is really important for having the right energy and mindset; weekend is important so I’m not distracted by work; consistency is important so that I don’t lose the habit.

-   I start the review by re-reading some parts of my favorite essays of life advice. (Different parts/essays every week. This also sometimes gets me to notice new parts of the essays that resonate or spark interesting thoughts.)

-   Next, I load the week back into working memory by reviewing what happened during the week.

-   Based on the above I’ll write down a list of topics to think about, taking written notes on each topic as I think about them.

-   I also have a set of recurring prompts that I think about every week. I tweak them over time as they get stale, but some examples would be:

    -   Was I consistent at my core habits this week (exercise, morning routine, todo system, etc.)? How can I tweak them to be more consistent or more useful?

    -   What did I do this week that was a mistake and how can I avoid repeating it?

    -   How much of this week did I spend on stuff that was truly my [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage)? For everything else, how can I get out of the loop?

* * *

As an appendix, some random tactical tips for weekly reviews:

-   Changing my physical environment helps me context-switch into a less focused, more reflective mindset. Back when cafes were open I’d often go to the cafe near my house. At home, I’ll work from a different room, play different background music, etc.

-   I still find it’s easy to get distracted during weekly reviews, so I make sure to close everything else on my computer when I start.

-   When I have granular, objective data on “what happened this week” (e.g. RescueTime, calendar, todo lists) I’ve found it helpful to review that because it occasionally surprises me. (See the points about RescueTime above.)

-   I find that taking notes while I think about things is really important—otherwise I lose track of what I’m thinking about or get distracted.

-   For note-taking, I’d recommend using hierarchical bulleted lists, not free-written paragraphs. Lists are more efficient because you can write in incomplete sentences and leave out transitions (relying on the bullet hierarchy to make the structure clear).

    Bulleted lists are also easier to reorder (especially if you use an app that gives you keyboard shortcuts for it), so if you’re like me, they’ll let you more efficiently exercise your nervous tic of stack-ranking all lists.
