# Threshold hiring（门槛式招聘）

---

## 正文

经常被列为 effective altruism 的一个“core idea”的是 [replaceability](http://80000hours.org/blog/35-the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1)：在 unethical industry 中 take a job 是可以接受的，因为即使你不这样做，也会有其他人这样做，所以无论你是否 take the job，这个 unethical industry 对世界的 counterfactual effect 都是 the same。在实践中，这个 argument 的 impact 往往被极度夸大，至少有三个原因。

## Threshold hiring（门槛式招聘）

许多 firms，包括例如备受 earning-to-give 推崇的 Jane Street，并不会为了 fill quotas 而 hire。他们遇到多少 qualified people，就 hire 多少。如果你在 Jane Street take a job，这不会 cause 他们 deny someone else a job——他们找到能 do the work 的人有多快，expanding 就有多快。Threshold hiring 可能不是最 market-rational 的做法（他们应该在 hiring 上 spending more resources），但是：

1.  STEM talent 存在 extreme shortage，因此，即使 Jane Street 大幅 raised its pay，也很可能无法获得 additional talent。
2.  Resource allocation 是一个足够 hard 的 problem，因此我们应该预期相当程度的 irrationality 会 persist，尤其是在具有 high margins 的 industries 中（因此，促使 purely rational allocation 的 competitive pressure 较小）。
3.  即使这是 irrational，归根结底，他们就是这样做的，所以我们的 decisions 应该以此为依据。

那么，如果你在这些 firms 中的一家 work，在 counterfactual 中，他们就 _actually have fewer resources_，也就能 do fewer unethical things。（注：另外，我也坚信 Jane Street 并不 do unethical things，因此它仍然是 earning to give 的一个好选择。）

第 1-2 点也 suggest，threshold hiring 在适合 earning to give 的 firms 中可能尤其 prevalent。我在 software 和 finance 方面的经历与此 consistent，但也高度 anecdotal。

## Supply and demand（供给与需求）

即使你为一家不 practice threshold hiring 的 firm work（也就是说，它像一个 rational microeconomic actor 那样具有 demand curve），replaceability 仍然不 hold。

让我们 consider 真正的 economists 所 use 的最 simplest model：存在一条由许多 firms 构成的 labor 的 demand curve，以及一条由许多人（即 applicants）构成的 supply curve。通过 applying，你会 shift the supply curve down。那么，由 producers（applicants）internalized 的 gains 的 proportion 是 `$\frac{e_d}{e_s+e_d}$`，由 consumers（firms）internalized 的 proportion 是 `$\frac{e_s}{e_s + e_d}$`，其中 `$e_s$` 是 elasticity of supply，`$e_d$` 是 elasticity of demand。如果 demand 是 highly elastic（例如，在“threshold hiring”中，talent elasticity of demand 是 infinite），那么 producers 就会 capture 你通过 joining the market 而 create 的 resources 的 gains 中的 significant portion 或全部。即使在许多不 practice threshold hiring 的 markets 中，talent elasticity 也相当 high。

## General illegibility（一般情形下的不可辨识性）

上述 model 是 highly simplified 的。但即使在更 general 的 case 中，firms 的 hiring decisions 也高度 opaque。由于存在 massive information asymmetries，labor market 是 inefficient 的（employees 没有 salary data；firms 无法 evaluate employees）。像所链接的文章试图做的那样，cleanly and generally 地 Evaluating the counterfactual，几乎是不可能的。Replaceability 可能在 specific cases 中 apply，但它所 depends 的，远远更多是 details，而不是对 labor movement 的 general considerations。

## Conclusion（结论）

在大多数实际情况下，replaceability consideration 不 hold，或者根本无法 assess。altruistically 地 choosing their career 的人，在 applying 它时应该谨慎，而且在任何情况下，都不应该将它用作针对 earning to give 所受到的“ends-justify-the-means” objections 的完全 general（甚至 reasonably general）的 counterargument。很可能存在其他 counterarguments，足以 show，earning to give 并不是 net negative，而且 counterfactual reasoning 的 general principle 是 sound 的，但 replaceability consideration 本身高度 suspect。

## 原文

A commonly-listed “core idea” of effective altruism is [replaceability](http://80000hours.org/blog/35-the-replaceability-effect-working-in-unethical-industries-part-1): it’s okay to take a job in an unethical industry because if you don’t, someone else will, so the counterfactual effect of the unethical industry on the world is the same whether or not you take the job. In practice, the impact of this argument tends to be extremely exaggerated, for at least three reasons.

## Threshold hiring

Many firms, including e.g. earning-to-give darling Jane Street, do not hire to fill quotas. They hire as many qualified people as come their way. If you take a job at Jane Street, it won’t cause them to deny someone else a job—they’re expanding as fast as they find people who can do the work. Threshold hiring may not be the most market-rational thing to do (they should be spending more resources on hiring), but:

1.  There’s an extreme shortage of STEM talent, so plausibly Jane Street couldn’t get additional talent even if it raised its pay substantially.
2.  Resource allocation is a hard enough problem that we should expect a fair amount of irrationality to persist, especially in industries with high margins (hence less competitive pressure towards purely rational allocation).
3.  Even if it is irrational, at the end of the day that’s what they do, so that’s what we should base our decisions on.

If you work at one of these firms, then, in the counterfactual, they _actually have fewer resources_ and can do fewer unethical things. (Note: I separately also strongly believe that Jane Street does not do unethical things, and is hence still a good choice for earning to give.)

Points 1-2 also suggest that threshold hiring may be especially prevalent among firms that are good for earning to give. My experience in software and finance is consistent with this, but also highly anecdotal.

## Supply and demand

Even if you work for a firm that doesn’t practice threshold hiring (i.e. has a demand curve, like a rational microeconomic actor), replaceability still doesn’t hold.

Let’s consider the simplest model that actual economists use: there’s a demand curve for labor made of a bunch of firms, and a supply curve made of a bunch of people (i.e. applicants). By applying, you shift the supply curve down. Then the proportion of the gains internalized by producers (applicants) is `$\frac{e_d}{e_s+e_d}$` and the proportion internalized by consumers (firms) is `$\frac{e_s}{e_s + e_d}$`, where `$e_s$` is the elasticity of supply and `$e_d$` is the elasticity of demand. If demand is highly elastic (e.g. in “threshold hiring,” talent elasticity of demand is infinite) then producers capture a significant portion or all of the gains in resources that you create by joining the market. Even in many markets that don’t practice threshold hiring, talent elasticity is rather high.

## General illegibility

The above model is highly simplified. But even in the more general case, firms’ hiring decisions are highly opaque. The labor market is inefficient due to massive information asymmetries (employees don’t have salary data; firms can’t evaluate employees). Evaluating the counterfactual cleanly and generally, as the linked post attempts to do, is nearly impossible. Replaceability may apply in specific cases, but it depends far, far more on the details than on general considerations of labor movement.

## Conclusion

In most practical circumstances, the replaceability consideration fails to hold or is impossible to assess. People choosing their career altruistically should be cautious when applying it, and should under no circumstances use it as a fully (or even reasonably) general counterargument to “ends-justify-the-means” objections to earning to give. There are likely other counterarguments sufficient to show that earning to give is not a net negative, and the general principle of counterfactual reasoning is sound, but the replaceability consideration itself is highly suspect.
