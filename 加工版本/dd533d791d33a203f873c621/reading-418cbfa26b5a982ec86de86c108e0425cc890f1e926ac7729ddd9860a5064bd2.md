## 核心洞察

一、YC筛选标准揭示创始人核心特质：Y Combinator合伙人寻找的创始人特质出奇简单：擅长动手建造东西，并且有持续建造的习惯。这一标准直接指向创业最难的部分——产品，即知道该造什么以及能够造出来。这种知识来自计算机科学、机械工程或分子生物学等学科，而非管理或金融课程。因此大学培养创始人的正确方式不是增设创业课程，而是做好它本来就擅长的事：教授这些硬学科。
[[#^pr-d9662f3ab371|正文跳转]]

二、建造能力应作广义理解：建造不限于工程学科。任何可被描述为建造或创造的专长都可能有用，例如乔布斯学的书法后来成为苹果主导桌面出版的原因之一。数学、科学、工程和设计是常见的好选择，但不应画一条硬线排除其他形式的建造。扎克伯格擅长编程却是心理学专业，说明专业标签不等于实际能力。
[[#^pr-7d09ea4081e1|正文跳转]]

三、大学只需改变两件事：大学要在培养创始人上做到近乎完美，只需做两件事：让学生感到创办初创公司是他们能做的事，以及鼓励他们做自己的项目。前者解决信念分布不均的问题，后者解决实践机会不足的问题。这两项改变不需要新课程、新建筑或新院长，成本极低但效果可能极大。
[[#^pr-c89f67b6ac3a|正文跳转]]

四、创业信念分布不均的证据：YC申请数据可作为不同大学对创业兴趣的代理指标：哈佛校友的申请率约为耶鲁和普林斯顿校友的两倍。哈佛学生与耶鲁、普林斯顿学生想必没有本质差异，差异只在于哈佛的创业氛围更浓厚。这意味着耶鲁和普林斯顿仅通过让学生感到创业是可行选项，就可能将创业人数翻倍。
[[#^pr-74f61fd98973|正文跳转]]

五、见到真实创始人能同时激发向往与可达感：在现实生活中见到创始人会戳破“创业是别人做的事”的泡沫。创始人看起来既令人印象深刻又有人性，尤其当他们谈论早期无知和犯错经历时。这种双重感受让学生同时觉得成为其中一员既令人向往又触手可及，产生“我想成为那样，而且我可以”的念头。
[[#^pr-e1cdb9b791f7|正文跳转]]

六、年轻创始人比亿万富翁更有激励效果：创始人对学生的激励程度大致等于其富有和出名程度除以他们比学生年长多少。因此不必把著名亿万富翁请到校园。二十多岁中期、在一家估值数亿的初创公司工作三年的创始人同样有效；他们可能只有前者二十分之一的财富和名气，但学生认同他们要容易二十倍。
[[#^pr-55e5731ae782|正文跳转]]

七、做项目是深度学习的最佳方式：学生做自己的项目有四个理由。第一，这是真正深入理解一个学科的最佳方式，创造新事物的兴奋感比考试恐惧是强大得多的动力。第二，一起做项目是联合创始人发现彼此的最佳方式，苹果和微软只是创始人共同做过的众多项目中的最后几个。第三，初创公司本身就是一个项目，习惯做项目的人创业会感到自然。第四，随机副项目正是最佳创业点子的来源。
[[#^pr-130e95faae13|正文跳转]]

八、最佳创业点子来自看似随机的副项目：最佳创业点子往往一开始显得如此不可信，以至于任何刻意寻找创业点子的人都会拒绝它们。谁会想到通过创建学生名录来创办一家巨型公司？因此发现最佳创业点子的方式不是去寻找创业点子，而是去做任何看起来有趣的随机项目。这些项目远非随机：擅长建造的年轻人是技术风向标，任何对他们显得有趣的念头都极有可能通向有价值的地方。
[[#^pr-e16ec78a7b13|正文跳转]]

九、阅读期制度催生万亿公司：微软和Meta都是在哈佛阅读期期间起步的。阅读期是课程结束和期末考试之间的空档，学生本应用来备考，但它恰好具备适合启动新项目的独特品质组合：学生都在校园，而且第二天没有任何到期任务。后一个约束对最有雄心的学生是巨大拖累，仅仅消除它几周就产生了两个万亿美元公司。
[[#^pr-e32ecdf35a14|正文跳转]]

十、大学应抵制将学生项目官方化：大学应抵制把学生项目纳入官方体系的诱惑。部分原因是学生做完全属于自己的项目会更兴奋，部分原因是许多项目无法在官方认可下存活，因为它们打破了某种规则。比尔·盖茨把非学生保罗·艾伦带进计算机实验室，扎克伯格因Facemash被留校察看，这些案例可能更多是规则而非例外。大学规则繁多，而新颖项目往往是不整洁的东西。
[[#^pr-a41f6559423b|正文跳转]]

十一、还时间给学生的代价与收益不对称：大学担心没有监督学生只会浪费自由时间，而他们确实会。但给最有活力的学生空间做得更好的代价，是让最没活力的学生有空间做得更差。这是值得付出的代价，因为最有活力的学生可以做得远好得多，而最懒的学生已经学得如此少，没有多少空间做得更差。把一些时间还给所有学生可以大幅提高平均结果，即使不改变中位数。
[[#^pr-a9564f854734|正文跳转]]

十二、创业无法被课堂教授：创办初创公司像化学或绘画一样必须边做边学。一门正确运行的创业课必须是实验课，学生实际创办公司。但YC在结构上与大学非常不同，塞进本科项目会变成笑话：学生应在无资金下创业吗？应在修三四门课的同时经营占用每一刻时间的初创公司吗？如果公司起飞，学生应放弃学业还是退学？经营初创公司与全职学生身份不相容。
[[#^pr-908586f68180|正文跳转]]

十三、商业计划竞赛具有积极误导性：商业计划竞赛让学生合作想出一个创业点子，然后向模拟投资者推销。这类练习不仅无用，而且具有积极误导性。它训练创始人认为融资是创业的关键步骤，即创业的核心是创造吸引投资者的故事。但事实并非如此：融资只是必要的恶，你需要打动的是用户而非投资者，打动用户的方式是原型而非文字。创业的核心是创造吸引用户的产品。
[[#^pr-e8233e8da04c|正文跳转]]

十四、最优方案看起来太安静：如果大学真正采取最优措施培养学生创业，表面看起来会太安静：学生在真正感兴趣的课程中获得建造知识，热切地与朋友做与学校无关的副项目，毕业时恰好具备预测创始人成功的特质。然而在家长和潜在学生看来，这所大学似乎什么都没做——创业课程在哪里？创新中心在哪里？解决办法不是避开最优路径，而是坚定立场，让结果自己说话。
[[#^pr-9c7a731064fc|正文跳转]]

十五、商学院只会增加误差：商学院的设计初衷不是培养创始人，而是培养20世纪初大型工业公司的管理阶层，是工业资本主义的西点军校，这就是其官方名称通常是管理学院的原因。它们教的技能在经营超过一定规模的公司时可能有用，但不是创办这些公司的关键成分，而关键技能已由其他院系教授。因此商学院对母大学培养创始人策略的影响只能是通过增加误差来实现。
[[#^pr-0c3eae7a1411|正文跳转]]

## 正文

# 234 How Universities Should Prepare Founders

August 2026

大学应该如何让学生为 start startups 做好准备？Y Combinator 处于回答这个问题的完美位置，因为我们接下来就会得到他们。我们就像 grad school。而且因为 YC 有 20 年时间来完善它关于一个 promising founder 看起来是什么样的 model，你大概找不到更好的目标了。

YC partners 寻找的是什么？这 surprisingly simple。他们想要那些 good at building things 并且有 doing it 的 habit 的人。

^pr-d9662f3ab371

startups 的 hard part 是 product：知道要 build 什么，以及能够 build 它。而那种 knowledge 来自 studying computer science 或 mechanical engineering 或 molecular biology，而不是 management 或 finance。[1](#fn1)

所以让 undergraduates 准备好成为 successful founders 的方式，不是给他们一些聚焦于“entrepreneurship”的 new curriculum。而是去做 universities 已经做得最好的事——教他们 computer science 和 mechanical engineering 和 molecular biology。[2](#fn2)

确实，让学生为 start startups 做好准备，比让他们为几乎任何其他 kind of career 做好准备都更接近 liberal education 的 ideal。Startups 的 succeed 或 fail 取决于 customers 有多喜欢 product。Customers 不在乎 founders 在大学里 studied 什么。所以 founders 可以自由地 study 任何他们想要的东西，只要他们 get good at building things。

但 building 应该在一个非常 broad 的意义上被理解。它并不意味着所有 would-be founders 都必须 study 某种形式的 engineering。几乎任何可以被描述为 building 或 creating 的 expertise 都可能有用。对 Steve Jobs 来说，studied calligraphy 是有用的，例如；这是 Apple dominated desktop publishing 的原因之一。所以虽然 math 和 science 和 engineering 和 design 往往是 good bets，我不想在它们周围画一条 sharp line，因为我能想象其他 forms of building 也可能有用。当然你不必 major in 某个东西才能 be good at 它。Mark Zuckerberg 擅长 programming，但他是 psychology major，不是 CS major。

^pr-7d09ea4081e1

描述 would-be founders 应该 study 什么的最好方式是，他们应该 seek out powerful ideas。但聪明人本来就 naturally attracted to powerful ideas。所以只要 teaching powerful ideas 的 departments 存在，那种会成为 good founders 的人就会找到它们。[3](#fn3)

事实上，universities 只需要改变两件事就能在 preparing founders 上做到完美：它们需要让学生感到 starting a startup 是他们能做的事，并且它们需要鼓励他们 work on 自己的 [projects](https://paulgraham.com/own.html)。

^pr-c89f67b6ac3a

目前，认为 start a startup 是可能的这一 belief 分布得非常不均匀。YC 现在收到如此多的 applications，以至于我们的 application data 是不同 universities 对 startups 的 interest 的一个合理 proxy，而例如 Harvard alumni 的 apply 率大约是 Yale 和 Princeton alumni 的两倍。想必 Harvard students 与 Yale 和 Princeton students 并没有那么不同；Harvard students 后来 start 更多 startups 的原因只是那里更 customary。这反过来意味着，仅仅通过让他们的学生感到 starting a startup 是一个 viable option，Yale 和 Princeton 就至少可以把这样做的人数翻倍。

^pr-74f61fd98973

一旦一个 university 有了 starting startups 的 culture，你就不必说服学生它是一个 viable option。New students 会从 older ones 那里学到这一点。但在一个还没有多少 startup culture 的 university，你可以做一些事情来帮助这一 realization 推进。最有效的可能是向学生展示做过这件事的人的 examples。

直到你在现实生活中见过一些 founders，你往往会认为 starting startups 是别人做的事。见到他们会戳破那个 bubble。事实上，在现实生活中见到 founders 是 doubly inspiring：他们看起来 impressive，但他们也看起来 human。尤其是当他们谈论 early years，那时他们 clueless 并犯了很多 mistakes。所以奇怪的是，在现实生活中见到 founders 会让成为其中一员同时显得 desirable 和 accessible。它让学生想“我想成为那样，而且我可以。”

^pr-e1cdb9b791f7

Founders 对学生有多 inspiring，大致是他们的 rich 和 famous 程度除以他们比学生大多少的函数。所以把 famous billionaires 带到 campus 并不是必需的。二十多岁中期、进入一个 valuation 为几亿的 startup 已有 3 年的 founders 也一样可以；他们可能只有前者二十分之一的 rich 和 famous，但学生认同他们要容易二十倍。

^pr-55e5731ae782

* * *

为什么想要学生 start startups 的 universities 需要让他们相信这是一个 viable option，这很明显。但为什么学生 work on 自己的 projects 如此重要？

有四个 reasons。第一个很简单，那就是它是真正深入地 understand 一个 subject 的绝佳方式，可能是最好的方式。creating something new 的 excitement 比 fear of doing badly on an exam 是强大得多的 motivator。

^pr-130e95faae13

第二，一起 working on projects 是 cofounders 发现彼此的最好方式。最成功的 startups 往往有多个 founders，而确定某人是否 good to work with 的唯一方式就是 work with 他们。Apple 和 Microsoft 只是他们的 founders 一起 worked on 的许多 projects 中的最后几个。

第三，一个 startup 就是一个 project，所以 start 一个对习惯于 work on 自己的 projects 的人来说会感觉很自然。没有 teacher 或 boss 告诉他们该做什么，这不会显得奇怪。他们习惯于告诉自己。

第四，也许最令人惊讶的是，random side projects 正是最好的 startup ideas 的来源。最好的 startup ideas 往往一开始显得如此 implausible，以至于任何有意识地寻找 startup ideas 的人都会 reject 它们。谁会想到通过创建一个 student directory 来 start 一个巨大的 company？所以发现最好的 startup ideas 的方式不是去寻找 startup ideas，而只是 work on 任何看起来有趣的 random projects。因为事实上这样的 projects 远非 random：擅长 building things 的年轻人是 technological bellwethers，所以任何对他们来说显得有趣的 idea 都极有可能通向有价值的地方，即使他们自己还没有意识到。

^pr-e16ec78a7b13

现在应该清楚了，为什么 YC partners 非常关心 applicants 曾经 worked on 的 projects，而完全不在乎他们的 GPAs。Projects 是 knowledge 的最好来源，是 founding teams 的最好来源，也是 startup ideas 的最好来源。

但鼓励学生 work on 自己的 projects 对 universities 来说可能很困难。这将意味着给学生更多 free time，而 universities 可能不喜欢这样做。

Microsoft 和 Meta 有一个很少有人意识到的共同点。它们都是在 Harvard 的 reading period 期间起步的。Reading period 是 classes 结束和 final exams 开始之间的 gap。它被称为 reading period，是因为学生 _supposed_ 用它来 preparing for exams。但 reading period 也恰好具有使其 perfect for starting new projects 的独特品质组合：学生们都在 campus，而且他们第二天没有任何 due 的东西。尤其是后一个 constraint，对最有雄心的学生是一个巨大的 drag。仅仅消除它几周就产生了两个 trillion dollar companies。想象一下，如果 Harvard 的 reading period 长一倍，US GDP 会是多少。

^pr-e32ecdf35a14

Universities 往往会抵制让学生少忙于 coursework 的想法。部分是因为 administrators 觉得，如果他们想 achieve 某件事，就必须通过采取 active measures 来做。仅仅通过不打扰学生来 achieve 某件事，与他们的本性相悖。

而且他们应该被不打扰。这些事情应该是学生自己的 projects；university 应该抵制让它们 official 的 temptation。部分是因为学生会更兴奋地 work on 一个完全属于他们自己的 project，部分是因为许多 projects 无法在 official recognition 下存活，因为它们打破了某种 rule。Bill Gates 和 Mark Zuckerberg 都因为他们在 undergrads 时 worked on 的 projects 而与 Harvard administration 发生麻烦。Bill 通过把不是学生的 Paul Allen 带进 computer lab 和他一起 work on Altair Basic，打破了 university rules。Zuck 因为 Facemash 惹了如此大的麻烦，以至于他被 put on disciplinary probation。而他们的案例可能更多是 rule 而不是 exception。Universities 有很多 rules，而 novel projects 往往是 untidy things。

^pr-a41f6559423b

现在有学生在 line of sight 之外 flying drones。对此 Turn a blind eye。

Universities 难以让学生少忙的另一个原因是，他们会担心，如果没有某种 oversight，大多数学生只会浪费给他们的任何 free time。而他们会的！给最有活力的学生空间去做得更好的代价，是让最没活力的学生有空间去做得更差。但这是值得付出的代价，因为如果最有活力的学生做得更好，他们可以做得 _lot_ 更好，而最懒的学生已经学得如此少，以至于他们没有多少空间做得更差。所以把一些时间还给所有学生，可以大幅提高 average outcome，即使它不改变 median。[4](#fn4)

^pr-a9564f854734

仅仅为了鼓励 would-be founders 而改变 university 的整个 schedule，可能显得有点 excessive。他们永远不会超过学生的 10%。而且如果这个改变只帮助 founders，那它可能确实 excessive。但事实上，把一些时间还给学生，会帮助所有最有活力和雄心的学生。只要 work 的压力减轻哪怕一两周，他们都会去 explore 某种类型的新事物。

* * *

既然我已经解释了 universities 应该如何 prepare founders，我应该解释如何不这样做。Universities 不能做的一件事是真正 teach 学生 how to start startups。Starting a startup 是那种像 chemistry 或 painting 一样，你必须 learn by doing 的事情之一。这意味着一个 properly run 的关于 how to start a startup 的 class 必须是一个 lab class：学生实际上必须 start startups。而我确切知道这种类型的 class 应该是什么样子，因为 YC 就是它。但 YC 在结构上与 university 非常不同，如果你试图把它塞进一个 undergrad degree program，它会变成一个 joke。学生们应该在没有任何 funding 的情况下 start 这些 companies 吗？他们应该 run startups——众所周知，properly done 时会占用你每一刻时间——同时还要上三四门其他 classes 吗？而如果尽管有这些 handicaps，一些 startups 实际上 take off 了呢？学生们应该就这样 abandon 它们吗？因为要么那样，要么 drop out。

^pr-908586f68180

Running a startup 与 being a full time student 不相容。Learn how to start a startup 的唯一方式就是 do it。这两句话如此明显，以至于它们几乎是 truisms。然而这么多人设法对它们所暗示的东西 remain in denial。你不能 teach 学生 how to start startups。

对这个 inconvenient truth 的一种常见回应，是假装教他们如何 start startups，例如组织 business plan competitions。学生们合作想出一个 startup idea，然后向 simulated investors 进行 pitch。这类 exercise 不仅毫无用处，而且具有积极的误导性。它训练 founders 认为 fundraising 是 starting a startup 的关键步骤——即 starting a startup 的核心是创造一个能吸引 investors 的 story。作为一名 investor，我可以告诉你，事实并非如此。Fundraising 仅仅是一种 necessary evil。你需要打动的人是 users，而不是 investors，而你打动他们的方式是 prototypes，而不是 words。Starting a startup 的核心不是创造一个能吸引 investors 的 story，而是创造一个能吸引 users 的 product。[5](#fn5)

^pr-e8233e8da04c

事实上，would-be founders 应该做的恰恰与学生们在 business plan competitions 中所做的相反。他们不应该在不 building 任何东西的情况下思考 startups，而应该 building things，而不去思考它们是否会变成 startups。

大学之所以倾向于组织 business plan competitions 这类 bogus 的东西，可能原因之一是：如果它们真的采取 optimal measures 来 prepare students to start startups，那看起来会太安静了。想象一下，如果一所大学把每件事都做对了。学生们会在出于真正兴趣而选修的课程中获得关于如何 build things 的深厚知识，并热切地与朋友们一起做与学校无关的 side projects。学生们毕业时将恰好具备预测 founders 成功的那些特质：the ability to build things 以及 a habit of doing it。此外，这些 side projects 中有相当一部分会是 incipient startups。然而，在家长和 prospective students 看来，这所大学似乎什么都没做。“entrepreneurship”的课程在哪里？Innovation Center 在哪里？

^pr-9c7a731064fc

而且，这确实是 preparing startup founders 的 optimal plan 的另一个伟大之处：它不需要额外花费。你不必聘请任何 deans of entrepreneurship，也不必建造任何新建筑。事实上，如果你这么做，这些东西往往会拖累你；它们没有必要，所以如果它们有任何影响，那也往往是负面的。如果你有闲钱，就把它给那些教 computer science 或 mechanical engineering 或 molecular biology 的人。[6](#fn6)

但如果 optimal route 看起来太安静，解决办法不是避开它。解决办法是坚定立场，知道自己做的是正确的事，最终 results 会自己说话。如果你能在学生中培养出一种 organic startup culture，并且每年都有多名学生后来成功 start 出成功的 startups，那么任何留意的人很快就会看到这一点。

# 234 How Universities Should Prepare Founders

August 2026

How should universities prepare students to start startups? Y Combinator is in the perfect position to answer this question, because we get them next. We’re like grad school. And because YC has had 20 years to refine its model of what a promising founder looks like, you probably won’t find a better target.

What do the YC partners look for? It’s surprisingly simple. They want people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it.

The hard part of startups is product: knowing what to build, and being able to build it. And that kind of knowledge comes from studying computer science or mechanical engineering or molecular biology, not management or finance. [1](#fn1)

So the way to prepare undergraduates to become successful founders is not to give them some new curriculum focused on “entrepreneurship”. It’s to do what universities already do best – to teach them computer science and mechanical engineering and molecular biology. [2](#fn2)

Indeed, preparing students to start startups is closer to the ideal of liberal education than preparing them for almost any other kind of career. Startups succeed or fail based on how much customers like the product. Customers don’t care what the founders studied in college. So founders are free to study whatever they want, as long as they get good at building things.

But building should be understood in a very broad sense. It doesn’t mean all would-be founders have to study some form of engineering. Almost any kind of expertise that could be described as building or creating could be useful. It was useful to Steve Jobs to have studied calligraphy, for example; it was one of the reasons Apple dominated desktop publishing. So while math and science and engineering and design tend to be good bets, I would not want to draw a sharp line around them, because I can imagine other forms of building that could be useful. And of course you don’t have to major in something to be good at it. Mark Zuckerberg was good at programming, but he was a psychology major, not a CS major.

The best way to describe what would-be founders should study is that they should seek out powerful ideas. But smart people are naturally attracted to powerful ideas anyway. So as long as departments teaching powerful ideas exist, the sort of people who’d make good founders will find them. [3](#fn3)

In fact there are only two things universities need to change to be perfect at preparing founders: they need to make students feel that starting a startup is something they can do, and they need to encourage them to work on their own [projects](https://paulgraham.com/own.html).

At the moment, the belief that it’s possible to start a startup is very unevenly distributed. YC now gets so many applications that our application data is a reasonable proxy for interest in startups at different universities, and Harvard alumni, for example, apply at about twice the rate of Yale and Princeton alumni. Presumably Harvard students aren’t that different from Yale and Princeton students; the reason Harvard students go on to start more startups is just that it’s more customary there. Which in turn implies that merely by making their students feel that starting a startup is a viable option, Yale and Princeton could at least double the number who do.

Once a university has a culture of starting startups, you don’t have to convince students that it’s a viable option. New students learn that from older ones. But at a university that doesn’t have much of a startup culture yet, there are things you can do to help this realization along. The most effective is probably to show students examples of people who’ve done it.

Until you’ve seen some founders in real life, you tend to think that starting startups is something done by other people. Seeing them pops that bubble. In fact seeing founders in real life is doubly inspiring: they seem impressive, but they also seem human. Especially when they talk about the early years, when they were clueless and made lots of mistakes. So strangely enough seeing founders in real life makes being one seem simultaneously both desirable and accessible. It makes students think “I want to be like that, and I could.”

How inspiring founders are to students is a function roughly of how rich and famous they are divided by how much older they are than the students. So it’s not essential to bring famous billionaires to campus. Founders in their mid twenties who are 3 years into a startup with a valuation of a couple hundred million will do as well; they may only be a twentieth as rich and famous, but they’re twenty times easier for students to identify with.

* * *

It’s obvious why universities that want their students to start startups need to make them believe it’s a viable option. But why is it so important for students to work on their own projects?

There are four reasons. The first is simply that it’s a great way, possibly the best way, to understand a subject really deeply. The excitement of creating something new is a much more powerful motivator than the fear of doing badly on an exam.

Second, working on projects together is the best way for cofounders to discover one another. The most successful startups tend to have multiple founders, and the only way to tell for sure if someone will be good to work with is to work with them. Apple and Microsoft were just the last of many projects their founders had worked on together.

Third, a startup is a project, so starting one will feel natural to someone who’s used to working on projects of their own. It won’t seem weird that there’s no teacher or boss telling them what to do. They’re used to telling themselves.

Fourth, and perhaps most surprisingly, random side projects are where the best startup ideas come from. The best startup ideas tend to seem so implausible at first that anyone consciously looking for startup ideas would reject them. Who’d expect to start a huge company by creating a student directory? So the way to discover the best startup ideas is not to look for startup ideas but just to work on whatever random projects seem interesting. Because in fact such projects are far from random: young people who are good at building things are technological bellwethers, so any idea that seems interesting to them is disproportionately likely to lead somewhere valuable, even if they themselves don’t realize it yet.

Now it should be clear why the YC partners care a lot about the projects that applicants have worked on and not at all about their GPAs. Projects are the best source of knowledge, the best source of founding teams, and the best source of startup ideas.

But encouraging students to work on their own projects may be difficult for universities. It will mean giving the students more free time, and universities may not like to do that.

Microsoft and Meta have something in common that few people realize. They both got started during reading period at Harvard. Reading period is the gap between the end of classes and the beginning of final exams. It’s called reading period because students are _supposed_ to spend it preparing for exams. But reading period also turns out to have the unique combination of qualities that make it perfect for starting new projects: the students are all on campus, and they don’t have anything due the next day. That latter constraint, especially, is a huge drag on the most ambitious students. Merely eliminating it for a few weeks resulted in two trillion dollar companies. Imagine what the US GDP would be if reading period at Harvard were twice as long.

Universities will tend to resist the idea of keeping students less busy with coursework. Partly because administrators feel that if they want to achieve something, they have to do it by taking active measures. Achieving something merely by leaving students alone is alien to their nature.

And they should be left alone. These things should be the students’ own projects; the university should resist the temptation to make them official. Partly because students will be more excited to work on a project that’s entirely their own, and partly because many projects wouldn’t survive official recognition, because they break some sort of rule. Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg both got in trouble with the Harvard administration over projects they worked on as undergrads. Bill broke university rules by bringing Paul Allen, who wasn’t a student, into the computer lab with him to work on Altair Basic. Zuck got in such trouble over Facemash that he was put on disciplinary probation. And their cases are probably more the rule than the exception. Universities have lots of rules, and novel projects are often untidy things.

Right now there are students flying drones out of line of sight. Turn a blind eye to it.

Another reason it will be hard for universities to keep students less busy is that they’ll worry that without some kind of oversight, most students will just waste whatever free time they’re given. And they will! The price of giving the most energetic students room to do even better is that it leaves the least energetic ones room to do even worse. But that’s a price that’s worth paying, because if the most energetic students do better they could do a _lot_ better, whereas the laziest students already learn so little that there’s not much room for them to do worse. So giving all the students some of their time back could improve the average outcome a lot, even if it doesn’t move the median. [4](#fn4)

It may seem a bit excessive to change the whole schedule of the university just to encourage would-be founders. They’re never going to be more than 10% of the students. And it probably would be excessive if this change only helped founders. But in fact giving the students some of their time back would help all the most energetic and ambitious ones. They’d all explore new things of one type or another if the pressure of work were relieved for even a week or two.

* * *

Now that I’ve explained how universities should prepare founders, I should explain how not to. One thing universities can’t do is actually teach students how to start startups. Starting a startup is one of those things, like chemistry or painting, that you have to learn by doing. Which means a properly run class on how to start a startup would have to be a lab class: the students would actually have to start startups. And I know exactly what a class of this type should look like, because YC is it. But YC is very different in structure from a university, and if you tried to cram it into an undergrad degree program, it would become a joke. Are the students supposed to start these companies without any funding? Are they supposed to run startups, which notoriously take every moment of your time when done properly, while simultaneously taking three or four other classes? And what if, despite these handicaps, some of the startups actually take off? Are the students just supposed to abandon them? Because it’s either that or drop out.

Running a startup is incompatible with being a full time student. The only way to learn how to start a startup is to do it. Those two statements are so obvious that they’re practically truisms. And yet so many people manage to remain in denial about what they imply. You can’t teach students how to start startups.

One common response to this inconvenient truth is to pretend to teach them how to start startups, for example by organizing business plan competitions. The students collaborate to come up with a startup idea, which they then pitch to simulated investors. This kind of exercise is not merely useless but positively misleading. It trains founders to think that fundraising is the essential step in starting a startup – that the core of starting a startup is to create a story that appeals to investors. As an investor, I can tell you that’s not true. Fundraising is merely a necessary evil. The people you need to impress are users, not investors, and the way you impress them is with prototypes, not words. The core of starting a startup is not creating a story that appeals to investors, but creating a product that appeals to users. [5](#fn5)

In fact would-be founders should be doing exactly the opposite of what students do in business plan competitions. Instead of thinking about startups without building anything, they should be building things without thinking about whether they’ll turn into startups.

Probably one of the reasons universities are tempted to organize bogus things like business plan competitions is that if they actually took the optimal measures to prepare students to start startups, it would look too quiet. Imagine if a university were doing everything right. Students would be getting a deep knowledge of how to build things in classes they were taking out of genuine interest, and working eagerly with their friends on side projects that had nothing to do with school. The students would graduate with exactly what predicts success in founders: the ability to build things and a habit of doing it. Plus a significant number of those side projects would be incipient startups. And yet it would look to parents and prospective students as if the university wasn’t doing anything. Where are the classes on “entrepreneurship”? Where is the Innovation Center?

And indeed this is another great thing about the optimal plan for preparing startup founders: it costs nothing extra. You don’t have to hire any deans of entrepreneurship or build any new buildings. In fact if you do, those things will tend to drag you down; there’s no need for them, so if they have any effect at all it will tend to be for the worse. If you have spare money, give it to the people teaching computer science or mechanical engineering or molecular biology. [6](#fn6)

But if the optimal route looks too quiet, the solution is not to avoid it. The solution is to stand firm, knowing that you’re doing the right thing, and eventually the results will speak for themselves. If you can develop an organic startup culture among your students and there are multiple students in every year who go on to start successful ones, this will soon become evident to anyone paying attention.
