# 1\. [Envy：关于 Social Behaviour 的 Theory](https://www.amazon.ca/Envy-Theory-Behaviour-Helmut-Schoeck/dp/0865970645)，作者 Helmut Schoeck

## 正文

希望你夏天过得愉快！我一直想写一期通讯，分享我对最近读过的几本书的一些感想。以下是几本我觉得有意思的书：

## 1\. [Envy：关于 Social Behaviour 的 Theory](https://www.amazon.ca/Envy-Theory-Behaviour-Helmut-Schoeck/dp/0865970645)，作者 Helmut Schoeck

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/51jzt8cSl3L._SL1400_-667x1024.jpg)

这本书一部分是 sociological theory，一部分是 political tirade；我觉得 Schoeck 对 society 中的 envy 所作的 analysis 既有意思，又发人深省。

Schoeck 认为，envy 是 caustic，而所有 functional societies 都会学会在一定程度上 suppress 它。envy 通过 discouraging individual effort and achievement，holds back 的不只是 productivity 和 wealth，甚至还有普通的 virtues，因为人们不想招来邻居的 envy 所带来的“evil eye”。

Schoeck 还认为，envy 作为一种 emotion，并不会按照 class-conscious political organizers 所希望的方式 function。相比于我们与远方的人之间的 large gaps，我们对身边 close associates 之间的 small differences 要 envious 得多。可以想想 China 的 Cultural Revolution：承受 malice 主要冲击的不是 ruling elites，而是 schoolteachers、small landowners，以及当地村庄里其他生活境况仅比别人稍好一些的人。

虽然 Schoeck 对建立在 envy 之上的 politics 所作的 tirades，针对的主要是 left-wing，但在如今席卷世界的许多 right-wing populism 中，也很难不读出类似的 sentiment。

虽然我们可能愿意相信，我们已经成熟到形成了一种能够 debate growth 与 equality、或 environment 与 economy 之间的 trade-offs 的 culture，但 _Envy_ 有力地提醒我们：在许多方面，我们仍然是由一群孩子组成的 society，只因隔壁孩子的玩具比自己的稍好一点就闷闷不乐。

## 2\. [Infinite Powers（无限的 Powers）](https://www.amazon.ca/Infinite-Powers-Calculus-Reveals-Universe/dp/0358299284?s=books)，作者 Steven Strogatz

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71-FAM2RapL._SL1500_-681x1024.jpg)

这是一本引人入胜的书，讲述 calculus 的 history。

我非常喜欢 Strogatz 的作品，尤其是他的[关于 chaos theory 的课程](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLbN57C5Zdl6j_qJA-pARJnKsmROzPnO9V)。这本书回顾了 mathematics 最辉煌的成就之一的 history，也同样出色。

我有一个小小的意见：Strogatz 似乎几乎想要使出浑身解数，让读者相信 calculus 很重要。仿佛有个咄咄逼人的编辑告诉他，大多数人觉得 calculus 无聊，要他增加 practical examples，尽可能激起读者的兴趣。我很难相信，那些一想到 advanced math 就两眼发直的人会费心读这本书，因此我觉得，这些为这个 subject 大力造势的努力有些多余。

## 3\. [Goodness 的 Fragility](https://www.amazon.ca/Fragility-Goodness-Ethics-Tragedy-Philosophy/dp/0521794722)，作者 Martha Nussbaum

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71plD178dHL._SL1360_-683x1024.jpg)

Life 是 fragile 的，我们常常没能 do the right thing，或没能得到自己想要的东西。这些大家都很清楚。   但 life 中是否存在根本不可能 do the right thing 的 situations？是否有些时候，我们没有任何 good options，而所有 choices 都不可避免地涉及 sacrificing 一个自己珍视的 value？

Nussbaum 认为有，而 ancient Greek tragedians 明白，这是 human condition 的一部分。他们的作品之所以是 tragedies，不是因为好人遭遇了坏事，而是因为人们别无选择，只能把事情做坏。做也遭殃，不做也遭殃。

自 Plato 以来，Philosophers 一直致力于 reorganize 我们的 interior life，使这类 tragedies 不可能发生；他们试图为 human values 提供 clear 而 consistent 的 ordering，使人始终能作出 correct decision。从 Socrates 远离 perceptible world、转向 pure ideas 的世界，到 Bentham 用同一把尺子 measures 所有 human values 的 utilitarian calculus，他们都在寻求某种办法，让我们摆脱一种痛苦的可能性：life 中的许多 problems 没有 good answer。

## 4\. [我们是谁，又如何来到这里](https://www.amazon.ca/Who-Are-How-Got-Here/dp/1101873469)，作者 David Reich

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71VftZ8l5wL._SL1500_-664x1024.jpg)

近来，geneticists 已经能够从 ancient humans 的 skeletons 中 extract DNA sequences。通过对这些 DNA 进行 sequencing，并将其与其他 archeological specimens 和 modern-day human beings 进行 comparing，我们对自身 ancestry 的认识正变得比以往丰富得多。

人们往往把我们当下的 cosmopolitan age 视为一种 aberration。他们假定，直到不久前，人们大体上一直生活在最初 human migrations 离开 Africa 后 settled 的地方。也就是说，Chinese people 来自 China，Europeans 来自 Europe，而 Americas 的 First Nations people，大致 settled 在数万年前 crossing the Bering Strait 后登陆的地方。按照这种看法，我们这种存在 ancestral ethnic groups 的 mixtures 的 multi-ethnic societies 是极不寻常的。

Reich 的研究表明，过去远比我们意识到的有意思，而 human history 充满了频繁的 mixtures。虽然我们没有那些时代的 written records，但我们的 genomes 讲述着 mass migrations、continent-scale conquests 和 repeated returns 的故事。

## 5\. [Sisyphus 的 Myth](https://www.amazon.ca/Myth-Sisyphus-Albert-Camus/dp/0525564454)，作者 Albert Camus

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/61HFcmUNHuL._SL1500_-664x1024.jpg)

“真正严肃的 philosophical problem 只有一个，那就是 suicide。”在 Camus 看来，life 显然是 absurd 而 meaningless 的。问题在于，过一种 meaningless existence 是否仍然值得。

Camus 认为值得，并提出这样一个问题：create 从根本上并不重要的 art，会是什么样的体验。他主张一种 experiential gluttony，maximizing 一个人的 experiences，把 living 本身视为面对 uncaring cosmos 时的一种 act of rebellion。

Sisyphus 是 Greek myth 中的人物，众神对他的 punishment 是让他永远反复把一块巨石推上山，再眼看着它滚下去；但他未必需要 suffering。Camus 认为，Sisyphus 拥有一种我们这些仍执着于 finding meaning in life 的人所缺少的东西：对自身 plight 的 true understanding。而正是从这种 understanding 中，他获得了 freedom。

Camus 发人深省，但在我看来，他的 solution 似乎 psychologically implausible。我相信，我们是深具 teleological、goal-directed 特性的 creatures。没有 purpose 地生活，是令人难以接受的。需要 reforming 的不是我们的 innate psychology，而是关于 meaning 的一种观念：它应当能够 accommodate 一个并不提供 eternal purposes 的 universe。

## 6\. [The Perfectionists](https://www.amazon.ca/Perfectionists-Precision-Engineers-Created-Modern/dp/0062652567)，作者 Simon Winchester

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/81e3sF5-17L._SL1500_-681x1024.jpg)

现代世界建立在一个观念之上：precision。通过越来越 exactly 地 making things，我们能够 solve 古人只能梦想的 technological puzzles。Winchester 带领读者游历 precision 的 history，从最早的 precision-machined cannons 开始——它们被 milled 到约一枚硬币宽度以内的精度——一直讲到用于 make high-end computer chips 的 lithographic processes，其提供的 precision 达到了几个 atoms 以内。

书中我最喜欢的故事之一 compares 了 Rolls-Royce 和 Ford automobiles。虽然 Rolls-Royce 以 exquisite craftsmanship 闻名，但实际上，Ford 的 Model T 才是更 precise 的。原因很简单：Ford 需要用 identical、interchangeable parts 来 build 所有 cars。由于不能反复调整 pieces，直到它们 lined up perfectly，这就意味着，每个 part 本身都必须比 hand-crafted automobile 的任何 component 更加 perfect。

## 7\. [The Marginal Revolution](https://tylercowen.com/marginal-revolution-generative-book/)，作者 Tyler Cowen

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/MR-AI-Cover-759x1024.jpg)

这本书被宣传为一种关于 generative AI 的新型书籍。Cowen 甚至还 build 了一个 [companion website](https://tylercowen.com/marginal-revolution-generative-book/app/)，让你能够向这本书提问。不过，我没有尝试这些，只是从头到尾读完了这本书的 Kindle edition。

Cowen 介绍了 [marginalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalism) 的 history，也就是这样一种 theory：analyze 一个 decision 的正确方式，不是问某件事物 costs on average 是多少，而是问你 do one more of something 的 costs 是多少。  想想那个著名的 [diamond-water paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_value)。Water 是 life 所必需的，因此它的 value 必定是 immense。相比之下，Diamonds 大多只是无用的小玩意，因此不应该非常 valuable。然而，water 是 cheap，而 diamonds 却是 expensive。

Marginalism 帮助 explain 了这个 phenomenon：它指出，water 的 price 并不来自它对你的 total value（那的确非常 high），而是来自它的 marginal value——也就是说，多一杯 water 对你而言 worth 多少。而如果你并不很渴，答案可能就是没多少。Water 是 abundant，因此，与 supply 相比，marginal value 是 low。

Cowen 认为，marginal thinking 对 economics 一直极为 productive，但它在这门 discipline 中的 centrality 正在 waning。将 marginalist principles 置于 central 地位的 Price theory，与 [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics) 及其他 sophisticated mathematical techniques 相比，正不再受青睐。甚至可以说，marginalist thinking 正在 declining on the margin。

## 8\. [Catch-22](https://www.amazon.ca/Catch-22-50th-Anniversary-Joseph-Heller/dp/1451626657)，作者 Joseph Heller

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71Ym0vDDWsL._SL1500_-668x1024.jpg)

这可能是我读过的最有趣的书。作为一部关于 war、bureaucracy 和人生 paradoxes 的 satire，书名中的 Catch-22 指的是一条针对想要摆脱 combat duty 的 bomber pilots 的虚构 rule：

> 只有一个圈套，那就是 Catch-22。它 specified，面对真实而迫在眉睫的危险时，关心自身安全是 rational mind 的 process。Orr 是 crazy，因此可以被 grounded。他只需要提出申请；而他一旦提出申请，就不再是 crazy，也就必须 fly 更多 missions。Orr 如果 fly 更多 missions，就是 crazy；如果不飞，就是 sane，但如果他是 sane，就必须去 fly。如果他去 fly，他就是 crazy，也就不必去；但如果他不想去，他就是 sane，也就必须去。Catch-22 这条 clause 的 absolute simplicity 深深打动了 Yossarian，他满怀敬意地吹了一声口哨。

整本书充满了对人生荒诞之处的有趣小观察：

> 他正努力 increasing 自己的 life span。他的做法是培养无聊。Dunbar 在 increasing 自己的 life span 上如此卖力，以至于 Yossarian 以为他已经死了。

> 那个得克萨斯人原来脾气温和、慷慨又讨人喜欢。三天之内，就没人受得了他了。

> McWatt 大概是他们所有 combat man 中最 crazy 的，因为他完全 sane，却仍然不介意这场 war。

> 针对 Clevinger 的 case 已经铁证如山。唯一缺少的，就是用来 charge 他的罪名。

> Morale 正在 deteriorating，而这全是 Yossarian 的错。国家处于危难之中；他竟敢 exercise 自己传统的 freedom 和 independence 的 rights，因而正在 jeopardizing 这些 rights。

> Nurse Duckett 觉得 Yossarian 棒极了，而且已经在试图改变他了。

我并非总有动力去读许多 classic works of fiction，尽管读过之后很少后悔。_Catch-22_ 提醒我，fiction 的好处不仅在于 entertainment，还在于它所 stimulates 的那种 thinking。

## 原文

I hope you’re having a good summer! I’ve been meaning to do a newsletter sharing some thoughts on a few books I’ve read recently. Here are some books I found interesting:

## 1\. [Envy: A Theory of Social Behaviour](https://www.amazon.ca/Envy-Theory-Behaviour-Helmut-Schoeck/dp/0865970645) by Helmut Schoeck

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/51jzt8cSl3L._SL1400_-667x1024.jpg)

Part sociological theory, part political tirade, I found Schoeck’s analysis of envy in society to be interesting and provocative.

Schoeck argues that envy is caustic, and all functional societies learn to suppress it to some extent. By discouraging individual effort and achievement, envy holds back not just productivity and wealth, but even ordinary virtues as people don’t want to attract the “evil eye” of their neighbors’ envy.

Schoeck also argues that envy, as an emotion, doesn’t function the way class-conscious political organizers would like it to. We are much more envious of small differences among our close associates than we are of large gaps between us and people far away. One can think of the Cultural Revolution in China where the brunt of the malice was borne not by ruling elites, but by schoolteachers, small landowners and other people just slightly better off than others in their local villages.

Although the targets of Schoeck’s tirades against a politics built on envy are largely left-wing, it was hard not to read a similar sentiment into much of the right-wing populism sweeping the world today.

Although we might like to think we’ve matured into a culture that can debate the trade-offs between growth and equality, or environment and economy, _Envy_ is a potent reminder that, in many ways, we’re still a society of children upset that the kid next door has a slightly nicer toy than we do.

## 2\. [Infinite Powers](https://www.amazon.ca/Infinite-Powers-Calculus-Reveals-Universe/dp/0358299284?s=books) by Steven Strogatz

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71-FAM2RapL._SL1500_-681x1024.jpg)

A fascinating book on the history of calculus.

I really enjoy Strogatz’s work, especially his [class on chaos theory](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLbN57C5Zdl6j_qJA-pARJnKsmROzPnO9V). This book, looking at the history of one of mathematics’ crowning achievements is also great.

One minor quibble I have is that Strogatz seems almost to want to bend over backwards to convince the reader that calculus is important. It’s almost as if a pushy editor told him that most people find calculus boring and to amp up the practical examples to maximize interest. I find it hard to believe that anyone whose eyes glaze over at the thought of advanced math would bother to read this book, so I find the efforts to hype the subject somewhat unnecessary.

## 3\. [The Fragility of Goodness](https://www.amazon.ca/Fragility-Goodness-Ethics-Tragedy-Philosophy/dp/0521794722) by Martha Nussbaum

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71plD178dHL._SL1360_-683x1024.jpg)

Life is fragile, and we often fail to do the right thing, or to get what we want. That much is well understood.   But are there situations in life where it’s impossible to do the right thing? Times we’re left with no good options, and all choices inevitably involve sacrificing a dearly held value?

Nussbaum argues that there are, and the ancient Greek tragedians understood this to be a part of the human condition. Their works are tragedies not because bad things happen to good people, but because people have no choice but to do things badly. Damned if they do, damned if they don’t.

Philosophers since Plato have been on a mission to reorganize our interior life so that such tragedies are impossible, to provide a clear and consistent ordering of human values so that there is always a correct decision. From Socrates’s pull away from the perceptible world into the world of pure ideas to Bentham’s utilitarian calculus which measures all human values using a single ruler, all seek to find some way to rid us of the painful possibility that many problems in life have no good answer.

## 4\. [Who We Are and How We Got Here](https://www.amazon.ca/Who-Are-How-Got-Here/dp/1101873469) by David Reich

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71VftZ8l5wL._SL1500_-664x1024.jpg)

Recently, geneticists have been able to extract DNA sequences from the skeletons of ancient humans. By sequencing that DNA and comparing it to other archeological specimens and modern-day human beings, we are getting a much richer picture of our ancestry than ever before.

There’s a tendency to view our current cosmopolitan age as an aberration. To assume that, until recently, people lived largely where they settled after the initial human migrations out of Africa. That Chinese people are from China, Europeans are from Europe, and First Nations people in the Americas settled roughly where they landed after crossing the Bering Strait tens of thousands of years ago. In this view, our multi-ethnic societies with mixtures of ancestral ethnic groups is highly unusual.

Reich’s work shows that the past was a far more interesting place than we realize, and that human history was one of frequent mixtures. While we have no written records from these times, our genomes tell stories of mass migrations, continent-scale conquests and repeated returns.

## 5\. [The Myth of Sisyphus](https://www.amazon.ca/Myth-Sisyphus-Albert-Camus/dp/0525564454) by Albert Camus

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/61HFcmUNHuL._SL1500_-664x1024.jpg)

“There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide.” To Camus, life is obviously absurd and meaningless. The question is whether it’s still worth living a meaningless existence.

Camus argues that it is, asking what it would be like to create art that, fundamentally, does not matter. He argues for a kind of experiential gluttony, maximizing one’s experiences, treating living itself as an act of rebellion in the face of an uncaring cosmos.

Sisyphus, the figure of Greek myth whose punishment by the gods was to spend eternity repeatedly rolling a boulder up a hill only to watch it roll down, need not be suffering. Camus argues that Sisyphus possesses something that those of us still attached to finding meaning in life lack, a true understanding of his plight. And from that understanding, he has freedom.

Camus is provocative, but his solution seems psychologically implausible for me. I believe we are deeply teleological, goal-directed creatures. To live without purpose is an anathema. What needs reforming is not our innate psychology, but an idea of meaning that can accommodate a universe that does not provide eternal purposes.

## 6\. [The Perfectionists](https://www.amazon.ca/Perfectionists-Precision-Engineers-Created-Modern/dp/0062652567) by Simon Winchester

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/81e3sF5-17L._SL1500_-681x1024.jpg)

The modern world is built on an idea: precision. By making things ever-more exactly, we can solve technological puzzles that ancients could only dream of. Winchester takes the reader on a tour through the history of precision, beginning with the first precision-machined cannons, milled to within about the width of a coin, to the lithographic processes used to make high-end computer chips, offering precision to within a few atoms.

One of my favorite stories in the book compares Rolls-Royce and Ford automobiles. While Rolls-Royce was famous for their exquisite craftsmanship, it was actually Ford’s Model T that was more precise. The reason was simple: Ford needed to build all the cars with identical, interchangeable parts. Being unable to fiddle with the pieces until they lined up perfectly meant that each part had to, on its own, be much more perfect than any component of a hand-crafted automobile.

## 7\. [The Marginal Revolution](https://tylercowen.com/marginal-revolution-generative-book/) by Tyler Cowen

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/MR-AI-Cover-759x1024.jpg)

This book was billed as a new kind of book on generative AI. Cowen even went so far as to build a [companion website](https://tylercowen.com/marginal-revolution-generative-book/app/) that allows you to ask the book questions. I didn’t do any of that, though, and simply read the Kindle edition of the book start-to-finish.

Cowen offers a history of [marginalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalism), or the theory that the right way to analyze a decision is not to ask how much something costs on average, but what it costs you to do one more of something.   Consider the famous [diamond-water paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_value). Water is necessary for life, so its value must be immense. Diamonds, in contrast, are mostly useless baubles, so they shouldn’t be very valuable. Yet water is cheap and diamonds are expensive.

Marginalism helps explain this phenomenon by pointing out that the price of water doesn’t come from its total value to you (which is indeed very high) but its marginal value—i.e., how much more one glass of water is worth to you. And, if you aren’t very thirsty, the answer might be not very much. Water is abundant, so the marginal value is low compared to the supply.

Cowen argues that marginal thinking has been enormously productive for economics, but its centrality to the discipline is waning. Price theory, which makes marginalist principles central, is out of favor compared to [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics) and other sophisticated mathematical techniques. One might even say marginalist thinking is declining on the margin.

## 8\. [Catch-22](https://www.amazon.ca/Catch-22-50th-Anniversary-Joseph-Heller/dp/1451626657) by Joseph Heller

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/71Ym0vDDWsL._SL1500_-668x1024.jpg)

This may be the funniest book I’ve ever read. A satire on war, bureaucracy and the paradoxes of life, the eponymous Catch-22 refers to a fictitious rule for bomber pilots who wanted to get out of combat duty:

> There was only one catch and that was Catch-22, which specified that a concern for one’s own safety in the face of dangers that were real and immediate was the process of a rational mind. Orr was crazy and could be grounded. All he had to do was ask; and as soon as he did, he would no longer be crazy and would have to fly more missions. Orr would be crazy to fly more missions and sane if he didn’t, but if he was sane he had to fly them. If he flew them he was crazy and didn’t have to; but if he didn’t want to he was sane and had to. Yossarian was moved very deeply by the absolute simplicity of this clause of Catch-22 and let out a respectful whistle.

The whole book is full of amusing little observations about the absurdities of life:

> He was working hard at increasing his life span. He did it by cultivating boredom. Dunbar was working so hard at increasing his life span that Yossarian thought he was dead.

> The Texan turned out to be good-natured, generous and likable. In three days no one could stand him.

> McWatt was the craziest combat man of them all probably, because he was perfectly sane and still did not mind the war.

> The case against Clevinger was open and shut. The only thing missing was something to charge him with.

> Morale was deteriorating and it was all Yossarian’s fault. The country was in peril; he was jeopardizing his traditional rights of freedom and independence by daring to exercise them.

> Nurse Duckett found Yossarian wonderful and was already trying to change him.

I haven’t always been motivated to read many classic works of fiction, although I seldom regret reading them. _Catch-22_ reminds me that fiction is good, not just for entertainment, but for the kind of thinking it stimulates.
