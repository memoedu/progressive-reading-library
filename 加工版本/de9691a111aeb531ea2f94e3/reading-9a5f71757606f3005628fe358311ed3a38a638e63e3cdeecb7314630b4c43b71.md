# 学什么｜What to learn

---

## 正文

人们常常会提倡学习自己掌握的 skills，或使用自己采用的 processes。例如，Steve Yegge 写过一组 blog posts，推荐阅读 compiler 书籍并学习 compilers。他的理由基本上是，如果你理解 compilers，就会到处看到 compiler problems，并 recognize 人们在没有使用 compiler knowledge 的情况下 solving a compiler problem 的所有情形。你不必 hacking together 某个永远无法 work 的半成品 solution，而可以 apply 一点 computer science knowledge，以更好的方式、更少的精力 solve the problem。这并非不对，但它也不能成为专门学习 compilers 的理由，因为对于 computer science 和 math 的许多不同 areas，你都可以这么说。比如 Queuing theory、computer architecture、mathematical optimization、operations research，等等。

对这种反对意见的一种回应是说，[人应该什么都学](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/899141882760110081)。虽然成为涉猎极广的 generalist 可能行得通，但要“什么都懂一点”并且做到 effective，已经难得多了，因为随着时间推移，所有东西都变得更多了（无论 breadth 还是 depth）。而且，即使并非如此，我也觉得说“应该”太强了；一个人是否喜欢拥有这样的 breadth，是个人喜好问题。另一个也可能行得通、而且更合我口味的办法，是[像 Gian Carlo Rota 所说的那样](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~cahn/life/gian-carlo-rota-10-lessons.html)，学会几招 tricks：

> 很久以前，一位年长而知名的 number theorist 对 Paul Erdos 的 work 发表了一些贬低的评论。你和我一样钦佩对 mathematics 的 contributions，所以，当那位年长的 mathematician 断然且明确地声称，Erdos 的所有 work 都可以 reduced to 他在 proofs 中反复依赖的几招 tricks 时，我感到很恼火。那位 number theorist 没有意识到，其他 mathematicians，甚至最优秀的那些，也都依赖几招反复使用的 tricks。以 Hilbert 为例。Hilbert 的 collected papers 第二卷收录了他在 invariant theory 方面的 papers。我曾特意仔细阅读其中一些 papers。令人遗憾的是，Hilbert 的一些 beautiful results 已被彻底遗忘。但在阅读 Hilbert 在 invariant theory 中那些 striking 且 deep 的 theorems 的 proofs 时，我惊讶地 verify，Hilbert 的 proofs 依赖的也是同样的几招 tricks。就连 Hilbert 也只有几招 tricks！

如果你看看人们在各种 fields 中如何取得成功，就会发现这是一种常见做法。例如，[这项针对 world-class judo players 的 analysis 发现，大多数人都依赖少数几种 throws](https://judoinfo.com/weers1/)，并得出结论[1](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:J)

> Judo 是一项讲究 specialization 的 game。你必须使用最适合自己的 skills。你必须坚持有效的做法，并 practice 自己的 skills，直到它们成为 automatic responses。

如果你看过“关于” fighting 的动漫或电视剧，就会发现，角色往往通过增加自己掌握的 techniques 数量来 improve，因为这很容易表现出来。但在现实生活中，把自己已经掌握的 techniques 练得更好，往往比拥有数百种“moves”的 portfolio 更 effective。

[与此相关，Joy Ebertz 说](https://staffeng.com/stories/joy-ebertz)：

> 我曾经得到过一条建议：amplify 我的 strengths。我们每个人都有 strengths 和 weaknesses，而且我们花了很多时间讨论‘areas of improvement’。人很容易觉得，最好的 advance 方式就是 eliminate 所有这些不足。然而，如果那确实是我们 weak 的 area，可能付出大量工作和精力，也只能取得微乎其微的进展。显然，你仍然要确保自己没有任何真正糟糕的 areas，但假设你已经做到了，就应转而专注于 amplifying 自己的 strengths。你怎样才能把自己擅长的东西变成自己的 superpower？

我自己在多种 disciplines 中都发现，情况确实如此。虽然要以近乎 objective 的方式 measure programmer effectiveness 非常困难，但我做过的一些事情并非如此，例如 competitive video games（如今看来，那已经是很久以前了，当时 competitive gaming 还没有“真正”的钱可赚）。让我从一个相当不错的 player 变成一个[非常出色的 player](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/#fn:B) 的，是放弃 practicing 那些自己并不特别擅长的事情，转而专注于 increasing 自己在少数格外擅长的事情上相对于其他所有人的 edge。

这对 games 和 sports 可以奏效，因为你可以更善于 maneuvering 自己进入能发挥 strengths 的 positions，同时 avoiding 暴露 weaknesses 的 situations。我觉得，这在工作中实际上比在 sports 或 gaming 中更 effective，因为与 competitive endeavors 不同，你并没有一个 opponent 会设法暴露你的 weaknesses，并 force 你进入 strengths 无关紧要的 positions。如果我学习的是 queuing theory，而不是 compilers，一个与你竞争的同事不会阻止我参与那些 queuing theory knowledge 有帮助的 projects，让我面对满眼都需要 compiler knowledge 的 projects。

值得注意的是，skills 不一定非得是人们会视为 fields of study 或 discrete techniques 的东西。过去三年里，我主要在 applying 和 improving 的 skill，或许可以称为“looking at data”；之所以加引号，是因为我不知道有什么好说法。我觉得它并不是大多数人会想到的“statistics”，因为我并不经常需要做 logistic regression 这种程度的 sophisticated 工作，更不用说真正 sophisticated 的工作了。也许有人会说，这是 data scientists 做的事情，但如果看看我做的事情，对照我们雇用的 data scientists 做的事情，以及我们在 data scientist interviews 中 screen 的内容，就会发现，我们似乎既不想雇用具备我一直在练习的这种 skill 的 data scientists，他们也不做我正在做的事情（这个话题足够长，也许我以后会单独写成一篇文章）。

与 Matt Might 或 Steve Yegge 不同，我不会说你应该采用某一种特定做法，但我可以说，专注于少数几件事，而不是力求特别 well rounded，在多个截然不同的 fields 中对我都行得通，而且对其他许多人似乎也行得通。

如果你想采用这种做法，仍然会留下一个问题：该学习哪些 skills。这是别人最常问我的问题之一，而我觉得，我的回答可能并不是大家真正想要的，也不会很令人满意，因为它既[显而易见，又很难付诸实践](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1428445465662603272)。

对我来说，要弄清该花时间学习什么，有两个要素：对某件事有 relative aptitude（relative to 我可能做的其他事情，而不是 relative to 其他人），以及有一个良好的学习 environment。说一个人应该寻找这些东西，模糊得近乎无用，但仍然好过通常的建议；那些建议归根结底就是“学我学过的东西”，于是就有了这样的建议：“职业进阶秘诀：如果你想善于，真正善于在 real-world environments 中 designing complex 且 stateful 的 distributed systems at scale，那就学习 functional programming。这几乎是 perfectly identical 的 skillset。”某些 language communities 的说法甚至更极端，比如 Chuck Moore 声称，Forth 的 productive 程度[至少是 boring languages 的 100x](https://danluu.com/boring-languages/)。

在职业生涯早期，我采纳过网上那些泛泛的建议，包括 language 方面的建议（当时，这类建议大多还相对新鲜，还不能轻易观察到：尽管许多人采纳这样的建议，采纳者并没有特别 effective，而那些特别 effective 的人却不太可能采纳过这样的建议）。我学了 [Haskell、Lisp、Forth](https://twitter.com/sc13ts/status/1448003352655060997)，[等等](https://malisper.me/there-is-more-to-programming-than-programming-languages/)。我职业生涯中曾有一段时间在一个两人 team 里，我们 implemented 了一款 Forth processor，即使在十年后的今天，它仍可能是现存 highest performance 的 Forth processor（那是一款 2GHz IPC-oriented processor），我也为它 programmed（有充分理由将它做成 stack processor，因此 Forth 看来是不逊于其他选择的选择）。[像 Yossi Kreinin 一样，我想我可以说，自己为 becoming proficient in Forth 付出的努力比大多数人都多；也像他一样，我不仅没有发现它是一种 100x productivity tool，甚至连它总体上能否在 productivity 上达到 1x 都不清楚](https://yosefk.com/blog/my-history-with-forth-stack-machines.html)。公平地说，其他不少 tools 在 productivity 上确实超过了 1x，但总体而言，我认为听从网上建议的 ROI 非常 low，而我学到的那些 high ROI 的东西，并不是人们当时在推荐的。

回头来看，当人们说“Forth 非常 productive”之类的话时，我怀疑他们真正的意思是：“Forth 让我非常 productive，而我没有考虑过，这对于具有不同 aptitudes，或处于不同 contexts 的人，能在多大程度上 generalizes。”我完全相信，Forth（或 Lisp、Haskell，以及任何其他 tool 或 technique）确实能对某些特定的人非常有效。但我认为，人们往往会高估某件事对自己有效，在多大程度上意味着它也对别人有效，[这让建议通常变得无用，因为它不 distinguish 针对特定 aptitude 或 circumstance 的建议与 generalizable advice；这与那些人们确实会讨论特定 techniques 的 pros and cons 的 fields 形成了鲜明对比](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1355661542155378688)[2](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:F)。

虽然 coach 可以在 1 对 1 或小组中给你量身定做的建议，但在网上很难做到这一点，所以，我在这里能做到的最好程度，就是那句模糊得毫无用处的“学习适合自己的 skills”。举个例子，对我特别合拍的两种 skills 是“having an adversarial mindset”和“looking at data”。一条也许没那么无用的建议是：如果你很难 identifying 这些 skills 可能是什么，可以问问非常了解你的人。例如，我的 manager 和 Ben Kuhn 都曾独立指出，提出 span many levels of abstraction 的 solutions，是我经常 apply 的一项 skill（在他们指出之前，我都没有意识到自己在这么做）。

另一种找到这些 skills 的办法，是寻找那些你忍不住会做、但大多数其他人似乎不会做的事情。对我来说，“looking at data”和“having an adversarial mindset”都是如此。就拿 having an adversarial mindset 来说，我曾就职的一家公司在 beta testing 一款新的 custom bug tracker 时，我提交了它最早的一批 bugs，并往 fields 里填入不寻常的东西，看看它会不会 break。有些人真的不理解，为什么有人会做这种事，感到困惑、反感或惊恐；但也有少数人（包括它的 authors，我知道他们不会介意）真正理解，而且很高兴看到这个 system 被 pushed past its limits。对我来说，Poking at 一个 system 的 limits，看看它在哪里 falls apart，并不像是在工作；如果我不想这么做，反而得阻止自己。因此，花十年时间把 testing 和 verification techniques 练得更好，感觉是件很难不去做的事情，而不是工作。Looking deeply into data 也是我至今已经投入十多年时间的一件事；对我而言，不在这件事上 improve，情感上几乎会让我觉得不对劲。

这些事情之所以 suited to 我，基本上是因为我的 personality，而不是 human beings 的某种 inherent 特性。其他人会有不同的事情，让他们真切地觉得 easy/right，这很好，因为如果人人都热衷于 looking at data，却没有人热衷于 building things，那就会很成问题（不过，在我看来，looking at data 在 on average 的意义上是 underrated 的）。

在我尝试学习的事情中，另一个主要因素是找到有利于学习的 environments，让我能学习与我的 skills 相契合、对我而言合理的事情。虽然建议其他人也这么做，听起来像是显而易见到毫无用处的建议，但根据我所见到的人们选择去哪一个 team、哪一家 company 工作的方式，我认为几乎没有人这么做，因此，讨论这件事或许也并非完全没有用处。

有一个没有这么做的例子，很能代表我平时见到的情况。我只是偶然了解到这件事：我和一位 manager 聊起了他们的 team 为什么失去了那位刚由 intern 转为 full-time 的 employee。我之所以问起，是因为这位 manager 很少流失任何人；他们非常善于 retaining people，手下 teams 的 turnover 也很 low。原来，那位 intern 本想从事 infra 工作，却加入了这位 manager 的 product team，因为他们不知道自己可以要求加入符合自己偏好的 team。manager 得知后，希望这位 intern 能开心，便促成了他们向一个 infra team 的 transfer。在这个例子中，问题是双重的，因为这位 new hire 在两个方面都没有考虑到，应当在有利于学习自己想要的 skills 的 environment 中工作。他们没有尝试在自己感兴趣的 area 工作，而且加入的 company 还有一个 dysfunctional infra org，其 design 和 operational practices 通常都很 poor；因此，除了根本没有尝试加入 infra team，这家 company 本身也是一个相对难以学习 infra 的地方。虽然这是一个格外糟糕的例子，但在我见到的 median case 中，人们在 learning 方面所做的 decisions，并不会带来特别好的 outcomes，尽管良好的学习机会是人们口中最想要的东西之一。

例如，Steve Yegge 曾指出：

> college candidates 最常问的 question 是：“你们提供什么样的 training 和/或 mentoring？”……一位 UW interviewee 刚刚向我讲述了 Ford Motor Company 的 mentoring program，Ford 显然把它当作向 interviewees 推介自己的说辞的一部分。\[我省略了细节，因为它们并不怎么相关。-stevey 3/1/2006\] 那位学生记住的细节之多，令人惊叹。这其实并不让我意外，因为这是 candidates 最关心的事情之一。

对我自己来说，我很幸运，第一份工作所在的 Centaur 是一个很适合在 testing 和 verification 方面培养 having an adversarial mindset 的地方。将那里的 verification team 所取得的成果拿来比较时，可以发现，它与其他 companies 的 peer projects 相当，而后者雇用了大得多的 teams 来做非常相似的事情，effectiveness 却相似甚至更差；这意味着那个 team 是 highly productive 的，也让那里成为了一个非常适合学习的地方。

而且，我不认为靠自己，或尝试遵循书籍或互联网上的建议，我能学得那么快。我认为，[真正擅长某件事的人，脑中有关如何做这件事的 information 有太多 bits，以至于这些 information 实际上无法被 compressible 地装进一本书，更不用说一篇 blog post](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/)。在 sports 中，好的 coaches 能够随着时间逐渐传递这类 information，但我不知道 programming 中有什么类似的东西，因此我认为，就 learning rate 而言，目前最好的办法是找到一个满是 experts 的 environment[3](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:M)。

对于“looking at data”，虽然我在那些人们并不真正重视 data 的 environments 中练习这项 skill，也进步了很多，但过去几年的 rate of improvement 要高得多；在这几年的 environment 中，我可以与一些人反复交流想法，他们非常善于理解 data 能告诉我们什么的 limitations，也善于用 deep domain knowledge 为 data analysis 提供依据。我会说，在当前这份工作中的每一年，我在这方面的进步，都超过了开始这份工作之前十年的进步。

或许有一点值得注意：environment，也就是你每天如何度过，本质上是 local 的。我目前的 employer，可能是我工作过的三家大型 tech companies 中最不 data driven 的，但我身边却是一个很适合提升 looking at data 能力的地方，因为我用相对 large fraction 的时间与 Rebecca Isaacs 这样非常擅长处理 data 的人共事，而与不重视 data 的人共事的时间只占相对 small fraction。

这篇 post 讨论了一些 strategies，着眼于它们为什么可能有价值，但我必须承认，我向 experts 学习的 motivation 并不是 create value。更多是因为我觉得学习很有趣，而且在某些 areas，无论 environment 如何，我都有足够的动力去 apply 那些 skills；向 experts 学习又是一个如此好的享受乐趣的机会，让人很难抗拒。这样做了二十来年，结果证明它确实有用，但很长一段时间里，我并不知道会有这样的结果（而且，直到我从 hardware 转向 software，我才知道这件事能够 effectively transfer 到一个新的 industry）。

我看到的许多 career advice，都以 career、success 或 growth 为导向。这类建议往往告诉人们，心中要有一个 long-term goal 或 strategy。它经常会提出这样的 argument：“random walk 只会让你在某个 direction 上 move sqrt(n)，而 directed walk 会让你在某个 direction 上 move n。”我不认为这有什么错，但我认为，对许多人来说，这类建议 implicitly underestimates 了找到适合自己的 area 的难度[4](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:S)，而我基本上是[通过 trial and error 做到这一点的](https://twitter.com/jeanqasaur/status/1074528356324892672)。

### 附录：本文未详细讨论的 problem 的部分方面

一个未讨论的重要 topic 是，如何 balance 要着力于哪个 "level" 的 skill：它可以是某种 high level 的东西，例如 "looking at data"，也可以是某种 lower level 的东西，例如 "Bayesian multilevel models"，还可以是某种更 lower level 的东西，例如 "typing speed"。这个 topic 足够庞大，值得单独写一篇文章，而且我预计会比本文更长；不过，目前先看看 [Gary Bernhardt 关于某个相关问题的一条评论，我认为它也适用于这个 topic](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/#appendix-one-way-to-think-about-what-to-improve)。

这里未讨论的另一个重要 topic 是，挑选那些相对更可能 applicable 的 skills。只是说一个人应该考虑 learning 自己有 aptitude 的 skills，却不考虑 applicability，未免有些过于天真。

不过，挑出那些很难对世界产生影响、赚到一笔像样的钱，或者实现你想实现的任何目标的 skills，例如 "basketball" 或 "boxing"，相当容易；而在看起来可行的 skills 之间进行挑选，例如 computer architecture 与 PL，就更难了。

但我认为，那些听起来还算合理的 skills，如果很适合某个人，就有足够大的可能带来 high return，因此在听起来还算合理的 skills 之间进行 trial and error 是可以的，不过，[能够快速尝试各种事情](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/)大概会有帮助。

-   Ben Kuhn 谈某种意义上[真正 learn 某样东西是什么样的体验](https://www.benkuhn.net/conviction/)
-   Holden Karnofsky 谈[对 careers 采取 aptitude-first approach，而非 career-path-first approach](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/holden-karnofsky-building-aptitudes-kicking-ass/)，这有点类似于考虑 "looking at data" 或 "having an adversarial mindset" 这样的 cross cutting skills，而不只是考虑 "compilers" 或 "queuing theory" 这样的 skills
-   Peter Drucker 谈[如何了解自己的 strengths 和 weaknesses，并从事与自身 inclinations 相符的工作](https://www.csub.edu/~ecarter2/CSUB.MKTG%20490%20F10/DRUCKER%20HBR%20Managing%20Oneself.pdf)
-   Alexy Guzey 谈[建议的 effectiveness](https://guzey.com/advice/)

    无论如何，尽管我在这方面的 sample 相当小，但我认为其中的 mechanism 是 plausible 的，因为我认识的 excellent managers 都有非常高的 retention，同时还有一大队人想在他们手下工作，这使他们相对容易 hire 和 retain 具有 world-class expertise 的人，因为[其他地方的状况实在太惨淡了](https://danluu.com/hiring-lemons/)。

    一种更常见的 strategy，是去做周围听起来最有趣和/或最难的 problems；我认为它通常不奏效，而我尝试过之后，效果也不太好。虽然在尝试[着手处理有趣 / 困难的 problems](https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/)时，我确实与一些非常出色的人共事过，其中包括我合作过的最好的 engineers 之一，但就与那些我真正想向其学习的人共事而言，我认为这种做法远不如寻找好的 management。我相信，这种 algorithm 的一般性 problem，与因为 video games 很酷和/或很有趣就去从事 video games 工作的 problem 是一样的。如此多的人想从事听起来令人兴奋的 problems，导致了可以无限期持续下去的 dysfunctional environments。

    有一次，我所在的 team 在九个月内出现了 100% turnover；如果不是其中一个人花了很长时间才找到可以 transfer 过去的 team，这个时间本来会是六个月。在 median case 中，我的 cohort（大致在我加入时前后加入的人）有大约 50% YoY turnover，而且我认为，人们离开都有相当充分的理由。这种 turnover 不仅表明，那个 environment 往往让人相当不愉快，而且这类 environments 往往会 differentially 导致那些我想与之共事和/或向其学习的人离开。例如，我曾在一个 team 与 Ikhwan Lee 共事，他非常出色，但那个 team 的 TL 不相信应该使用 version control、automated testing 或 pipelined designs。当然，Ikhwan 很快就离开了，而那位 TL 留了下来，六年后仍然在那里。

    [\[返回\]](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fnref:M)
4.  我在熟人中多次见到的一种情况是，人们还完全不知道某个方向是否适合自己，就先选定了它。往往过了相当长一段时间（有时超过十年），他们才意识到，自己实际上对所走的方向非常不满意，有时是因为它不符合自己的 temperament，有时是因为那其实是自己不擅长的事情。无论如何，与沿着一条令自己不开心的路走出 n 相比，随机地四处走走，发现自己在一条令自己开心的路上走出了 sqrt(n)，似乎也没那么糟。[\[返回\]](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fnref:S)

## 原文

It's common to see people advocate for learning skills that they have or using processes that they use. For example, Steve Yegge has a set of blog posts where he recommends reading compiler books and learning about compilers. His reasoning is basically that, if you understand compilers, you'll see compiler problems everywhere and will recognize all of the cases where people are solving a compiler problem without using compiler knowledge. Instead of hacking together some half-baked solution that will never work, you can apply a bit of computer science knowledge to solve the problem in a better way with less effort. That's not untrue, but it's also not a reason to study compilers in particular because you can say that about many different areas of computer science and math. Queuing theory, computer architecture, mathematical optimization, operations research, etc.

One response to that kind of objection is to say that [one should study everything](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/899141882760110081). While being an extremely broad generalist can work, it's gotten much harder to "know a bit of everything" and be effective because there's more of everything over time (in terms of both breadth and depth). And even if that weren't the case, I think saying “should” is too strong; whether or not someone enjoys having that kind of breadth is a matter of taste. Another approach that can also work, one that's more to my taste, is to, [as Gian Carlo Rota put it](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~cahn/life/gian-carlo-rota-10-lessons.html), learn a few tricks:

> A long time ago an older and well known number theorist made some disparaging remarks about Paul Erdos' work. You admire contributions to mathematics as much as I do, and I felt annoyed when the older mathematician flatly and definitively stated that all of Erdos' work could be reduced to a few tricks which Erdos repeatedly relied on in his proofs. What the number theorist did not realize is that other mathematicians, even the very best, also rely on a few tricks which they use over and over. Take Hilbert. The second volume of Hilbert's collected papers contains Hilbert's papers in invariant theory. I have made a point of reading some of these papers with care. It is sad to note that some of Hilbert's beautiful results have been completely forgotten. But on reading the proofs of Hilbert's striking and deep theorems in invariant theory, it was surprising to verify that Hilbert's proofs relied on the same few tricks. Even Hilbert had only a few tricks!

If you look at how people succeed in various fields, you'll see that this is a common approach. For example, [this analysis of world-class judo players found that most rely on a small handful of throws](https://judoinfo.com/weers1/), concluding[1](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:J)

> Judo is a game of specialization. You have to use the skills that work best for you. You have to stick to what works and practice your skills until they become automatic responses.

If you watch an anime or a TV series "about" fighting, people often improve by increasing the number of techniques they know because that's an easy thing to depict but, in real life, getting better at techniques you already know is often more effective than having a portfolio of hundreds of "moves".

[Relatedly, Joy Ebertz says](https://staffeng.com/stories/joy-ebertz):

> One piece of advice I got at some point was to amplify my strengths. All of us have strengths and weaknesses and we spend a lot of time talking about ‘areas of improvement.’ It can be easy to feel like the best way to advance is to eliminate all of those. However, it can require a lot of work and energy to barely move the needle if it’s truly an area we’re weak in. Obviously, you still want to make sure you don’t have any truly bad areas, but assuming you’ve gotten that, instead focus on amplifying your strengths. How can you turn something you’re good at into your superpower?

I've personally found this to be true in a variety of disciplines. While it's really difficult to measure programmer effectiveness in anything resembling an objective manner, this isn't true of some things I've done, like competitive video games (a very long time ago at this point, back before there was "real" money in competitive gaming), the thing that took me from being a pretty decent player to a [very good player](https://danluu.com/look-stupid/#fn:B) was abandoning practicing things I wasn't particularly good at and focusing on increasing the edge I had over everybody else at the few things I was unusually good at.

This can work for games and sports because you can get better maneuvering yourself into positions that take advantage of your strengths as well as avoiding situations that expose your weaknesses. I think this is actually more effective at work than it is in sports or gaming since, unlike in competitive endeavors, you don't have an opponent who will try to expose your weaknesses and force you into positions where your strengths are irrelevant. If I study queuing theory instead of compilers, a rival co-worker isn't going to stop me from working on projects where queuing theory knowledge is helpful and leave me facing a field full of projects that require compiler knowledge.

One thing that's worth noting is that skills don't have to be things people would consider fields of study or discrete techniques. For the past three years, the main skill I've been applying and improving is something you might call "looking at data"; the term is in quotes because I don't know of a good term for it. I don't think it's what most people would think of as "statistics", in that I don't often need to do anything as sophisticated as logistic regression, let alone actually sophisticated. Perhaps one could argue that this is something data scientists do, but if I look at what I do vs. what data scientists we hire do as well as what we screen for in data scientist interviews, we don't appear to want to hire data scientists with the skill I've been working on nor do they do what I'm doing (this is a long enough topic that I might turn it into its own post at some point).

Unlike Matt Might or Steve Yegge, I'm not going to say that you should take a particular approach, but I'll say that working on a few things and not being particularly well rounded has worked for me in multiple disparate fields and it appears to work for a lot of other folks as well.

If you want to take this approach, this still leaves the question of what skills to learn. This is one of the most common questions I get asked and I think my answer is probably not really what people are looking for and not very satisfying since it's both [obvious and difficult to put into practice](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1428445465662603272).

For me, two ingredients for figuring out what to spend time learning are having a relative aptitude for something (relative to other things I might do, not relative to other people) and also having a good environment in which to learn. To say that someone should look for those things is so vague that's it's nearly useless, but it's still better than the usual advice, which boils down to "learn what I learned", which results in advice like "Career pro tip: if you want to get good, REALLY good, at designing complex and stateful distributed systems at scale in real-world environments, learn functional programming. It is an almost perfectly identical skillset." or the even more extreme claims from some language communities, like Chuck Moore's claim that Forth is [at least 100x as productive as boring languages](https://danluu.com/boring-languages/).

I took generic internet advice early in my career, including language advice (this was when much of this kind of advice was relatively young and it was not yet possible to easily observe that, despite many people taking advice like this, people who took this kind of advice were not particularly effective and people who are particularly effective were not likely to have taken this kind of advice). I learned [Haskell, Lisp, Forth](https://twitter.com/sc13ts/status/1448003352655060997), [etc](https://malisper.me/there-is-more-to-programming-than-programming-languages/). At one point in my career, I was on a two person team that implemented what might still be, a decade later, the highest performance Forth processor in existence (it was a 2GHz IPC-oriented processor) and I programmed it as well (there were good reasons for this to be a stack processor, so Forth seemed like as good a choice as any). [Like Yossi Kreinin, I think I can say that I spent more effort than most people have becoming proficient in Forth, and like him, not only did I not find it find it to be a 100x productivity tool, it wasn't clear that it would, in general, even be 1x on productivity](https://yosefk.com/blog/my-history-with-forth-stack-machines.html). To be fair, a number of other tools did better than 1x on productivity but, overall, I think following internet advice was very low ROI and the things that I learned that were high ROI weren't things people were recommending.

In retrospect, when people said things like "Forth is very productive", what I suspect they really meant was "Forth makes me very productive and I have not considered how well this generalizes to people with different aptitudes or who are operating in different contexts". I find it totally plausible that Forth (or Lisp or Haskell or any other tool or technique) does work very well for some particular people, but I think that people tend to overestimate how much something working for them means that it works for other people, [making advice generally useless because it doesn't distinguish between advice that's aptitude or circumstance specific and generalizable advice, which is in stark contrast to fields where people actually discuss the pros and cons of particular techniques](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1355661542155378688)[2](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:F).

While a coach can give you advice that's tailored to you 1 on 1 or in small groups, that's difficult to do on the internet, which is why the best I can do here is the uselessly vague "pick up skills that are suitable for you". Just for example, two skills that clicked for me are "having an adversarial mindset" and "looking at data". A perhaps less useless piece of advice is that, if you're having a hard time identifying what those might be, you can ask people who know you very well, e.g., my manager and Ben Kuhn independently named coming up with solutions that span many levels of abstraction as a skill of mine that I frequently apply (and I didn't realize I was doing that until they pointed it out).

Another way to find these is to look for things you can't help but do that most other people don't seem to do, which is true for me of both "looking at data" and "having an adversarial mindset". Just for example, on having an adversarial mindset, when a company I was working for was beta testing a new custom bug tracker, I filed some of the first bugs on it and put unusual things into the fields to see if it would break. Some people really didn't understand why anyone would do such a thing and were baffled, disgusted, or horrified, but a few people (including the authors, who I knew wouldn't mind), really got it and were happy to see the system pushed past its limits. Poking at the limits of a system to see where it falls apart doesn't feel like work to me; it's something that I'd have to stop myself from doing if I wanted to not do it, which made spending a decade getting better at testing and verification techniques felt like something hard not to do and not work. Looking deeply into data is one I've spent more than a decade on at this point and it's another one that, to me, emotionally feels almost wrong to not improve at.

That these things are suited to me is basically due to my personality, and not something inherent about human beings. Other people are going to have different things that really feel easy/right for them, which is great, since if everyone was into looking at data and no one was into building things, that would be very problematic (although, IMO, looking at data is, on average, underrated).

The other major ingredient in what I've tried to learn is finding environments that are conducive to learning things that line up with my skills that make sense for me. Although suggesting that other people do the same sounds like advice that's so obvious that it's useless, based on how I've seen people select what team and company to work on, I think that almost nobody does this and, as a result, discussing this may not be completely useless.

An example of not doing this which typifies what I usually see is a case I just happened to find out about because I chatted with a manager about why their team had lost their new full-time intern conversion employee. I asked them about it since it was unusual for that manager to lose anyone since they're very good at retaining people and have low turnover on their teams. It turned out that their intern had wanted to work on infra, but had joined this manager's product team because they didn't know that they could ask to be on a team that matched their preferences. After the manager found out, the manager wanted the intern to be happy and facilitated a transfer to an infra team. In this case, this was a double whammy since the new hire doubly didn't consider working in an environment conducive for learning the skills they wanted. They made no attempt to work in the area they were interested in and then they joined a company that has a dysfunctional infra org that generally has poor design and operational practices, making the company a relatively difficult place to learn about infra on top of not even trying to land on an infra team. While that's an unusually bad example, in the median case that I've seen, people don't make decisions that result in particularly good outcomes with respect to learning even though good opportunities to learn are one of the top things people say that they want.

For example, Steve Yegge has noted:

> The most frequently-asked question from college candidates is: "what kind of training and/or mentoring do you offer?" ... One UW interviewee just told me about Ford Motor Company's mentoring program, which Ford had apparently used as part of the sales pitch they do for interviewees. \[I've elided the details, as they weren't really relevant. -stevey 3/1/2006\] The student had absorbed it all in amazing detail. That doesn't really surprise me, because it's one of the things candidates care about most.

For myself, I was lucky that my first job, Centaur, was a great place to develop having an adversarial mindset with respect to testing and verification. When I compare what the verification team there accomplished, it's comparable to peer projects at other companies that employed much larger teams to do very similar things with similar or worse effectiveness, implying that the team was highly productive, which made that a really good place to learn.

Moreover, I don't think I could've learned as quickly on my own or by trying to follow advice from books or the internet. I think that [people who are really good at something have too many bits of information in their head about how to do it for that information to really be compressible into a book, let alone a blog post](https://danluu.com/hardware-unforgiving/). In sports, good coaches are able to convey that kind of information over time, but I don't know of anything similar for programming, so I think the best thing available for learning rate is to find an environment that's full of experts[3](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:M).

For "looking at data", while I got a lot better at it from working on that skill in environments where people weren't really taking data seriously, the rate of improvement during the past few years, where I'm in an environment where I can toss ideas back and forth with people who are very good at understanding the limitations of what data can tell you as well as good at informing data analysis with deep domain knowledge, has been much higher. I'd say that I improved more at this in each individual year at my current job than I did in the decade prior to my current job.

One thing to perhaps note is that the environment, how you spend your day-to-day, is inherently local. My current employer is probably the least data driven of the three large tech companies I've worked for, but my vicinity is a great place to get better at looking at data because I spend a relatively large fraction of my time working with people who are great with data, like Rebecca Isaacs, and a relatively small fraction of the time working with people who don't take data seriously.

This post has discussed some strategies with an eye towards why they can be valuable, but I have to admit that my motivation for learning from experts wasn’t to create value. It's more that I find learning to be fun and there are some areas where I'm motivated enough to apply the skills regardless of the environment, and learning from experts is such a great opportunity to have fun that it's hard to resist. Doing this for a couple of decades has turned out to be useful, but that's not something I knew would happen for quite a while (and I had no idea that this would effectively transfer to a new industry until I changed from hardware to software).

A lot of career advice I see is oriented towards career or success or growth. That kind of advice often tells people to have a long-term goal or strategy in mind. It will often have some argument that's along the lines of "a random walk will only move you sqrt(n) in some direction whereas a directed walk will move you n in some direction". I don't think that's wrong, but I think that, for many people, that advice implicitly underestimates the difficulty of finding an area that's suited to you[4](https://danluu.com/learn-what/#fn:S), which I've basically [done by trial and error](https://twitter.com/jeanqasaur/status/1074528356324892672).

### Appendix: parts of the problem this post doesn't discuss in detail

One major topic not discussed is how to balance what "level" of skill to work on, which could be something high level, like "looking at data", to something lower level, like "Bayesian multilevel models", to something even lower level, like "typing speed". That's a large enough topic that it deserves its own post that I'd expect to be longer than this one but, for now, [here's a comment from Gary Bernhardt about something related that I believe also applies to this topic](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/#appendix-one-way-to-think-about-what-to-improve).

Another major topic that's not discussed here is picking skills that are relatively likely to be applicable. It's a little too naive to just say that someone should think about learning skills they have an aptitude for without thinking about applicability.

But while it's pretty easy to pick out skills where it's very difficult to either have an impact on the world or make a decent amount of money or achieve whatever goal you might want to achieve, like "basketball" or "boxing", it's harder to pick between plausible skills, like computer architecture vs. PL.

But I think semi-reasonable sounding skills are likely enough to be high return if they're a good fit for someone that trial and error among semi-reasonable sounding skills is fine, although it probably helps [to be able to try things out quickly](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/)
