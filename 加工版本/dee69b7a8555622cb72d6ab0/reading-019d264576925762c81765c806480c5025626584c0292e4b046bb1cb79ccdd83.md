## 正文

## 引言

我花了不少时间与人进行各种 mentoring conversations。我通常很享受这件事，但有些做法比其他做法更能让我觉得这些交谈有趣，而且是有趣_得多_！这篇文章试着汇集其中的一些做法。

显然，写这篇文章多少有些为自己打算，因为我希望以后接受我 mentor 的人能读到它！不过，我预计其中至少_有些_内容很有可能也能 generalise 到其他人身上。也希望它对 mentees 有用！根据我自己尝试的经历，向别人寻求 mentorship 可能相当令人焦虑。而对于如何让对方更享受交谈、更尊重对方的时间，有一个更清晰的 model，会让主动联系变得容易些。不过请注意，这篇文章列了不少事情——请把它们当作一些建议和锦上添花的做法，而不是觉得漏掉其中某些，我就会对你有什么看法！

**背景：** 我在这篇文章中设想的 mentoring chat，主要是一场 career coaching chat：对方想从事 AI Safety，我此前不认识这个人，也不一定预期以后还会再聊。不过，其中许多内容也能 generalise 到其他种类的 mentoring chats，例如我更侧重于提供人生或 productivity advice，或试着帮助朋友／更熟悉的人等。

## 建议

### 交代 Context

在通话开始时做下面这些事会很有帮助，让我了解情况，以及怎样才能最好地帮到你：

-   说清楚你想从这次通话中得到什么，以及我怎样才能最有帮助

    -   一个简单的做法，是一开始就明确说出你的 goals 和 key uncertainties

        -   这里一个常见错误，是只想说那些听起来不错／rational 的 key uncertainties。如果你的 key uncertainty 是一个不那么具体的 emotional crux，例如“我基本认同 AI Safety 很重要的那些 arguments，但想到要从事一件只有 small chance 能产生影响的事情，情绪上还是感到不舒服”，那就说出来！如果我关注的东西并不 relevant，就没法帮到你。

        -   一个很好的 litmus test——如果你对我们正在讨论的 question 改变了想法，这会怎样改变你的 actions？

    -   另一个角度，是说明你想要哪一_种_通话。你是否有几个 concrete options，希望有人帮你 deciding between？这是否更像一场 exploratory conversation，想弄清楚有哪些可能？你是想深入探讨某个 specific question，还是有一长串 questions，想简短地逐一谈到？

-   让我了解你如何看待这次通话——你是否把我视为一个 authority figure，希望我给你 answers；你是否把这看作许多 advice 来源之一；你是否相当 self-confident，预期自己大体会 stick to your priors，还是愿意大幅 updating，等等。

    -   直接说这些相当尴尬，但在讨论你想从通话中得到什么时，很容易间接体现出来

-   你的背景与处境

    -   To first order，我在这里主要希望能大致对你进行 type-cast——例如，你只是想听一个 introductory pitch，还是已经 heavily engaged、也已经获得了不少 low-hanging fruit 的人，等等。

        -   由我来弄清楚这一点还挺麻烦的！我的主要办法，是接连问一些 questions，了解你已经熟悉什么，但这样可能会让 engaged 的人觉得我居高临下，也可能让 less engaged 的人感到疏离

    -   To second order，听到你身上任何特别、而且可能 relevant 的情况，也非常有帮助，有时这会 significantly adjust 我想给出的 advice。

        -   在这里不妨降低分享的门槛，因为很难判断什么 relevant、什么不 relevant！

        -   这也包括你身上不那么具体的 traits，例如你是否特别喜欢 reading papers 或 crunching through code，觉得 self-study 困难还是容易，等等。

-   让我听听你从别人那里得到过的 advice，以及已经尝试过的事情——这有助于我 specialise，从而提供最大的 marginal add

    -   听到你已经尝试过什么，尤其有帮助！我经常能提供的一项主要 value add，就是试着给出“[obvious advice](https://mindingourway.com/obvious-advice/)”——对于哪些事情显然应该去做，人们的看法可能差得非常远！但如果我只是列出一堆 mentee 早已尝试过的事情，就会有点尴尬。

        -   例如，对有些人来说，‘cold emailing relevant people’ 是显而易见、而且早已试过的事，另一些人却完全不会想到。

### 少费力气

我理想中的 mentoring chat，是不觉得自己需要推动交谈的那种——对方知道自己想要什么，能向我提问，而我只需 reactive 地回应。这比那种让我觉得有压力、需要推动交谈并琢磨怎样才能最有帮助的对话，轻松_得多_。有时，reactive 地回应也意味着说很多话，或试着通过交谈做某件具体的事，但关键区别在于，不_需要_由我推动，也不会出现让人尴尬的冷场。

-   知道自己想要什么

    -   理想的做法，是事先 brainstorm 出一份 concrete questions 清单

        -   如果你在这里觉得卡住／有 aversion，就设一个 [5 分钟计时器](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/post-28-on-creativity-the-joys-of-5-minute-timers)，看看自己能 generate 多少 questions！

    -   这里常见的一种说法是“做好功课”。我觉得，比起大多数人，我没那么在意你是否读过所有显而易见的 resources 等等，但我_确实_在意人们有没有做好这样的功课：知道自己想要什么、对什么 uncertain、希望得到怎样的帮助，等等。

-   写一份 doc，交代 context 和你的 uncertainties/questions

    -   我觉得 zero-prep calls 要省力得多，所以理想情况下，我只需到时参加通话，当场阅读并评论

        -   注意：对我来说，2 次 1hr 的 zero-prep calls，比一次需要 1hr prep 的 1hr 通话省力得多——需要专门腾出时间 prep，感觉比到时直接参加通话费力得多！不过，我预计这种偏好不太能 generalises 到别人身上。

-   让交谈继续下去，事先准备好 questions，在自然停顿时提出

-   reactive 比 active 容易得多——如果你给我一堆 information，我就能很轻松地回应、提出 relevant points、correct 其中一些部分，等等

    -   例如，根据你已有的 knowledge 和 goals，描述你目前的 plan

    -   例如，描述你对某个仍感困惑的 topic，目前的 best-guess understanding

    -   例如，对一个 key uncertainty 给出你的 best guess

    -   在这里很容易感到不安——如果你对某件事还没有清晰的认识，试着描述它、冒着在比你懂得更多的人面前说错的风险，可能会令人尴尬！但我觉得，这样做非常值得，能帮我弄清楚怎样才能最好地帮助你，以及你目前处在什么位置。我建议你在开口前加上一句，例如“我预计自己遗漏了一些重要 details，但我目前的 best guess 是……”

-   reactive 的一个子要点是 [**paraphrasing**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) \- 在我说完一些话后，试着用你自己的话向我复述。这会让 correct 任何 misunderstandings 都变得非常容易。

    -   如果你在这里感到不安，我的建议是在前面加上“好，那么如果我正确 understood 了你，你的意思是……”

    -   Paraphrasing _非常棒_。我有相当高的 confidence，预期无论我说什么，对方都会 misunderstand _某些_ details。Paraphrasing 能帮我 identify 并 target 那些没有传达清楚的内容，也能帮我建立一个更好的、关于你的 model。

### 让它对_你_更有用

一个常见的 mistake 是，过分关注如何让 mentor 自我感觉良好，并试图针对你以为他们想要的东西进行 optimise——例如，对自己没太听明白的内容也跟着点头，因为你不想说自己没弄懂。

-   做到这一点的主要方式，是在通话中直接给出 feedback：

    -   如果我说的内容你已经知道了，就说出来！

    -   如果我讲得太快或太慢，就说出来！

    -   如果我误解了你的话，就说出来！

    -   如果我在谈你不关心的事情，或者没有触及你的 crux，就说出来！

    -   如果我在通话中做的某件事对你不起作用，就说出来！

        -   这也包括这样的话：“其实，我现在真的很累，这让我很难从这次通话中获得 value。我们能改个时间吗？”

-   总的来说，我对 mentoring chats 的看法是：_你_才是自己人生的 expert，而不是我。我有一些你可能没有的 general knowledge 和 priors，但你远比我更适合判断哪些内容对你是 applicable 的、哪些不是，而且每个人需要的 advice 都有细微差别。这意味着我会说很多东西，其中并非所有内容都会 apply，有些还需要补充细微的 nuances——只有你提出 pushback 并对我进行 correct，我才能弄清这些！

    -   你不需要把 pushback 表达得很清楚才能这样做——只要说类似“嗯，我不太被你刚才说的话说服，但很难讲清楚为什么”，就已经很好了——我们可以一起试着弄清哪里出了问题。

-   Prioritisation——总的来说，我预计一次 mentoring chat 中的 90% 都没什么用，而 10% 会有特别高的 value。对于哪些 topics 可能带来 high value advice、哪些不会，你大概有一些想法。而且，这因人而异。试着把谈话引向那些 topics！我们永远不可能有时间聊完所有 relevant 的事情，所以需要 triage！

-   我与人进行 mentoring chats，是因为我想_真正_ add value、帮上忙，而不只是觉得自己做到了！这意味着，我希望你直接针对什么能让这次交流对你有用来进行 optimise。

### 尽量减轻我的 anxiety

让 mentoring chats 更加耗神的一类事情，是那些引发 anxiety 的情况，例如，我担心自己做得不好、伤害对方，或给出其他 harmful advice。这一部分很难理清，也很难妥善 ameliorate，不过我觉得有些做法能有所帮助！

-   让我知道你对 bluntness/discouragement/negative feedback 的接受程度

    -   我经常与有 ambitious goals、想进入 competitive paths 的人交谈。这有很高的 failing 概率！既可能是因为能力不够，也可能是因为莫名其妙的 random noise。我认为，带着 well-calibrated expectations 去做这类事情很重要；而且，例如，如果你对 failure 格外 non-robust，就应该认真考虑不做。因此，我认为自己能发挥的一个重要作用，是 setting expectations，指出 flawed 或 overly ambitious 的 plans，并给出 blunt feedback。但这样做让人不好受，也确实可能伤害一些人，所以这相当难！

        -   这种情况很常见，因为 maximise 你的 impact 的最佳方式，往往是 pursuing high-risk, high-reward strategies。而 high-risk strategies，嗯，就是 high-risk。尤其是，大多数 EA careers 和大多数与 AI 有关的 careers，competitive 得要命。很多时候，pursuing 一条 competitive path _确实是_最佳 decision，因为其中有大量 randomness 和 uncertainty，而且事情_可能_进展得特别好，但建议别人这样做，可能很难！

    -   请只有在你确实能接受 blunt negative feedback 时，才 signal 你能接受！！

-   别把我的话看得太重

    -   我希望别人以这样的心态与我交谈：“我会试着给出有用的想法，有些会是对的，有些会是错的。我对自己在做什么也只有模糊的认识。你对自身 situation 的 context 比我多得多，需要由你来 filter 我的 advice，判断哪些对你有益、哪些没有。”

    -   有时候，人们对我的评价比我应得的高出_太多_，也过于看重我的话。我觉得这既是 empirically incorrect 的，也让我感觉 stakes 高出了太多！

        -   总的来说，给 advice 很难，而且是 noisy 的。如果你去问别人，他们很可能会在许多 points 上持不同意见！

    -   在社交场合，我往往显得非常 confident，因此我尤其担心这一点

-   不切实际的 high expectations

    -   给 advice 时，会遇到许多相当困难、棘手的 questions。你该如何度过自己的人生？如何在截然不同的 options 之间 choose？

        -   涉及 AI Alignment 的事情尤其如此：哪个 AI alignment agenda 最有前景？AI Alignment 比 AI Governance 更有 valuable 吗？AGI timelines 是怎样的？

    -   我很愿意就这类 questions 给出 hot takes。但这些问题真的很难，我没有好的或 confident 的 answers！面对这种 confusion，想让别人给你 answers，可能会让人觉得艰难、沮丧。这完全可以理解！但如果我觉得对方期待我能给出这些 answers，和他们交谈就会让我很不好受。

        -   不过，这里面相当一部分也可能只是我自己想多了，\*耸肩\*

        -   一个可能有帮助的具体做法，是在一个难题后加上：“显然，这个问题很难，我也不指望你对此有 confident 或完美的 answer，但我很想听听你的 takes！”

### 表达感谢

我做 mentoring chats，部分是因为真正 adding value 会带来一种暖心、满足的感觉。如果一次交谈能让我更强烈地感受到这一点，那就太好了！但仅仅表达感谢，效果并不太好，因为这更多反映的是你的性格，而不是这次交谈究竟很棒、还不错，还是平平无奇。对我而言，关键在于真诚、明确而具体。

-   提到你觉得有 valuable 的具体 takeaways

-   提到我在交谈中做了哪些具体的事情帮到了你

-   对交谈中本可以更有帮助的地方给出 feedback——在 object level 上，这本身就很有用；在 meta level 上，愿意给出 negative feedback，会让我更加信任 positive feedback！

-   长期的 follow-up

    -   听到你具体做出了哪些不同的行动，或者几个月后过得如何，会让我很开心！相比一个人在通话结束时的感受，这是真正 adding value 的_好得多的_ proxy

        -   我尤其喜欢听到人们对 counter-factual value add 的 estimate（如果是“可能没那么 counter-factual，不好意思”，也没关系）

## 原文

## Introduction

I spend a bunch of my time having various kinds of mentoring conversations with people. I generally enjoy doing this, but some things make these conversations _much_ more fun for me than others! This post is an attempt to compile some of these.

Obviously, writing this post is somewhat self-serving, since I hope future people I mentor might read it! But I expect there’s decent chance that at least _some_ of this will generalise to other people. And, hopefully this is also useful to mentees! Based on my experiences trying this, approaching people for mentorship can be pretty anxiety inducing. And having a clearer model of how to make this more fun for them and more respectful of their time helps make reaching out feel easier. But note that there’s a bunch of things in this post - please take them as some tips and nice-to-haves, rather than as eg things where I’ll judge you if you miss out some of them!

**Context:** The central example of a mentoring chat I imagine in this post is a career coaching chat, where I talk to someone who wants to work on AI Safety, who I don’t already know, and don’t necessarily expect to talk to again. But many of these generalise to other kinds of mentoring chats, eg where I’m orienting more towards giving life or productivity advice, trying to help out a friend/someone I know better, etc.

## Tips

### Context Setting

Useful things to happen at the start of a call, to give me an idea of what’s going on and how I can best help:

-   Making it clear what you want out of the call, and how I can be most useful

    -   An easy way to do this is to explicitly state your goals and key uncertainties at the start

        -   A common mistake here is trying to only state key uncertainties that sound good/rational. If your key uncertainty is a less tangible emotional crux, like “I basically buy the arguments that AI Safety matters, but feel emotionally uncomfortable with the idea of working on something with a small chance of mattering”, say! I can’t be useful if I’m focusing on stuff that isn’t relevant.

        -   A good litmus test - if you changed your mind on the question we’re discussing, how would this change your actions?

    -   Another angle is setting out the _kind_ of call you want. Do you have a few concrete options you want help deciding between? Is this more of an exploratory conversation to figure out what’s out there? Do you want to dive deep into a specific question, or do you have a long list of questions you want to cover briefly?

-   A sense of how you’re orienting to the call - are you seeing me as an authority figure who you want to give you answers, are you taking this as one source of advice among many, are you fairly self-confident and expect to mostly stick to your priors vs open to updating a lot, etc.

    -   This is pretty awkward to explicitly say, but is fairly easy to make implicit when discussing what you want out of the call

-   Your background and situation

    -   To first order, the main thing I want here is the ability to roughly type-cast you - eg someone who just wants an introductory pitch, someone who’s heavily engaged and already gotten a bunch of the low-hanging fruit, etc.

        -   This one is pretty annoying to figure out on my end! My main tactic is asking a series of questions about what you’re already familiar with, but this risks being patronising to engaged people and alienated to less engaged people

    -   To second order, it’s also really useful to hear anything weird and maybe relevant about you, which can sometimes significantly adjust the advice I want to give.

        -   Have a low bar for sharing things here, it’s hard to judge what is and is not relevant! 

        -   This includes less tangible traits about you, eg if you really love reading papers or crunching through code, find self-study hard vs easy, etc.

-   Hearing about advice you’ve gotten from other people, and things you’ve already tried - helps me specialise into having the biggest marginal add

    -   Hearing about what you’ve already tried is particularly helpful! Often a major value add is for me to try giving “[obvious advice](https://mindingourway.com/obvious-advice/)” - people’s sense of the obvious things to do can differ wildly! But it can be kind of awkward to just list a bunch of things the mentee has already tried.

        -   Eg, for some people ‘cold emailing relevant people’ is an obvious thing they’ve already tried, while some people would never think of that.

### Low Effort

My ideal mentoring chat is one where I don’t feel a need to drive the conversation - the other person knows what they want, can ask me things, and I can just be reactive. This feels _much_ easier than a conversation where I feel pressure to drive it and figure out how best to be useful. Sometimes being reactive involves talking a bunch, or trying to do something specific with the conversation, but the key difference is not _needing_ to drive it, and not having awkward-feeling gaps.

-   Know what you want

    -   The ideal thing is to brainstorm a list of concrete questions beforehand

        -   If you feel stuck/aversion at this, set a [5 minute timer](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/post-28-on-creativity-the-joys-of-5-minute-timers) and see how many questions you can generate!

    -   A common handle here is “do your homework”. I think I care less than most about whether you’ve eg read all the obvious resources, etc, but I _do_ care about people doing their homework in the sense of knowing what they want, what they’re uncertain about, how they want to be helped, etc.

-   Write up a doc with context and your uncertainties/questions

    -   I find zero-prep calls much lower effort, and so ideally I could just turn up to the call and live-read and comment

        -   Note: 2 1hr zero-prep calls feel much lower effort than to me than a 1hr call with 1hr of prep - needing to make time for prep feels much more effortful than just turning up to a call! I expect this preference generalises less though.

-   Keep the conversation going, have questions queued up that you ask at natural pauses

-   It’s much easier to be reactive than active - if you give me a bunch of information, I can react to it, give relevant points, correct parts, etc all pretty easily

    -   Eg, describe your current plan, given your existing knowledge and goals

    -   Eg, describe your current best-guess understanding of a topic you’re confused about

    -   Eg, give your best guess for a key uncertainty

    -   It’s easy to feel insecure here - if you don’t have a clear picture of something, it can feel embarrassing to try describing it and risk messing up in front of someone who knows more than you! But I think doing this can be really worthwhile for helping me figure out how best to help and where you’re currently at. I recommend prefixing what you say with eg “I expect I’m missing some important details, but my current best guess is…”

-   A sub-point of being reactive is [**paraphrasing**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) \- after I’ve said something, try repeating it back to me in your own words. This makes it super easy to correct any misunderstandings.

    -   If you feel insecure here, my advice is to prefix it with “OK, so if I understood you correctly, what you’re saying is…”

    -   Paraphrasing is _great_. I expect with fairly high confidence that someone will misunderstand _some_ details of anything I say. Paraphrasing helps me identify and target the things that didn’t come across, and helps me build a better model of you.

### Make it more useful to _you_

A common mistake is to focus too much on helping the mentor feel good about themselves and trying to optimise for what you think they want - eg nodding along to things that didn’t quite make sense to you, because you don’t want to say you’re confused. 

-   The main way of doing this is by directly giving feedback in the call:

    -   If you already know what I’m saying, say so!

    -   If I’m going too fast or too slowly, say so!

    -   If I’ve misunderstood what you said, say so!

    -   If I’m talking about stuff you don’t care about, or failing to engage with your crux, say so!

    -   If something I’m doing in the call isn’t working for you, say so!

        -   This includes things like “actually, I’m really tired right now and this is majorly preventing me from getting value from this call. Can we reschedule?”

-   In general, the way I orient to mentoring chats is that _you_ are the expert on your life, not me. I have some general knowledge and priors you may not have, but you are much better placed to judge what is and is not applicable to you, and everyone needs subtly different advice. This means I will say a bunch of stuff, and not all of it will apply, and some will need subtle nuances - the only way I can figure this out is if you pushback and correct me on things!

    -   You don’t need to have well articulated pushback to do this - just saying something like “hmm, I’m not convinced by what you just said, but find it hard to articulate why” is great - we can try to figure out what went wrong together.

-   Prioritisation - in general, I expect 90% of a mentoring chat to be kinda useless, and 10% to be particularly high value. You likely have some idea of what kinds of topics could lead to high value advice vs not. And this differs between differently people. Try to target the conversation onto those! We’ll never have time to talk about everything relevant, and need to triage!

-   The reason I have mentoring chats with people is because I want to _actually_ add value and be helpful, not just think I did! This means I want you to directly optimise for what would make this useful to you.

### Minimise my anxiety

One thing that can make mentoring chats much more draining is things that cause anxiety, where I’m eg afraid of doing a bad job, hurting the other person, or otherwise giving harmful advice. This is a messy section and hard to properly ameliorate, but I think some things can help!

-   Give a sense of how receptive you are to bluntness/discouragement/negative feedback

    -   I often talk to people who have ambitious goals and want to get into competitive paths. This has a high chance of failing! Both due to maybe not being good enough, and due to dumb random noise. I think it’s important to go into this kind of thing with well-calibrated expectations, and to seriously consider not doing it if eg you’re unusually non-robust to failure. And thus, I think an important role I can play is setting expectations, calling out flawed or overly ambitious plans, and giving blunt feedback. But this feels bad, and can really hurt some people, which makes this pretty hard!

        -   This comes up a lot because often the best way to maximise your impact is by pursuing high-risk, high-reward strategies. And high-risk strategies are, well, high-risk. In particular, most EA careers and most careers to do with AI are competitive as fuck. Often, pursuing a competitive path _is_ the best decision, since there’s a lot of randomness and uncertainty and it _could_ go super well, but this can be a difficult thing to advise someone to do!

    -   Please only signal that you’re OK receiving blunt negative feedback if you actually are!!

-   Don’t take me too seriously

    -   My ideal way for someone to orient to a conversation with me is “I will try to give useful thoughts, some will be right, some will be wrong. I only vaguely know what I am doing. You have way more context on your situation than me, and it’s up to you to filter my advice into what is and is not good for you”

    -   Sometimes, people have a _way_ higher opinion of me than is deserved, and put way too much stock in what I say. I think this is both empirically incorrect, and makes things feel way higher stakes for me! 

        -   In general, giving advice is hard and noisy. If you ask someone else, they’ll likely disagree on a bunch of points!

    -   I tend to present socially as being very confident, which makes this something I’m particularly concerned about

-   Unrealistically high expectations

    -   There’s a bunch of pretty hard and thorny questions that go along with giving advice. What should you do with your life? How to choose between wildly different options?

        -   This is particularly bad with AI Alignment related things: Which AI alignment agenda is most promising? Is AI Alignment more valuable than AI Governance? What are AGI timelines?

    -   I’m pretty happy to give hot takes on questions like this. But these are really hard and I don’t have good or confident answers to them! And it can feel hard and frustrating to confront this kind of confusion, and to want someone else to give you answers. Which is totally understandable! But it feels pretty bad to chat to someone who I think expects me to be able to give them answers to this.

        -   A decent chunk of this may just be in my head though, \*shrug\*

        -   A concrete thing that could help with this is suffixing a hard question with “obviously this is a hard question, and I don’t expect you to have a confident or perfect answer to this, but I’d love to hear your takes!”

### Appreciation

Part of why I do mentoring chats is the warm fuzzy feeling of actually adding value. And having chats where I feel this more strongly is great! But just expressing thanks doesn’t work great, because this says more about your personality than about whether this was a great vs decent vs meh chat. The key things that help this for me are being sincere, being specific, and being concrete.

-   Mention specific takeaways you found valuable

-   Mention specific things I did in the chat that were helpful

-   Give feedback on things in the chat that could have been more helpful - on an object level this is just useful, and on a meta level the willingness to give negative feedback makes me trust positive feedback way more!

-   Long-run follow-up

    -   Hearing about concrete things you did differently, or how you’re doing a few months on feels great! This is a _much_ better proxy for actually adding value than how someone feels at the end of the call

        -   I particularly like getting people’s estimate of the counter-factual value add (it’s fine if this is ‘probably not that counter-factual, sorry’)
