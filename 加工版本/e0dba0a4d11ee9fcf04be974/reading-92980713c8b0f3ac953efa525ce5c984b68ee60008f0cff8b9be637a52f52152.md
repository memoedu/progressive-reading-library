# 为什么 Impractical Knowledge 更 Efficient

---

## 正文

写作二十年后，我已经能感受到读者想要什么。我并非总能满足他们，但至少，我能强烈地直觉到，自己写的一篇文章究竟会广受欢迎，还是主要只有少数忠实读者阅读。

依我的经验，造成这种区别的最大因素，是文章的 practicality。由于我的写作并非纯粹为了娱乐，如果它没有明显的 usefulness，读者群就会小得多。

我理解这一点。大家都很忙。你今天来到这里，是想寻找可能改变生活的 ideas 和 advice。为什么要花大量时间 learning 一些甚至并不 practical 的东西呢？

我认为这是个错误。

如果你在意 usefulness，那么把大部分时间用于 learning “advice”或“tips”是 counterproductive 的。相反，如果你把大部分时间用于 learning fundamental principles，效果会更好。[1](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2026/09/21/you-should-care-less-about-practical-advice/#easy-footnote-bottom-1-18674 "尽管这篇文章本身可能因此不那么受欢迎（也就是说，它讨论的是一个更深层的 idea，而不是一条“tip”），我仍希望会有一些读者喜欢它。")

## 为什么 Impractical Knowledge 更 Efficient

想象一下，knowledge 就像一棵树：

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Tree-practical-advice.jpg)

树的根基处是 principles。它们最不 contingent on 世界的 specific states，也最 generally true。这一 category 包括 _F = ma_，以及 momentum 或 energy 的 conservation 等内容。

这些 principles 是 general 的，但仅凭它们本身，往往还不足以应对许多你关心的情况。要 design 一座好的 bridge 或 rocket ship，你需要知道的远不止 _F = ma_。沿着树干向上，我们会来到分叉处。它们 represent 世界的 state 中的 splits。

例如，heavier objects 和 lighter objects 确实会以 same speed 进行 fall。然而，在某些 domains 中，air resistance 实际上是 irrelevant 的（例如 lead ball vs. solid gold ball）；而在另一些 domains 中，它会成为一个 noticeable factor（例如 feathers vs. rocks）。

随着你对自己所处的 specific、concrete situation 了解得越来越多，你会沿着越来越多的分叉前进，直到最终来到树叶处。树叶就是 practical advice。对于那些对某个 domain 缺乏深入了解、只想知道自己应该 take 什么 action 的人，你可能会给出这类“tips”。比如：“把你的 propeller blades 做成恰好这样的 shape，以 minimize air resistance。”

树叶远比树干多。而每片树叶只 covers 世界的 possible states 中一个极其 narrow 的 subset，因此，要合理地“cover”space of possibilities 的很大一部分，你需要针对大量不同的 cases，learning 大量不同的 advice“树叶”。

因此，如果你想 maximize 自己 useful knowledge 的 breadth，也就是它 applies to 的 concrete situations 的 number，那么最好在 knowledge 之树更靠下的位置 learning，把注意力放在那些不那么 dependent on specifics 的 ideas 和 knowledge 上。

## 几个 Examples

让我们用几个 examples 来试试这个 analogy。

**考虑一下 getting in shape 这个 goal。**

在这里，树叶就是你决定遵循的某个 workout plan。沿着树枝往下，则是 progressive overload 和 recovery 这样的 principles。再往树干处，则是更 basic 的 ideas，例如 energy metabolism、muscle 和 cardiac physiology，等等。

Following 某个 specific workout plan 没有问题——只要你的情况与这个 plan 在 developed 时针对的情况足够接近。但如果这个 workout plan 是为二十多岁的 serious athlete 制定的，而你已经 55 岁，正在从 injury 中 recovering 呢？那么，这个 specific plan 很可能不适合你，甚至可能是 counterproductive 的。

相比之下，关于更 basic principles 的 knowledge 仍然能够 apply，还可能引导你在 knowledge 之树的另一根树枝上，找到更 applicable 的 advice。

**或者，考虑一下 starting a business。**

这里的树叶是具体的 tactics，例如“在 Facebook 上 buy ads”或“对 mid-sized firms 的 purchasing managers 进行 cold call”。同样，如果你所处的 domain 正好适用这些 advice，也许给你 advice 的人经营着非常相似的 business，那么这可能正是你需要听到的。

但如果并非如此，这些 tactics 可能就是 counterproductive 的。相反，你应该把注意力放在更接近树干的 ideas 上。沿着树再往下一点，是 entrepreneurship 的 basic ideas，例如“product-market fit”、“minimum viable product”和“market research”。而靠近根基处，则是“supply and demand”这样的 fundamental concepts。

**或者，考虑一下我写得最多的 topic：effective learning。**

如果你正在为 exam 而 studying，那么关于如何 pass 你那场 exam 的 advice 就是树叶。沿着树枝往下，则是一些更 general 的 ideas，涉及 tackling multiple-choice questions 或 organizing 你的 studying materials。最后，在树干处，你会接触到关于 learning 的 bedrock ideas，例如 spacing effect 或 retrieval practice。

后面这些 principles 最为 universal，尽管它们对于你应该如何应对任何 particular situation，仍会留下 ambiguity。

## 回应一些 Objections

对于把注意力放在 principles 而非 practicalities 上，存在几个常见的 objections：

**首先，有一种 objection 认为，principles 不足以 constrain decision-making。**这是真的。Learning 一个深层的 principle，很少能为你提供在现实情况中 decide 最佳 course of action 所需的一切。通常，还有太多其他 considerations 在起作用。

但是，learning principles 仍然更 efficient。只不过，要让你得到 correct answer，还需要用更 specific 的 knowledge 来 supplemented 这些 principles。

继续沿用树的 analogy，想象你正在树上寻找一只小鸟。如果我告诉你“它在从右边数第三根树枝上”，这并没有告诉你它的确切位置，但确实给了你一个值得寻找的地方。相比之下，只知道它是否在某片树叶上，只有当它确实在那里时才有帮助——大多数情况下，这条信息无法很有效地指引你的寻找。Specific advice 只有在 applies to 你所处的 situation 时才有帮助。

**其次，有一种 objection 认为，这些 principles 实际上并不 describe 世界。**说到底，就是某位 theoretician 提出了一个听起来很优美的 principle，却无法直接转化为 useful advice。

这是一个问题，但更常见的情况是，这些 principles 仍大多是 undiscovered 的，或者还不足以 constrain 这个 problem。回到树的 analogy，这就像我们能看到一堆树叶，却不知道树冠下的所有树枝都在哪里。我们可以猜出其中一些，但这些猜测可能是错的。

在我们已有的范围内，我们的 knowledge 是 correct 的，但它可能还没有达到我们所需的范围。在 principles 能 strongly determine practical advice 的 domains 中，例如 physics 和 engineering，learning fundamental principles 有充分的 rationale。在 principles 只能 weakly determine practical advice 的 domains 中，例如 entrepreneurship 或 second-language learning，principles 仍然值得 learning，但必须用大量 situation-specific advice 来 supplemented。

这些都没有 negates 这样一个 idea：learning principles 更 efficient。只不过，有时我们不知道 principles 是什么，因此只能 generated practical advice。

**最后，还有一种可能：从 fundamental principles 出发 figuring things out 是 intractable 的，因此我们还是不得不依照 practical advice 行事。**

继续这个 analogy，你越靠近树干，就越需要付出更多 mental work，才能弄清分叉在哪里，以及树叶最终位于何处。

这也是一个合理的 critique，而这正是我们最初会有 hierarchical domains 的原因之一。但这仅仅意味着，对于非常 complicated 的 domains，我们偶尔需要从树上更高的位置获取更多 knowledge，才能让 principles 变得 useful。即便如此，由于 principles 的 span 如此 broad，它们对于 learning 而言，往往仍然更 efficient。

## 在现实生活中的 Applications

把这个 idea 进行 reflexively Applying，“learning principles 比 practical advice 更 efficient”这个 idea 本身就是一个更深层的 principle。那么，它有哪些 practical consequences 呢？

想想那些讨论 productivity、diet 或 personal finance 等主题的大量 practical advice 书籍。其中有多少只是 leaves？是那种声称能让你在十五天内 losing 十五 pounds 的新“fat-busting” diet plan？是你最喜欢的作者写书时采用的那些 specific habits？是那个让你在 45 岁之前 retiring 的 budget plan？

如果你只打算读一本书，也许一本 practical 的书会是最好的选择。毕竟，fundamental principles 并不能 sufficiently constrain advice，因此它们不会给你一些 concrete 的事情去做。不过，也许你恰好处于作者描述的那个完全相同的 situation，因此能从他们的 advice 中受益？

但如果你打算读不止一本书，就不要从 practical advice 的 leaves 开始。如果 knowledge tree 的 trunk 大体上属于某个 academic field，比如 nutrition 或 psychology，就读一本 textbook。如果这棵树的 trunk 属于某种 professional practice 的范围，就读一本旨在以 unbiased 的方式提供 basic information 的书；For Dummies 系列书籍和其他 general readers 就是很好的 examples。

更 generally 地说，把尽可能多地阅读各个 domains 的“trunks”作为自己的座右铭。这样，当你确实遇到一个需要关于 leaves 的 detailed knowledge 的 practical problem 时，你可能仍有一些工作要做，但你已经完成了一些最重要的 learning。

After twenty years of writing, I’ve developed a feel for what my audience wants. I don’t always deliver, but I have, at the very least, a strong intuition whether an essay I write is going to have broad appeal, or mostly be read by a handful of fans.

The biggest factor that drives this distinction, in my experience, is an essay’s practicality. Since my writing isn’t pure entertainment, it has a much narrower audience if it isn’t obviously useful.

I get it. Everyone is busy. You’re here today looking for ideas and advice that may change your life. Why spend a lot of time learning about something that isn’t even practical?

I think this is a mistake.

If you care about usefulness, spending most of your time learning “advice” or “tips” is counterproductive. ## Why Impractical Knowledge is More Efficient

Imagine knowledge is like a tree:

![](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Tree-practical-advice.jpg)

At the base of the tree are principles. These are the things that are least contingent on specific states of the world, and are most generally true. This category includes stuff like _F = ma_ or the conservation of momentum or energy.

These principles are general, but, by themselves, they’re often insufficient for a lot of situations you care about. You need to know a lot more than just _F = ma_ to design a good bridge or rocket ship. As we travel up the trunk, we get to branching points. These represent splits in the state of the world.

For example, it is true that heavier objects and lighter objects fall at the same speed. Yet there are domains where air resistance is practically irrelevant (e.g., lead ball vs. solid gold ball), and domains where this becomes a noticeable factor (e.g., feathers vs. rocks).

As you learn more about a specific, concrete situation you’re in, you branch more and more until you finally come to the leaves of the tree. The leaves of the tree are practical advice. They’re the kinds of “tips” you might give someone who doesn’t know much about a particular domain, deep down, and just wants to know what sorts of action they should take. This would be things like “make your propeller blades exactly this shape to minimize air resistance.”

There are far more leaves than there are trunks. And each leaf only covers an incredibly narrow subset of possible states of the world, so, to reasonably “cover” much of the space of possibilities, you need to learn tons and tons of different advice “leaves” for tons and tons of different cases.

Thus, if you want to maximize the breadth of your useful knowledge, meaning the number of concrete situations it applies to, you’re better off learning lower down the tree of knowledge, focusing on ideas and knowledge that are less dependent on specifics.

## Some Examples

Let’s try this analogy with a few examples.

**Consider a goal of getting in shape.**

Here, the leaves would be a particular workout plan you decide to follow. Further down the branches, would be principles like progressive overload and recovery. Towards the trunk are more basic ideas like energy metabolism, muscle and cardiac physiology, and so on.

Following a specific workout plan is fine—as long as your situation is close enough to the situation that plan was developed for. But what if the workout plan was made for a serious athlete in their twenties, and you’re 55 and recovering from an injury? Then, the specific plan is unlikely to work for you, and it may even be counterproductive.

In contrast, the knowledge of more basic principles would still apply and could steer you to more applicable advice on a different branch of the tree of knowledge.

**Or consider starting a business.**

The leaves here are particular tactics like “buy ads on Facebook” or “cold call purchasing managers at mid-sized firms.” Again, if you’re in a domain where this advice applies, perhaps you’re receiving it from someone in a very similar business, this may be what you need to hear.

But if you’re not, these tactics may be counterproductive. Instead, you should focus on ideas closer to the trunk. A little further down the tree are basic ideas of entrepreneurship like “product-market fit”, “minimum viable product” and “market research.” And towards the base are fundamental concepts like “supply and demand.”

**Or consider the topic I write most about: effective learning.**

If you’re studying for an exam, advice for how to pass your particular exam would be the leaves. Further down the branches are somewhat more general ideas for tackling multiple-choice questions or organizing your studying materials. Finally, at the trunk, you get to bedrock ideas about learning like the spacing effect or retrieval practice.

The latter principles are most universal, even if they leave ambiguity for how you should deal with any particular situation.

## Dealing with Some Objections

There are a few common objections to focusing on principles over practicalities:

**First, there’s the objection that principles aren’t enough to constrain decision-making.** This is true. Rarely does learning a deep principle give you all you need to decide the best course of action in a real-life situation. Usually there are too many other considerations at play.

But, learning principles is still more efficient. It’s just that principles need to be supplemented with more specific knowledge to get you to the correct answer.

To continue our arboreal analogy, imagine you’re trying to find a small bird in a tree. If I tell you “he’s on the third branch from the right”, it doesn’t tell you exactly where he’s located, but it does give you a good place to look. In contrast, only knowing whether or not he is on a particular leaf is only helpful if he is on that leaf—in most cases, that information doesn’t inform your search very effectively. Specific advice is only helpful if it applies to the situation you’re in.

**Second, there’s the objection that the principles don’t actually describe the world.** Essentially, a theoretician has come up with an elegant-sounding principle that doesn’t translate directly into useful advice.

This is an issue, but it’s more typically the case that the principles are still largely undiscovered or don’t constrain the problem enough. Going back to our tree analogy, this is like being able to see a bunch of leaves while we don’t know where of all the branches underneath the canopy are. We can guess at some, but those guesses may be wrong.

Our knowledge is correct, as far as it goes, but it may not go as far as we need it to. In domains where principles strongly determine practical advice, such as physics and engineering, there’s a strong rationale for learning the fundamental principles. In domains where principles only weakly determine the practical advice, such as entrepreneurship or second-language learning, principles are still worth learning, but they’ll have to be supplemented with a lot of situation-specific advice.

None of this negates the idea that learning principles is more efficient. It’s simply that sometimes we don’t know what the principles are, and so only practical advice can be generated.

**Finally, there’s the possibility that figuring things out from fundamental principles is intractable, so we again have to go by practical advice.**

Continuing our analogy, the closer to the trunk you go, the more mental work needs to be done to figure out where the branch points are and where the leaves end up.

This is also a fair critique, and it’s one reason we have hierarchical domains in the first place. But this simply means that, for really complicated domains, we occasionally need more knowledge from higher up the tree to make the principles useful. Even so, because the span of principles is so broad, they still tend to be more efficient for learning.

## Applications for Real Life

Applying this idea reflexively, the idea that “learning principles is more efficient than practical advice” is itself a deeper principle. So, what are the practical consequences?

Consider the mass of practical advice books on topics like productivity, diet or personal finance. How many of these are just leaves? The new “fat-busting” diet plan that claims it will have you losing fifteen pounds in fifteen days? The specific habits your favorite author uses to write books? The budget plan for retiring by 45?

If you were only going to read one book, perhaps one practical book would be best. After all, fundamental principles don’t sufficiently constrain advice, so they won’t give you something concrete to do. Whereas maybe you happen to be in the exact situation the author describes and thus can benefit from their advice?

But if you’re going to read more than one book, don’t start with the leaves of practical advice. If the trunk of the knowledge tree is largely within an academic field, such as nutrition or psychology, read a textbook. If the trunk of the tree is within the scope of some professional practice, read a book that aims to give the basic information in an unbiased way; For Dummies books and other general readers are good examples.

Even more generally, make it a personal maxim to try to read as much as possible of the “trunks” of various domains. Then, when you do have a practical problem that requires detailed knowledge of the leaves, you may still have some work to do, but you will have already completed some of the most important learning.
