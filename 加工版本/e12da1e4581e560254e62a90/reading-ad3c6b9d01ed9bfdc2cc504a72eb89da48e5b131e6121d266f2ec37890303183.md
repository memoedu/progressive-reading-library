### 没有 biased coins

Andrew Gelman [狠狠批驳了](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/published/diceRev2.pdf)各地 statisticians 最喜爱的那个 tool：biased coin。以下摘自 abstract：

> Dice 可以是 loaded——也就是说，人们可以轻易地 alter 一个 die，使 landing on the six sides 的 probabilities 呈现 dramatically unequal。然而，不可能对 coin flip 进行 bias——也就是说，例如，人们不能通过 weight 一个 coin，使它在以通常方式 flipped 并用手接住时，land “heads” 比 land “tails” substantially more likely。

可惜没人告诉过 [Professor Mitzenmacher](http://mybiasedcoin.blogspot.com/)。

### Syriza 与 1871-73 年的 French indemnity

Michael Pettis [做了一番精彩的 economic history 研究](http://blog.mpettis.com/2015/02/syriza-and-the-french-indemnity-of-1871-73/)，将当今的 Eurozone crisis 与 Franco-Prussian War 后从 France 流向 Germany 的 massive capital outflow 作了 comparing（其形式是一笔 massive reparations bill，相当于任一国家 GDP 的 20%）。令人意外的是，这对 Germany 的 destabilized 程度远远超过了 France。Pettis 认为，当今 Eurozone 内的各种 distortionary mechanisms 已经产生了 similar effects，并指出，这场 conflict 实际上不应该围绕 national moralizing（“挥霍无度的 Greeks vs. 勤劳的 Germans”），而应该围绕由谁来 absorbs 一场（基本上 inevitable 的）debt restructuring 的 costs：

> 现代历史上的大多数 currency and sovereign debt crises，最终 represent 的都是一场 conflict：costs 应当如何在两个不同的 groups 之间 assigned。一方是 creditors、real estate 和其他 assets 的 owners，以及从现有 currency distortions 中 benefit 的 businesses。另一方则是以 low wages 和 unemployment 的形式 pay 的 workers，以及最终以 income 或 savings 的 value 逐渐 erosion 的形式 pay 的 middle class household savers 和 taxpayers。历史上，在 currency and sovereign debt crises 期间，political parties 逐渐开始 represent 这些 groups 中的一方或另一方，而且无论它们属于 left 还是 right，都能够赢得这些 groups 的 allegiance。

Pettis 很擅长用清晰而信息密集的解释，呈现关于 macroeconomics 的有趣见解。我也推荐他博客上的其他文章。

## 原文

### No biased coins

Andrew Gelman [eviscerates](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/published/diceRev2.pdf) that favorite tool of statisticians everywhere, the biased coin. From the abstract:

> Dice can be loaded—that is, one can easily alter a die so that the probabilities of landing on the six sides are dramatically unequal. However, it is not possible to bias a coin flip—that is, one cannot, for example, weight a coin so that it is substantially more likely to land “heads” than “tails” when flipped and caught in the hand in the usual manner.

Too bad nobody told [Professor Mitzenmacher](http://mybiasedcoin.blogspot.com/).

### Syriza and the French indemnity of 1871-73

Michael Pettis [does some neat economic history](http://blog.mpettis.com/2015/02/syriza-and-the-french-indemnity-of-1871-73/), comparing today’s Eurozone crisis to a massive capital outflow from France to Germany after the Franco-Prussian War (in the form of a massive reparations bill, 20% of either country’s GDP). Surprisingly, this destabilized Germany much more than France. Pettis argues that various distortionary mechanisms within the Eurozone today have had similar effects, and points out that the conflict should not really be about national moralizing (“wasteful Greeks vs. industrious Germans”) but about who absorbs the costs of a (basically inevitable) debt restructuring:

> Most currency and sovereign debt crises in modern history ultimately represent a conflict over how the costs are to be assigned among two different groups. On the one hand are creditors, owners of real estate and other assets, and the businesses who benefit from the existing currency distortions. One the other hand are workers who pay in the form of low wages and unemployment and, eventually, middle class household savers and taxpayers who pay in the form of a gradual erosion of their income or of the value of their savings. Historically during currency and sovereign debt crises political parties have come to represent one or the other of these groups, and whether they are of the left or the right, they are able to capture the allegiance of these groups.

Pettis is great at lucid yet dense explanations of interesting takes on macroeconomics. I recommend the rest of his blog as well.

### Effective Altruism Books

The [Effective Altruism Handbook](http://www.careyryan.com/files/EA_Handbook.pdf), a project of Ryan Carey, compiles a bunch of foundational EA essays into one convenient package. In terms of intellectual bang for the buck, this may be the densest compilation I know of. I’m hoping they’ll put together a bulk order of nice printed copies sometime soon.

Peter Singer’s new book, [The Most Good You Can Do](http://smile.amazon.com/Most-Good-You-Can-Effective/dp/0300180276/), is out! Not much new for fans of effective altruism, but it’s very clearly written, concise, and hits all the major points. (Ironically, Amazon’s recommender system told me to buy David Brooks’s new book after I looked at it. Eww.)

Finally, Nick Cooney has written [How to be Great at Doing Good](http://smile.amazon.com/How-To-Great-Doing-Good/dp/1119041716), which appears to be much the same material written from the perspective of people who already buy into the “altruism” part of effective altruism. I haven’t read it yet, but I’m looking forward to it!
