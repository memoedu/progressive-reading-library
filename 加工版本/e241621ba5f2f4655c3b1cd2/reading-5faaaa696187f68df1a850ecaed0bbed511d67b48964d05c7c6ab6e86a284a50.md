# Post 38: On Slack - Having room to be excited — Neel Nanda

---

## 正文

Slack（余裕）

几个月前，我遇到了一个叫作 **Slack** 的 concept，并且越来越觉得，这是理解自己生活的一个有价值的 lens。Slack 大致就是在我的生活中拥有 **spare capacity**。不被逼到自己的 limits。有 downtime，也有 explore 的空间。面对 small risks 时能够保持 robust。不至于一直感到 stressed 和 anxious，心里总有事情压着。在这篇文章中，我打算探讨 Slack 这个 concept，为什么世界的 default state 是 _lack_ slack，为什么这很重要，以及对此可以做些什么。

我最初是在 Zvi 的这篇 [文章](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/09/30/slack/) 中遇到这个 concept 的，文章写得相当 abstractly。而且这很有道理！我一直很难 precisely articulate 这个 concept 究竟 _是_ 什么。所以，为了看看这是否能引起共鸣，下面是一些拥有 Slack 时的 examples：

-   如果突然出现一项 urgent task，你可以 reprioritise，推迟当前的工作，先处理它

-   如果你累了，而且整个上午什么也没做成，这也没关系。你可以提前结束工作，休息很长一段时间，干脆把这一天放过去，明天再回来继续

-   如果你遇到一件 _很棒_ 的事，可以把手头正在做的事放一放，花几个小时深入其中，看看会有什么收获

-   如果你有一笔 unexpected expense——弄坏了昂贵的东西、生病了，等等——这不是什么大事，你的 savings 能够 absorb 它

我认为，Slack 是拥有值得一过的生活的一个 _极其_ 重要的 component！拥有 Slack 会给你 **freedom**。你有空间去 explore、be spontaneous 和 try new things。你有空间去 zoom out、reflect on your life、[notice problems 并 fix 它们](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-7-problems-are-for-fixing)。你可以投入 time 和 energy，去 break out of 你陷在其中的任何 [bad local optima](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-22-the-8020-rule)。你可以 _relax_——生活中总有 risks 和 setbacks，但你知道，当它们出现时，你能够应对。你可以摆脱持续的 short-term anxiety。

我反复强调这一点，是因为 **世界的 default state 是你的生活 lacks Slack**。这样表述时——zoomed out，从 abstract 的层面来看——Slack 的重要性显而易见。但 Slack 被 defined 为 **某种你可以 spend 的东西**。愿意在适当的时候 _use_ 你的 Slack，同时也意识到它是一种值得珍视的 quantity——值得珍惜、prioritise 和守护的东西——这两者之间存在着微妙的 balance。

而且，有些时候，拥有 Slack 确实格外困难！可能是因为生活处境，比如 illness 或 money problems。也可能出于选择，例如为了 long-term reward，选择一份 intensive 的工作或攻读一个 intensive 的学位。但我认为，也有许多时候，Slack 被毫无必要地 spent 了。你 _总是_ 把 spare capacity allocate 给下一个 priority，总想让自己的生活在当下达到 optimal。却没有注意到自己正在制造的 bottlenecks，以及自己正在放弃什么。这篇文章就是想帮助你注意到自己 _waste_ Slack 的方式，更清楚地看见自己正在放弃什么，并看看这是否真的值得。

Units of Exchange（交换单位）

稍微离开 Slack 说一点相关的内容：我觉得另一个有用的 lens，是 **units of exchange** 这个想法。我们经常从 quantifiable resources 的角度来思考自己的生活，例如 time 和 money。这样做很有价值，因为我们经常通过拿其中一种换取另一种，在这两种 resources 之间进行 trade-off，而 tracking resource costs 可以告诉我们，这是不是一笔划算的 trade！例如，工作往往只是一种用 time 和 labour trade 取 money 的方式。反过来，我们也经常花 money 来 save time。例如，我用 Squarespace 制作了这个 website，为此支付的 money 比使用 free alternative 多出不少，但因为各种东西都能正常 working，我 save 了 _大量_ time。

units of exchange 这个想法，是要引入更多 **abstract** resources。也就是那些更难 quantify、却很重要的东西，例如 energy、willpower、sleep、focused time、attention、health 等。我们在生活中做的每一件事都是一种 trade-off，但我们往往也在 trading 这些更 abstract 的 resources，而思考它们可以让我们看清，_实际的_ trade off 究竟是什么。Examples：

-   如果已经是深夜，我会付钱坐 Uber 回家，而不是花时间走路，但白天不会——白天我是在用 money trading **time**，夜里则是在用 money trading **sleep**

-   比起开车，我更愿意坐火车上班，即使耗时更长，因为我可以利用火车上的时间阅读或完成工作——真正的 trade 所换取的不是 **time**，而是 **productive time**

-   工作时，我会 turn off 所有 notifications——现在回复和稍后回复所需要的 **time** 一样多，但真正的 cost 落在我的 **attention** 和 **focus** 上

这种 mindset 起初听起来可能显而易见，但在尝试 diagnose 一个 problem 时，它往往很有启发。例如，当我感到 overwhelmed 和 stressed 时，正确的 solution 很大程度上取决于我缺少哪一种 resource！也许我只是没有足够的 **time**，可以通过退出某件事，或减少社交来 resolve 这个问题。也许我没有足够的 **sleep**，所以在已有的 time 里极其 unproductive，而我可以通过更严格地遵守就寝时间、不熬夜来 resolve 这个问题。也许我太 **stressed** 了，真正需要的是 focus on 让我 stressing 的 root cause，从而能够更多地 relax。

Units of exchange 可能很难思考，因为它们很难 quantify——我们不能像处理 money 和 time 那样，直接 calculate exchange rates，它们太 abstract 了。但这往往并不 decision relevant。重要的往往是 identifying **bottlenecks** \- 也就是那些我 spend 得太多、需要时却不够用的 resource(s)。而 _这_ 正是 units of exchange 能帮助我们更好地理解 Slack 的地方。**你的 Slack 会被你最紧的 bottleneck 所 constrained**。要真正拥有 Slack，你需要在 _所有_ resources 上都有 spare capacity——有足够的 energy 和 rest 去追求自己的兴趣，有足够的 time 可以自由支配，有足够的 willpower 和 attention 来保持 focused，有足够的 money，能够 relax 并尝试一些事。

因此，如果你 _find_ 了一个 bottleneck，**fixing 它就应该是你的 priority**。这很可能是你的生活和 Slack 所面对的最大的 constraints 之一。注意你在生活中把它 spend 在了哪里，并问问自己，是否满意这种 allocation。问问自己，哪里可以 cut back。如果是 money，就看看自己的 budget。如果是 time，就想想自己最大的 commitments 和 time sinks，想想自己最不看重什么。如果是 sleep，就想想自己的 sleep routine。如果是 willpower，就想想生活中的 systems。问题不在于你是否承受得起放弃某件事。你 _已经_ 在放弃自己的 Slack 了。问题在于，为了 fix 它而不得不放弃的东西，与自己 _已经_ 在放弃的东西相比，是否更值得放弃。一切都是 trade-off

到了这里，我觉得 [think in systems](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower) 很有价值。注意到一个 bottleneck，然后决心 [Try Harder](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-6-stop-pressing-the-try-harder-button) 并 fix 它，这很容易。但你的 resources 是在日常生活中一点一点 spent 的。如果有一个大的 fix，那当然很棒！例如，一项你可以直接退出的不必要的 time commitment。但问题往往来自你采取的 **default actions**。例如，每次手机振动，都在一点点流失 attention。而唯一 sustainable 的 solution，就是 **change the default**。

**Exercise**：想想 _你的_ 生活中的 abstract resources。什么感觉像是一个 bottleneck？什么在阻碍你？你感觉缺少什么？一旦找到了一个，就想想你是怎样 _spend_ 它的。有没有哪里可以 cut back？你是否满意这种 allocation，以及它让你付出的 Slack？

Naive Optimisation（朴素优化）

另一种让我个人很有共鸣的无法拥有 Slack 的情况，源于 **perfectionism**，以及一种想要“optimise”自己生活的 neurotic desire——我把它叫作 naive optimisation**。**

我非常推崇 _true_ optimisation——在我看来，它就是 defining goals、identifying 最能 achieve 那些 goals 的 actions，并 shaping my life，让 default path 成为 _take_ 那些 actions。我觉得这很棒，也是过去几年我 personal growth 的一个重要 component。但正如我 [在这篇文章中](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-9-what-it-means-to-optimise) 谈到的，**naive optimisation** 存在一种我很容易陷入的 failure mode。我的脑海中有一个 neurotic 的声音，每时每刻都在用 guilt 和 stress，推动我去做当下看起来最好的 action。

而 naive optimisation 很糟糕，因为这个内在的声音 _充满_ 了 biases！它没有 account for [value of information，以及 trying new things——这些事 _可能_ 会有很棒的结果](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/33-upside-risk)。它总是需要掌握 control，并且害怕 vulnerability 和 risk。它是个 perfectionist，用 [unrealistically high standards](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/35-standards) 来要求我。忽略它需要 energy，而要真正把它关掉又非常困难。

但对本文而言，这一点之所以重要，是因为 **naive optimisation 会 burn away 你的 Slack**。Slack 是需要守护和保护的东西，用来应对 risks、把握 opportunities、回应 unknown unknowns。拥抱 Slack，本身就意味着放弃生活中某些方面的 control。Slack 总是很珍贵，却很少让人觉得 _necessary_。所以，我脑海中那个 neurotic 的声音总想把它放弃掉。要做一个 overachiever，再多选那门课，因为我 _显然_ 有足够的 time。要答应那项额外的 commitment。要在我有 time 可以 rest、没有把自己逼到 limits 时，感到难受和 guilty。

不用说，**这很糟糕**。这种 mindset 会严重妨碍我拥有精彩而 fulfilling 的生活。即使没有任何真正的 hard constraints，听从 naive optimiser，也可能 burn away 我全部的 Slack，而我甚至始终没有意识到自己放弃了什么。此外，[**这是一种 bias**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/31-overcoming-bias)**。** 你根本不能相信自己能够在当下妥善保护和珍惜自己的 Slack，你需要某种形式的 manual override。建立 systems 来保护你的 Slack。为各种事情给自己 Budget time，并且 _遵守_ 它。为自己 Schedule downtime，并让它显得 **sacrosanct**。

我觉得，这种 mindset 与进行 [meaningful rest](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/29-meaningful-rest) 所需的 mindset 很相似。休息从来不是 _necessary_ 的。而且，对于任何 _单次_ 休息来说，如果不休息，我也许能完成更多事情！到了该休息的时候，我也经常不会觉得自己 _需要_ rest。但如果在 policy level 上，我 systematically 没有足够的休息，我就会变得 stressed、疲倦、unfocused，完成的事情也会少得 _多_。所以，我需要克服这种 systematic bias，建立一个 manual override，并把休息设为 default action，即使感觉没有必要也要这样做。而且，如果我真的想 win，就不能只听从脑海中的 naive optimiser。在 preserving 我的 Slack 这件事上，也是如此。

Motivation（动机）

我生活中另一个对我极其重要的领域是 motivation。具体而言，是 **intrinsic** motivation 与 **extrinsic** motivation 之间的 divide——前者指做事是因为我 _想_ 做，因为我对此感到 excited，并且有 driven 的感觉；后者指做事是因为我不得不做，用 guilt 和 obligation 来 driving 自己。而我一直在努力的是[摆脱凡事都依赖 extrinsic motivation 这根拐杖](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/35-standards)，以及[培育一种充满 intrinsic motivation 的生活](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)。总体而言，我觉得 intrinsic motivation 有趣得多，也有用得多，但同时也要 fragile 得多，更容易 lost；而 extrinsic motivation 往往容易获得、随时可用，却很少能达到它本可以达到的 effective 程度。

而这在 Slack 的语境下很重要，因为当我的 Slack 是 _low_ 时，我就觉得自己需要 **try hard**。一切都显得 scarce，我无法承受 slack off，也不能充分休息。我需要 push myself，还需要 optimise。我需要拿出 guilt、obligation、unrealistic standards 这些标准的、现成的 tools。我需要处于 control 之中。我的生活被 naive optimiser 的声音所 driven，而我深深陷入 extrinsic motivation。并且，即使实际上我 _确实_ 有 Slack，只要 naive optimiser 处于 control 之中，我就仍然会被困在 guilt based motivation 这根拐杖上。

另一方面，_Intrinsic_ motivation 的特征是 play、curiosity 和 exploration。做某件事是因为我 _想_ 做，而不是因为我 _不得不_ 做。这往往可以带来多得多的 fulfilling 感，_并且_ productive 得多，但这里存在 inherent lack of control。我需要能够 zoom out，给自己留出 explore 的 _空间_。我不能让 inner optimiser 对我正在做的事的每一个细节都进行 control。**要有 intrinsic motivation，我必须先有 Slack**。

对我而言，这很好地凸显了 Slack 作为 **spare capacity** 的 notion，也就是 **break out of bad local optima** 的 ability。如果我在 pushing myself hard，我就是 stuck 在一个 local optima 中。stop、slack off 都让人觉得 _不对_。_即使_ 把这件事做好能给我留出空间，让我对自己正在做的事感到 excited，并在 long-run 做成多得多的事，我也必须先有足够的 Slack，才能 _stop_ 并 reconsider。

这目前是一个对我 _极其_ salient 的 problem，因为我接下来的 3 个月都要用来尝试 academic research。而 research 的特征是 exploration、following curiosity、尝试不同的事并看看什么 works。但人又 _太_ 容易滑进 guilt 和 obligation 的 trap，给自己设定 unrealistic standards，然后 push myself 去 meet 它们，却没有真正给自己留出享受正在做的事的空间。（如果有人能就如何在 research 中 address 这个 problem 提出 Suggestions，我会非常感激！）

**Exercise：** 你在生活中的哪些地方 use extrinsic motivation 和 guilt？你对此满意吗？你是否给了自己足够的 Slack，能够暂时退开一步，试试另一种 approach？

Whimsy（随兴而行）

我认为，支持 use intrinsic motivation 的 utilitarian argument _非常出色_，而且我确信它在 long-term 要 effective 得多。但这种 mindset 感觉像是在用 extrinsic motivation 来 _force_ 自己感受 intrinsic motivation，我认为这注定会 failure。在追求这样的重大改变时，我会试着想象一个我 _已经_ achieved 这一改变的未来，并让自己解释它是如何发生的。而我无法想象，仅仅去想 intrinsic motivation 有多么 _有用_，或者自己多么 _应该_ 更经常地感受到它，就能让这种改变发生。相反，我认为 intrinsic motivation 的 core skill 是 **pursue something for its own sake**。

有一种特定的 mindset，我有时会在确实感受到 intrinsic motivation 时进入，它让人觉得带有 flow state 的特征。在这种状态下，我不再 focused 于 long-term goals，而是活在当下。我在 introspecting，与心中 curiosity 和 excitement 的细微信号保持联系。我跟随这种 curiosity 和 drive，在当下 pursuing 任何看起来最 exciting 的事，并且热爱自己正在做的事。我把这种 state of mind 称为 **whimsy** \- 与自己的 emotions 和 whims 保持联系，与自己真正关心的东西保持联系，并跟随它们的指引。而我认为，擅长这种 mindset，是获得可靠的 intrinsic motivation source 的关键。

这种 mindset 对我来说并不是特别自然而然，但我感受到它的那些时候，往往是我记忆中最 productive _而且_ 更有趣的时光。而我认为这是一项 _trainable_ skill——我既可以练习进入这种 mindset，也可以[学习如何 shape 我的生活来 encourage 它](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about)。如果我能在 long-term 把它更好地融入生活，那么为 training 这项 skill 付出一些 short-term costs，是 _极其_ 值得的。我的理想生活不会是 100% 都在 pursuing whimsy，但它会是一个占比相当大的 long-term component。

而这并不是一项仅仅靠 trying harder 就能 trained 的 skill。我发现的主要 approach，是定期给自己留出空间，然后看看会发生什么。过去几个月里，我一直把周日下午称为我的 **Afternoons of Whimsy**，不在这段时间安排其他事情，并确保这个 routine 让我感到 **sacrosanct** \- 它很珍贵，我不愿轻易放弃。在这段时间里，我不允许自己去关心 long-term goals，也不允许自己去关心各种 obligations。这是专门留出来 introspect、弄清自己对做什么感到 _excited_，然后 pursue 它的时间。

正如我所说，这是我仍在努力摸索的事，并不是自然而然就会的。有时我很累，陷入 procrastination 的 spiral，做一连串 un-rejuvenating 的事，比如在 Reddit 上没完没了地刷。有时我没能把 long-term goals 排除在外，结果觉得自己有义务去做当前 highest priority 的事，并骗自己把它当成“有趣”的事。但有时我会有一个 whim，想做某件平时不会从中获得乐趣的 _有趣_ 的事。比如在雷雨中来一次说走就走的跑步，或者一边跟唱 Disney 歌曲一边收拾房间。有时我会有一个 whim，想单纯为了某件事本身，去做一些很酷而且 virtuous 的事——比如观看一个我觉得有趣的 online course 中的 lectures，随兴写一篇 blog post，试着 code up 某个很酷的东西，或者读一本 textbook，了解某个我想 understand more deeply 的东西。也许每两三个下午里，会有一个下午让我事后觉得自己做了某件非常 _棒_ 的事。但从另一个意义上说，这并不是重点。我不 _在乎_ 自己究竟做成了什么——这是我专门划出来的时间，在这段时间里，achieve 我的 long-term goals 的最佳方式，就是 _不去_ 在乎它们。因此，对这段时间过得如何感到 regret，就偏离了重点。我确信自己正在慢慢改善 whimsy 的 mindset，而 _这_ 才是 achieve 我的 long-term goals 的最佳方式

如果这个想法让你产生共鸣，这里有一些建议：

-   如果你对我所概述的 problem 已经有些认同，却仍怀疑是否真该为这个 solution 投入这么多时间，那就[seek upside risk](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/33-upside-risk)，试试看！如果效果很好，那就 _太棒了_，你可以继续做下去，而且它可能会对一个重大的 life bug 带来显著帮助。如果没有效果，你直接放弃就好，cost 不过是花了几个下午做有趣的事

-   这是一个为 pursuit 某个 long-term goal 而进行的 experiment，所以它是你想 long-term 做下去的事！围绕它 Build 一个 system，并让它显得 **sacrosanct**。我把自己的时间定在周日下午，既因为周日本来就很适合安排一段 rest，也因为这是一个 regular routine，能帮助我 protect 并珍惜它。

-   给自己留出足够长的时间！我发现，3 hours minimum 往往是必要的——我一开始可能觉得有点懒，procrastinate 一阵子，但之后便可能产生一个 whim，想做点很酷的事。

-   尽量不要提前 plan 得太用力——刚开始时，我发现自己会陷入一种 failure mode：早上就 planning 当天下午要做什么，等到了那个时候，又觉得有点乏味，但还是试图硬撑着做下去

    -   我目前的 approach 是先去散个步，清清脑子。然后写一份 wish list——设一个 [5 minute timer](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/post-28-on-creativity-the-joys-of-5-minute-timers)，接着把脑中冒出的任何自己可以做的事都写下来；一旦发现某件吸引我的事，就停笔，放手去做！如果没有什么看起来有趣，就去做点别的

        -   记住，goal 不是找到 _最好_ 的事——那是 naive optimiser 在说话。你要找到的是某件 _有趣_ 的事，而一旦某件事吸引了你，你就应该放手去做！

        -   我发现，把 tasks 写成“我可以读……？”“我可以看……？”很有帮助。即使我整个下午什么都没做，也 _没关系_；把这些写成 **wishes**，会把我的 anchor 设在什么都不做上——这样一来，我做成的任何事都是 upside！否则，我很容易进入这样的 mindset：写下一长串清单，又因为没能把一切都做完、错过了一些事而难过——相当于把自己的 anchor 设在做完 _所有事情_ 上。

-   要愿意放弃一些事——如果你当下并不享受正在做的事，就换一件别的事！

-   记住，什么都没做成也 _没关系_——不要给自己施加 pressure

-   至少对我而言，whimsy 的精神 _不完全_ 是想做什么就做什么。它关乎 noticing 并 **nurturing** excitement 的感觉。如果我觉得某件事吸引着自己，就把注意力放在它为什么 _棒_ 上。Encouraging 自己去 pursue 它，想的是 upside，而不是 risks 和 costs

总体而言，我发现这是生活中一个很棒的 routine。我认为 rest 是我生活中重要的 component，但我的所有 rest 都很容易变成单纯的 [recovery period](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ximou2kyQorm6MPjX/rest-days-vs-recovery-days)——也就是一天工作结束后的晚上，当我觉得 tired、drained，只想 switch off 并 recharge 时所做的事。我的 afternoons of whimsy 则安排在我有 _energy_ 的时候——在那时，我能 pursue 自己真正关心的事。这些事仍然有点像工作，也仍然需要一些 energy，但又足够 fulfilling，让我获得的 energy 比投入的更多。在那时，我能找到生活中那些有工作形态、却让我觉得 intrinsically 值得为其本身而做的事，并提醒自己，我其实确实在乎它们。

Conclusion（结论）

总而言之，Slack 是一种 _极其_ 重要的 resource！它是我整体 life happiness 的重要 component。如果你还没有明确地思考它，第一步就是意识到 Slack 有意义、很重要、值得珍惜，并留意你的生活是否 lacks 它。要 guard 并 protect 你确实拥有的 Slack，如果不够，就再腾出更多。

拥有足够 Slack 的一个 necessary condition，是不被 _bottlenecked_ \- 用 units of exchange 来思考你的生活，留意是否有什么让你觉得 missing 或 constraining。如果你发现一个 bottleneck，fixing 它就应该成为一个重要的 priority！你可以怎样 shift 生活中的 default，来更好地 preserve 那种 resource？

另一个 necessary condition 是 _relax_。要 resist naive optimiser 的召唤，resist 那种想从生活中 squeeze out every last drop of optimality 的冲动，并意识到，_真正的_ optimality 就是这种 fuzzy、imprecise 的 balance。要 _真正_ 做到 optimal，你就不能完全处于 control 之中，也不能 trying as hard as possible。

拥有足够的 Slack，并 cultivating 这项 skill，是获得 intrinsic motivation 和 drive 的一个 key component！如果你和我差不多，这并不会自然而然地发生，但它确实是一项非常重要、值得投入切实努力的 skill。我推荐的一种做法，是明确留出时间，_practice_ 拥有 intrinsic motivation 和 whimsy——do things for their own sake，放下外界的顾虑和 obligations，并跟随自己的 curiosity。

你对生活中 Slack 的 amount 满意吗？如果不满意，什么能让你更接近一个拥有更多 Slack 的世界？你又可以怎样朝那个方向[迈出第一步](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step)？

## 原文

Slack

A few months ago, I came across a concept called **Slack**, and I have found this an increasingly valuable lens for understanding my life. Slack is, roughly, having **spare capacity** in my life. Not being pushed to my limits. Having downtime and room to explore. Being robust to small risks. Not feeling constantly stressed and anxious, having things weighing on my mind. In this post, I’m going to explore the concept of Slack, why the default state of the world is to _lack_ slack, why this matters, and some thoughts on what to do about this.

I first came across the concept in this [post](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/09/30/slack/) by Zvi, and it’s written fairly abstractly. And for good reason! I’ve struggled to articulate precisely what the concept really _is_. So, to see whether this resonates, here are some examples of what having Slack looks like:

-   If an urgent task comes up, you can reprioritise, put off your current work, and take care of it

-   If you’re tired and have spent the morning getting nothing done, this is fine. You can stop working early, take a long break, write the day off entirely, and get back to things tomorrow

-   If you come across something _awesome_, you can put off what you’re currently doing to take a few hours to dive deeply into it, and see what comes out of it

-   If you have an unexpected expense - you break something expensive, fall ill, etc - it’s not a big deal, your savings can absorb it

I think Slack is an _incredibly_ important component of having a life worth living! Having Slack gives you **freedom**. You have room to explore, be spontaneous and try new things. You have room to zoom out, reflect on your life, [notice problems and fix them](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-7-problems-are-for-fixing). You can take the time and energy to break out of whatever [bad local optima](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-22-the-8020-rule) you’re stuck in. You can _relax_ - life always contains risks and setbacks, but you know that you’ll be able to deal with them when they arise. You can be free of constant short-term anxiety.

I’m labouring this point, because **the default state of the world is that your life lacks Slack**. It is obvious that Slack is important when phrased like this - zoomed out, in the abstract. But Slack is defined as **something you can spend**. There is a delicate balance between being willing to _use_ your Slack where appropriate, but also realising that it is a quantity worth treasuring - something to cherish, prioritise and guard.

And there are times when it is exceptionally hard to have Slack! Due to life circumstances, like illness or money problems. By choice, eg by choosing to do an intensive job or degree for long-term reward. But I think there are also many times where Slack is spent needlessly. Where you _always_ allocate your spare capacity away to the next priority, always trying to make your life optimal in the moment. Without noticing the bottlenecks you’re creating, and what you’re giving up. And this post is aimed at noticing ways you _waste_ your Slack, making it clearer what you’re giving up, and seeing if it’s really worth it.

Units of Exchange

Digressing somewhat from Slack, a related lens I find useful is the idea of **units of exchange**. We often think of our lives in terms of quantifiable resources, such as time and money. This is valuable, because we often trade-off between these two resources, by trading one for the other, and tracking resource costs can tell us whether it was a good trade! For example, often jobs are just a way to trade time and labour for money. Going the other way, we often pay money to save time. For example, I made this website with Squarespace and pay a fair bit more for that than a free alternative, but I save a _lot_ of time due to things just working.

The idea of units of exchange is to introduce more **abstract** resources. Harder to quantify but important things, such as energy, willpower, sleep, focused time, attention, health, etc. Everything we do in life is a trade-off, but often we’re trading these more abstract resources too, and thinking about them can make it clear what the _actual_ trade off is. Examples:

-   I’d pay for an Uber home rather than taking the time to walk if late at night, but not during the day - during the day I’m trading money for **time**, at night I’m trading money for **sleep**

-   I’d prefer to take a train to work than drive, even if it takes longer, because I can take the time on the train to read or get work done - the true trade isn’t for **time**, it’s for **productive time**

-   I turn off all notifications when I’m working - it takes the same amount of **time** to respond now vs later, but the real cost is to my **attention** and **focus**

This mindset can sound obvious at first, but it’s often insightful when trying to diagnose a problem. For example, when I feel overwhelmed and stressed, the right solution depends a lot on which resource I lack! Maybe I just don’t have enough **time**, and can resolve this by quitting something, or being less social. Maybe I don’t have enough **sleep**, so I’m being extremely unproductive in the time I do have, and I can resolve this with better discipline around bedtimes and not staying up. Maybe I’m too **stressed**, and I really need to focus on the root cause of what’s stressing me out so I can relax more.

Units of exchange can be hard to think about, because they’re difficult to quantify - we can’t just calculate exchange rates like we can with money and time, they’re too abstract. But often this isn’t decision relevant. Often the important thing is identifying **bottlenecks** \- the resource(s) that I spend too much of, and don’t have enough of when I need them. And _this_ is where units of exchange help us to understand Slack better. **Your Slack is constrained by your tightest bottleneck**. To really have Slack, you want to have spare capacity in _all_ of your resources - enough energy and rest to pursue your interests, enough time to spare, enough willpower and attention to keep focused, enough money to be able to relax and try things.

And, accordingly, if you _find_ a bottleneck, **fixing this should be your priority**. This is likely one of the biggest constraints on your life and your Slack. Notice where you spend it in your life, and ask whether you feel happy with this allocation. Ask yourself where you can cut back. If it’s money, look at your budget. If it’s time, think about your biggest commitments and time sinks, and think about what you least value. If it’s sleep, think about your sleep routine. If it’s willpower, think about the systems in your life. The question is not whether you can afford to give something up. You’re _already_ giving up your Slack. The question is whether what you’d have to give up to fix it is better than what you’re _already_ giving up. Everything is a trade-off

I find it valuable at this point to [think in systems](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-19-on-systems-living-a-life-of-zero-willpower). It’s easy to notice a bottleneck and resolve to [Try Harder](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-6-stop-pressing-the-try-harder-button) and fix it. But your resources are spent in day-to-day life, bit by bit. If there is one big fix, then that’s awesome! Eg, an unnecessary time commitment you can just quit. But often the problem is from the **default actions** you take. Eg, bleeding attention away every time your phone buzzes. And the only sustainable solution is to **change the default**.

**Exercise**: Think about the abstract resources in _your_ life. What feels like a bottleneck? What is holding you back? What feels missing? And once you’ve found one, think about how you _spend_ it. Is there anywhere you could cut back? Are you happy with this allocation, and the Slack it costs you?

Naive Optimisation

Another failure to have Slack that I personally resonate with a lot stems from **perfectionism**, and a neurotic desire to “optimise” my life - what I call naive optimisation**.**

I’m a very big fan of _true_ optimisation - which I consider to be defining goals, identifying the actions that best achieve those goals, and shaping my life so that the default path is to _take_ those actions. I think this is awesome, and a big component of my personal growth over the past few years. But as I talk about [in this post](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-9-what-it-means-to-optimise), there’s a failure mode of **naive optimisation** that I find it easy to fall into. Where there’s a neurotic voice in my head that, in every moment, uses guilt and stress to push me towards whatever action seems best in the moment.

And naive optimisation is terrible, because this inner voice is _full_ of biases! It doesn’t account for the [value of information and trying new things, that _could_ go awesomely](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/33-upside-risk). It always needs to be in control, and fears vulnerability and risk. It’s a perfectionist, and holds me to [unrealistically high standards](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/35-standards). It takes energy to ignore, and is very hard to ever properly turn off.

But, for the purposes of this article this is important because **naive optimisation will burn away your Slack**. Slack is something to be guarded and protected to help with risks, to take opportunities, to respond to unknown unknowns. To embrace Slack is to inherently give up some aspect of control in my life. Slack is always precious, but rarely feels _necessary_. And so the neurotic voice in my head always wants to give it up. To be an overachiever, and take that extra course, since I _obviously_ have time for it. To say yes to that extra commitment. To feel bad and guilty when I have time for rest, and am not pushing myself to my limits.

Needless to say, **this is terrible**. This mindset is a significant impediment to having an awesome and fulfilling life. Even without any true hard constraints, listening to the naive optimiser can burn away all of my Slack, without me ever realising what I’m giving up. Further, [**this is a bias**](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/31-overcoming-bias)**.** You fundamentally cannot trust yourself, in the moment, to properly protect and cherish your Slack, you need to have some form of manual override. Build systems to protect your Slack. Budget yourself time for things, and _keep_ to it. Schedule yourself downtime, and make it feel **sacrosanct**.

I find this mindset similar to the one needed to take [meaningful rest](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/29-meaningful-rest). It’s never _necessary_ to take a break. And, for any _single_ break, I might get more done if I don’t take it! And I often won’t feel like I _need_ rest when it’s time to take a break. But if, on a policy level, I systematically don’t take enough breaks, then I get stressed, tired, unfocused and get _way_ less done. So I need to overcome this systematic bias, create a manual override, and make the default action to take breaks even if it feels unnecessary. And that I can’t just listen to the naive optimiser in my head if I really want to win. Just so when it comes to preserving my Slack.

Motivation

Another area of my life that is extremely important to me is motivation. Specifically, the divide between **intrinsic** motivation - doing things because I _want_ to, because I feel excited about them and driven - and **extrinsic** motivation - doing things because I have to, driving myself with guilt and obligation. And an ongoing quest is [getting over the crutch of using extrinsic motivation for everything](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/35-standards), and [cultivating a life full of intrinsic motivation](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about). Generally, I find intrinsic motivation far more fun and far more useful, but also far more fragile and easily lost, while extrinsic motivation is often easy and available, but rarely as effective as it could be.

And this is important in the context of Slack, because when I have _low_ Slack, I feel like I need to **try hard**. Things feel scarce, I can’t afford to slack off, or take enough breaks. I need to push myself, and optimise. I need to pull out all the standard, available tools of guilt, obligation, unrealistic standards. I need to be in control. My life is driven by the voice of the naive optimiser, and I fall deep into extrinsic motivation. And, even if in reality, I _do_ have Slack, so long as the naive optimiser is in control, I’ll still be stuck on this crutch of guilt based motivation.

_Intrinsic_ motivation, on the other hand is characterised by play, curiosity and exploration. Doing something because I _want_ to, not because I _have_ to. This can often be far more fulfilling _and_ far more productive, but there is an inherent lack of control here. I need to be able to zoom out and give myself the _room_ to explore. I can’t let the inner optimiser control every last detail of what I’m doing. **To have intrinsic motivation, I must first have Slack**.

To me, this highlights well the notion of Slack as **spare capacity**, the ability to **break out of bad local optima**. If I’m pushing myself hard, I am stuck in a local optima. It feels _wrong_ to stop, to slack off. _Even though_ doing this well could give me the room to feel excited about what I’m doing, and get far more done in the long-run, I must first have enough Slack to _stop_ and reconsider.

This is an _extremely_ salient problem to me at the moment, because I’m spending the next 3 months trying out academic research. And research is characterised by exploration, following curiosity, trying things and seeing what works. But it’s _so_ easy to slip into the trap of guilt and obligation, to set myself unrealistic standards, and then push myself to meet them, without really giving myself the room to enjoy what I’m doing. (Suggestions on how to address this problem in research would be very highly appreciated!)

**Exercise:** Where do you use extrinsic motivation and guilt in your life? Are you happy with this? Have you given yourself the Slack to take a step away and try another approach?

Whimsy

I think there is an _excellent_ utilitarian argument for the use of intrinsic motivation, and I feel confident it’s far more effective in the long-term. But this mindset feels like using extrinsic motivation to _force_ myself to feel intrinsic motivation, which I think is doomed to failure. When aiming for an ambitious change like this, I try to imagine a future where I _have_ achieved the change, and ask myself to explain how this happened. And I cannot imagine that change happening by thinking about how _useful_ intrinsic motivation is, or how I really _ought_ to feel it more often. Instead, I think the core skill for intrinsic motivation is to **pursue something for its own sake**.

There is a particular mindset I sometimes slip into, at the times when I do feel intrinsic motivation, that feels characteristic of a flow state. Where I’m not focused on long-term goals, and am living in the moment. Where I’m introspecting, in touch with the subtle hints of curiosity and excitement in my mind. Where I’m following this sense of curiosity and drive, pursuing whatever seems most exciting in the moment, and loving what I’m currently doing. I call this state of mind **whimsy** \- being in touch with my emotions and my whims, in touch with what I really care, and following those where they lead. And I think that getting good at this mindset is key to a reliable source of intrinsic motivation.

This is not a mindset that comes to me particularly naturally, but the times when I have felt it have often been the most productive _and_ more fun times I can remember. And I think this is a _trainable_ skill - both one where I can practice slipping into the mindset, and where [I can learn how to shape my life to encourage it](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-11-live-a-life-you-feel-excited-about). And it’s a skill that is _extremely_ worth some short-term costs training, if I build it better into my life in the long-term. My ideal life wouldn’t be 100% pursuing whimsy, but it would be a fairly large long-term component.

And this isn’t a skill that can be trained by just trying harder. The main approach I’ve found is to give myself regular space for it, and see what happens. For the last few months, I’ve been calling my Sunday afternoons my **Afternoons of Whimsy**, keeping them clear, and ensuring this routine feels **sacrosanct** \- something precious that I am unwilling to give up easily. And in this time, I am not allowed to care about my long-term goals, or my various obligations. This is time carved out to introspect, find out what I feel _excited_ about doing, and pursue it.

As I said, this is something that I struggle with, and not something that comes naturally. Sometimes I’m tired and fall into a spiral of procrastination, and do a range of un-rejuvenating things like scrolling endlessly through Reddit. Sometimes I fail to keep out my long-term goals, and end up feeling obliged to do my current highest priority and trick myself into considering it “fun”. But sometimes I feel a whim to do something _fun_, that I wouldn’t normally take joy in. Like going for a spontaneous run through a thunderstorm, or cleaning up my room while singing along to Disney songs. Sometimes I feel a whim to do something cool and virtuous for its own sake - like watching lectures from an online course I’m finding interesting, writing a spontaneous blog post, trying to code up something cool, or reading a textbook on something I want to understand more deeply. Maybe one in two or three afternoons I do something really _awesome_ in hindsight. But in another sense, this isn’t the point. I don’t _care_ about exactly what I get done - this is time I carve out where the best way to achieve my long-term goals is to _not_ care about them. And so feeling regret about how it goes is missing the point. I feel confident that I’m slowly improving at the mindset of whimsy, and _that_ is the best way to achieve my long-term goals

If this idea resonates with you, some tips:

-   If you’re feeling somewhat sold on the problem I’ve outlined, but skeptical at actually dedicating so much time to this solution, [seek upside risk](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/33-upside-risk) and try it! If it works well, this is _awesome_, something you can keep doing, and could significant help with a major life bug. If not, you can just give up, at the cost of a few afternoons spent doing fun things

-   This is an experiment in pursuit of a long-term goal, so it is something you want to do long-term! Build a system around it, and make it feel **sacrosanct**. I make mine Sunday afternoons, both because Sunday is a natural time for a period of rest, and because this is a regular routine, that can help me protect and cherish it.

-   Leave yourself a long time! I find that 3 hours minimum is often necessary - I might feel a bit lazy at the start, procrastinate for a while, but then feel a whim to do something cool.

-   Try not to plan too hard in advance - early on I found myself in the failure mode of planning what to do that afternoon in the morning, and in the moment it felt a bit stale, but I tried to push through anyway

    -   My current approach is to go for a walk at the start and clear my head. Then to make a wish list - set a [5 minute timer](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/post-28-on-creativity-the-joys-of-5-minute-timers), then write down anything that pops into my head that I could do, and the moment I find something that grabs me, stop writing and run with it! And if nothing seems fun, go do something else

        -   Remember, the goal isn’t to find the _best_ thing - that’s the naive optimiser talking. You want to find something _fun,_ and the moment something grabs you you should run with it!

        -   I find it helpful to write tasks down as “I could read …?” “I could watch …?”. It is _fine_ if I spend this afternoon doing absolutely nothing, and writing these as **wishes** anchors me to doing nothing - now anything I achieve is an upside! I otherwise find it easy to slip into the mindset of writing down a long list and getting sad at what you’re missing out on by not doing everything - anchoring yourself to doing _everything._

-   Be willing to give up on things - if you aren’t enjoying what you’re doing in the moment, switch to something else!

-   Keep in mind that it’s _fine_ to get nothing done - don’t put pressure on yourself

-   The spirit of whimsy, at least for me, isn’t _quite_ doing whatever I want. It’s about noticing and **nurturing** the feeling of excitement. If I feel drawn to something, focusing on why its _awesome_. Encouraging myself to pursue it, thinking about the upside, not about the risks and costs

Overall I’ve found this a great routine to have in my life. I think rest is an important component of my life, but it’s easy for all of my rest to just be a [recovery period](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ximou2kyQorm6MPjX/rest-days-vs-recovery-days) - something I do in the evening after a day of work, when I feel tired and drained, and just want to switch off and recharge. My afternoons of whimsy are done when I have _energy_ - where I can pursue things I actually care about. Where things still feel somewhat like work, and still take some energy, but feel fulfilling enough that I get out more energy than I put in. Where I can find the work-shaped things in my life that feel intrinsically worth doing for their own sake, and remind myself that I actually care about them.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Slack is an _incredibly_ important resource! It’s a major component of my overall life happiness. If you aren’t explicitly thinking about it, the first step is just to realise that Slack is meaningful and important, and something to value, and to notice if your life lacks it. To guard and protect the Slack that you do have, and to carve out more if you don’t have enough.

A necessary condition for having enough Slack is not being _bottlenecked_ \- think of your life in terms of units of exchange, and notice if anything feels missing or constraining. And if you find a bottleneck, fixing that should be a major priority! How can you shift the default of your life to better preserve that resource?

A further necessary condition is to _relax_. To resist the call of the naive optimiser, to resist the urge to squeeze out every last drop of optimality in your life, and to realise that _true_ optimality is this fuzzy, imprecise balance. That to _truly_ be optimal, you can’t fully be in control, and can’t be trying as hard as possible.

Having enough Slack, and cultivating this skill is a key component of getting intrinsic motivation and drive! Which, if you’re anything like me, won’t come very naturally, but is a really important skill, and one worth putting meaningful effort into. And a way I recommend to pursue this, is to explicitly carve out time to _practice_ having intrinsic motivation and whimsy - to do things for their own sake, to let go of outside concerns and obligations, and to follow your own sense of curiosity.

Are you happy with the amount of Slack in your life? And if not, what could bring you closer to a world with more Slack? And how could you [take the first step](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-21-taking-the-first-step) in that direction?
