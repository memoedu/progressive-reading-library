# Trust as a bottleneck to growing teams quickly

---

## 正文

这篇文章改编自我为 [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) 撰写的一份内部 doc。

我最近注意到，teams 在 grow 的过程中要保持 effective，一个常见的重大 blocker 是 _trust_。

“Alice 不 trust Bob”听起来好像 Alice 是坏人，但在某些方面，人们彼此不 trust 往往完全是合理的：

-   一个人可能有明确的理由，认为另一个人不擅长某件事。例如，最近我并不完全 trust 我的两位 managers 去 set 他们 teams 的 roadmaps……因为他们大约一周前才入职，连 laptops 都刚刚弄到能用。（两个月后，他们做得非常好！）

-   一个人也可能只是没有 data。例如，我还没见过我的大多数 direct reports 去 deal with 一个 underperforming team member，而这对许多 managers 来说是常见的 blind spot，因此我不应该 assume，他们在没有 support 的情况下也能在这方面 reliably 做到 effective。

一般来说，如果 Alice 是 Bob 的 manager，而且在比如 prioritizing research directions 方面是 authority，那么 Bob 很可能正在积极尝试 build 一个好的 mental “Alice simulator”，这样他就能 prioritize autonomously，而不必总是 checking in。但他的 simulator 可能还不够好，或者 Alice 可能还没有 verified 它是否足够好。Trust 来自对 shared mental models 的 [common knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_knowledge_%28logic%29)，而 build 这些需要双方的 investment。

如果 low trust 有时是合理的，那么问题在哪里？问题在于，_trust 才能让 collaboration 得以 scale。_ 如果我有一位 colleague，我不 trust 他能够（比如）make 好的 software design decisions，我就不得不更加仔细地 review 他的 designs，并要求他事先 make 更周全的 plans。如果我有一位 report，我并不完全 trust 他能够 handle underperforming team members，我就不得不更 granularly 地 manage 他，深入细节，了解正在发生什么，形成我自己对于接下来应该如何处理的 views，并反复 checking 情况，确保它正朝正确的方向发展。这不仅让我的 work 大幅增加，也让我的 teammates 的 work 大幅增加，因为他们不得不花更多时间，以这种方式使自己的 work 变得“inspectable”。

这里的好处在工作紧张起来时最为明显。例如，Anthropic 最近经历了一段 crunch time，我们的一个 teams 在极大压力下，需要 quickly debug 一个非常棘手的 issue。我们能够以显著更高的效率处理这件事，是因为这个 team（包括从其他地方加入 debugging 工作的大多数人）对彼此的 competence 都有 high trust；在高峰时，我们大概有 ~25 人在处理相关 tasks，但我们基本上能够把他们 split 成 independent workstreams，每个人都 trusted 其他事情会有人完成。在类似情形下，面对 lower-mutual-trust 的 team，我见过局面陷入无休止的 FUD 和关于 technical direction 的争论，导致 forward progress 慢得多。

随着 stakeholders 的数量 increases，Trust 也变得更加重要。对我来说，closely supervise 一位正在 dealing with 一个 underperformer 的 report，完全是 manageable 的；但如果比如说，需要 5 位 senior managers 针对一个 product decision 做 deep dives，那就会 costly 得多，也会有更多 high-friction。有一个极端的例子：我曾见过一个 deadline 很紧的 engineering team，选择 build 一个他们认为没有必要的东西，因为取得 cut scope 所需的 sign-off，比直接完成这项 work 花的时间还长。从 organization 作为 information-processing entity 的角度来看，考虑到当时的人员和 relationships，这很可能是正确的 call；但这也确实表明，如果他们努力 build 足够的 trust，让这类 decision 的效率高到值得做，那么他们整体上可能会推进得快得多。

与人共事的时间越长，你们自然就会有更多相互了解的经历，并 build 更多 trust。因此，在大多数 teams 中，这类事情会随着时间推移自行化解。但如果你正在经历 hypergrowth，那么除非你在这方面非常主动，否则在任何一个时刻，你的同事中_大多数_都会存在某种 trust deficit。

我有时会注意到以下 Symptoms，它们可能意味着 trust deficits 正在累积：

-   需要被 escalated 的 decisions 太多
-   需要许多 stakeholders 进行 deep involvement 的 decisions 太多
-   人们对自己没有参与的 projects 是否 on track 存在大量 FUD
-   Leaders 频繁需要针对个别 topics 做“deep dives”
-   Leaders 需要把大部分时间花在“in the system”的工作上（针对具体 issues 进行 problem-solving），而不是“on the system”的工作上（unblocking 未来的 growth）
-   Hiring 更多人并不能让你（明显）不那么忙

注意到这些现象后，人们很容易以为 solution 就是让大家“再多 trust 彼此一些”。对于某些情况和某些性格的人来说，这确实是正确的建议。但很多时候，暂时不 trust 某个人是合理的！在这种情况下，更好的 tactic 是_更加主动地 building trust_。在一家规模大、fast-growing 的 company 中，你可能永远无法达到人人之间都有 full pairwise trust 的乌托邦理想——build 它需要太长时间。但 on the margin，多投入一些努力仍然大有帮助。

以下是我见过行之有效的一些方法，可以让你投入更多努力去 trusting 他人：

-   **广泛 Share 你最重要的 mental models。** 在 Anthropic，Dario 大约每两周会做一次“informal vision updates”（用一小时的 talks，介绍 company strategy 某些部分的重要 updates），我认为这就是这方面的 canonical example。Anthropic 几乎每个人都在尝试 build 一个内在的“Dario simulator”，以便在真正的 Dario 太忙时向它咨询（也就是 ~始终如此）。在 high level strategy 方面，这些 updates 出色地实现了这一点。

-   **投入时间。** 除了 one-way broadcasts，trust-building 也能从 one-on-one bidirectional communication 中获益良多，因为这样你可以获得 feedback，了解对方 building 正确的 models 做得如何。这也是我除了与自己的 team 沟通之外，还会安排许多与 peers 的 recurring 1:1s 的原因之一。Offsites 在这方面也很有帮助。

-   **试着把事情交给别人做。** 如果你不确定自己 team 中的某个人是否会擅长某件事，可以试着给他一个 trial task，并比默认情况下更密切地 monitoring 进展，以便尽早 catch issues。这是 invest 于你长期 delegate 事务的能力的一种好方法。

-   **Give feedback。** 人们很容易觉得某件事情“太小了”，不值得 give feedback，于是就放过去了，尤其是在总有太多事情要做的时候。但我从未后悔过宁可多 giving feedback，反而经常后悔自己决定“忍着”或者保持沉默。这里有一个实用建议：如果你对给某个人 negative feedback 感到焦虑，不妨想想，你是否已经给了他足够的 _positive feedback_——这能起到有益的 buffer 作用，避免人们把 negative feedback 理解为“你整体上做得不好”。

-   **Inspection forums**，也就是 leadership 通过 setting goals，并 tracking 相对于这些 goals 的 progress，来 monitors 许多 projects 的 status 的 recurring meetings。以上 tactics 大多是 1:1 或 one-to-all，但有时你希望与一个 small group 一起工作，而这就是一种 efficient 的方法。

要帮助其他人 trust 你：

-   **接受一开始别人对你的 trust 是 incomplete 的。** 比如，当有人试图比我认为必要的程度更密切地 monitor 我的 work 时，我最初的反应是产生 defensive 心态，要求他更多地 trust 我。设身处地为他们着想，并提醒自己，他们很可能还没有一个足够好的关于我的 model，因而还无法 trust 我，这需要付出努力。

-   **对 status 进行 Overcommunicate。** 这在两个方面有所帮助：第一，它让 stakeholders 更有 confidence，相信如果事情出了岔子，他们很快就会知道。第二，它给他们更多 data，帮助他们 build 一个关于你如何 operate 的 higher-fidelity model。

-   当事情进展不顺利时，**Proactively own up**。这可以说是 overcommunicating 的一个 special case，但尤其重要，必须做好：如果别人可以信赖你，相信你会在需要的时候寻求帮助，那么让你在他们对你的 trust 范围边缘的事情上“try you out”，对他们而言就会远没有那么 risky。

## 原文

This is an adaptation of an internal doc I wrote for [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/).

I’ve been noticing recently that often, a big blocker to teams staying effective as they grow is _trust_.

“Alice doesn’t trust Bob” makes Alice sound like the bad guy, but it’s often completely appropriate for people not to trust each other in some areas:

-   One might have an active reason to expect someone to be bad at something. For example, recently I didn’t fully trust two of my managers to set their teams’ roadmaps… because they’d joined about a week ago and had barely gotten their laptops working. (Two months later, they’re doing great!)

-   One might just not have data. For example, I haven’t seen most of my direct reports deal with an underperforming team member yet, and this is a common blind spot for many managers, so I shouldn’t assume that they will reliably be effective at this without support.

In general, if Alice is Bob’s manager and is an authority on, say, prioritizing research directions, Bob is probably actively trying to build a good mental “Alice simulator” so that he can prioritize autonomously without checking in all the time. But his simulator might not be good yet, or Alice might not have verified that it’s good enough. Trust comes from [common knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_knowledge_%28logic%29) of shared mental models, and that takes investment from both sides to build.

If low trust is sometimes appropriate, what’s the problem? It’s that _trust is what lets collaboration scale._ If I have a colleague I don’t trust to (say) make good software design decisions, I’ll have to review their designs much more carefully and ask them to make more thorough plans in advance. If I have a report that I don’t fully trust to handle underperforming team members, I’ll have to manage them more granularly, digging into the details to understand what’s going on and forming my own views about what should happen, and checking on the situation repeatedly to make sure it’s heading in the right direction. That’s a lot more work both for me, but also for my teammates who have to spend a bunch more time making their work “inspectable” in this way.

The benefits here are most obvious when work gets intense. For example, Anthropic had a recent crunch time during which one of our teams was under intense pressure to quickly debug a very tricky issue. We were able to work on this dramatically more efficiently because the team (including most of the folks who joined the debugging effort from elsewhere) had high trust in each other’s competence; at peak we had probably ~25 people working on related tasks, but we were mostly able to split them into independent workstreams where people just trusted the other stuff would get done. In similar situations with a lower-mutual-trust team, I’ve seen things collapse into endless FUD and arguments about technical direction, leading to much slower forward progress.

Trust also becomes more important as the number of stakeholders increases. It’s totally manageable for me to closely supervise a report dealing with an underperformer; it’s a lot more costly and high-friction if, say, 5 senior managers need to do deep dives on a product decision. In an extreme case, I once saw an engineering team with a tight deadline choose to build something they thought was unnecessary, because getting the sign-off to cut scope would have taken longer than doing the work. From the perspective of the organization as an information-processing entity, given the people and relationships that existed at the time, that might well have been the right call; but it does suggest that if they worked to build enough trust to make that kind of decision efficient enough to be worth it, they’d probably move much faster overall.

As you work with people for longer you’ll naturally have more experience with each other and build more trust. So on most teams, these kinds of things work themselves out over time. But if you’re going through hypergrowth, then unless you’re very proactive about this, any given time _most_ of your colleagues will have some sort of trust deficit.

Symptoms I sometimes notice that can indicate a buildup of trust deficits:

-   Too many decisions needing to be escalated
-   Too many decisions requiring deep involvement from many stakeholders
-   People having lots of FUD about whether projects they’re not involved in are on track
-   Leaders frequently needing to do “deep dives” on individual topics
-   Leaders needing to spending most of their time working “in the system” (problem-solving specific issues) rather than “on the system” (unblocking future growth)
-   Hiring more people doesn’t make you (much) less busy

It’s easy to notice these and think that the solution is for people to “just trust each other more.” There are some situations and personalities where that’s the right advice. But often it’s reasonable not to trust someone yet! In that case, a better tactic is to _be more proactive about building trust_. In a large, fast-growing company you’ll probably never get to the utopian ideal of full pairwise trust between everyone—it takes too long to build. But on the margin, more effort still helps a lot.

Some ways to invest more effort in trusting others that I’ve seen work well:

-   **Share your most important mental models broadly.** At Anthropic, Dario gives biweekly-ish “informal vision updates” (hour-long talks on important updates to parts of company strategy) that I think of as the canonical example of this. Just about everyone at Anthropic is trying to build an internal “Dario simulator” who they can consult when the real one is too busy (i.e. ~always). For high level strategy, these updates do an amazing job of that.

-   **Put in time.** In addition to one-way broadcasts, trust-building benefits a lot from one-on-one bidirectional communication so that you can get feedback on how well the other person is building the right models. This is one of the reasons I schedule lots of recurring 1:1s with peers in addition to my team. Offsites are also very helpful here.

-   **Try people out.** If you’re unsure whether someone on your team will be great at something, try giving them a trial task and monitoring how it’s going more closely than you would by default, to catch issues early. This is a great way to invest in your long-term ability to delegate things.

-   **Give feedback.** It’s easy to feel like something is “too minor” to give feedback on and let it slide, especially when there’s always too much to do. But I’ve never regretted erring on the side of giving feedback, and often regretted deciding to “deal with it” or keep quiet. One pro-tip here: if you feel anxious about giving someone negative feedback, consider whether you’ve given them enough _positive feedback_—which is a helpful buffer against people interpreting negative feedback as “you’re not doing well overall.”

-   **Inspection forums**, i.e., recurring meetings where leadership monitors the status of many projects by setting goals and tracking progress against them. The above tactics are mostly 1:1 or one-to-all, but sometimes you want to work with a small group and this is an efficient way of doing that.

To help other people trust you:

-   **Accept that you start out with incomplete trust.** When someone, say, tries to monitor my work more closely than I think is warranted, my initial reaction is to be defensive and ask them to trust me more. It takes effort to put myself into their shoes and remind myself that they probably don’t have a good enough model of me to trust me yet.

-   **Overcommunicate status.** This helps in two ways: first, it gives stakeholders more confidence that if something goes off the rails they’ll know quickly. And second, it gives them more data and helps them build a higher-fidelity model of how you operate.

-   **Proactively own up** when something isn’t going well. Arguably a special case of overcommunicating, but one that’s especially important to get right: if you can be relied on to ask for help when you need it, it’s a lot less risky for people to “try you out” on stuff at the edge of what they trust you on.
