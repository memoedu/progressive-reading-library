# Deliberate Practice 的两个问题

---

## 正文

很少有观点比 [Anders Ericsson 的](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Anders_Ericsson) deliberate practice 研究更深刻地塑造了我对 learning 的思考。他令人信服地论证了，practice 的 quantity 在很大程度上能够 explaining chess、music 和 athletics 等 fields 中的 performance differences；此外，某些 practice 的 types 对 learning 而言远比其他类型更 effective。

我最近一直在努力制作新的 video essays，因此决定做一期，谈谈那些为我的思考带来诸多启发的 research：

## Deliberate Practice 的两个问题

虽然 Ericsson 对我的工作影响深远，但近年来，他的一些观点也遭到了质疑。

**第一个问题在于，Ericsson 坚持认为 practice explains elite performers 之间全部（或大部分）的 difference，**在我看来，这种说法有些夸大。

有 research 表明，在各个 fields 中 achieve eminence 都需要相对漫长的时间；还有 evidence 表明，extensive knowledge bases 在 experts 身上发挥着作用。这些确实为 excellence 是 cultivated 而非 innate 的观点提供了支持。

即便如此，我仍认为 talent 和 intelligence 确实发挥着作用。Practice 也许是必需的，但谁能从 practice 中 benefits most，可能取决于 fixed traits，而这些 traits 所起的作用超出了 Ericsson 愿意承认的范围。

在我看来，这并不 undermine 那些更 basic 的 claims：大量的 practice 对 learning 很重要，而且某些 effortful/guided practice 可能远比单纯花几个小时 performing 这项 skill 更 effective。我相信这些 claims 有相当扎实的依据，尽管 Ericsson 关于 deliberate practice 能够在很大程度上 explain away talent 的 argument 似乎有些牵强。

**deliberate practice 的第二个问题更关乎实际操作。**在 chess、music 和 athletics 等 domains 中，objectives 是 clear 的，training 也很 straightforward。这并不意味着 deliberate practice 很容易，但确实使它相对 workable。

相比之下，对于我们真正希望擅长的大多数 skills，“improvement”究竟意味着什么，就远没有那么 clear。当每篇 essay 都涉及不同的 topic 时，你如何在 writing essays 方面 get better？当现实世界中 drive performance 的 skills 往往是 ill-defined 的，你又如何成为更好的 programmer、architect 或 entrepreneur？

多年来，这个问题一直占据着我的思考。十年前，Cal Newport 和我一起着手创建一门 course，试图回答这个问题。此后，[Top Performer](https://join.ingeniumcourses.com/topperformer/) 已有五千多名 students；与这么多人一起工作的经历，让我们对 real-world career skills 与 chess 和 music 的 precision 有何不同有了许多认识。

## 将 Deliberate Practice Applying 到你的 Career 的三条经验

下面总结一下，十年来与 students 围绕这个话题一起工作，我学到了什么：

### 1\. 知道要 learn _什么_，与知道如何 learn 它同样重要。

时间有限，而 practice 需要很长时间（即便你做得 efficiently）。因此，在现实生活中考虑你 learning 得如何时，最重要的问题通常是你应该 practicing 什么。在 career contexts 中尤其如此：skills 只是让你在工作中产生更大 impact 的 instruments，而不是单纯为了 learned 它们本身。

 任何 career-development project 的起点，都必须是 understanding career landscape：哪些 skills 才真正 valuable？applying 它们的 pathway 是什么？

### 2\. _Showing_ 你拥有一项 skill，可能与 learning 它同样棘手。

Ericsson 将他的 research 集中在具有 objective performance measures 的 domains，在这些 domains 中，真正的 experts 能够与仅仅拥有 high-status 的冒牌者清楚地 separated。对于 scientific study 而言，这一 restriction 很重要。如果 expertise 不是依据 unbiased standard，以 repeatable、superior performance 来 defined，那么这项 study 就从 cognitive development 转向了 sociology。

然而，real-world skills 往往缺乏 Ericsson 所要求的那种 objectivity。这又让我们重新面对 social factors 的重要性。确实存在 social factors trump raw ability 的情况——nepotism 和 networks 比 meritocratic ability 更重要。（如果你想进一步了解，可以看看我的 [Pedigree 的书评](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2022/05/10/pedigree-review/)。）

但我认为，更 typical 的情况是两者的 mixture。那些登上顶峰的人通常是 talented 的。但由于 skill 很难 assess，success 不仅是 skills 的 mixture，也包含能够 signal 一个人拥有那些 hard-to-assess skills 的 career assets。其中包括 credentials 等显而易见的东西，也包括一些不那么明显的东西，例如 successful projects 的 track record，或与能够为你担保的人建立的 good relationships。

因此，Deliberate practice 在 acquiring 一些 basic competencies 方面发挥着 critical role，但它需要与一种意识相配合：你要如何 demonstrate 那些 skills。我们并不会因此得出那种彻底的 sociological critique，认为 success “仅仅”取决于你认识谁，或者你是否曾代表合适的学校参加赛艇队；但这也并非人们在 chess 或 athletics 等 domains 中想象的、完全以 excellence 为基础的 meritocracy。

### 3\. Real-world skills 需要 real-world practice。

Ericsson 非常强调 performance 与 practice 之间的 distinction。Performance 是 doing a job。Practice 则是 getting better at 它。

 在 chess 和 music 等 areas 中，这种 distinction 很 clear。Chess grandmasters 当然会 play games，但他们的大部分 improvement 来自 study。仅仅 playing 大量 games 对 learning 而言是 inefficient 的，因为 playing 时的 aim 是 win，而不是 acquire knowledge。同样，musician 可能会在 recitals 中 play，但真正的 skill 并不是在舞台上 built 的；它是在 practice room 中，通过反复 playing 棘手的 sections，直到它们 flawless 而 built 的。

类似的道理也_必然_适用于 jobs，因为关于 deliberate practice 的 efficacy 的 cognitive explanations 同样 apply 于 professional skills。但 real-world skills 往往不可能在完全不与 real context 进行某种 integration 的情况下 practice。不 managing 一个 real project，却在 project management 上 get really good，这究竟意味着什么？

即便有些 skills 原则上可以与其 professional context 相 isolated（例如 programming 或 writing），它们往往仍需要一定程度的 real-world engagement，才能让 practice effective。参加 programming competitions 很可能确实有助于 real-world coding，但大多数 programming jobs 的重点在于处理 large、legacy codebases，以及 understanding 其他 team members 做过的事情。而这些又只能在 real context 中 practiced effectively。

这表明，在你的 field 中进行 practice 的正确方式，并不是让 practice 与 performance 相脱离，而是尝试 augment real-world performance，让它 functions 得更像 practice。选择能够 stretch 你的 abilities 的 projects，并 apply constraints，让更多 learning 得以发生。

最近，随着我和团队进一步涉足 video，这些话题经常萦绕在我的脑海中。我正在进行 deliberate practice，重点是提升自己以 audiovisual format convey ideas 的 ability。希望我也能分享一些在 deliberately practicing 这项 skill 时，对我有效的做法。

## 原文

Few ideas have shaped my thinking about learning more than [Anders Ericsson’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Anders_Ericsson) work on deliberate practice. He argued persuasively that quantity of practice goes a long way in explaining performance differences in fields like chess, music and athletics and, further, that certain types of practice are far more effective than others for learning.

I’ve been working hard on making new video essays, so I decided to make one about some of the research that has inspired so much of my thinking:

## The Two Problems with Deliberate Practice

While Ericsson’s influence on my work has been deep, some of his ideas have faced pushback in recent years.

**The first problem is Ericsson’s insistence that practice explains all (or most) of the difference between elite performers,** which seems to me to be overstated.

The research showing the relatively long periods of time required to achieve eminence in various fields along with the evidence for the role of extensive knowledge bases among experts do lend credence to the idea that excellence is cultivated, not innate.

Even so, I expect that talent and intelligence do play a role. Practice may be required, but who benefits most from practice may depend on fixed traits that go beyond what Ericsson was willing to admit.

To me, this doesn’t undermine the more basic claims that a large quantity of practice is important for learning and that certain kinds of effortful/guided practice may be much more effective than simply spending hours performing the skill. I believe these claims are on fairly solid ground, even if Ericsson’s argument that deliberate practice could largely explain away talent seems to be a stretch.

**The second problem with deliberate practice is more practical.** In domains like chess, music and athletics, the objectives are clear, and training is straightforward. This doesn’t make deliberate practice easy, but it does make it relatively workable.

In contrast, what “improvement” means for most of the skills we actually want to get good at is much less clear. How do you get better at writing essays when every essay is on a different topic? How do you become a better programmer, architect or entrepreneur when the skills that drive performance in the real world are often ill-defined?

This question has dominated my thinking for years. Ten years ago, Cal Newport and I worked to create a course to answer this question. [Top Performer](https://join.ingeniumcourses.com/topperformer/) has since had over five thousand students, and working with so many people has taught us a lot about how real-world career skills differ from the precision of chess and music.

## Three Lessons for Applying Deliberate Practice to Your Career

To summarize what I’ve learned from ten years of working with students on this topic:

### 1\. Knowing _what_ to learn is just as important as how to learn it.

Time is short, and practice takes a long time (even if you do it efficiently). Thus, the question of what you ought to be practicing dominates most real-life considerations about how well you’re learning it. This is particularly true in career contexts, where skills are merely instruments to make a greater impact in your work, not simply learned for their own sake.

 The starting point for any career-development project has to be understanding the career landscape: What sorts of skills are truly valuable? What’s the pathway for applying them?

### 2\. _Showing_ you have a skill can be just as tricky as learning it.

Ericsson focused his research on domains with objective performance measures, where true experts could be clearly separated from mere high-status pretenders. For a scientific study, this restriction is important. If expertise isn’t defined in terms of repeatable, superior performance according to an unbiased standard, then the study shifts from cognitive development to sociology.

However, real-world skills often lack that very objectivity Ericsson required. This, in turn, pushes us back into the importance of social factors. There are certainly cases where social factors trump raw ability—nepotism and networks mattering more than meritocratic ability. (See my [book review of Pedigree](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2022/05/10/pedigree-review/) if you want to learn more about that.)

But, I think a mixture of both is the more typical case. Those who rise to the top are, generally, talented. But, since skill is difficult to assess, success is a mixture of not just skills but career assets that signal a person has those hard-to-assess skills. This includes obvious things like credentials, but also less obvious things like a track record of successful projects or good relationships with other people who can vouch for you.

Deliberate practice thus plays a critical role in acquiring some of the basic competencies, but it needs to be paired with an awareness of how you demonstrate those skills. We don’t end up with the full-blown sociological critique that success is “just” who you know or whether you rowed crew for the right school, but it’s also not the complete meritocracy of excellence imagined in domains like chess or athletics.

### 3\. Real-world skills require real-world practice.

Ericsson made a big deal of the distinction between performance and practice. Performance was doing a job. Practice was getting better at it.

 In areas like chess and music, this distinction is clear. Chess grandmasters play games, of course, but the bulk of their improvement comes from study. Simply playing a lot of games is inefficient for learning since the aim when playing is to win, not to acquire knowledge. Similarly, a musician may play in recitals, but the real skill isn’t built on the stage; it’s built in the practice room, playing tricky sections over and over again until they are flawless.

Something like this _must_ be true of jobs as well, because the cognitive explanations for the efficacy of deliberate practice apply equally to professional skills. But real-world skills are often impossible to practice without some integration with a real context. What on earth would it mean to get really good at project management without managing a real project?

Even skills which can, in principle, be isolated from their professional context (like programming or writing) often require a degree of real-world engagement in order to make practice effective. Competing in programming competitions probably does help real-world coding, but most programming jobs are heavily weighted toward working with large, legacy codebases and understanding what other team members have done. That, in turn, can only be practiced effectively in a real context.

This suggests that the right way to practice in your field isn’t to divorce practice from performance, rather it’s to try to augment real-world performance so it functions more like practice. Choose projects that will stretch your abilities, and apply constraints so that more learning occurs.

These topics have been on my mind a lot lately as my team and I venture more into video. I’m working on deliberate practice focused on my ability to convey ideas in an audiovisual format. Hopefully I’ll be able to share some of what has been working for me in terms of practicing that skill deliberately as well.
