## The pale blue dot（暗淡蓝点）

The pale blue dot 是一张著名的 Earth photograph，由 _Voyager 1_ spacecraft 在距离 Earth 3.7 billion miles 时 taken：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/pale_blue_dot.png)

Carl Sagan 在同名著作「Pale Blue Dot」的一段著名文字中，对这张 photograph 作了思考：

> 从这个遥远的 vantage point 看，Earth 也许并不显得有什么特别值得关注的地方。
> 
> 但对我们而言，情况不同。再看看那个 dot。那就是这里。那就是家园。那就是我们。在它上面，你爱的每个人、你认识的每个人、你听说过的每个人、曾经存在过的每一个 human being，都度过了自己的一生。我们所有的欢乐与苦难，数以千计自信笃定的 religions、ideologies 和 economic doctrines，每个 hunter 和 forager，每个英雄和懦夫，每个 civilization 的 creator 和 destroyer，每个国王和农民，每对相爱的年轻恋人，每位母亲和父亲、满怀希望的孩子、inventor 和 explorer，每位 morals 的教师，每个腐败的 politician，每个“超级明星”，每位“最高领袖”，我们 species 的 history 中每个圣人和罪人，都曾在那里生活——在悬浮于一道 sunbeam 中的一粒 dust 上。
> 
> Earth 是浩瀚 cosmic arena 中一个极小的舞台。想想那些将军和帝王为了在荣耀与胜利中，短暂成为一个 dot 的一小部分的主人，所造成的血流成河。想想这个 pixel 某个角落的 inhabitants，对另一个角落几乎无法区别的 inhabitants 施加的无尽残酷；他们的误解多么频繁，他们多么急于彼此杀戮，他们的仇恨多么炽烈。
> 
> 我们的装腔作势，我们自以为是的重要性，我们以为自己在 Universe 中拥有某种 privileged position 的 delusion，都受到了这点苍白光芒的挑战。我们的 planet 是四周浩瀚 cosmic dark 中孤独的一粒微尘。在我们的默默无闻中，在这一切的广袤之中，没有任何迹象表明会有援助从别处到来，将我们从自己手中拯救出来。
> 
> Earth 是迄今唯一已知 harbor life 的 world。至少在不远的将来，我们的 species 无法 migrate 到任何其他地方。Visit，可以。Settle，还不行。无论喜欢与否，眼下 Earth 就是我们坚守的地方。
> 
> 有人说，astronomy 是一种使人谦逊、塑造品格的体验。也许没有什么比我们这个微小 world 的遥远 image，更能展现人类自负的愚蠢。对我而言，它凸显了我们的责任：更友善地对待彼此，preserve 并珍爱 pale blue dot——我们唯一熟悉的家园。

我觉得这段文字令人惊叹，尤其是由 Sagan 亲自朗读时：

Denis Diderot 在 theatrical actors 身上观察到一种 dual consciousness。要 act 得好，如果他们能够真正_成为_自己的 characters，尽可能深入地 experiencing 这些 characters 的 emotions 和 desires，会大有帮助。但与此同时，他们又以 actors 的身份，隔着一层 conscious 到自己。我不是 actor，不过有时也被请去承担一些 roles（host、emcee、usher 等），似乎也需要类似的 dual consciousness。

关于这种 dual consciousness 的三则见证：

(1) World War 1 movie _1917_ 中有极长的 long shots，有时长达 5-10 分钟。一位 lead actors 说，他有时在 long shots 中 immersed 得如此之深，以至于 shot 结束、意识到自己其实并不是 1917 年 trenches 中的一名 soldier 时，会感到震惊。

(2) comedian Rowan Atkinson 曾说，有时在 stage 上 performing 时，一场 performance 会开始“自行失控地向前奔跑……有时会出现一个非同寻常的时刻，它仿佛翻到了另一边，脱离你自行奔跑，而你实际上已经不再掌控这场 performance。”

(3) 有人问 Michael Caine 如何 remembered lines，他说，当一个 script 写得格外出色时，你会如此 immersed 于 scene 的 reality 中，以至于可以“从另一个 character 的脸上 read your lines”。

Sagan 在这段文字中，让读者产生了一种相关的 dual consciousness。我们反复在两种截然不同的 points of view 之间 switch：在其中一种里，他有着深切的 empathetic 和温暖的人情味：

> 那就是这里。那就是家园。那就是我们。在它上面，你爱的每个人、你认识的每个人、你听说过的每个人、曾经存在过的每一个 human being，都度过了自己的一生。我们所有的欢乐与苦难，数以千计自信笃定的 religions、ideologies 和 economic doctrines，每个 hunter 和 forager，每个英雄和懦夫，每个 civilization 的 creator 和 destroyer，每个国王和农民，每对相爱的年轻恋人，每位母亲和父亲、满怀希望的孩子、inventor 和 explorer，每位 morals 的教师，每个腐败的 politician，每个“超级明星”，每位“最高领袖”，我们 species 的 history 中每个圣人和罪人，都曾在那里生活——在悬浮于一道 sunbeam 中的一粒 dust 上。

但他也反复 switches 到另一种 point of view，在那里，humanity 是 tiny 而 insignificant 的。随后，他又会 switch 回 human level。这个过程反复发生，有力地 juxtaposing 两种截然不同、表面上似乎存在 strong conflict 的 points of view。当你开始 merge 这两种 consciousness 时，总体效果便是 consciousness 的 expansion。

（在前面几段中，我把“points of view”和“consciousness”混用了。我不会坚持非用哪一个 term 不可。但非常粗略、带有猜测地说，当一种 powerful point of view 被深入体会到足够程度时，它有时便会成为一种 consciousness。而 Sagan 做到了接近这一点的事情。也许“consciousness”这个 term 应当被当作一种追求！）

我最喜欢的事情之一，是我所谓的“syncresis”[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn1)：在其中，两种（或更多）看似 opposed 的 points of view，被看出实际上是同一种更 powerful 的 point of view 的 instances。neo-Darwinian synthesis 就是一个 example。在 evolutionary theory 的早期，有两个 schools of thought：Darwinians 相信 evolution by natural selection 的 primacy；Mendelians 则相信 genetics 的 primacy。直到 1920s 的 neo-Darwinian synthesis，人们才意识到，存在一种更 powerful 的 point of view，在其中 genetics 可以是 evolution 背后的 mechanism。此前被视为处于 opposition 或（至少）tension 中的两种 points of view，实际上是一种更 powerful 的 point of view 的 special cases。

这是一个 intellectual example。在 pale blue dot 那段文字中，我觉得 Sagan 不仅提供了一种 intellectual syncresis，还提供了一种 consciousness 的 syncresis，一种关于两种极为不同的 points of of view 的 syncresis。我不禁想，对于其他哪些“opposed”的 points of view，也可以尝试这样做。

这类 syncresis 的另一个 example，是 movie _Interstellar_ 的 trailer 中的这个 shot；它很精彩，尽管更为常见：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/Interstellar.jpg)

Mascelli 关于 cinematography 的书中有一段精彩的评论，他写道：

> 只要 cameraman 和 film editor 愿意，audience 就可以被 positioned 在_任何地方_——_瞬间_从_任何 angle_ view _任何东西_。这就是 motion picture 的力量！这就是 choosing 合适的 camera angle 的重要性！

_Interstellar_ 的这个 shot 极其有效地运用了这一想法，将两个常被认为处于 tension 中的 worlds 进行 juxtaposing，creating 了另一种（局限得多的）consciousness 的 syncresis。你也可以把 pale blue dot photograph 看作这样的一个 example，只不过，知道这个 shot 是真实的，还会额外带来一阵震颤。

pale blue dot photo 之所以存在，部分原因是 Sagan 当时是 _Voyager_ 团队的成员。按照他的叙述，他受到了那张著名的 Earth photograph“Blue Marble”的启发，它由 _Apollo 17_ astronauts taken：

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/Blue_Marble.jpg)

他深入思考了那个 shot：它如何抹去了 national boundaries；它如何使那么多人类的关切与成就显得渺小。他又想到，一个来自远得多的 _Voyager_ 的 shot，会带来另一种 point of view。尽管面临重大的 technical obstacles，以及一些团队成员的反对，这件事还是做成了：

> XNASA’s Voyager Project 中有许多人表示支持……\[但\]尽管 spacecraft 都位于合适的 spots，instruments 仍然 working 得非常好，也没有其他 pictures 要 take，少数项目人员还是反对。他们说，这不是 science……在最后一刻——实际上，就在 Voyager 2 encounter with Neptune 期间——当时的 NASA Administrator、Rear Admiral Richard Truly 出面，确保了这些 images 得以 obtained。

Sagan 是世界知名的 astronomer 和 astrophysicist，也是 _Voyager_ 团队的成员，有足够的影响力说服团队，用他的话来说，“turn the cameras around”。但要想到 turning the cameras around，他需要惊人的 insight 和 visual imagination。接着，那段文字本身也十分优美。Elegant，几乎没有一个字是多余的。它需要极大的 empathy，把关于 human beings 的 insight 与关于我们在 cosmos 中所处位置的 insight 结合起来。我觉得，这是令人惊叹的技能组合。

**自大：** 对这些笔记余下内容的一点说明：和讨论任何可能开展的创作项目一样，总会有一种自大而尴尬的感觉。“我是谁，竟然在考虑这些事情？”感觉很荒唐。尤其是在考虑“宏大”主题时！但其中许多问题，我们别无选择：我们每个人都在某种层面上面对着它们。因此我想，思考这类项目可能涉及什么，对我个人总有些益处（即使对别人没有！）。如果别人也能有所收获，那就太好了。当然，有些人会不喜欢我做出的任何东西（也无疑会不喜欢这些笔记）；那也没关系。

## 与 doubt、uncertainty 和 not knowing 共处

有一段 Richard Feynman 接受 BBC documentary 采访的片段。他被问及 religion，说了一阵，颇有些磕磕绊绊。显然，他十分投入，但自己的想法也尚不确定——给人的感觉是，一个人正在把自己对某件重要事情的思考说出来，而他的想法或许正在变化，或仍在形成。

不过，有一个有意思的时刻，他转而自信得多地谈起了他所说的“doubt and uncertainty and not knowing”的价值：

对我而言，真正令人震撼的是 Feynman 感受之深。这件事非常接近他内心的核心[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn2)：

> 我可以与 doubt、uncertainty 和 not knowing 共处……我不必知道一个 answer。我不会因为不知道一些事情，因为迷失在一个 mysterious、没有任何 purpose 的 universe 中而感到害怕；据我所能判断，事情确实就是这样……也许吧。

我确信，Feynman 可以花上几个小时谈论 doubt、uncertainty 和 not knowing。对他而言，这是 foundational 的。

这里有一个引人注目的 inversion。人们有时会说，失去 religion 会带来“doubt and uncertainty”的感觉，带来“迷失在一个 mysterious、没有任何 purpose 的 universe 中”的感觉。而且，他们谈论它时，_仿佛这真的是一种损失_。然而我相信，Feynman 已经把这种损失变成了自己人生的支柱；它不是需要掩饰或忽略的东西，而是可以在其上构建的 foundation。我真希望能和他更深入地谈谈这件事。我猜，许多 scientists 多少都有这种感受（我就有）；但我怀疑，很少有人像 Feynman 的 conviction 所暗示的那样，把它想得如此透彻。

我猜，这种 inversion pattern 很常见：把从 religious belief 转向 secularism 时“失去”的某种东西，转变为 fundamental 的东西，转变为可以在其上构建的 foundation。

在 Lois Bujold 的 novel《Shards of Honor》中，两个 characters 的初次见面十分精彩。其中一位是濒死的皇帝，他问另一位，她究竟看中了新婚丈夫的什么。下面是他们的对话：

> 我想——我看到了自己。或者说，一个像我自己的人。我们都在寻找同一样东西。我们给它取了不同的名字，也在不同的地方寻找。我想，他把它叫作 honor。我猜，我会把它叫作 grace of God。我们两人大多时候都一无所获。
> 
> 啊，是的。我记得，你的档案里说，你算是某种 theist。我自己是个 atheist。一种简单的 faith，却在这最后的日子里给了我莫大的安慰。
> 
> 是的，我自己也常常感受到它的吸引。

这又是对 conventional view 的 inversion：“一种简单的 faith，却在这最后的日子里给了我莫大的安慰。”这让我想知道，我们能在多大程度上，逐一列出 atheism 或 secular humanism 的许多所谓 drawbacks，反而更加坚定地把它们作为一种 worldview 的 foundation。

physicist Steven Weinberg 曾写道：“Universe 越显得 comprehensible，就越显得 pointless。”他受到了许多批评，在我看来，他似乎（多少）有些歉意。但我想，他是否本可以反而更加坚持，试着把这一 point of view 变成一种 virtue，变成进一步思考的 basis。我不知道该怎样做到——也许会失败。但只要有足够的想象力，或许也可能成功。

我在这里一直反复谈论 religion。这有点奇怪：虽然 religion 是 _Cosmos_ 中一个鲜明的 theme，却不是 dominant theme。尽管如此，我直觉上觉得，_Cosmos_ 在回应 human beings 内心的同一种需求。所以我总是回到这个话题。也可能我就是对 religion 感兴趣！Christian Bible 有一点乍看很吸引人：universe 的 creator 显然也十分关心你的 individual behavior（你买邻居的山羊时，有没有公平地付钱？）。而当你意识到 Christian cosmology 荒唐透顶时，cosmological 与 individual 就被割裂开了[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn3)。_Cosmos_ 似乎是在尝试以一种不同、也令人满意得多的方式，把它们重新结合起来（至少部分如此）。但我想知道，还能做些什么。

## 对 numinous 更为开阔的感受

_Cosmos_ 的一个美妙之处，是你可以通过 Sagan 的双眼看世界。你能部分地看到他所看到的东西。那是一种非凡的、拓展视野的 universe 图景。

有一种相关的 phenomenon，我把它叫作“science porn”或“science-as-superstimulus”——人们分享漂亮的 galaxies、biological nanomachines 的 images，以及任何能引起“哇”这一反应的东西。Sagan 有时也会这样做：他的“billions and billions”就有这种味道。近年来，在 science documentaries 和 social media 上的 science 内容中，这种做法越来越常见。

我本能地反感 science-as-superstimulus。一部分原因是那种粗俗、自利的 commercialization：许多人已经发现，可以借此卖 T 恤和咖啡杯。另一部分原因是，它经常带有 tribal 色彩；人们因为自己属于“team science”而欢欣鼓舞，借此获得优越感，或者把它当成攫取 power 的棍棒。

面对这种傲慢，人很容易以更傲慢的态度回应，转而要求拓展 precise articulated understanding。声称“billions and billions”不是真正的 understanding，而“不过”是一种 aesthetic。声称真正的 understanding 应当是了解 galaxies 的 actual sizes，或许还要能够详细 explain cosmic distance ladder，等等。

我猜，还有一种有成效得多的 point of view。

毕竟：aesthetic understanding 是真正的 understanding。知道一个 galaxy 是美的，能够 recognize 那个 image，就是对我们思想的拓展。“哇，太棒了！”这种反应被人挪用，并不意味着这种反应本身不好。完全不是！那是在 confusing effect with cause。

总的来说，思考哪些 works 能够 expand 人们对 numinous 的感受[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn4)，以及它们如何做到这一点，是件有意思的事。_Cosmos_ 做到了。_The Feynman Lectures_ 做到了。也许 _A Brief History of Time_ 也做到了[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn5)。我猜，这构成了 Rembrandt 很大一部分吸引力；对我而言，肯定如此。我对 Bach 的反应远没有那么强烈，但我猜，人们在 Bach 那里回应的也是这一点。我也喜欢 Bill Bryson 的 _A Brief History of Nearly Everything_ 中的这一点。

我在自己的 essay [The Artist and the Machine](https://michaelnielsen.org/blog/the-artist-and-the-machine/) 中略微谈过这个问题：“super-realist art，这种 art 不仅 capture 我们用眼睛直接看到、或用耳朵听到的东西，还使用新的 sensors 和 methods of visualization，来 reveal 一个我们无法 directly perceive 的世界。”一个 galaxy、ribosome 或苍蝇眼睛的 representation 中，蕴含着某种极其强大的东西。当这些东西呈现在眼前时，我们会本能地 grok，它们背后存在着_新的、强大的 pattern 种类_。我猜，这就是它们能够充当 superstimuli 的原因。一个 spiral galaxy 看起来几乎不像你曾见过的任何其他东西；spiral pattern 背后有深刻的原因（它为什么会在较老的 galaxies 中 fades，也有深刻的原因）。你_理应_作出强烈反应：你正在世界中看到一种新颖而重要的 pattern，即便你不知道它是什么。它正在 expanding 你对 numinous 的感受。

一个极大的挑战与机遇是 mathematics。无论就其本身而言，还是就其在我们的 universe 中所发挥的作用而言，很少有东西比它更令人惊叹、更美。在 Sagan 的 novel _Contact_ 中，main character 问一位 theoretical physicist，他是否曾有过带来转变的 religious experience：

> “有过，”他说。
> 
> “什么时候？”有时候，你得鼓励他开口。
> 
> “我第一次拿起 Euclid 的时候。还有我第一次 understood Newtonian gravitation 的时候。还有 Maxwell's equations，以及 general relativity。还有我研究 superunification 的时候。我很幸运，有过许多 religious experiences。”

当然，我不知道 Sagan 写这段话时的用意。但当一个人 understands geometry、Newtonian gravitation 或 general relativity 时[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn6)，确实会感受到某种辉煌，感受到某种大大拓展了的 external reality。总的来说，mathematics 和 science 是 numinous 的惊人源泉。人们常说，religion 的一个益处，是培养对某种超越自我的东西的感受；science 也是如此。

遗憾的是，这里有一个巨大的挑战：大多数人没有能力做 mathematics。其中一部分原因肯定是，他们并没有真正地_体验_过它。我猜，许多人的 mathematically able 程度远远超出了他们自己的认识。试着创造这样一种体验，会很有意思。3Blue1Brown、Vi Hart 和其他几个人，已经在这个方向做出了出色的工作。想想还能走多远，是件有趣的事。

## 以 belief 为 foundation

Alan Watts 曾指出，secular 的人常常相信深深持有的 religious beliefs 的价值。但 believing in belief 并不够。你确实需要 believe。

我遇到过不少 secular 的人，他们自己编造 rituals、fables、traditions。而我常常（并非总是）觉得这样不对。他们极其真诚，正在寻觅。然而，他们往往是在 _模仿_ 过去，编造一些东西。他们没有问：我最深的 beliefs 是什么？除非他们这样问，否则他们的 rituals 就会显得乏味无力。他们在寻找 Yahweh。就像 Hesse 的 _Siddhartha_ 中的 Govinda，他们在寻找一个告诉他们答案的人，而不是 exploring 自己实际上 believe 什么。

这正是我欣赏 Feynman 谈论“doubt 与 uncertainty，以及 not knowing”的原因之一。Jonathan Blow 的 video game“The Witness”也是如此。Penrose 的“Road to Reality”也是如此。说起来，Sagan 的“Cosmos”同样如此。在每一种情况下，他们都在更加坚定地 exploring 对他们个人而言重要的 beliefs。其中有一种你能感受到的真诚。即便我并不赞同，或者觉得它与我自己的感受格格不入，我仍然能感受到它。那是一种强烈而真诚的兴趣，以他们某些最深的 beliefs 为 foundation，随后尽其所能地 developed 下去。

这或许与我为什么从 philosophy 或 theology 中（很多，并非全部）所得甚少有关。它们常常像是干巴巴的 intellectualizing。mathematician Littlewood 曾说，他不明白，研究 ethics 为什么会影响一个人的行为是好是坏；在我看来，这是一个极其有力的 critique。这类枯燥论著的作者或许很聪明，却不一定显得明智；wisdom 似乎部分来自艰难的 decisions，来自 sacrifice 和 struggle。当你听 Simone Weil 或 Hannah Arendt 这样的人说话时，moral philosophy 就有了切实的力量。她们或许是错的，但那些思想来之不易，是她们为之奉献一生的东西。那绝不是廉价的空谈，绝不是。在这方面，我有些怀疑自己是否有能力作出贡献：虽然我的生活并非没有挑战，但与许多人相比，已经相对轻松[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn7)。这也是我欣赏 Sagan 作品的一点：他对 science 的投入既 deep 又 humane，并且 interconnects 了 science 的多个 areas、history，以及他自己的生活经历和对他人的 empathy。

## 关于 Grayling

一个非常有意思的 experiment 是 A. C. Grayling 的“The Good Book”。Grayling 是一位英国 philosopher 和 humanist。“The Good Book”的构想，是成为一部“从世界伟大的 humanist traditions 中汲取 insight、wisdom、solace，以及关于 human condition 的 commentary 的作品……一种 epic、激动人心的 narrative，最终揭示 life——一种 good life——应当如何度过。”

我敬佩 Grayling 写了这本书。但我不太喜欢读它。逐行读下来，似乎都缺少切身的感受——在我看来，Grayling 与他的 material 之间似乎缺少联系。套用一个惯常的 metaphor，这是用头脑而非用心写出的作品。它存在我前面描述的问题，与自己的 material 保持着一种干巴巴的距离。与其说它关乎生与死，不如说它是一集 crossword puzzles。

不过，读了其中一部分，还是促使我作出了一些有用的 observations。

书中很多内容 adapted 自超过 1,000 个 sources——包括 Emerson、Epictetus 和 Epicurus 这样的作家。material 的组织方式与 Christian Bible 类似，分为 Genesis、Songs、Parables 等部分。它的 style 很奇特。以下是 Epistles 一书的开头：

> 1.  我亲爱的儿子：你必须先对我们的 human species 有一个 true estimate，
>     
> 2.  然后才能开始 formulate，你将如何使自己成为其中更好的 specimens 之一。
>     
> 3.  当你行走各地、与人交往时，这将是我写给你的这些信的目的，
>     
> 4.  以便你能将自己与他们相处、在各地所得的经验，同我提供给你的经验结合起来。
>     
> 5.  有些人将 human species 捧上天，把人描绘成 paragon；
>     
> 6.  另一些人则执意强调 human nature 最坏的一面，除了 vanity 和 folly，在人身上什么也看不到，使人显得并不比其他 animals 更好。
>     
> 7.  对 morals 的细腻感受，容易使人厌恶世界，并对人间事务的寻常进程感到愤慨和不满。
>     
> 8.  就我而言，我认为，那些 favorably 且 sympathetically 看待 mankind 的人，比那些对 human nature 抱有鄙薄看法的人，更能 promote virtue。
>     

我赞同其中的意思。但 Grayling 刻意使用 archaic language，并选择了不寻常的 meter。我怀疑他是否这样思考，或者是否有多少这样写作的经验。不出所料，他并不擅长。我猜他是在模仿某种 style。就好像他在用一种自己不太熟悉的语言写书。这样做更难把事情想清楚。作为 experiment，它有意思，也值得敬佩。但作为读者，我觉得它行不通：它制造了一道人为的屏障，而一旦穿过那道屏障，里面的内容又没什么意思。如果去掉 archaic embellishments，重写上面那段话，就会变成：

> 人很容易过分 exalt 或过分 demonize human nature。但如果我们 favorably 且 sympathetically 看待 humankind，而不是抱有 negative 的看法，就有助于我们成为更好的人。

并非完全没意思，但也不是特别有意思。exploring 这类 ideas，有好得多（也丰富得多）的方式。

正如 Susan Rigetti [在 Twitter 上](https://twitter.com/susanthesquark/status/1492577755409498117)评论这段话时所说：“哎哟，这也太多空话了。”

Context 很重要。把 Grayling 与 Sermon on the Mount 作一番 compare，是件有意思的事。也可以尝试沿着类似的思路对 Sermon 作出 critique：它包含的许多内容都是 platitudes。然而，我无法相信那些话在当时就是 platitudes。相反，它们当时一定是 radical、fresh 的 ideas。即便在今天，其中一些 ideas _仍然_ 显得新鲜。在 Bible 的 context 中，整篇似乎是一个或多个头脑发挥到极限的产物。Sermon 令我深深感动；而“The Good Book”主要让我感到失望。我相信，Grayling 需要更深入地 metabolize 他的 sources[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn8)，才能获得同样的 freshness。

确实，Sagan 做到了这种 metabolization。把“the pale blue dot”与 Huygens 的“Cosmotheoros”中这段话 compare 一下：

> 那些 Orbs 必定何等 vast，而与它们相比，这个 Earth——我们所有宏伟的 Designs、所有 Navigations，以及所有 Wars 得以进行的 Theatre——又是何等 inconsiderable。对于那些牺牲众多人的生命，只为满足自己成为这个小小 Spot 上某个可怜角落的主人的野心的国王和王公而言，这实在是极其值得思量、值得 Reflection 的事。

我非常确信，作为 Huygens 的仰慕者，Sagan 在写那段著名文字时，有意识地 drawing on 了这些 ideas 和 language。我在 Twitter 上提到这一点，一位记者得出的 conclusion 是，Sagan“他妈的 plagiarized 了 Christiaan Huygens”。我同意，Sagan 应该在 endnotes 中 cited Huygens，说明 inspiration 的来源（他并没有这样做，不过他在书中其他地方确实多次 cite 了 Huygens）。但除此之外，这是一种狭隘的 conclusion[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn9)。Sagan 在 metabolizing 和 reimagining 前代伟大思想家那些具有 fundamental importance 的 ideas。在正文中停下来遵守 academic citation norms，只会令人分心。Lincoln 在 Gettysburg Address 中没有 credit Euclid；他也不应该这样做。

**那些相信自己应该喜欢这本书的人：**读 reviews 可以发现，许多人很欣赏 Grayling 的书。我想，这些人是不是大多相信，自己 _应该_ 喜欢 _这本书的构想_？也许我只是不是它的 intended audience。我要说的是：书中有许多单独的句子值得玩味。但作为 narrative，它并不成功。Grayling 尚未 metabolized 他 drawing upon 的那个 ideas 的 canon。

## 随手勾勒

只是随手勾勒一些想法！琢磨整体的 themes，随口提出一些具体想法。其中许多也可能成为一部更大作品中的 subthemes 或 subessays。只要有合适的 overarching question 或 theme，就可能把其中许多 themes 编织在一起。

这些笔记余下的部分比上面写的还要粗糙得多；你也许想略读，或者干脆全部跳过！只是我一个人在摆弄想法！

我知道的当然还不足以在这里勾勒一本书或其他任何 media form。我没有整体的 narrative。即便有，我也需要先写各种小 essays，来加深自己的 understanding。先积累大量粗略笔记，再逐渐形成一些更精致的 essays。也许还可以结合 teaching——其实就是召集一小群背景各异、思考深入的人，带领一场 discussion。规划这些很有趣。就让我先在这里快速倒出脑中的想法，算是第二遍梳理的一个 prototype。

**“我已成为死神，世界的毁灭者”** Trinity。Hiroshima。源头在于 symbols：energy-mass relation。energy 的 _actual_ source 是什么？纸上的 marks 如何使我们能够终结 humanity。Hydrogen bomb。那些 marks 究竟是什么？纸上的 marks 为什么会有 power？Galileo。mathematics 是什么？我们为什么能够 understand 这个 world？Existential threats。Toba。我们应该如何思考它们？我们能如何应对它们？

**Doubt、uncertainty 与不 knowing：** Myth 与 magic。从 Magic 到 science。Newton 作为最后一位 magicians。freedom of enquiry 为什么如此重要？它为什么遭到反对？Bruno 与 Galileo 的 myth 和 reality。Nullius in verba。Decentralization 与 idea machine。Confusion。ignorance 以及新的 knowing 方式的 history。edict 与 infallibility 的 history。以 Doubt、uncertainty 与不 knowing 作为更坚实的 foundation。

**Becoming transhuman：** 21st century 深层的 tension 与 project 也许在于，这是 humanity becomes posthuman 的世纪。Technology 正在 changing 我们的 individual 和 collective minds；它正在 changing 我们的 reproduction；它让我们对自己的 bodies 和 minds 拥有多得多的 agency 与 insight；它已赋予我们 end life on earth 的能力；它也许会赋予我们 create successor minds 的能力。我们应该如何回应这一切？一种 humane transhumanism 会是什么样？

**一个 proton 的 Life：** 从 birth 到（possible）death。

**为 technocracy 欢呼两声：** 它的 Benefits。I, Pencil，以及 Hayekian hidden knowledge。以 Capital、truth、evolution 和 ethics 作为（competing！）orienting forces。technology 实际上做了什么。Technopoly。Science 和 technology 与 power 的关系。为什么不是 military 在 rule。Collective action。是否可能有一个让我们的 public goods 丰富充裕的 world？也许可以与下一项合并：

**Unequal（不平等）：** Inequality 是 history 中普遍存在的 theme：有些人拥有，有些人没有。然而，它眼下正在迅速变化。其中许多方面已广为人知（更多 winner-take-all dynamics 的 emergence、globalization、urbanization、human rights）。其他部分似乎较少得到讨论（perception 的 globalization）。与此相关的还有：

**increased self-knowledge 所 posed 的 moral quandaries：** 一个尤为紧迫的 instance 是 behavioral genetics，它正在对我们有关 responsibility、fairness 和 justice 的 notions 施加缓慢、却 inexorably increasing 的 pressure。但还有许多其他 problems。我们是 East Africans Plains apes，这造成了如此多的 problems 和 tensions。

**Acceleration：** 生活在 future shock 的时代。Amish。Sabbath。past、present 和 future 的 unequally distributed。wave phenomenon（waves 与 constituent particles 的 speeds 极其 disparate）对 human systems 也有 implications。Durkheim、anomie、alienation，以及 division of labor。还有 hikimori。

**Thoughts 作为 symbol systems 的 emergent properties**

**对 numinous 的更广阔感受 \[可能是一个 theme\]**

**我们所有的 knowledge：** 一幅 panorama。我有时会想，如果有一部面向我们“所有” fundamental knowledge 的“The Feynman Lectures”，它会是什么样？（“所有”取某个 value）。不是 encyclopedia（它的 affect 和 remit 都相当乏味），而是一部观点鲜明得多的作品？

**Syncresis（theme）：** James Burke 做过“Connections”。我更感兴趣的是 tensions，以及对它们的 understanding 和 resolving。

**其他 themes、questions 和 values：** 什么是 good？对 science 的 emotional 和 cognitive experience。The Long News。The Long View。我们如何 know，我们 know 什么。science 与 power 之间恰当的 relationship 是什么？Superhero problems：如何应对 power？如何应对 powerlessness？conversation 是为了什么？什么比 democracy 更好？我最深切地 believe 什么？awe 与 science 之间的 relationship 是什么？Revealing hidden beauty 和 hidden connections。不回避令人不适的事物。Experiencing 我们如何逐渐 know。提供 aesthetic、emotional 和 intellectual experiences。找到能让我产生强烈 emotional response 的东西；找到我所 believe 的东西；找到不同寻常的东西，那些让我觉得自己正在作出某种 creative contribution 的东西，无论它们多么古怪。

## 关于过程的观察

**学习，不要模仿：** 我很容易过分看重自己喜欢的 examples。我当然写不出 _Cosmos_ 或 _其他类似作品_。部分原因是那些人的 skills 与我不同。但至少同样重要的原因是，他们的 taste 很不一样。你需要尽可能信任并深化自己的 taste，而不是依赖别人的。事实上，在某种意义上，_Cosmos_（以及 Sagan 的许多作品）属于我的过去。我依然喜欢其中的大段内容，也依然对某些部分（比如 pale blue dot）满怀敬畏。但我也不再是那个看得入迷的 8 岁孩子了。

**这类作品的榜样与灵感来源：** Melodysheep；Grant Sanderson；Vi Hart；Jonathan Blow；Richard Feynman；Carl Sagan；E. O. Wilson；Bryan Moriarty；Christopher Nolan；Cirque du Soleil；Nightwish；Roger Penrose，尤其是“The Emperor's New Mind”（错了，但绝对精彩）和“The Road to Reality”；Simone Weil；Po Bronson 的“What should I do with my life?”；Rembrandt；Picasso；Technopoly；Long Now；Stewart Brand（包括那部短片）；Brian Eno；Meow Wolf；Sleep No More；Bret Victor；David Chapman。

**Music、Motion 与 Images：** Media form 非常重要。它改变了你能够获得的 experience 的 type。emotion 的 range；understanding 的 range；immersion 的感受；agency 的感受。在某种程度上，这里就是存在 tradeoffs，或者 tensions。但 Grant Sanderson 和 Nightwish 这样的 examples 表明，这些 tradeoffs 可能比通常设想的要 malleable 得多。

**Outsider art：** 这里常见一种奇怪的 category error。人们往往把东西分成“pop science / pop writing”和“expert writing”。上面提到的大多数事情，我都不是 expert。所以，很容易默认把自己归入 pop science。但我对 pop science 没什么兴趣，对纯粹为 exposition 而作的 exposition 也没什么兴趣。我非常感兴趣的是作出一些 creative contributions，它们与任何现有 discipline 都略有不同。你能在 _Cosmos_ 中看到这一点；事实上，我举过的许多 examples 都是如此。它们是在 solving 作者真正面对的 creative problems，而不是“仅仅”[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn10)作 exposition。在某种意义上，它们都是 outsider art。按照 insider 的标准，它们也许不那么好，也许会失败。但它们并不是在 solving 同样的 problems；它们实际上是在 solving insiders 根本想不到要去 solve、甚至可能都察觉不到的 problems。

**以 Writing 为 core：** 我热爱 art、music 和 film。我相信自己对它们有一定的 taste。但我不是这些 mediums 中的 creator。在考虑这个 project 时，这一点一直困扰着我。不过，成年后我重看 _Cosmos_，很快便意识到：这是一场（极其出色的）university lecture。它的 foundation 在于 Sagan 知道如何 write 关于 science 的内容。其他东西——music、images、staging——极大地 enhances 它。但 _Cosmos_ 的 animating force 是其底层 writing 的 quality。将它与 _Planet Earth_ 对比，差别十分鲜明；后者很精彩，但 writing 介于糟糕和平庸之间。在 _Planet Earth_ 中，images 才是 animating force。或者想想 _Nightwish_，它的 core 是 music 和 operatic staging。因此，我必须让 writing 成为任何 project 的 core：尽管我需要大幅 improve 自己的 writing，至少我 _能够_ write。而且，如果有合适的 collaborators，也许还能用 music 和 film 做出一些奇妙的东西。

（谈论自己 writing 的 quality，总会让人尴尬而不自在。让我照例先清清嗓子：这些笔记当然都是匆忙写下的边想边说，因此写得很差。）

**Writing 作为 creating 一种 consciousness 感受的方式：** Edwin Schlossberg 说得很好：“writing 的 skill 在于 create 一个让其他人能够 think 的 context。”事实上，不只是其他人，也包括 writer。这一想法的一种 extreme form 是，writing 的 skill 在于在 reader 的 mind 中 create 一种特定的 consciousness。其他 media forms 也是如此。帮助别人像你一样看见；帮助别人以某种特定方式 experience 这个 world。重要的 question 往往不是“这本书讲什么？”，而是“它 create 了怎样一种 consciousness 的感受？”在我看来，这对伟大的 books（以及 music、film 和 _其他作品_）尤其成立。_Pride and Prejudice_、_Gatsby_、_Les Mis_ 等等：在每一种情况下，你都进入了这个奇妙的 world，而你的 consciousness 随之 changes。一个人能够在人们身上 evoke 哪些 types 的 consciousness？

## The pale blue dot

The pale blue dot is a famous photograph of the Earth taken by the _Voyager 1_ spacecraft, when it was 3.7 billion miles from the Earth:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/pale_blue_dot.png)

Carl Sagan reflects on the photograph in a famous passage in his book of the same name, "Pale Blue Dot":

> From this distant vantage point, the Earth might not seem of any particular interest.
> 
> But for us, it’s different. Look again at that dot. That’s here. That’s home. That’s us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives. The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child, inventor and explorer, every teacher of morals, every corrupt politician, every “superstar,” every “supreme leader,” every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived there—on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.
> 
> The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. Think of the endless cruelties visited by the inhabitants of one corner of this pixel on the scarcely distinguishable inhabitants of some other corner, how frequent their misunderstandings, how eager they are to kill one another, how fervent their hatreds.
> 
> Our posturings, our imagined self-importance, the delusion that we have some privileged position in the Universe, are challenged by this point of pale light. Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves.
> 
> The Earth is the only world known so far to harbor life. There is nowhere else, at least in the near future, to which our species could migrate. Visit, yes. Settle, not yet. Like it or not, for the moment the Earth is where we make our stand.
> 
> It has been said that astronomy is a humbling and character-building experience. There is perhaps no better demonstration of the folly of human conceits than this distant image of our tiny world. To me, it underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another, and to preserve and cherish the pale blue dot, the only home we’ve ever known.

I find this an astonishing passage, especially when narrated aloud by Sagan:

Denis Diderot observed in theatrical actors a kind of dual consciousness. To act well, it helps greatly if they come to _be_ their characters, experiencing the emotions and desires of their characters as deeply as possible. But they are simultaneously conscious of themselves one step removed, as actors. I am not an actor, but have sometimes been asked to fulfill roles (host, emcee, usher, and so on) that seem to require a similar dual consciousness.

Three testimonies to this dual consciousness:

(1) The World War 1 movie _1917_ featured exceptionally long shots, sometime 5-10 minutes. One of the lead actors said he sometimes became so immersed during long shots that he experienced shock when the shot finished, and he realized that he was not, in fact, a soldier in the trenches in 1917.

(2) The comedian Rowan Atkinson has remarked that sometimes when performing on stage a performance will start to "run away with itself… sometimes there's an extraordinary moment when it sort of tips over the other side and runs away from you, and actually you are no longer in charge of the performance."

(3) Michael Caine was asked how he remembered lines, and observed that when a script is exceptionally well written, you become so immersed in the reality of the scene that it becomes possible to "read your lines off the other character's face".

Sagan fosters a related dual consciousness in readers in his passage. Over and over we switch between two vastly different points of view: in one, he is deeply empathetic and warmly human:

> That’s here. That’s home. That’s us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives. The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child, inventor and explorer, every teacher of morals, every corrupt politician, every “superstar,” every “supreme leader,” every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived there—on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.

But he also repeatedly switches to another point of view, in which humanity is tiny and insignificant. And then he will switch back again to the human level. This repeats multiple times, powerfully juxtaposing two vastly different points of view, points of view in what might seem superficially like strong conflict. The net effect is an expansion of consciousness, as you begin to merge these two types of consciousness.

(I'm conflating "points of view" with "consciousness" in those last paragraphs. I won't insist on either term. But, very roughly and speculatively, when a powerful point of view is inhabited deeply enough it sometimes becomes a consciousness. And Sagan achieves something close to that. Perhaps the term "consciousness" should be treated aspirationally!)

One of my favorite things is what I call "syncresis"[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn1), in which two (or more) apparently opposed points of view are seen to really be instances of a single more powerful point of view. An example is the neo-Darwinian synthesis. In the early days of evolutionary theory, there were two schools of thought: the Darwinians, who believed in the primacy of evolution by natural selection; and the Mendelians, who believed in the primacy of genetics. It wasn't until the neo-Darwinian synthesis in the 1920s that it was realized that there was a more powerful point of view in which genetics could be the mechanism underlying evolution. Two points of view which had previously been seen as in opposition or (at the very least) tension were, in fact, special cases of a more powerful point of view.

That's an intellectual example. In the pale blue dot passage, it seems to me that Sagan is providing not just an intellectual syncresis, but also a syncresis of consciousness, of two remarkably different points of of view. And I wonder with what other "opposed" points of view this may be attempted.

Another example of this kind of syncresis, very nice albeit more commonplace, is this shot from the trailer for the movie _Interstellar_:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/Interstellar.jpg)

There's a great comment in Mascelli's book on cinematography, where he notes:

> The audience may be positioned _anywhere_ — _instantly_ to view _anything_ from _any angle_ — at the discretion of the cameraman and film editor. Such is the power of the motion picture! Such is the importance of choosing the right camera angle!

The _Interstellar_ shot uses this idea to great effect, juxtaposing two worlds often thought to be in tension, creating another (much more limited) syncresis of consciousness. You can view the pale blue dot photograph also as an example of this, too, albeit with the additional frisson of knowing the shot is real.

Part of the reason the pale blue dot photo exists is because Sagan was a member of _Voyager_ team. In his account, he was inspired by the famous "Blue Marble" photograph of the Earth, taken by the _Apollo 17_ astronauts:

![](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/assets/Blue_Marble.jpg)

He reflected deeply on that shot: how it erased national boundaries; how small it made so many human concerns and achievements seem. And he had the insight that a shot from _Voyager_, so much further away, would yield yet another point of view. This was done in spite of considerable technical obstacles, and opposition by some team members:

> Many in NASA’s Voyager Project were supportive… \[but\] Although the spacecraft were in the right spots, the instruments were still working beautifully, and there were no other pictures to take, a few project personnel opposed it. It wasn’t science, they said… At the last minute—actually, in the midst of the Voyager 2 encounter with Neptune—the then NASA Administrator, Rear Admiral Richard Truly, stepped in and made sure that these images were obtained.

Sagan was a world renowned astronomer and astrophysicist, a member of the _Voyager_ team, with enough influence to convince the team to, in his words, "turn the cameras around". But he needed astonishing insight and visual imagination to conceive of turning the cameras around. And then the passage itself is beautiful. Elegant, barely a word wasted. It requires immense empathy, marrying insight about human beings with insight about our place in the cosmos. I find it an astonishing combination of skills.

**Hubris:** A comment on the remainder of these notes: as always in discussing any prospective creative project there is a sense of hubris and embarrassment. "Who am I to be considering any of this?" It feels ludicrous. This is especially true when one is considering "grand" themes! But with many of these questions, we don't get any choice: we are, all of us, confronting them at some level. And so I think there's some benefit to me personally (if no-one else!) in considering what might be involved in a project of this sort. And if others might get some benefit too, wonderful. And, of course, some will dislike anything I make (and will no doubt dislike these notes); that's fine too.

## Doubt and uncertainty and not knowing

There is a clip of Richard Feynman being interviewed for a BBC documentary. He's asked about religion, and he stumbles around for a bit. It's evident that he's deeply engaged, but also unsure in his thinking – the sense is of a person thinking aloud about something they think is important, but where his thinking is perhaps changing, or still being formed.

There is an interesting moment, however, at which he transitions to talking much more confidently, about what he refers to as the value in "doubt and uncertainty and not knowing":

What is really striking, to me, is the depth of Feynman's feeling. This is something very close to the core of him[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn2):

> I can live with doubt and uncertainty and not knowing…I don't have to know an answer. I don't feel frightened not knowing things, by being lost in a mysterious universe without any purpose, which is the way it really is as far as I can tell…possibly.

I'm certain Feynman could talk for hours about doubt and uncertainty and not knowing. For him, this was foundational.

There's a striking inversion here. People sometimes talk about the loss of religion as creating a sense of "doubt and uncertainty", of "being lost in a mysterious universe without any purpose". And they speak of it _as though it's a genuine loss_. And yet Feynman, I believe, has turned this loss into a lynchpin of his life, not something to gloss over or ignore, but rather a foundation upon which to build. I wish I could talk to him far more about this. I suspect many scientists have something of this sense (I do); but I doubt many have thought it through as much as Feynman's conviction implies he had.

I suspect this inversion pattern is a common one: taking something which has been "lost" in the transition from religious belief to secularism, and turning it into something fundamental, a foundation on which to build.

In Lois Bujold's novel "Shards of Honor" there is a remarkable first meeting between two characters. One, a dying Emperor, asks the other what it is that she sees in her new husband. Here's the back and forth:

> I suppose—I see myself. Or someone like myself. We're both looking for the same thing. We call it by different names, and look in different places. I believe he calls it honor. I guess I'd call it the grace of God. We both come up empty, mostly.
> 
> Ah, yes. I recall from your file that you are some sort of theist. I am an atheist, myself. A simple faith, but a great comfort to me, in these last days.
> 
> Yes, I have often felt the pull of it myself.

Again, the inversion of the conventional view: "A simple faith, but a great comfort to me, in these last days". It makes me wonder to what extent it is possible to enumerate many of the supposed drawbacks of atheism or secular humanism, and double down on them as the foundation of a worldview.

The physicist Steven Weinberg once wrote that "the more the Universe seems comprehensible, the more it seems pointless". He received a lot of criticism, and seemed to me (somewhat) apologetic. But I wonder if instead he might have doubled down, and tried to turn this point of view into a virtue, a basis for further thought. I don't know how that would be done – it might fail. But with sufficient imagination, perhaps it might succeed.

I've been harping here on religion. Which is peculiar: while religion is a strong theme in _Cosmos_, it's not a dominant theme. Still, I have this instinct that _Cosmos_ is appealing to the same need within human beings. And so I keep coming back to it. Or maybe I'm just interested in religion! One superficially appealing thing about the Christian Bible is that the creator of the universe is, apparently, also deeply concerned with your individual behavior (did you pay your neighbor fairly for their goats?) And when you realize that Christian cosmology is bonkers, it cleaves the cosmological and the individual[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn3). _Cosmos_ seems like a (partial) attempt to put them back together, in a different and much more satisfying way. But I wonder what else might be done.

## An expanded sense of the numinous

A beautiful thing about _Cosmos_ is that you get to see through Sagan's eyes. You see, in part, what he sees. And it's a remarkable, enlarging view of the universe.

There's a related phenomenon, which I think of as "science porn" or "science-as-superstimulus" – people sharing pretty images of galaxies, biological nanomachines, anything which can evoke a "wow" response. Sagan sometimes did it: his "billions and billions" had this flavor. It's become more common in recent years, in science documentaries, and science on social media.

I have an instinctive revulsion against science-as-superstimulus. Part of it is because of the crass self-interested commercialization: many people have figured out this is a way they can sell tshirts and coffee mugs. Part of it is because it is so often tribal, people who exult in being on "team science", a way of feeling superior, or a club they can wield for power.

It's tempting to respond to such snottiness with more snottiness, to demand instead an expansion in precise articulated understanding. To claim that "billions and billions" isn't real understanding, but "merely" an aesthetic. That a genuine understanding would be knowledge of the actual sizes of the galaxies, perhaps an ability to explain the cosmic distance ladder in detail, and so on.

I suspect there is a much more productive point of view.

After all: an aesthetic understanding is a genuine understanding. To know that a galaxy is beautiful, to recognize the image, is an expansion of our thought. The mere fact that "wow, awesome!" has been co-opted doesn't mean that response is intrinsically bad. Not at all! It's confusing effect with cause.

In general, it's interesting to think about works which expand people's sense of the numinous[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn4), and how they do it. _Cosmos_ does it. _The Feynman Lectures_ does it. Perhaps _A Brief History of Time_ did[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn5). I suspect this is much of the appeal of Rembrandt; certainly, it is for me. I do not respond nearly as strongly to Bach, but I suspect that is what people are responding to in Bach, as well. It's something I enjoy about Bill Bryson's _A Brief History of Nearly Everything_ too.

I've written a little about this in my essay [The Artist and the Machine](https://michaelnielsen.org/blog/the-artist-and-the-machine/): "super-realist art, art which doesn’t just capture what we can see directly with our eyes or hear with our ears, but which uses new sensors and methods of visualization to reveal a world that we cannot directly perceive." There is something very powerful in a representation of a galaxy, or the ribosome, or a fly's eye. When shown these things, we instinctively grok that there are _new kinds of powerful pattern_ underlying them. And I suspect that's the reason they work as superstimuli. A spiral galaxy looks like almost nothing else you have ever seen; there are deep reasons for the spiral pattern (and for why it fades in older galaxies). You _ought_ to respond strongly: you're seeing a novel and important pattern in the world, even if you don't know what it is. It's expanding your sense of the numinous.

One very great challenge and opportunity is mathematics. There are few things more astonishing and beautiful, both intrinsically, and also for the role they play in our universe. In Sagan's novel _Contact_ the main character asks a theoretical physicist if he had ever had a transforming religious experience:

> "Yes," he said.
> 
> "When?" Sometimes you had to encourage him to talk.
> 
> "When I first picked up Euclid. Also when I first understood Newtonian gravitation. And Maxwell's equations, and general relativity. And during my work on superunification. I have been fortunate enough to have had many religious experiences."

I do not, of course, know what Sagan intended by this passage. But certainly there is something glorious, a sense of some vastly expanded external reality, when one understands geometry, Newtonian gravitation, or general relativity[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn6). In general, mathematics and science are amazing sources of the numinous. People often talk about a benefit of religion as being developing a sense of something larger than one's self; it's true of science, as well.

Unfortunately, there is a tremendous challenge here: most people are not able to do mathematics. Some of this is certainly that they have not really _experienced_ it. I suspect many people are vastly more mathematically able than they realize. It would be interesting to try to create an experience of that. 3Blue1Brown, Vi Hart and a few others have done beautiful work in that direction. It's fun to ponder how much further one might go.

## The foundation in belief

Alan Watts has observed that secular people often believe in the value of deeply held religious beliefs. But believing in belief is not enough. You actually need to believe.

I meet a fair number of secular people who make up rituals, fables, traditions. And it often (not always) seems wrong to me. They are tremendously sincere people, searching. And yet they are often making stuff up in _imitation_ of the past. They are not asking: what are my deepest beliefs? Unless and until they do so their rituals will seem lame. They're searching for Yahweh. Like Govinda in Hesse's _Siddhartha_, they're looking for someone to tell them the answer, rather than exploring what they actually believe.

This is one of the things I enjoy about Feynman on "doubt and uncertainty and not knowing". Ditto Jonathan Blow's video game "The Witness". Or Penrose's "Road to Reality". Or for that matter, Sagan in "Cosmos". In each case they are doubling down and exploring beliefs that are important to them, personally. There's a sincerity in it that you can feel. Even when I disagree with it, or find it foreign to my own sensibility, I can still sense it. Tremendous, sincere interest, founded in some of their deepest beliefs, and then developed as far as they can go.

This is perhaps connected with why I get so little out of (much, not all) philosophy or theology. It often seems like dry intellectualizing. The mathematician Littlewood observed that he didn't see why a study of ethics should have any impact on whether one behaved poorly or well; this seems a damning critique to me. The writers of such dry tracts may well be smart, but they do not necessarily seem wise; wisdom seems to arise, in part, from making difficult decisions, and from sacrifice and struggle. When you hear from people like Simone Weil or Hannah Arendt, the moral philosophy has teeth. They may be wrong, but it is hard won, something they've given their life to. It's not cheap talk, not at all. I have some doubts about my own ability to contribute, in this regard: while my life has not been without challenges, it has been relatively easy compared to many[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn7). It's something I admire in Sagan's work: his engagement with science is deep and humane and interconnects multiple areas of science, history, and his own experience of life and empathy for other people.

## Grayling

A very interesting experiment is A. C. Grayling's "The Good Book". Grayling is a British philosopher and humanist. The idea of "The Good Book" is to be a "work of insight, wisdom, solace, and commentary on the human condition drawn from the world's great humanist traditions… an epic stimulating narrative that ultimately reveals how life – a good life – should be lived."

I admire Grayling for writing the book. But I don't much enjoy it. Line by line it seems unfelt – there seems, to me, to be a lack of connection between Grayling and his material. To use the conventional metaphor, it's a work of the mind, not of the heart. It suffers the problem I described above, being at a dry remove from its own material. It's more a collection of crossword puzzles than life and death.

Still, reading (part of) it stimulated some useful observations.

Much of the book is adapted from more than 1,000 sources – writers like Emerson, Epictetus, and Epicurus. The material is organized along lines similar to the Christian Bible, into Genesis, Songs, Parables, and so on. The style is curious. Here is the beginning of the book of Epistles:

> 1.  My dear son: you must begin by having a true estimate of our human species,
>     
> 2.  Before you can begin to formulate how you will advance yourself to being one of the better specimens of it.
>     
> 3.  That will be the purpose of these letters to you, as you take your journey among people and places,
>     
> 4.  So that you can combine your experience of them with the experience I offer you.
>     
> 5.  There are those who exalt the human species to the skies, and represent man as a paragon;
>     
> 6.  And there are those who insist on the worst of human nature, and can discover nothing except vanity and folly in man, making him no better than other animals.
>     
> 7.  A delicate sense of morals is apt to give one a disgust of the world, and to make one consider the common course of human affairs with indignation and dislike.
>     
> 8.  For my part I think that those who view mankind favorably and sympathetically do more to promote virtue than those who have a mean opinion of human nature.
>     

I agree with the sentiment. But Grayling is using deliberately archaic language, with unusual choices of meter. I doubt he thinks this way, or has much experience writing this way. And, unsurprisingly, he's not very good at it. I'd guess he's aping some kind of style. It's as though he's writing a book in a language he only knows poorly. Doing so makes it harder to think well. As an experiment, it's interesting and admirable. As a reader, it doesn't work: it creates an artificial barrier, and once you pierce that barrier it turns to be uninteresting. If you rewrite the above passage without the archaic embellishments it becomes:

> It's tempting to overly exalt or overly demonize human nature. But it helps us be better people if we view humankind favorably and sympathetically, rather than having a negative opinion.

Not entirely uninteresting, though also not especially interesting. There are vastly better (and much richer) ways of exploring such ideas.

As Susan Rigetti put it [on Twitter](https://twitter.com/susanthesquark/status/1492577755409498117), commenting on this passage: "oof this is so much fluff".

Context matters. It's a curious exercise to compare Grayling to the Sermon on the Mount. It's possible to attempt a critique of the Sermon along similar lines: that much of what it contains is platitudes. However: I cannot believe that at the time they were platitudes. Rather, they must have been radical, fresh ideas. And even today some of the ideas _still_ seem new. In the context of the Bible, the whole thing seems the product of a mind or minds extended to their uttermost limit. I find myself deeply moved by the Sermon; I'm mostly frustrated by "The Good Book". I believe Grayling needed to metabolize his sources[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn8) far more deeply, to get that same freshness.

Indeed, Sagan did this kind of metabolization. Compare "the pale blue dot" to this passage from Huygens "Cosmotheoros":

> How vast those Orbs must be, and how inconsiderable this Earth, the Theatre upon which all our mighty Designs, all our Navigations, and all our Wars are transacted, is when compared to them. A very fit consideration, and matter of Reflection, for those Kings and Princes who sacrifice the Lives of so many People, only to flatter their Ambition in being Masters of some pitiful corner of this small Spot.

I am quite certain that Sagan, a fan of Huygens, was consciously drawing on these ideas and language in writing his famous passage. On Twitter, I mentioned this and one journalist's conclusion was that Sagan "fucking plagiarized Christiaan Huygens". I agree that Sagan should have cited Huygens in endnotes as inspiration (he does not, although he does cite Huygens multiple times elsewhere in the book). But otherwise this is a small-minded conclusion[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn9). Sagan was metabolizing and reimagining ideas of fundamental importance, from great thinkers of earlier times. Pausing to obey academic citation norms within the body of the text would merely be distracting. Lincoln did not credit Euclid in the Gettysburg Address; nor should he have.

**People who believe they should like the book:** Reading the reviews, many people admire Grayling's book. I wonder if this is mostly people who believe they _should_ like the _idea of the book_? Perhaps I am simply not the intended audience. I will say this: there are many single lines which are interesting to reflect upon. But as a narrative it does not work. Grayling has not metabolized the canon of ideas he is drawing upon.

## Sketching

Just sketchin' some ideas! Noodling on overall themes, and spitballing particular ideas. Many of these might also be subthemes, or subessays in some larger work. It'd be possible to weave many of these themes together, with the right overarching question or theme.

The remainder of these notes are much more scratchy than even what is written above; you may wish to skim or skip entirely! Just me, messing around with ideas!

I certainly don't know enough to sketch a book or any other media form here. I don't have an overall narrative. And even if I did I would need to make various small essays first, to deepen my understanding. Lots of rough notes, building up to some more polished essays. And perhaps coupled with teaching – really, convening a small group of deeply thoughtful people with different backgrounds, and leading a discussion. Fun to plan out. Let me just do quick braindump here, a kind of prototype for a second pass.

**"I am become death, destroyer of worlds"** Trinity. Hiroshima. The origin in symbols: the energy-mass relation. What is the _actual_ source of energy? How marks on paper enabled us to end humanity. The Hydrogen bomb. What are those marks, anyway? How do marks on paper have power? Galileo. What is mathematics? Why are we able to understand the world? Existential threats. Toba. How should we think about them? How can we deal with them?

**Doubt and uncertainty and not knowing:** Myth and magic. Magic into science. Newton as the last of the magicians. Why is freedom of enquiry so important? Why is it opposed? The myth and reality of Bruno and Galileo. Nullius in verba. Decentralization and the idea machine. Confusion. The history of ignorance and new ways of knowing. The history of edict and infallibility. Doubt and uncertainty and not knowing as a firmer foundation.

**Becoming transhuman:** The underlying tension and project of the 21st century may be that it's the century in which humanity becomes posthuman. Technology is changing our individual and collective minds; it is changing our reproduction; it is giving us far more agency over and insight into our bodies and minds; it has given us the ability to end life on earth; it may give us the capacity to create successor minds. How should we respond to that? What does a humane transhumanism look like?

**Life of a proton:** From birth to (possible) death.

**Two cheers for technocracy:** Benefits. I, Pencil, and Hayekian hidden knowledge. Capital, truth, evolution, and ethics as (competing!) orienting forces. What technology actually does. Technopoly. Science and technology in relation to power. Why the military doesn't rule. Collective action. Is there a world possible in which we public goods abound? Perhaps merged in with the next:

**Unequal:** Inequality is a pervasive theme in history: some have, some do not. And yet it's changing rapidly at the moment. Much of this is well known (emergence of more winner-take-all dynamics, globalization, urbanization, human rights). Other parts seem less discussed (globalization of perception). Related to:

**The moral quandaries posed by increased self-knowledge:** A particularly pressing instance is behavioral genetics, which is exerting slow but inexorably increasing pressure on our notions of responsibility and fairness and justice. But there are many other problems, too. We are East Africans Plains apes, and this causes so many problems and tensions.

**Acceleration:** Living in an age of future shock. The Amish. The Sabbath. The past, present, and future being unequally distributed. The wave phenomenon (vastly disparate speeds of waves and constituent particles) has implications for human systems too. Durkheim, anomie, alienation, and the division of labor. The hikimori.

**Thoughts as emergent properties of symbol systems**

**An expanded sense of the numinous \[probably a theme\]**

**All our knowledge:** A panorama. I sometimes wonder what "The Feynman Lectures" look like if it's for "all" our fundamental knowledge? (For some value of "all"). Not an encyclopedia (which has a rather dull affect and remit), but something vastly more opinionated?

**Syncresis (theme):** James Burke did "Connections". I'm more interested in tensions, understanding and resolving them.

**Other themes and questions and values:** What is good? An emotional and cognitive experience of science. The Long News. The Long View. How we know, what we know. What is the right relationship between science and power? Superhero problems: how to deal with power? How to deal with powerlessness? What is conversation for? What's better than democracy? What do I most deeply believe? What is the relationship between awe and science? Revealing hidden beauty and hidden connections. Not shying away from the uncomfortable. Experiencing how we come to know. Providing aesthetic and emotional and intellectual experiences. Find what I have a strong emotional response to; find what I believe; and find the unusual, things where I sense I'm making some creative contribution, no matter how oddball.

## Observations on Process

**Study, don't imitate:** I'm tempted to over-index on examples I like. I certainly could not have written _Cosmos_ or _etc_. In part it's because those people have different skills than I do. But at least equally it's because their taste is very different. You need to trust and deepen your own taste as much as possible, not rely on someone else's. Indeed, in some sense _Cosmos_ (and much of Sagan's work) is a thing of my past. I still enjoy large sections, and I'm in awe of certain pieces (like pale blue dot). But I am not a spellbound 8 year old, either.

**Heroes and inspirations for this type of work:** Melodysheep; Grant Sanderson; Vi Hart; Jonathan Blow; Richard Feynman; Carl Sagan; E. O. Wilson; Bryan Moriarty; Christopher Nolan; Cirque du Soleil; Nightwish; Roger Penrose, especially "The Emperor's New Mind" (wrong, but absolutely wonderful) and "The Road to Reality"; Simone Weil; Po Bronson's "What should I do with my life?"; Rembrandt; Picasso; Technopoly; the Long Now; Stewart Brand (inc that short); Brian Eno; Meow Wolf; Sleep No More; Bret Victor; David Chapman.

**Music. Motion. Images:** Media form matters, enormously. It changes the type of experience you can have. The range of emotion; the range of understanding; the sense of immersion; the sense of agency. To some extent there are simply tradeoffs here, or tensions. But examples like Grant Sanderson and Nightwish show that the tradeoffs may be much more malleable than is commonly assumed.

**Outsider art:** There's a strange category error common here. People tend to divide stuff up into "pop science / pop writing" and "expert writing". I am not an expert on most of the things mentioned above. So by default it's easy to slot oneself in as pop science. But I have little interest in pop science, and little in exposition for its own sake. What I have a great deal of interest in is making creative contributions that are a little different than any existing discipline. You see this in _Cosmos_; indeed, it's in many of the examples I've given. They are solving genuine creative problems that the authors had, not "mere"[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/cosmos/index.html#fn10) exposition. In some sense they're all outsider art. On insider terms they may not be so good, or they may fail. But they're not solving the same problems; they're actually solving problems the insiders would never even think to solve, indeed, might not even perceive.

**Writing as the core:** I love art and music and film. I believe I have some taste for them. But I am not a creator in those mediums. This has bugged me as I consider this project. But I rewatched _Cosmos_ as an adult, and quickly realized: this is an (extremely good) university lecture. The foundation is that Sagan knows how to write about science. The other stuff – the music, the images, the staging – greatly enhances it. But _Cosmos_'s animating force is the quality of the underlying writing. It's striking to contrast to _Planet Earth_, which is marvelous, but has terrible-to-mediocre writing. In _Planet Earth_ it's the images which are the animating force. Or consider _Nightwish_, where the core is music and operatic staging. And so I must make writing the core of any project: while I'd need to improve my writing a great deal, at least I _can_ write. And, with the right collaborators, maybe it's possible to do something magical with music and film, too.

(There's always an awkward self-consciousness writing about the quality of one's writing. Let me do the obligatory throat clearing: these notes are, of course, hastily written thinking aloud, and in consequence are poorly written.)

**Writing as creating a sense of consciousness:** Edwin Schlossberg puts it well: "The skill of writing is to create a context in which other people can think." Indeed, not just other people, but also the writer. One extreme form of this idea is that the skill of writing is to create a particular consciousness in the mind of the reader. This is true of other media forms, too. Helping others see as you see; helping others experience the world in a particular way. The important question is often not "what is the book about?" but rather "what sense of consciousness does it create?" This is, in my opinion, especially true of great books (and music and film and _etc_). _Pride and Prejudice_, _Gatsby_, _Les Mis_, and so on: in each case, you enter this amazing world, and your consciousness changes. What types of consciousness can one evoke in people?
