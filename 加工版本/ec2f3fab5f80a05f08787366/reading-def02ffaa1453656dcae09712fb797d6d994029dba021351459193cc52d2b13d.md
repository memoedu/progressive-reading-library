# 这些 examples

---

## 正文

Scott Alexander 前些时候写过一篇题为 [Meditations on Moloch](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/) 的文章。据我所知，有两三篇回应，分别在[这里](http://www.patheos.com/blogs/hallq/2014/12/the-fault-dear-brutus-lies-not-in-our-incentives-but-in-ourselves/)和[这里](https://eccentric-opinion.tumblr.com/post/130764226892/anti-anti-moloch)。他在[这里](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/08/01/misperceptions-on-moloch/)回应了一些 critiques。

正如我们将看到的，这篇 essay 的 theme 是对一般 human condition 以及我们未来的 reflection。不过，Scott 太 pessimistic 了，接下来我会给他一些理由，让他相信 niceness 毕竟能够 triumph。基本上只需要一两处小改动，比如 relax 他似乎持有的 100% selfish behaviour 的 assumption，将其设为[仅 95%](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2014/12/the_evidence_of.html)。

和我正在评论的那篇文章一样，这篇也很长，所以请做好准备。不过，与它不同，这篇没有那么 metaphoric，也没那么鼓舞人心（或者说令人泄气？）。如果我也尝试那样写，篇幅可能还要翻倍，而这会违背本文的 truth/words ratio。

我读过 Scott 写的大部分内容，包括他的 tumblr 和旧博客的每一页，还有他早年在一些不知名的 internet forums 上写的 micronationalist 内容，因此我觉得自己多少能理解他看待世界的方式。通常，internet 上聪明人群体中的 received view 是：世界上的许多 problems 并不是因为人们 intrinsically evil，而是因为他们 ignorant 和/或 irrational。他们无法看到 cooperative equilibriums，或者被 emotional responses 裹挟，最终导致 war、corruption、crime 等等。

Scott 说，Moloch 比那更可怕。Assume 我们是 perfectly rational 且 intelligent 的。Moloch 指的是：看得见我们 problems 的 solutions，知道必须做什么才能 solve 它们，却因为 bad incentives 而无法做到。一个 Molochian scenario 就是一个 prisonners’ dilemma：作为一个 prisonner，你知道 cooperative equilibrium 在哪里，但无论对方做什么，你显然都会因 defecting 而 better off，于是双方都 defect，双方最终都 worse of，即使双方从一开始就预见了这一结果。我不太喜欢在这个博客里使用情绪化的语言，不过这里破个小例，说一句：在 Scott 看来，这或许显得悲伤而压抑。他通过 Ginsberg 的 Howl 第二部分（Moloch），向我们传达了这样的想法：一个丑陋的 society，在 incentives 的驱动下走向由 local optimization processes 产生的 globally inefficient equilibria。

接下来，我会采用一种通常被称为 fisking 的做法：顺着他的文章往下读，边读边评论。

那么，开始吧：

> 大地本可以公平，人人本可以快乐而明智。然而，我们拥有的却是监狱、烟囱、精神病院。

我们为什么就不能和睦相处呢？我几乎能听见这样的发问。我也认同这背后的 worldview。conservatives 相信，构成人类的曲木不可能得到合理的矫正；与他们不同，我确实认为，只要我们足够努力，progress 就是可能的。显然，如果没有人做任何事，事情就不会变好。

> 隐含的 question 是——如果所有人都憎恨当前的 system，那么是谁在 perpetuates 它？

在这一点上，我基本认同 Scott：世界上有许多 problems，其中许多是由我们 respond to incentives 的方式造成的。

> Moloch。Elua。那些 perverse incentives。这是真的。全都是真的。它们确实存在。（[Han Solo 在这里可能会说的话](https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/5hT5whUh3iyH4n9PLCkzVs3dQsFICGuxc0Q55sSjOPwtfJ9SPlU9eIJJwvgH99210ahG6BXEoD-43jo4_Ig4Ajo=s240)）

然后，他让我们想象一个 Molochian scenario：一个有两条 rules 的世界：每个人每天必须花八小时用 electric shocks 电击自己；如果有人没有 follow 一条 rule、出言反对它，或没有 enforce 它，citizens 就必须杀死那个人。每个人都 expects 这些 rules 会被遵守。于是你 assume，如果不电击自己，别人就会杀死你。其他人也作出同样的 assume。于是，每个人都想停止电击自己，却无法做到。正如他所说，这个 example 确实有些牵强，但让我们看看为什么。

在这个 system 中，你电击自己八小时，之后就可以随心所欲地做事（除了出言反对这个 system）。但我想，你有朋友、家人等等。于是有一天，和亲人吃饭时，你说：这里只有我们，没有人能听见，所以仔细听着：我觉得我们生活的这个 system 根本就是胡闹，我们应该停止互相电击。你的家人知道，你说了这话，他们就应当杀死你，但他们也认为这个 system 是胡闹。而且，因为是一家人，他们并不真的 expects 其他家庭成员互相残杀。于是，他们表示同意，并把他们的 disagreement 变成 common knowledge。这样一来，这条 rule 对这个家庭就失效了。在与其他朋友进行的一系列一对一交谈中，这个 innovative idea 在各个 groups 之间传播，直到这个 system 被 effectively abolished。

因此，在这个 example 中，Moloch 被 slayed 了。我确实遵循了 Scott 这个 example 的字面设定，却没有遵循他的本意，因为我加入了一些额外的 norms 或 traditions，比如 family、friendship，以及那些 close moral circles 中的 respect；但我认为自己可以这样做，因为这在现实生活中太常见了。

事实表明，formal game theoretical model 通常无法很好地 capture 实际存在的 social arrangements 的 nuances。

如果我们只有一个描述这个 masochist system 如何 works 的 formal model，而且 rules 只有那些，等等，那么那些 conclusions 就会 follow。这个 system 应当被 abolished，并不会成为 common knowledge。关于 common knowledge 和 cooperation，参见 \[1\]。

如此极端的情况，在现实世界中唯一 plausible 的 examples 是 religious sects 或特别 opressive 的 political regimes（比如 North Korea）；在那里，人们甚至不认为这个 system 有任何问题，因为他们掌握的 information 本身就是 controlled 的。当人们 fully aware 自己的 situation，而这种 situation 又是从外部 imposed 的时候，我们甚至可能看到 uprisings，即使一个 individual 显然并没有 rebel 的 incentives。\[2\]

## 这些 examples

Scott 接着向我们介绍了一系列 examples：

### Prisoner’s dilemma（囚徒困境）

像 Scott 这样通常很友善的人会提到 libertarians，倒是有些奇怪，除非他把 libertarians 等同于 purely selfish agents，或类似的东西。（也许是 Objectivists。）如前所述，在 PD 中，你能看出 solution，却没有 incentive 去 solve 它。不过：

-   在现实生活中，Prisoner’s Dilemma 在许多情况下实际上是被 solved 的，甚至是 _由 prisonners_ 来解决的 \[3\]，而 prediction 却是它不可能被 solved\*。\*
-   作为一个 thought experiment，你也会与你的家人或朋友 cooperate。为什么？因为你也会把他们的 preferences 纳入考虑，而且知道他们也会这样做，你也知道他们会 cooperate。在那种 situation 中，你的行为是 [superrationally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superrationality) 的。不论原因是什么，事实如此是一个 empirical fact。
-   其次，prisoner’s dilemmas 很少见。在社会生活中，你遇到的主要是 repeated games，而其中的 equilibrium，尤其对聪明人来说，就是 cooperate。这些才是重要的 Prisoner’s Dilemma（以及类似的 games，例如 Stag Hunts），只要我们足够聪明，就能相当好地 solve 它们。（当然，这并不意味着每一个都会被圆满地 resolved）\[4,5,6\]

你可以在这里主张，这些其实不是真正的 prisonner dilemmas，因为 incentive structure 并非教科书里的那一种；但这样一来，真正的 prisonner dilemmas 在世界上也就没有那么普遍，因此便无关紧要了。

### Dollar auctions（美元拍卖）

这个 case 相当 _有趣_。先看这个 [视频](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pA-SNscNADk)，看看 Moloch 的 invisible hand 是如何戏弄人的。但这样的事情通常不会发生。你只需要有一个人说：嘿！这是个 Dollar Auction game！如果你 play，你就会 loose，看看这些 rules 是如何导致这个结果的就知道了。_此处插入解释_。然后就 solved 了。

另一种赢得 auction 的方式是 precommitment：你 bid one cent，然后说：“如果其他任何人 bids，只要他的 bids less than one dollar，我就会 bid one dollar；如果他 bids more，我就会 bid 1 cent plus what the other bids。”然后你迅速 sign 一份 enforceable contract。其他人退却，你便赢得 19.99 monetary units。\[7\] 而且，正如那篇文章所说，

> 我们能从这个 formal structure 出发，generalize 到 interorganization fights 或 international escalation 吗？这种 generalization 只有在有限的范围内才有用。international negotiation 的 communication conditions 与 parlor game 有很大不同。Signals 和 quasi-commitment 都是可能的，也很常见。对 extensive form 的 game 所作的 game theory analysis 向我们表明，单靠 game theory model 似乎并不足够。

如果世界上到处都是随便什么人，试图 auction off dollars，那这会是个问题。起初会是。随后，一种认为你不应参与其中的 norm 就会形成。

### 来自 Non-Libertarian FAQ 2.0 的 fish farming 故事

剧透一下：我正在写一篇针对整份 FAQ 的 rebuttal（看到菜单里那个“即将推出.. ”标签了吗？就在那儿！）。容我说一句，这是目前对 libertarianism 最好的 critiques 之一，而且它的优点之一，就是这是一篇 _友善_ 的 critique。他没有表现出仇恨，也没有构造关于 libertarianism 的明显 strawmans。如果其他 critics 都被 Scott Alexander 的复制人替代，全职致力于寻找其中的 flaws，那么 libertarian criticism 的世界——以及 libertarianism 本身——都会好得多。顺带一提，只要他们能用自己真名的不同 anagrams 为博客取名，博客圈也会因此更加兴旺。

现在，来看这个故事。

假设有一个 lake，其中有 1000 家 separately owned fish farms，各自赚取 $1000/month each. Farms pollute the lake, reducing the profits one can make by $1/month 的 profit。问题是：由于有 1000 家 farms，就会有 $1000/month per farm, and so no one makes a profit. But they have an option: Install a filter for $300/month 的 loss。每个人都这样做，于是 profit 现在是 $700/month. Then, one fisherman shirks, stops paying for the filter. His profit is now $999，而其他人的是 $699。其他人看到 Steve 赚了大钱，于是也 disconnect 他们的 filters，一切又回到了原点。

他们试图通过 signing Filter Pact 来 solve 这个问题，但随后有个人不再 complying。于是，pact-signers 现在赚取 $699/month, and Mike is making $999/month。接着，人们开始 disconnecting 自己的 filters，因为他们想像 Mike 一样。

他最后说道：

> 一个 self-interested person 从来没有任何 incentive 去 use a filter。一个 self-interested person 有一些 incentive 去 sign a pact，让所有人都 use a filter，但在许多情况下，他有更强的 incentive，等其他所有人都 sign 这样一份 pact，自己却 opt out。这可能导致一种 undesirable equilibrium，在其中，没有人会 sign 这样的 pact。
> 
> 我越想这件事，就越觉得这是我反对 libertarianism 的核心所在，而且 Non-Libertarian FAQ 3.0 将不过是把这一个 example copy-pasted 两百遍。从 god’s-eye-view 来看，我们可以说 polluting the lake 会导致 bad consequences。从 system 内部来看，没有哪个 individual 能 prevent the lake from being polluted，而 buying a filter 也许并不是个好主意。

在再次斩杀 Moloch 之前，先对最后这几段作些评论：作为对 libertarianism 的一般性 critique，它不算糟糕，但也不算很好。据说，Free markets 是 locally 而非 globally optimising 的。因此，存在一些能让每个人获得 higher gains 的 situations（而且 agents 知道这一点），却没有达到它们。如果 gains 很微小，这未必是个问题；但如果 gains 很大，就可能是了。（例如，一颗 asteroid 正朝 Earth 飞来，人们想阻止它，却因为 discoordination 而无法 finance solutions）。但是，知道自己处于这种 situation 的 agents，难道不能 solve 它吗？

文章的转折来了：我稍后再回答这个问题，以免这么早就进入 politics。

### The Malthusian Trap（马尔萨斯陷阱）

在其 canonical version 中，Malthusian trap 是这样一种 situation：

> 在任何给定时间，technology 的 level 只允许一定数量的人依靠任何给定的一块 land 生活。carrying capacity 可以随 land 的 natural ecology 而 vary，因为有些 environments 天生就比其他 environments 更 productive。不同的 peoples 也拥有不同的 levels of technology；这里以尽可能宽泛的意义，将 technology defined 为关于 manipulation of the environment 的 stock of knowledge。当一个 people 进入新的、空无一人的 land 时，他们会 reproduce themselves，直到其 population 达到 carrying capacity——就像 caribou 或 horse flies 一样。\[8\]

虽然 Malthus 关于 societal dynamics 的 logic 在当时如此 operated 的判断是正确的，但我们在 Industrial Revolution 之后逃离了它。在这个 case 中，并不是人们明确知道发生了什么，然后着手 fix 它。而是 innovative activity 出现了 explosive growth，斩杀 Moloch 是其附带结果。如今，在 advanced economies 中，我们距离 Malthusian conditions 已经如此遥远，以至于 fertility rates 甚至 below two！这意味着，如果这一趋势持续下去，而且我们没有 defeat aging，那么几百年后就不会再有 human race 了。但 social norms 可以 change（encouraging more fertility），或者我们确实可以 defeat aging，也可以两者兼有。

至于 Scott 的 rats，如果它们完全不关心其他 rats，或者没有用于 coordinate themselves 的 internal rules，日子就会很艰难。如果它们拥有一座巨大、commonally owned 的 island，它们也许会一致同意 limiting consumption，并同意 punish defectors。反过来，如果它们拥有小块的 private property，一些 rats 就可以在自己的地盘上 impose 一项 offspring limitation policy，从而在拥挤的 island 上创造出 wellbeing 的花园。当然，前提是 rats respect rights。

有趣的是，Scott 未必需要把这看作坏事。他是个 utilitarian，而 utilitarianism endorses The Repugnant Conclusion \[9\]（某种程度上，我也是！）。但实际上，我不认为他是 _[那么彻底](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/04/08/whose-utilitarianism/)_ 的 utilitarian。我想，他只是想要一个运转良好的世界，有 art、science、很酷的 buildings、gardens、forests、nature、love、music，等等。他极有可能支持的是一个 [Shining Garden](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/06/raikoth-laws-language-and-society/)。他可能会说自己是 utilitarian，是因为尽管他发现它存在 problems，却 [没有其他 alternatives](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/12/18/why-so-many-utilitarians/)。完全公允地说，The Repugnant Conclusion 并不是说 max population=max U，而是说，如果随着 increasing population，utility 存在 decreasing returns，而且它们 turn negative，那么一个非常 high population 确实 = max U。

### Capitalism（资本主义）

想象一个 perfect competition 的世界，其中一个 capitalist 想做一件不同于 perfectly competing 的事。按照 perfect competition 的 definition，他做不到。这基本上就是 Scott 在那里所说的。他说，companies 在 sufficiently intense competition 下，被迫 abandon 除了 profit maximizing 以外的所有 values，否则它们就会被 outcompeted。他把 capitalism 比作 evolution。Fit companies 会 survive（也就是那些让 customer 想向它们购买的 companies）、expand，并激发未来的尝试，而 unfit companies 则会 die。他甚至说：

> Nature 之所以是 red and tooth and claw，与 market 之所以是 ruthless 和 exploitative，原因是一样的
> 
> 从 god’s-eye-view 来看，我们可以构想一个 friendly industry，其中每个 company 都向它的 workers 支付 living wage。但在 system 内部，却没有办法 enact 这一点。

我把这段当作一篇高度 metaphoric 的文章中的 metaphoric 段落来读，以免露出这种[表情](https://artir.files.wordpress.com/2015/12/mkbhpxfv.jpg)。

在现实世界中，也就是 Scott 描述的情形里，sweatshops 的确得到 low wages，但这些 wages 比 alternative jobs 的更高，而且还在 increasing。Moloch 在这里并没有死，但也不存在多么严重的邪恶。\[10-12\]。这并不是要完全为 sweatshops 开脱。我认为，[contra Sumner](http://www.themoneyillusion.com/?p=22188)，而且出乎意料地与 Paul Krugman 一致，如果签订的 contracts 不够 clear，就有理由对 sweatshops impose safety standards。如果一个 worker 想在 sweatshop 中冒生命危险，sweatshop owner 就应该向未来的 employee disclose relevant information。在 poorer countries，Moloch 也许正在阻止这种事发生，但在 richer countries 并没有，而且这不仅仅是因为存在 health and safety regulations：人们更关心 safety，而且已有这样的 expectations，即 workplaces 应当是 safe，除非明确说明情况相反；如果违反了这一点，就可能被视为 contract 或 law violation。在 richer countries，Courts 的运作也更好，这会 decreases enforcement costs，并 increases compliance。

那么，现实生活中的 Moloch 是存在的，但并没有那么糟。或者，也许它并不存在：因为现实生活中的 sweatshop owners 似乎并不那么关心他们的 workers，所以这不是一个 coordination problem，而只是他们的 preferences 所带来的 outcome，以及他们没有 revealing 自己掌握的、关于其 buildings 的 safety 的 knowledge。你可以说，如果他们有不同的 preferences，他们就会更友善；但他们之所以没有更友善，并不是因为 inability to coordinate。

如果我们生活在 perfect competition 的环境中，Moloch 也许确实存在。但那种环境并不存在。幸好如此！存在的是 dynamic competition。现实生活中的 Perfect competition 可能是 harmful：

> perfect competition 是一个有用的 thought experiment，可以用来与现实世界 compare，但把它看作一个 attainable goal 则是 misleading。在 2007，market 上仅有的两家 US satellite radio stations——Sirius 和 XM——想要 merge。Regulators 表示反对，理由是从两家 firms 变为一家会 reduced competition。而从 market concentration 来看，这确实是正确的。但正如 Steve Chapman 指出的，‘一家（merged）satellite radio company 的 alternative，也许不是两家 companies，而是一家也没有’。它们想要 merge 的主要原因是它们在 losing money。\[13\]

> 当我们在 1999 年末 released PayPal product 时，Elon Musk 的 X.com 正紧追着我们$$…$$我们在 PayPal 的许多人每周工作时间达到了 100-hour。这无疑是 counterproductive，但关注点并不在 objective productivity 上；关注点是 defeating X.com。$$…$$但在 Febryary 2000，比起彼此争斗，Elon 和我更害怕 rapidly inflating tech bubble：一场 financial crash 会在我们结束争斗之前就毁掉我们双方。因此，在 March 初，我们在中立地点见面，negotiated 一项 50-50 merger。在 post-merger 阶段 De-escalating rivalry 并不容易，但就问题而言，这倒是一个值得拥有的问题。作为一个 unified team，我们得以挺过 dot-com crash，随后 build 一个 successful business。\[14\]

这里的重点不在故事的细节——正如有人在 twitter 上提醒我的，那些细节可能经过了理想化——而在于有两家 companies pursued 同样的东西，而它们选择了 merged，而不是 competing。这是公开已知的事，已经足以算作对 Moloch 的反抗。

还有[这篇](https://translate.google.es/translate?sl=es&tl=en&js=y&prev=_t&hl=es&ie=UTF-8&u=http%3A%2F%2Fvozpopuli.com%2Fblogs%2F5580-juan-r-rallo-en-defensa-del-cartel-de-la-leche&edit-text=&act=url)完整的文章，讨论为什么 milk cartel 实际上可能是一件好事，其中有一句充满反讽的话：

> 异端：人们在 capitalism 内部 cooperating，而不是 competing 以求彼此消灭。

Labour unions 也是 cartels，consumer groups 等也一样。Cartels 不过是一群人，以某些 violate 了 perfect competition 的 basic presuppositions 的方式 cooperating，但总体而言，它们不一定是 inefficient 或 welfare-reducing。如果它们是这样，那么只要 market 是 contestable，其他人就会有 enter the market 的 profit opportunity。

[另一篇文章](https://translate.google.com/translate?sl=es&tl=en&js=y&prev=_t&hl=es&ie=UTF-8&u=http%3A%2F%2Fjuanramonrallo.com%2F2012%2F12%2Fla-eurocracia-no-defiende-a-los-consumidores%2F&edit-text=&act=url)也一样：同样的想法，不同的 sector：cathodic ray tubes。想象一群 CRT manufacturers。它们正处于 decline，因为人们购买的带有 CRTs 的 TVs 和 screens 更少了。为了在这个 declining demand 的环境中 survive，它们彼此 fiercely competing。从 God’s eye view 来看，最好的做法似乎是联合起来，orderly scale down production。实际上，发生的正是如此：companies formed a cartel 来做这件事。

> 那些一再轻蔑地说 markets 受 law of the jungle 及其 self-destructive tendencies 支配的人，恰恰也是那些在发现事实并非如此、markets 中也存在 limit cutthroat competition 的 scope 的 cooperative tools 时感到震惊的人。

此外，在 markets 更 freer 的地方，我们似乎并没有看到这种 cutthroat competition。这是因为 capitalism 是 regulated 吗？还是也因为 workers prefer 某些事物，而不 prefer 另一些？想象一家 company，你必须非常努力地工作，并 earn 更多 money；另有一家，你工作得更少，也 earn 更少 money。即使不考虑 decreasing returns to scale（超过某个点，它们总会出现，这就是为什么我们看不到 companies 的 size 不断 increasing \[13\]），愿意在你的 company 工作的 workers 也会被你招光。因此，要么你给其他 workers 不同的 conditions，要么你就无法 expand 那么多。另一种可能是，拥有这样的 workforce 本身就是好事。SpaceX 就是一个例子，而实际上，SpaceX 的存在似乎是一件好事，employees 也似乎热爱自己正在做的事。

Econ 101 texbooks 也许把 capitalism 描绘为 ruthless（perfect competition 如果不是如此，又是什么？），而 Adam Smith 的著名格言“这并非出于 butcher 的 benevolence……”或 Milton Friedman 的“business 的 social responsability 是 increase profits”，也无助于以不同的方式描绘它。但它并没有 ruthless 到那么糟的程度！按照 **The Soylent Green Principle of Institutional Analysis**，Capitalism 就是人，而且深嵌于 institutions、rules、governance、norms 之中。世界并非仅由 incentives 支配，还受关于世界应当如何的 beliefs 支配。而我们并不喜欢世界变成那样。

最后，capitalism（或者更准确地说，human action）与 evolution 并没有 _那么_ similar：它要好得多，好得多。[而 Scott 应该知道这一点](http://lesswrong.com/lw/l6/no_evolutions_for_corporations_or_nanodevices/)，因为他曾是一位知名的 Less Wronger。作为 metaphor，它不算糟糕，但也并不完全 accurate。Evolution 就是在字面意义上 mindless trial and error，以 spawn 更多 copies 的 ability 来 judged。corporation 是一种 consciously designed 的事物，尽管存在大量 trial and error，capitalism 用以 adapts 的 mechanisms 并不是 blind survival of the fittest，而是 human choice。Evolution 从已有的东西出发，一小步一小步地前进，耗费极长的 scales of time。世界某个地方发生的事，并不影响另一个地方发生的事。它不顾及任何东西，只是 organisms 在 outreproducing 其他 organisms。此外，organisms 会把它们的 traits pass 给 descendence。而在 capitalism 中，每个人都能向其他所有人 learn，system 可以 quickly readapt，因为其中的 actors 明白正在发生什么。如果有一颗 meteorite 正向我们飞来，evolution 不会做任何事来阻止它。但我们可以察觉到它并采取行动。如果我们 estimate 某种 resource 的 availability 将在未来 decrease，我们现在就能开始 adapting，而 evolutionary process 无法预见这一点。Evolution 始终是 backwards looking，human action 则是 forward looking。Evolution 要花 millions of years 才能给你 homo sapiens sapiens，human action 则花一些 millenia 就能弄清 universe 的秘密，[并 build reusable rockets](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1B6oiLNyKKI)。

### The two income trap（双收入陷阱）

这需要再读一整篇文章，所以我基本略过。你可以根据我在这里说的内容，试着想想我会如何回应。

### Agriculture（农业）

也许，从 local 的角度来看，agriculture 确实 locally worsen 了人们的 life conditions，而后 competitive preassures 确保了 farmers outcompeted hunter-gatherers（或者，更确切地说，是 nomads conquered farmers，并把他们变成 war machines，参见 stationary bandit model）。

但从 gods-eye-view 来看，当时应该做的好事是拥有 balanced diet，并且不 fight。而不是永远保持 hunter-gatherer 的生活！

但这似乎不是 Moloch 的 example，而是 ignorance 的 example（不知道 diet 如何 works，再加上关于 war 和 violence 的 glorified beliefs，可能还有 religion，等等。参见通常的 Pinkerian explanations，解释为什么当时如此，以及为什么如今不再如此）。这里的 competition 是为了 beat 其他 groups，因此 health 被 inadvertedly trampled over

### Arms races（军备竞赛）

这在一定程度上是对的。有两个主要的 equilibria：好的那个是没有人在 offense 上 spends。坏的那个则是每个人都把一切 spends 在 defense 上。我们既不处于前者，也不处于后者；真要说的话，我们正在走向那个好的 equilibrium。

虽然没有哪个 country 能够 unilaterally enforce 任何事情，但 countries 可以相互 check。这一点，加上所有人都不愿 going to war 的愿望，带来了[全球 nuclear stockpile 的 reduction](https://www.google.es/search?q=stockpile+nuclear+weapons&source=lnms&tbm=isch&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwijwLjr1fLJAhWGuhoKHZ4DAg4Q_AUICCgC&biw=928&bih=943)、[US spending 占 GDP 的 % 正在 going down](https://www.google.es/search?q=stockpile+nuclear+weapons&source=lnms&tbm=isch&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwijwLjr1fLJAhWGuhoKHZ4DAg4Q_AUICCgC&biw=928&bih=943#tbm=isch&q=US+military+spending+gdp&imgrc=vGHWE8Lu9RlchM%3A)，以及有些 countries [完全 without armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_without_armed_forces)也生活得很愉快这一事实。

除此之外，再加上所有那些邻近更强大 countries 的 countries：Canada、Caribbean 的所有 countries、Mexico、Pacific 的所有 countries。Canada 或 Mexico 都没有能够 stopping United State’s army 的 army，然而 United State’s 却没有 invade？为什么？因为 r[ealist theory of international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(international_relations\)) 是胡说。如今人们通常不想 go to war，politicians 也会顺应这一点。只有当你不仅假定 agents 是 purely selfish，而且还假定他们看不出 selfishness 会把自己引向何处时，Arms races 才真正 problematic。更多讨论，参见 \[22\] 的 sec. 12.3.6。另外，关于把 defense 视为 public bad 的观点，参见 \[23\]。

Arms races 是真实存在的：Cold War 就是一场规模庞大的 Arms races，但我们仍然活着。支持 escalation 的 game-theoretical argument 是 valid，但不是 sound。

### Cancer（癌症）

Cells 并不是 conscious 到 incentives 和 improving 自身 situation 的方法的 agents，所以，借用 Wolfgang Pauli 的说法，这甚至算不上 Moloch。并不是某个 cell decides to defect，而只是某些 mollecular operation 中出了 mistakes，于是 cancer 就发生了。而且，即使我比 Scott 再进一步，承认 cells aware 到自己的 situation，rational 的做法也不会是 go cancer，而是留在原处。每个 cell 都会知道自己有能力 kill 那个 organism，而如果它 dies，这个 cell 也会 dies。每个 cell 都会配备一枚 nuke，而且没有哪个 cell 会率先 fire。与 Cold War 不同，cell 甚至在 principle 上都不可能 win：payoff matrix 告诉 cell 要友善。

### “race to the bottom”（逐底竞争）

唉，要是这是真的就好了！但通常并非如此。在 country 之间和 country 内部的 levels 上，我们都没有看到这一点。如果它真那么 prevalent，世界就会是一个巨大的 Hong Kong，但事实并非如此。反过来说倒更接近 truth。为什么这个通常 benign 的 Moloch incarnation 几乎 nonexistant？原因在于 politics 不是 market。在一定程度上，companies 会尝试 shop around，寻找 looser regulation。同样，[人们也会为 courts 而 shop around](http://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2006/02/tyler_cowen_on_.html)，但我们并没有生活在 lawless 的世界里。有一些 evidence 表明，在某些 countries 的 labour market regulation 上，这确实会发生 \[15\]，但我甚至觉得这很好。Unregulated labour markets [是好的](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/12/labor_econ_vers.html)。至于 pollution control，无论在 emissions \[16\] 还是 environmental certifications (ISO 14001) \[17\] 上，都不存在 racing to the bottom，这是好事，因为 pollution 是 negative externality。

归根结底，race to the bottom 是否确实如 simple model 所 predicts 的那样 work，是一个 empirical question；该给 Moloch 记分的地方，我会给它记分。

显然，以上是更 Molochian 的 cases，

> 在继续之前，还有一种略有不同的 multi-agent trap 值得 investigating。在这种 trap 中，competition 被某种 outside force——通常是 social stigma——遏制住。因此，实际上并没有 race to the bottom——system 可以继续在 relatively high level 上 functioning——但却不可能 optimize，而且 resources 总是无缘无故地被扔掉。为了免得还没开始你就已经精疲力竭，我在这里仅举 four 个 examples。

### Education（教育）

这里我同意 Scott。Education 在很大程度上是 [signalling](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/04/educational_sig_1.html#)。不过，隧道尽头还是有一线光明：随着 online learning 的出现，以及人们真正认识到这确实是 signaling，情况或许会开始改变。如果 companies 知道这一点，仅仅出于 self-interest，它们也会减少对你来自哪所 college 的关注。Students 会更专注于自主 learning，等等。我必须承认，这是现有 examples 中最 Molochians 的 examples 之一。

### Science（科学）

我再次同意，至少对 social sciences 而言如此，因为 results 存在 inherent fuzziness，再加上一些 ideological bias。但我认为，人们大声反对这种情况，以及人们认同这些 critique，都是最近才有的事。因此，虽然我会因为这里的 coordination problems 给 Moloch 记上几分，但 Science 的 problems 也源于人们根本不知道正在发生什么。

### Government corruption（政府腐败）

> 我不知道有谁真的从 principled 的立场认为 corporate welfare 是个好主意。

Corporations。还有受它们 employed 的人。这就是下一小节所描述的 mechanism 会发生的原因。Politicians 可能认为，通过 passing corporate welfare laws，他们是在帮助 American business 相对于世界其他地方更具 competitive，或为自己的 constituents maintaining employment，或为自己日后在 corporation 里谋个舒服的职位。普通人也可能喜欢帮助 agriculture 等 [一些 crucial sectors](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2007/04/farm_subsidies.html) 的想法，不过，这其中当然牵涉大量的利益，也许不是 campaign donations，但肯定有 [votes 本身](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306919214001523)。

这不是 Moloch 的一个 case。有些 agents 确实想要 subsidies，还有些 agents 并不知道这件事发生的 scale 有多大。这个 problem 是 fixable 的吗？也许。在 Europe 和 US，farm subsidies 泛滥，在 New Zeland 则没有。对此进行 inquiry 会很有意思。

在 [这里](http://mercatus.org/sites/default/files/Mitchell-web.pdf) 阅读更多关于 cronyism 世界的内容。

### Congress（国会）

人们正在逐渐认清现实。如今，人们对自己那位 member of Congress 的 approval rating [在 US 是 49%](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/the-fix/wp/2014/08/05/a-majority-of-people-dont-like-their-own-congressman-for-the-first-time-ever/)。话虽如此，对此的 explanation 很可能是，人们更喜欢 in-groupers，而不是 out-groupers。来自你所在地区的人，比起一群你从未听说过、而且从来无法就任何事情达成一致的人的 abstract assembly，更显得 in-groupy。

Scott 又说对了，但只是在一定程度上。与前一个 case 一样，这并不完全是 Mollochian，因为有些 agents 对 Congressional favours 感兴趣，而 approval ratings 说明不了多少问题。US 离 civil revolt 还远着呢。人们甚至没有多少 incentive 去了解 Congress 里发生的许多事情，这就是这种情况发生的原因。情况本来可能更糟：Public Choice 描绘的 [图景更加黯淡](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/politics-government/why-we-vote-the-way-we-vote/)，比实际观察到的还要糟。

不过，你可能会说，应该有办法从 system 内部打破这种局面。确实有：long-term 的办法既痛苦又非常困难，那就是年复一年地 criticising cronyism，直到 politicians 将 anti-cronyism rule interiorise。并非所有 countries 都同样 corrupt，而且它似乎也没有在迅速增加。short-term、同样极其困难的 Public Choice solution，则是 ban government，使其无法做那些事情。

## III

> 一个 basic principle 将上面所有的 multipolar traps 联系在一起。在某种 optimizing for X 的 competition 中，会出现为了 improved X 而牺牲其他某种 value 的机会。抓住它的人 prosper。不抓住它的人 die out。最终，每个人的 relative status 都与以前差不多，但每个人的 absolute status 都比以前更差。这个 process 会持续下去，直到所有能够 traded off 的其他 values 都已被 traded off——换句话说，直到 human ingenuity 再也想不出任何让事情变得更糟的办法。

然而，他的 examples 并不完全是这样运作的：我认为他把不同的 problems 混在了一起，正如我们稍后会在他的 Las Vegas example 中看到的那样。

在 Prisonner’s Dilemma 或 Dollar Auction 中，被 sacrificed 的其他 values 在哪里？即使在 rat example 中，他也偏离了 classical Malthusian logic，转而谈论 rats 因 evolutionary pressures 而 sacrificing 其他 values。Malthus 讲的不过是，对更多 offspring 的 preferences 与 limited resources clash，从而形成 births=deaths equilibrium，仅此而已。capitalism 也是一样：Companies 试图 please customers，但它们也必须 please workers 和 shareholders，而且这一切都在 private property 的 framework 内进行。它们必须 strike a balance，在 satisficing 一些人与 satisficing 另一些人之间 make tradeoffs。多种 values 都得到了 served，只是 ratios 各不相同。即便 profit maximizing 是对 market 完全准确的 description，这一点也 holds：它无处不在、始终都是 _constrained_ profit maximizing。

至于 agriculture，Scott 那一段中的说法更 plausible：人们当时在为 killing each other 而 optimising，所以 hunter-gatherer 的 good life 被抛弃了，因为要赢得那场 competition，就需要 agriculture。

总的来说，在那些 examples 于现实生活中的实际情形里，我没有看到人们被迫 reduced to subsistance，而且 examples 11-14 中的 problems 也没有 _那么_ 可怕。

关于 utopia 和 incentives，

> 正如你可以看着一片 arid terrain，通过假定 water 会 obey gravity，来 determine 一条 river 将来会呈现什么 shape，你也可以看着一个 civilization，通过假定人们会 obey incentives，来 determine 它的 institutions 将来会呈现什么 shape。

这是一个有趣的 empirical claim。如果它是 true，它的最强 version 就 implies，在给定相同 incentives 的情况下，每个人都会 behave 得一样，因为如果不是这样，就存在能够 explain differences 的 non-incentive factors。而这些因素确实存在，正如 \[6\] 和 \[4-5\] 中所讨论的。你可以拿来 Finland 的 incentive structure，强行塞进 Liberia，却仍然得不到一个 Nordic 天堂。

> 但这意味着，正如 rivers 的 shapes 不是为 beauty 或 navigation 而 designed，而是 randomly determined terrain 的 artifact，institutions 也不会为 prosperity 或 justice 而 designed，而会是 randomly determined initial conditions 的 artifact。

事实上，intitutions 是可以为 justice 而 designed 的。关于人们认为什么是 just 或 fair 的 Beliefs，最终才是起支配作用的东西：它们划定 society 中什么是 permissible 的边界，而一旦这些边界遭到 violated，最终发生的就会是 civil revolts。

> 正如人们可以 level terrain 并 build canals，人们也可以 alter incentive landscape，以 build 更好的 institutions。但只有当他们被 incentivized 去这么做时，他们才能这么做，而这种情况并非总会发生。因此，一些相当狂野的 tributaries 和 rapids 会在一些非常奇怪的地方 form。

但仅仅是想要 solve 那些 problems，就可以让人们被 incentivised！相反的说法，就是假定我们只 care about 自己，而且这会反映到 institutions 中。要 solve 我们面临的许多 coordination problems，我们不需要成为彻底的 altruists，只需要有一点点 other-regarding preferences。而我们确实有这些 preferences。若非如此，就连 Hobbesian contractarianism 也无法起步。

### Las Vegas 的神秘体验

> 就是说，按照什么标准，在 North America 最不宜居的 desert 中央，并排建起巨大、高达四十层的 Venice、Paris、Rome、Egypt 和 Camelot 的室内 replicas，里面满是 albino tigers，能算是对我们 civilization 的 limited resources 哪怕稍微 sane 的 use？

人们经常这样反思，而且一般不只针对 casinos，也针对一切超出 basic needs 的东西。明知道那些钱可以用来挽救别人的生命，我却花钱吃 Apfelstrudel，这能算是对 resources 哪怕稍微 sane 的 use 吗？这基本上就是[把死去的孩子当作 currency 使用](http://www.raikoth.net/deadchild.html)背后的想法：我们作出的 choices 很重要，为自己做一些事，就意味着没有为别人做一些事，反过来也一样。

> 我忽然想到，Earth 上也许没有任何 philosophy 会 endorse Las Vegas 的 existence。即使是 Objectivism——通常我总靠这个 philosophy 来为 capitalism 的过度之处辩护——至少也以 capitalism improves 人们生活这一 belief 为 grounds。Henry Ford 是 virtuous，因为他让许多原本没有 cars 的人得以拥有 cars，从而让他们 better off。Vegas 做了什么？向一群傻瓜许诺免费给钱，却又不给他们。

积极赞美 Las Vegas，把它视为 good，是一回事；endorse 它的 destruction（或积极避免它的 construction），把这视为 right，则是另一回事。对 utilitarian 来说，这种区分不 make sense：right 就是 good，而且越多越好。但对其余的人来说，这个 difference 很重要。

即便如此，对 utilitarians 来说，拥有 Las Vegas 似乎也没有那么糟：很多人喜欢挥霍钱，一些人喜欢赢钱，还有少数人喜欢在这个过程中发财。提出反对 Las Vegas 的 utilitarian case 并不 trivial。但 Scott 直接就提出了这样的 case，而从他实际的 worldview 来看，这倒是 make sense 的：

> 于是，我们拥有的这一切惊人的 technological 和 cognitive energy，这份 human species 的才智，全都浪费在背诵由 poorly evolved cellular receptors 和 blind economics 写好的台词上，仿佛众神被一个白痴呼来喝去。

如前所述，Scott 看到 Las Vegas 时，大概把自己对 good society 的构想叠加到了 Las Vegas 上。这些人本可以运用我们的 higher faculties of reasoning，或创作 fine art，拥有 meaninful social relations，或做些别的什么，结果他们却只是在 gambling。我猜，即使 casino 里的 expected winnings 没有那么 negative，这个反应也会一样：人们会被说成是在花钱买刺激，诸如此类。

我不属于那种主张“嗨，只要大家不互相伤害，一切就都没问题”的 school of thought。在我看来，good society 的 ideal 与 Scott 的构想相当接近，值得努力去接近。

我没有过这样的体验，最接近的大概是读 Tabarrok & Beito 的 The Voluntary City \[19\]。有些人说，自从读了 Meditations on Moloch，他们看什么都能看到 perverse incentives。自从读了那本书，我看什么都能看到 cooperation，以及绕过 perverse incentives 的办法。

## IV

不过，他终于承认，事情也没有那么糟。接着，他讨论了其中的一些原因。

一个原因是 **excess resources**：如果 resources 很充足，我们就可以做些不必与所有人 compete 的事，比如 music、art、philosophy 和 love。他在这里引用了 Hanson 的一篇短文，不过那篇文章的观点并不明显，甚至没有得到恰当的论证。这些 non-adaptative 的事，这些向 biology 和 evolution 竖起中指的时刻，都是我们最具人性的一些时刻。analytic topologist 或 philosopher 在黑板上画出的每个 symbol，都是对塑造了我们的那些 forces 发出的自豪嘲笑。

假设未来不再有 technological advancement，technology 保持 constant，我们需要不断提高 fertility levels (>2)，才会回到 Malthusian scenario；但如果 fertility <2，并且假设 resources 相较现在会 increase 或 stagnate，那么未来不会有 scarcity，反而会有更多 abundance。

第二个原因是 **physical limitations**。Slaves 不会被累死，因为他们活着时更 profitable。Scott 说，Southern US 的 slavery 很奇怪，因为它是 economically inefficient。按照 Robert Fogel 的说法，并非如此；如果我们相信 Pseudoerasmus 对 literature 的解读 \[18\]，这还是 consensus opinion。不过，他对实际情况的解读是对的：Overworked、mistreated 的 slaves，不如那些待遇稍好一点的 slaves 那么能干。想想 Slaffer’s Curve：不 work 的 Slaves 不 produce，而被迫以 100% work 的 slaves 会 exhaust，也不 produce，所以你必须让他们以中间某一点的强度 work。或者，人们可以认为 slavery 是 wrong，因而彻底不再实行它，即使它是 economically efficient。

第三个原因是 utility maximisation

> 但是，modern civilization 中许多最重要的 competitions / optimization processes，都是在 optimizing for human values。你在 capitalism 中 win，部分靠 satisfying customers’ values。你在 democracy 中 win，部分靠 satisfying voters’ values。

确实如此。我在前面各节所做的，一部分就是把这一段的 logic 代入所描述的 examples。

关于他那个 Ethiopians 使用 pesticides 的 example，coffee growers 知道 Americans 不想要那样的东西。他们也许能蒙混过关一段时间，直到某个 independent lab、government 或 consumers 发现这种 pesticide 及其未公开的 toxicity，并 sue 这些 farmers。Ideally，这应当算作 contract violation（人们买的是“coffee”，而“coffee”意味着一组不包含 toxic pesticides 的 expectations），并应向 farmers 施加 costs。如果 farmers 的 temporal preference 足够 low，他们就会看出，使用这种 pesticide 对自己并不好。实际上，由于 time preference 的 differences，以及 cross-nationally suing 贫困 farmers 的困难，这会很难做到。但在 advanced nations，这些 problems 很容易 dealt with。

关于 baby boom，一切都取决于人们愿意忍受什么。最终可能是一些 companies 让人 overworking，也可能是更多一些 companies，在相同 employment level、也许相同 wages 的情况下，提供更 reasonable 的 wage。

有了 robot coffee picker，Ethiopians 可以去做别的事；或者，人们就必须把 employment 视为 end in itself，通过购买保证是 picked by hand 的“Fair Employment” coffee 来体现这一点。不过，无论 economically 还是 morally，他们去从事其他 activity，而我们购买 robot coffee，大概都更好。但如果我们真的关心这些 problems，如果我们足够 valued 他们的 employment，我们就会通过那种 Fair Employment coffee 来 solve 它。

> 或者，假设有某种重要的 value，既不是 employees 的 value，也不是 customers 的 value。也许 coffee plantations 位于某种 rare tropical bird 的 habitat，而 environmentalist groups 想要 protect 它。也许 plantations 位于某个 tribe 的 ancestral burial ground，而 plantation 雇用的是另一个 tribe 的人，前一个 tribe 希望这片地方得到某种尊重。也许 coffee growing 以某种方式 contributes to global warming。只要这种 value 不会阻止 average American 向他们购买，或阻止 average Ethiopian 为他们工作，它就会被牺牲掉。

我们之所以有 property 和 laws，就是为了这个！有了它们，一些人 valued 的东西就不会进入其他人的 action plans，而且 externalities 被要求必须 internalised。在前面一节，我讨论了 city pollution，以及那个 Moloch 如何被杀死。关于 global warming，人们尚未就该怎么办达成全面 agreement，但 technological advancement（solar power、更 efficient 的 turbines，以及但愿能实现的 fusion energy）似乎正在走向 solve 它。Farmers 即使知道自己对 GW 的 contribution，也知道自己 contribute 的只是一点点。较大的 industries 更清楚这一点，而且更容易 monitor 它们，因此，通过这种方式更容易 solved 这个 problem。

> 我知道，“capitalists 有时会做坏事”算不上什么新鲜 talking point。但我确实想强调，这并不 equivalent to“capitalists 是 greedy”。我是说，他们有时_确实是_ greedy。但另一些时候，他们只是身处 sufficiently intense competition，任何不那么做的人都会被 outcompeted，并被那些那么做的人 replaced。Business practices 是由 Moloch set 的，其他任何人在这件事上都没有 choice。

如果 market 是一个 unconstrained profit maximisation process，那或许是真的。但它不是：markets 是人，而人有 values。Business practices 不是由 Moloch set 的，而是由人 set 的。人们在不同 values 之间作出 tradeoffs，而且有些 equilibriums 就是 socially forbidden 的。

> 而且，尽管 capitalist example 已经被充分理解，我觉得人们还没有同样充分地认识到，democracy 也有相同的 problems。是的，理论上它在 optimizing for voter happiness，而 voter happiness 与 good policymaking 存在 correlates with 的关系。但只要 good policymaking 与 electability 之间出现哪怕最轻微的 disconnect，good policymaking 就_必须_被牺牲掉。

理论上，democracy 并不 optimize for voter happiness。Democracy 只是 implements 掌权的那批 politicians 想 implement 的任何东西。而这些 politicians 又是根据投票给他们的人们的 preferences 被 elected 的。但人们可能投票支持违背自身 happiness 的 policies，或更广泛地说，违背其 meaningful project pursuing 的 policies。有时，甚至很多时候，违背人们投票支持的东西，最终反而是 beneficial 的。有些人 argue，democracy 之所以 works，恰恰是因为它与 people desires 并没有那么 tightly coupled，[而且 elite preferences 被 overrepresented 了](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2012/09/why_is_democrac.html)。而 elites 比其余的人 better informed，也没那么疯狂。electability 与 good policymaking 之间当然存在 disconnects：并非每个 country 的 economic policy 看起来都像 economists designed 的，但总体而言，它看起来也不像 average citizen designed 的。

> 例如，不断延长的 prison terms 对 inmates 是 unfair 的，对必须为其付出代价的 society 也是 unfair 的。Politicans 不愿对此采取任何行动，因为他们不想显得“soft on crime”；而他们曾帮助 release 的 inmate，只要有一个人做了坏事（而 statistically，其中一个人必然会如此），广播电视上就会到处都是“因 Congressman 的 policies 而获释的 Convict 杀害一家五口，这位 Congressman 夜里怎么还能睡得着，更别提声称自己值得 reelection 了？”所以，即使 decreasing prison populations 是 good policy——而它确实是——也会非常难以 implement。

我同意这一点。我不认为，人们因为不知道自己做的某件事是 wrong，就理应遭受痛苦。所以这对 inmates 是 unfair 的。人们[确实想](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/wonk/wp/2015/10/01/heres-how-much-americans-hate-mandatory-minimum-sentences/)在 US abolish mandatory minimum sentences，如果这在几年后发生，我不会感到惊讶。

不过，我不会说这些 outcomes 是 utility maximization 的 result。它们是 respecting 人们的 rights、关心他人的 result，而且是在受此 contrained 的前提下，pursue 你自己的 conception of the good。在许多情况下，这会涉及赚钱；而其中相当多的情况下，又会涉及试图比其他人赚更多的钱。

关于 **coordination**，Scott 讨论了 garden 这个 concept：所有人在 single authority 之下联合起来，由它确保好的 cooperative situations 得以发生。Scott 说，Governments 就是一个这样的 example。Governments 可以终结 pollution、prisoner dilemmas、workers 受到伤害、arms races，而 World Government 将能够为整个世界 solve 这些问题。

Scott 将 governments 描述为 rule patterned violence：agreements 加上 enforcement。但他提醒我们，不同于 governments 的事物也会这样做：teachers 通过制定反对 cheating 的 rules，像 governments 那样行事，limiting students 在 competing for grades 时可以采取的 actions。students 之间的 Norms 也可以起到 government 的作用：rules（不要 cheat）和 punishment（否则我们就会 shun 你）。

> Social codes、gentlemens’ agreements、industrial guilds、criminal organizations、traditions、friendships、schools、corporations 和 religions 都是 coordinating institutions，通过 changing 我们的 incentives，让我们避开 traps。

第一段很好地列出了为我们无情斩杀 Molochs 的各路军团。第二段提醒我们，那些 institutions 仍然可能成为 Moloch 的猎物，包括 governments。Governments 是人，它们的行为也正是人的行为。

> 但这样一来，我们就不再遵循 random incentive structure，而是在遵循某一个人的任性。Caesar’s Palace Hotel and Casino 是对 resources 的疯狂浪费，但真正的 Gaius Julius Caesar Augustus Germanicus 也算不上那个 perfect benevolent rational central planner。

monarchy 与 Scott 上面描述的某个 organisations（companies、associations……）并没有 qualitatively different 之处。区别在于，它们通常规模很大。但设想一个 small monarchy，旁边有一个 taxes 和 regulations 都非常低的 country。¿这个 monarchy 能避免通过 lowering taxes 和 regulations 来 attract capital 吗？如果 monarch 只在乎 money，她就会 lower 它们。如果 monarch 在乎让她的 garden 保持原样，她就不会。但任何其他 organisation 也是如此。

> Political Compass 上的 libertarian-authoritarian axis，是 discoordination 与 tyranny 之间的 tradeoff。你可以让一个拥有 god’s-eye-view 的人，把一切都 perfectly coordinated——但这样你就要冒出现 Stalin 的风险。你也可以完全摆脱所有 central authority——但这样你就会困在 Moloch 能设计出的每一个愚蠢的 multipolar trap 里。
> 
> libertarians 为其中一方提出了有说服力的 argument，neoreactionaries 则为另一方提出了有说服力的 argument，但我想，[和大多数 tradeoffs 一样](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/01/searching-for-one-sided-tradeoffs/)，我们只能捏着鼻子承认，这确实是一个难题。

Freedom 加上 uncoordination，或者 coordination 但伴随着 opression 的风险。这在一定程度上是对的。但更准确地说，是 freedom 加上 uncoordination 的风险，或者 coordination 但伴随着 opression 的风险。Scott 认可 NRx 的 theory 在理想情况下会带来 coordination 和 niceness，只是在 practise 中未必如此。但对于 libertarians，他却不认可其 theory：即使在 theory 上也无法 achieved coordination，更不用说在 practise 中了。我稍后会再谈这一点。

给定 Scott 的 premises，他 reaches 了 right conclusion：要 stop competitive processes，你就需要把一切置于 single power 的 control 之下，并祈祷这个 power 是 good。Elua 会 meet 这两项 requirements。问题在于——这或许会让 Scott 满心绝望——要 design 并 implement 一个能把工作做好 Elua，似乎都很困难。因此，我想，Scott 和 Meditations of Moloch 的许多读者就落入了与 existentialists 相同的处境：我们终将死去，世界是 absurd，而且无处可逃。幸运的是，这些 premises 是 wrong。

在这篇文章中，我已经指出了一些办法，说明事情未必如此。在这最后一节，我会进一步 develop 这些想法，并 argue：Scott 对 Moloch 的担忧，源于无意中把一系列 problems mixing together，使它们显得比实际更严重。

## 将 Spotting 和 annihilating Molochs 作为一种 social activity

### Molochs 的 Pantheon（万神殿）

Moloch 这个 metaphor _究竟_ refer to 什么？

1.  指 ignorance，以及无法 conceive 那种能让人 benefits more than otherwise 的 cooperative equilibriums 的 inhability
2.  指 irrationality，指 choosing suboptimal means 来 achieve goals
3.  指 perverse incentives：遵循 ‘rational’ decisions，却 leading to magnitude 各异的 aggregate problems
4.  指我们 biology 的 imperfections
5.  上述内容的 combination

Prisonner dilemma 是 3 的一个 case（或者是 2，取决于你对 rationality 的 conception）

dollar auction 是 1 的一个 case：并不 ex ante 知道那个 game 会 leads to 什么。democracy 的一些 problems 也是由此造成的。

Las Vegas 这个 example 主要属于 4。在 casino 中 play 是 irrational 的吗？未必，如果你在意的东西不同于实际 winning。它是 good life 的一部分吗？在我和 Scott 的 worldviews 中，大概不是。

这些 Malthusian cases 是 3-4 的 combination。

那么，Scott described 的 problems 可以 sorted out 到两大类中：

1.  有人知道如何 solve 的 Problems
2.  我们不知道如何 solve 的 Problems

em world 和 SAI 属于这 second case，而 Scott 所说的大多数内容属于 1st case。Scott 先从这里那里对 mini Molochs 的零星 observations 开始 building his case，然后全面展开，将那些 type 1 problems connect 到 type 2 problems。

如果你脑子里同时装着许多东西，problems 就会显得比实际更严重。这里有一首诗、一幅 Las Vegas 的景象、irrationality、ignorance、incentives、实际存在的 problems，以及 potential problems。围绕 Moloch 的这整件事和纷扰，都可以 cleared up，并 reduced to ems 和 SAI 的 problem。正如前面所说：Moloch 现在没什么大不了。我不会在这里 solve 这个 em-SAI problem disjunction，至少要等我读完 Hanson 关于这个话题的书再说。目前只需说，在那些 problems 袭来之前，我们相当安全。如果它们永远不来，看来我们就会永远安全。

### Moloch 的 Politics（政治）

促使我写下这篇长得离谱的文章的，是 [Devon Marisa Zuegel 在 twitter 上的一条评论](https://twitter.com/devonzuegel/status/679010843481083905)（按我的标准而言，我不赞成篇幅过长的文章）。虽然 Moloch 无处不在，而且 Scott 的文章并不以 politics 为 main theme，但我们可以对它作一种 political reading：我们需要 government 来遏制 Moloch。Individual self-interest 和 competition 不会 slay 它。因此，可以说，Moloch 的 reality 为 libertarianism 提出了一个 problem。

有时有人说，libertarian 就是那种只学过半个学期 economics 的人，学到的内容解释了 markets 的 niceness，却跳过了描述 market failures 的第二个学期。我要更进一步说，大多数 economists 的学习也就到此为止了。进一步学习会把你带到 Public Choice，在那里你会了解到：即便 market fails，即便在 theory 中 governments 能够 correct market failures，在 practise 中它们也未必会这样做；而且在 plausible assumptions 下，让 market 自行运作可能更好。最后，第四步是意识到，我们可以在某种程度上 escape incentives，并狠狠戏弄那些天真的 economic textbooks。我们能够察觉自己处在 lose-lose situations 中，并 devise institutions 来 solve 它们，从而 changing 我们的 incentives。我们可以联合起来 solve 一个 problem。我们可以 sign contracts 来 credibly bind ourselves。Scott 把许多这样的东西称为 ‘government’。但这过于 simplistic。还记得我转述过的 Scott 的说法吗：

> Scott 将 governments characterises 为 rule patterned violence：agreements 加上 enforcement。但他提醒我们，governments 以外的东西也会这样做：teachers 通过制定反对 cheating 的 rules，limiting student 在 competing for grades 时可以采取的 actions，来像 governments 那样行事。students 之间的 Norms 也能像 government 那样起作用：rules（不要 cheat）和 punishment（否则我们会排斥你）。

Governments 不是 rule patterned violence。不仅仅是它们不止于此，而是它们根本就是另一种东西。Governments 是 control States 的 institutions：这些 sovereign entities 的 legitimacy 得到了其所 rules over 的 people 中相当一部分人的 recognition。因此，它拥有 political authority，即使大多数 philosophers 都说这种东西是 undefensible \[24\]。我们要找的是 Governance，而这是 governments 能做、并且几乎总会做的一件事。我与你 signing a contract 是 governance，请一位 trustworthy third part 来 punish 一个 defector 也是 governance。不是 government。Government 更像是 omnicomprensive、large 且 unchallengeable 的。Governance 则更 specific、small 且 polycentric。在这些 extremes 之间，有一系列 possibilities。如今，众所周知，即使没有 government，也可以有 effective rules \[25-29\]，哪怕是为了 form pirate bands 或 prison gangs。请记住，cooperation 的 bounds 是由关于什么是 permissible 的 beliefs 所 set 的，所以并非所有 cooperation 都是 nice。即使在人们本该相互对抗的环境中，你也能看到这一点，比如 online games，例如 EVE Online \[30-31\]。我们能否将这一点推广到整个世界，让没有 governments 的 extensive cooperation 真正存在？也许可以 \[32\]

Minimal-state libertarianism 要容易 defend 得多。Economic arguments 即使 overlooking 我们的 cooperative skills，也能让你 justify 一个规模高达 GDP 的 30-35% 的 government。\[33\]。那并不是 minimal government，但大约处于 Switzerland 的水平，而 Switzerland 正是 libertarians 通常提到的 model countries 之一。其他 meet 这一 target 的 examples 是 Hong Kong 和 Singapore。而且，这还包括了 assist the poor 的需要！如果你对 markets 更 optimistic，就可以将其降至 GDP 的 5%，正如最近这里的一篇西班牙语文章所 defended 的那样 \[34\]。

我们可以就这些 arguments 展开争论。但说 libertarianism 在 theory 中不起作用，就是不符合事实的。在 Empirically 的层面上，它或许不起作用。给定关于 human being 是什么样的 realistic assumptions，在 theory 中，Market failures 可以在 market 本身内部得到 solved。这些 assumptions 并不包括 pure selfishness 或 perfect competition。

最后，如果还剩下一些 coordination failures，它们可以由 minimal-State government 来 solved，也可以留在那里，在非常长远的未来，单靠 pure cooperation 来 solved。

### 对 Non-Libertarian FAQ 3.0 的快速 debunking，或者说，帮助一些 fishermen

让我们回到 fishermen example，终于来 solve 它。

于是，我们有这 1000 家 firms，每一家都 causing 一个 -1$ loss to others, from an initial gain of $1000，所以它们的收益都是 zero。fishermen 聚在一起，说明自己的处境。他们同意 signing 一份 conditional contract，与在 Kickstarter 上能做的事情没有太大区别：每个人都会 pay 300$ for a filter, so that everyone makes \$700 一个月。这份 contract 只有在所有人都 signs 它时才会是 binding，而他们会到 court 去，在那里 sign 它，使它成为 binding，并且能够由 external agency 来 enforceable。现在，他们已经 changed 自己的 incentives：他们可以选择什么也不做（payoff=0），或者 sign（expected payoff=700）。所以他们 sign 了。一旦他们去过 court，就可以选择 cooperate（expected payoff=700），或者 shirk。但现在，他们的 payoff 不再是 999，而是 paying a fine，直到他们 cooperate，因此现在 cooperate 就是 rational 的了。解决了。

但你用了 court！那又怎样？Libertonia 里有 courts，你原本以为会怎样？Third-party enforceability 是一种有价值的 service。

但如果有个固执的 fisherman，要求其他人 pay 他，他才肯真正 sign 这份 contract，那又怎么办？这里我们就碰到了这样一个问题：fishermen 最初究竟是否拥有 right to pollute the water。\[35\]。

那么，suppose fishermen 提出了这个问题。他们中的大多数 argue，既然 lake 是一种 common resource，而且让它保持 clean 符合他们所有人的 interest，他们就应该把它视为 common property，在其中可以 imposed rules，以确保 inner coordination。那个 hypothetical free rider 可以 argue，既然他是第一个来到 river 的人，他就拥有 right to pollute，其他人应该 pay 他。这可能是某种 Coasean bargain。在这种情况下，如果他要求其他每个人向他支付 payment P，他的收益就会是 700+999P。其他人的收益则会是 700-P。在 cooperative case 中，total earnings 是 700_1000=0.7 megadollars。在 noncooperative case 中，700+999P+700_999-999P=0.7 megadollars，amount 相同，distribution 不同。这里的 P 会 depend 于多种 factors，但 total gains 是相同的。court system 将不得不处理这样的 cases，并且 resolve disputes、set precedents，努力在 respecting rights 的同时确保 good outcomes。这不是一项 trivial task，但也不是一项 impossible 的任务。

如果这就是 Scott 对 libertarianism 的 core objection，我想他现在会离它更近一步。而且，他本来就已经非常接近了，甚至可能早已认同它！

## 结论（Conclusion）

这篇 essay 的 point 一直是：Moloch problem 被高估了，而我们能够克服它。今天活着的 Molochs，就是明天 History books 中死去的 Molochs。还剩下一些似乎难以杀死的“What-if” Molochs：Em World 和一个 ungood Superintelligence。在它们于未来到来之前，我们基本上不会受到 Molochlike threats 的威胁。Scott 为什么 overestimated 了这种 threat？在我看来，这是因为他对 markets 如何 work，以及人们如何 behave，持有一种尤为狭隘的看法。

至于 political systems，Molochlike problems 对 democracy 构成的 dangers，要比对 capitalism 更大。Capitalism 既允许小规模、step-by-step solutions，也允许大规模的 solutions；只要在 property rights 的 limits 之内，即使其他人不同意，也能实施。sovereignty 的 logic 禁止了这一点。它使得人们很难 experiment 不同的 political organisations，而这些 organisations 或许有助于 solve Scott 所 describes 的 problems。在 market 之内，gardens 可以被 built，并长久存在。对我而言，Elua 与其说是一个 benevolent Superintelligence，不如说是一个 polycentrically governed world，再加上我们这边的一些 altruism。虽然两者都很困难，我的 solution 似乎稍微容易一点。

他的 speculations 关注的是 AI 如何能够 solve 我们的 problems。我的则涉及我们这些实实在在的 humans，如何从我们生活的 system 内部 crafting institutions 和 rules，以做到这一点。我们拥有用于 cooperation 的 tools，却没有任何保证能确保我们足够明智地使用它们。至于 Scott 的 plan，它涉及两个 hard problems 的 solution：Building a SAI，然后 making it nice。

从某种意义上说，这个 problem 的 solution 就是大规模的 monarchy：让我们所有人都成为 kings，并以彼此 recognise 的 rights 筑起 walls。但与此同时，我们不会把这道 wall 筑得 infinitely high。在某些 situations 下，violate 它们也许并不是坏事。可能就是它们的 definition 不太清楚的那些 cases，比如 fishermen case，或者 benefit/cost ratio 确实很高的 cases。Ideally，我们最终会拥有一个遍布 institutions 的世界，这些 institutions 涵盖了容易出现此类 problems 的 resources。让 lakes 或 rivers 成为其 users 的 common resources，而 atmosphere 则可以被视为一个庞大的 common property。Streets、buildings 和 cities 也可以用同样的方式处理。我向你保证，我并没有淡化这件事的困难。

我们 necessarily 会 cooperate 吗？不会。我们在 theory 上能做到吗？能。我们很可能会这样做吗？如果我们能够 envision 并 implement adequate rules 和 institutions，而且我们关心他人，那么，是的，我们会。

Scott 已经描述了我们的敌人。我描述了我们，以及我们拥有的武器。现在，去继续努力让事情变得更好吧！Moloch 可不会自己杀死自己！

## 原文

Scott Alexander wrote some time ago a piece titled [Meditations on Moloch](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/). As far as I know, there are two or three replies to it, [here](http://www.patheos.com/blogs/hallq/2014/12/the-fault-dear-brutus-lies-not-in-our-incentives-but-in-ourselves/) and [here](https://eccentric-opinion.tumblr.com/post/130764226892/anti-anti-moloch). He replied to some critiques [here](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/08/01/misperceptions-on-moloch/).

The theme of the essay, as we will see, is a reflection on the general human condition, and on our future. Still, Scott is too pessimistic, and I’ll proceed to give him reasons to believe that niceness, after all, can triumph. It basically takes one or two little things, like to relax his apparent assumption of 100% selfish behaviour and set it [at just 95%](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2014/12/the_evidence_of.html).

Like the post I’m commenting, this one is pretty long, so brace yourself. Unlike it, though, this is not as metaphoric, or (un?)inspiring. It could be twice that long if I tried to do that, and that would be against the truth/words ratio of this post.

Having read most of what Scott has written, including every single page of his tumblr and his old blog, plus some of his older micronationalist stuff in some random internet forums, I think I can undertand a bit the way he sees the world. Usually, the received view among the smart side of the internet is that the many problems of the world are not due to people being intrinsically evil, but ignorant and/or irrational. They aren’t able to see cooperative equilibriums, or get dragged along by emotional responses that end up leading to war, corruption, crime, and so on.

Moloch is more fearsome than that, says Scott. Assume we were perfectly rational and intelligent. Moloch is seeing the solutions to our problems, to know what we would have to do to solve them, but not being able to due to bad incentives. A Molochian scenario is a prisonners’ dilemma: As a prisonner, you know where the cooperative equilibrium lies, but whatever the other does, you’re apparently better off defecting, and so both defect, and both end worse of, even when both saw this coming from the beginning. Not being much of a fan of emotional language occurring in this blog, I’ll make a little exception and say that, to Scott, this may look sad, and depressing. Via Ginsberg’s Howl, second Part (Moloch), he conveys us that idea, an ugly society driven by incentives to globally inefficient equilibria that arise from local optimization processes.

In what follows, I will do what is popularly known as fisking: I will be going down his article and commenting as I go.

So here it goes:

> Earth could be fair, and all men glad and wise. Instead we have prisons, smokestacks, asylums.

Why can’t we just get along? I can almost hear there. And I agree with the worldview behind that. Unlike conservatives, who believe in the impossibility of a reasonable straightening of the crooked timber out of which humanity is made, I do think that progress is possible, if we try hard enough. Things will not get better if no one does anything, obviously.

> The implicit question is – if everyone hates the current system, who perpetuates it?

Here is where I basically agree with Scott: There are many problems in the world, and many of them are caused by the way we respond to incentives.

> The Moloch. Elua. The perverse incentives. It’s true. All of it. They’re real. ([Something Han Solo could have said here](https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/5hT5whUh3iyH4n9PLCkzVs3dQsFICGuxc0Q55sSjOPwtfJ9SPlU9eIJJwvgH99210ahG6BXEoD-43jo4_Ig4Ajo=s240))

He then asks us to imagine one Molochian scenario: A world with two rules: Every person must spend eight hours a day shocking themselves with electric shocks, and if someone fails to follow a rule, speak against it, or does not enforce it, citizens must kill that person. Everyone expects the rules to be followed. You then, assume, that if you don’t shock yourself, others will kill you. Others assume the same. And so everyone wants to stop shocking themselves, but fail to do so. This example is kind of contrived, as he says, certainly, but let’s see why.

In the system, you shock yourself eight hours, but then you’re free to do as you please (Save for speaking against the system). But, I assume, you have friends, family, and so on. So one day, when having dinner with your relatives, you say: We are alone here, no one can hear us, so listen well: I think the system we are living in is fundamentally nonsense, and we should stop shocking each other. Your family knows that they are expected to kill you for having said that, but they also think that the system is nonsense. And, being family, they don’t really expect other family members to kill each other. So they agree, and make common knowledge their disagreement. The rule is thus defused for the family. On a series of ony-by-one talks with other friends, this innovative idea spreads along groups until the system is effectively abolished.

Moloch is thus slayed for this example. I did follow the letter of Scott’s example, but not his spirit, as I plugged in some additional norms or traditions, such as family, friendship, and respect in those close moral circles, but I think I’m allowed to do that, since it’s so commonplace in real life.

It turns out formal game theoretical model usually don’t capture that well the nuances of actually existing social arrangements.

If we just had a formal model of how the masochist system works, and the rules were only those, and so on, then the conclusions would follow. It wouldn’t be common knowledge that the system ought to be abolished. On common knowledge and cooperation, see \[1\].

The only plausible real world examples of something as extreme as this would be religious sects or particularly opressive political regimes (Like North Korea), where people don’t even think there’s anything wrong with the system, as the information they have is itself controlled. When people are fully aware of their situation, and it is imposed from the outside, we may even get uprisings, even when an individual does not have, apparently, incentives to rebel. \[2\]

## The examples

Scott then presents us with a series of examples:

### Prisoner’s dilemma

The reference to libertarians is odd coming from someone as generally nice as Scott, unless he equates libertarians with purely selfish agents, or something like that. (Objectivists, maybe). As said before, in the PD, you can see the solution, but have no incentive to solve it. However:

-   The Prisoner’s Dilemma is actually solved in real life, even _by prisonners_ \[3\], in many cases, while the prediction is that it cannot be solved\*.\*
-   As a thought experiment, you would also cooperate with your family or friends. Why? Because you take their preferences into consideration too, and know they also do, and you know they will cooperate. You behave [superrationally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superrationality) in that situation. Whatever the reason, it is an empirical fact that it is like that.
-   Next, prisoner’s dilemmas are rare. Socially, you mostly have repeated games, and the equilibrium there, especially for smart people, is to cooperate. Those are the Prisoner’s Dilemma (And similar games like Stag Hunts) that matter, and we can solve them pretty well, if we are smart enough. (This does not mean that every one will be resolved nicely, of course) \[4,5,6\]

You could argue here that either these are not real prisonner dilemmas, as the incentive structure is not the textbook one, but then, real prisonner dilemmas aren’t that prevalent in the world, so they would be irrelevant.

### Dollar auctions

This case is quite _funny_. Watch this [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pA-SNscNADk) first to see the invisible hand of Moloch trolling people. But things like that don’t usually happen. You just need one guy to say: Hey! This is a Dollar Auction game! If you play, you’ll loose, just see how the rules lead to that. _Insert explanation_. And solved.

Another way to win the auction is precommitment: You bid one cent and say “If anyone else bids, I will bid one dollar if he bids less than one dollar, or 1 cent plus what the other bids, if he bids more”. You then swiftly sign an enforceable contract. Others back off, and you win 19.99 monetary units. \[7\] And as said in the article,

> Can we generalize from this formal structure to interorganization fights or international escalation? Only in a limited manner is the generalization useful. The international negotiation has communication conditions considerably different from the parlor game. Signals and quasi-commitment are possible and common. The game theory analysis of the game in extensive form shows us that the game theory model alone does not appear to be adequate.

This would be a problem if the world were full of random people trying to auction off dollars. Initially. Then a norm would develop that you should not participate in this.

### The fish farming story from the Non-Libertarian FAQ 2.0

Spoiler: I’m writing a rebuttal of the whole FAQ (See that “Soon.. ” tab in the menu? There!). Let me say that this is one of the best critiques of libertarianism that are around, and counts among its virtues that it’s a _nice_ critique. He doesn’t show hate, or constructs blatant strawmans of libertarianism. The world of libertarian criticism -and libertarianism itself- would be much better if the other critics were replaced with copies of Scott Alexander, dedicated full time to find flaws in it. As a side effect, the world of blogging would also receive a boost, as long as they can find different anagrams of their real names to form names for their blogs.

Now, to the story.

Let there be a lake with 1000 separately owned fish farms, earning a profit of $1000/month each. Farms pollute the lake, reducing the profits one can make by $1/month. Problem: Since there are 1000 farms, there’s a loss of $1000/month per farm, and so no one makes a profit. But they have an option: Install a filter for $300/month. Everyone does, and the profit is now $700/month. Then, one fisherman shirks, stops paying for the filter. His profit is now $999, while the others is $699. Others see Steve is making loads of money, so they disconnect their filters and back to square one.

They try to solve this by signing the Filter Pact, but then a guy stops complying. So the pact-signers are now making $699/month, and Mike is making $999/month. Then, people start disconnecting their filters because they want to be like Mike.

He then finishes:

> A self-interested person never has any incentive to use a filter. A self-interested person has some incentive to sign a pact to make everyone use a filter, but in many cases has a stronger incentive to wait for everyone else to sign such a pact but opt out himself. This can lead to an undesirable equilibrium in which no one will sign such a pact.
> 
> The more I think about it, the more I feel like this is the core of my objection to libertarianism, and that Non-Libertarian FAQ 3.0 will just be this one example copy-pasted two hundred times. From a god’s-eye-view, we can say that polluting the lake leads to bad consequences. From within the system, no individual can prevent the lake from being polluted, and buying a filter might not be such a good idea.

Before slaying Moloch once more, some comments on these final paragraphs: As a general critique of libertarianism it’s not terrible, but it’s not very good either. Free markets, it is said, are locally but not globally optimising. Thus there are situations of higher gains for everyone (and the agents know this), yet they are not reached. This need not be a problem if the gains are trivial, buy may be if they are huge. (e.g. an asteroid is coming towards the Earth, people want to stop it, but can’t finance solutions because of discoordination). But can’t agents who know they are in this situation solve it?

### The Malthusian Trap

The Malthusian trap, in its canonical version, is a situation in which

> the level of technology at any given time permitted only a certain number of people to live off any given piece of land. The carrying capacity could vary according to the natural ecology of the land, because some environments are naturally more productive than others. Different peoples also possessed different levels of technology, defined in the widest possible sense as the stock of knowledge about the manipulation of the environment. When a people entered new, empty land, they would reproduce themselves until their population hit the carrying capacity — just like caribou or horse flies. \[8\]

While Malthus was right that the logic of societal dynamics operated like that back then, we escaped it after the Industrial Revolution. In this case, it wasn’t that people explicitly knew what was going on and then went on to fix it. There was an explosive growth in innovative activity, that had the slaying of the Moloch as a side effect. Nowadays, in advanced economies, we are so far from Malthusian conditions that fertility rates are even below two! Meaning that if that continues, and if we don’t defeat aging, there will be no human race left in some hundred years. But social norms can change (encouraging more fertility), or we can actually defeat aging, or both.

For Scott’s rats, they have things hard if they don’t care at all about others, or don’t have internal rules to coordinate themselves. If they have a huge, commonally owned island, they may all agree in limiting consumption, and agree to punish defectors. If on the contrary they have small plots of private property, some rats can impose an offspring limitation policy in theirs, thus creating gardens of wellbeing among the crowded island. This will work as long as rats respect rights, that is.

Interestingly, Scott need not see this as something bad. He’s an utilitarian, and utilitarianism endorses The Repugnant Conclusion \[9\] (So do I, in a way!) . But in practise, I don’t think he is _[that much](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/04/08/whose-utilitarianism/)_ of an utilitarian. I think he just wants a world that works nicely, with art, science, cool buildings, gardens, forests, nature, love, music, and so on. He’s for a [Shining Garden](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/06/raikoth-laws-language-and-society/), most probably. He may say he is because altough he finds problems with it, [there are no other alternatives](https://artir.wordpress.com/2015/12/18/why-so-many-utilitarians/). To be totally fair, The Repugnant Conclusion is not about max population=max U, but that a really high population does = max U, if there are decreasing returns of utility with increasing population and they turn negative.

### Capitalism

Imagine a world of perfect competition, where a capitalist wants to do a different thing from perfectly competing. He can’t, by definition of perfect competition. That’s basically what Scott is telling there. Says he that companies, under a sufficiently intense competition are forced to abandon all values except profit maximizing, or they will be outcompeted. He analogizes capitalism to evolution. Fit companies survive (those that make the customer ant to buy from them), expand, and inspire future efforts, and unfit companies die. He even goes as far as to say that

> The reasons Nature is red and tooth and claw are the same reasons the market is ruthless and exploitative
> 
> From a god’s-eye-view, we can contrive a friendly industry where every company pays its workers a living wage. From within the system, there’s no way to enact it.

I read this as a metaphoric paragraph inside a highly metaphoric piece to avoid getting this [face](https://artir.files.wordpress.com/2015/12/mkbhpxfv.jpg).

In the real world, the case Scott describes, sweatshops do get low wages, but those are higher than that of alternative jobs, and increasing. Moloch is not dead here, but there is not great evilness. \[10-12\]. This is not to fully excuse sweatshops. I think, [contra Sumner](http://www.themoneyillusion.com/?p=22188), and, unexpectedly, with Paul Krugman, there are reasons to impose safety standards on sweatshops if the contracts signed are not clear enough. If a worker wants to risk his life in a sweatshop, the sweatshop owner should disclose relevant information to the future employee. Moloch may be stopping that from happening in poorer countries, but it doesn’t in richer ones, and it isn’t just because there are health and safety regulations: People care more about safety, and expectations are in place for workplaces to be safe unless the contrary is explicitly stated, and if this is violated, it may be considered a contract or law violation. Courts work better, too, in richer countries, which decreases enforcement costs and increases compliance.

The real life Moloch, then, exists, but it’s not that bad. Or maybe it doesn’t exist: Because in real life, the sweatshop owners don’t care, seemingly, that much about their workers, so it’s not a coordination problem, but just an outcome of their preferences, and not revealing the knowledge they have about the safety of their buildings. You can say that if they had different preferences, they would be nicer, but they are not being nicer because of an inability to coordinate.

The Moloch may be real if we lived in an environment of perfect competition. But that doesn’t exist. Luckily! There’s dynamic competition. Perfect competition, in real life, can be harmful:

> perfect competition is a useful thought experiment to compare with the real world, but it’s misleading to see it as an attainable goal. In 2007 the only two US satellite radio stations on the market – Sirius and XM – wanted to merge. Regulators objected on the grounds that moving from two firms to one reduced competition. And indeed in terms of market concentration, this is correct. But as Steve Chapman pointed out, ‘the alternative to one (merged) satellite radio company may not be two companies but none’. The main reason they wanted to merge was because theywere losing money. \[13\]

> When we released the PayPal product in the late 1999, Elon Musk’s X.com was right on our heels\[…\] Many of us at PayPal logged 100-hour workweeks. No doubt that was counterproductive, but the focus wasn’t on objective productivity; the focus was defeating X.com. \[…\] But in Febryary 2000, Elon and I were more scared about the rapidly inflating tech bubble than we were about fighting each other: a financial crash would ruin us both before we could finish our fight. So in early March we met on neutral ground, and negotiated a 50-50 merger. De-escalating the rivalry post-merger wasn’t easy, but as far a problems go, it was a good one to have. As a unified team, we were able to ride out the dot-com crash and then build a successful business. \[14\]

The point there is not the details of the story, which may be idealised, as I was reminded on twitter, but that there were two companies that pursued the same, and they merged instead of competing. This is publicly known, as is enough to count as Moloch defiance.

Then there’s [this](https://translate.google.es/translate?sl=es&tl=en&js=y&prev=_t&hl=es&ie=UTF-8&u=http%3A%2F%2Fvozpopuli.com%2Fblogs%2F5580-juan-r-rallo-en-defensa-del-cartel-de-la-leche&edit-text=&act=url) entire article on why a milk cartel is may actually be a good thing, including a sentence full of irony:

> Anathema: people cooperating within capitalism rather than competing to annihilate each other.

Labour unions are also cartels, as are consumer groups, and others. Cartels are no more than groups of people cooperating in certain ways that violate the basic presuppositions of perfect competition, but they need not be inefficient, or welfare-reducing in general. If they were, there would be a profit opportunity for others to enter the market, if it is contestable.

Same for [this other article](https://translate.google.com/translate?sl=es&tl=en&js=y&prev=_t&hl=es&ie=UTF-8&u=http%3A%2F%2Fjuanramonrallo.com%2F2012%2F12%2Fla-eurocracia-no-defiende-a-los-consumidores%2F&edit-text=&act=url): same idea, different sector: cathodic ray tubes. Imagine a bunch of CRT manufacturers. They are in decline because people buy less TVs and screens with CRTs. They are fiercely competing with each other to survive in this environment of declining demand. From a God’s eye view, it seems that the best thing to do is to band together, and orderly scale down production. Well, this is actually what happened: companies formed a cartel to do that.

> The same who contemptuously say over and over that markets are ruled by the law of the jungle and its self-destructive tendencies are who are shocked to discover that no, in markets there are also cooperative tools that limit the scope of cutthroat competition.

Furthermore, we don’t seem to see this cutthroat competition in places with freer markets. Is it because capitalism is regulated? Or also because workers prefer one things and not others? Imagine a company where you have to work really hard, and earn more money, and another where you work less, and earn less money. Even without taking into account decreasing returns to scale (Beyond a point, you always have them, which is why we don’t see an increasing size of companies \[13\]), you will run out of workers who want to work in your company. So either you give other workers different conditions, or you won’t expand that much. Alternatively, it may be the case that it is good to have such a workforce. SpaceX is an example of that, but actually, it seems a good thing that SpaceX is there, and employees seem to love what they are doing.

Econ 101 texbooks may depict capitalism as ruthless (What is perfect competition if not that?), and famous dictums by Adam Smith “It is not from the benevolence of the butcher…” or Milton Friedman “The social responsability of business is to increase profits” don’t help either to picture it differently. But it is not ruthless in so bad of a way! Following **The Soylent Green Principle of Institutional Analysis**, Capitalism is people, and is enmeshed in institutions, rules, governance, norms. The world is not ruled solely by incentives, but also about beliefs about how the world should be. And we don’t like it like that.

Finally, capitalism (or human action, rather) is not _that_ similar to evolution: It is much, much better. [And Scott should know](http://lesswrong.com/lw/l6/no_evolutions_for_corporations_or_nanodevices/), as he was a prominent Less Wronger. As a metaphor, it’s not terrible, but not completely accurate either. Evolution is about literally mindless trial and error, judged by the ability to spawn more copies. The corporation is a consciously designed thing, and while there is substantial trial and error, the mechanisms by which capitalism adapts is not blind survival of the fittest, but human choice. Evolution works from what is given, and taking little steps, taking really long scales of time. What happens in one part of the world doesn’t affect what happens in the other. There is no regard for anything, it’s just organisms outreproducing other organisms. Furthermore, organisms pass their traits to their descendence. With capitalism, everyone can learn from everyone else, the system can quickly readapt, because actors in it understand what’s going on. If there were a meteorite coming our way, evolution would do nothing to stop it. But we can notice it and act. If we estimate a future decrease in the availability of a resource, we can begin adapting right now, while an evolutionary process can’t see it coming. Evolution is always backwards looking, human action is forward looking. Evolution takes millions of years to give you homo sapiens sapiens, human action takes some millenia to figure out the secrets of the universe [and build reusable rockets](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1B6oiLNyKKI).

### The two income trap

Requires reading another whole post, so I mostly skip it. You can try to think of ways I would reply based on what I say here.

### Agriculture

Maybe, from a local point of view, agriculture did locally worsen people’s life conditions, and then competitive preassures ensured farmers outcompeted hunter-gatherers (Or, rather, nomads conquered farmers and turned them into war machines, see the stationary bandit model).

But from a gods-eye-view, the nice thing to do would have been to have a balanced diet, and not fight. Not stay hunter-gatherer forever!

But this doesn’t seem an example of Moloch, but of ignorance (of how diet works, plus glorified beliefs about war and violence, probably religion, and so on. See the usual Pinkerian explanations why it was like that, and why it isn’t anymore). The competition here was to beat other groups, so health was inadvertedly trampled over

### Arms races

This is somewhat true. There are two big equilibria: the nice one is no one spends on offense. The bad one: everyone spends everything in defense. We are in neither of those, and if anything, moving towards the nice equilibrium.

While no country can unilaterally enforce anything, countries can check each other. This, helped with the desire, by everyone, of not going to war, leads to the [reduction of the world’s nuclear stockpile](https://www.google.es/search?q=stockpile+nuclear+weapons&source=lnms&tbm=isch&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwijwLjr1fLJAhWGuhoKHZ4DAg4Q_AUICCgC&biw=928&bih=943), [US spending as % of GDP going down](https://www.google.es/search?q=stockpile+nuclear+weapons&source=lnms&tbm=isch&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwijwLjr1fLJAhWGuhoKHZ4DAg4Q_AUICCgC&biw=928&bih=943#tbm=isch&q=US+military+spending+gdp&imgrc=vGHWE8Lu9RlchM%3A), the fact that there are countries out there, happily living [without armies at all.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_without_armed_forces)

To these, add all those countries that are near countries stronger than them: Canada, everything in the Caribbean, Mexico, everything in the Pacific. Canada or Mexico don’t have an army capable of stopping the United State’s yet they don’t invade? Why? Becaue the r[ealist theory of international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(international_relations\)) is bunk. People don’t generally want to go to war nowadays, and politicians follow. Arms races are truly problematic if you assume not only that agents are purely selfish, but that can’t see where selfishness leads them to. For more on, this, sec. 12.3.6 in \[22\]. Also, for a view of defense as a public bad, see \[23\].

Arms races are real: The Cold War was a massive one, but we’re still around. The game-theoretical argument for escalation is valid, but not sound.

### Cancer

Cells are not agents conscious of incentives and ways of improving their situation, so paraphrasing Wolfgang Pauli, it’s not even Moloch. It’s not that a cell decides to defect, but just that there are mistakes made in some mollecular operation, and cancer happens. And even if, going beyond Scott, I granted that cells were aware of their situation, the rational thing to do wouldn’t be to go cancer, but to stay where they are. Each cell would know it has the power to kill the organism, and if it dies, the cell dies too. Each cell would be armed by a nuke, and no cell would be the first to fire. Not even in principle could the cell win, unlike in the Cold War: the payoff matrix tells the cell to be nice.

### The “race to the bottom”

Oh, if this were true! But it generally isn’t. At the between and within country levels we don’t see this. If this were that prevalent, the world would a giant Hong Kong, but it isn’t. The opposite is closer to truth. Why is this generally benign incarnation of Moloch almost nonexistant? In that politics is not a market. Up to some point, companies try to shop around for looser regulation. In the same way, [people shop around for courts](http://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2006/02/tyler_cowen_on_.html), yet we don’t live in a lawless world. There is some evidence that this does happen for labour market regulation in some countries \[15\], but it even seems good to me. Unregulated labour markets [are good](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/12/labor_econ_vers.html). For pollution control, there is no racing to the bottom neither in emissions \[16\] nor in environmental certifications (ISO 14001) \[17\], which is good, because pollution is a negative externality.

Ultimately, it is an empirical question whether a race to the bottom does work as the simple model predicts, and I’ll grant Moloch points where it is due.

These were the more Molochian cases, apparently,

> Before we go on, there’s a slightly different form of multi-agent trap worth investigating. In this one, the competition is kept at bay by some outside force – usually social stigma. As a result, there’s not actually a race to the bottom – the system can continue functioning at a relatively high level – but it’s impossible to optimize and resources are consistently thrown away for no reason. Lest you get exhausted before we even begin, I’ll limit myself to four examples here.

### Education

Here I agree with Scott. Education is, to a great extent, [signalling](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2015/04/educational_sig_1.html#). However, there’s some light at the end of the tunnel: With the advent of online learning, and with the actual realisation that it is indeed signaling, things may begin to change. If companies knew that, just because of self-interest, they would pay less attention to which college you came from. Students would focus more on learning on their own, etc. This is one of the most Molochians examples around, I have to admit.

### Science

Agreement again, at least for the social sciences, because of the inherent fuzziness of the results, plus some ideological bias. But people spoking loudly against it, and people agreeing with the critique is something recent, I think. So while I grant a few Moloch points here for coordination problems, the problems of Science are also due to sheer ignorance of what is going on.

### Government corruption

> I don’t know of anyone who really thinks, in a principled way, that corporate welfare is a good idea.

Corporations. And people who are employed by them. Which is why the mechanism described in the next subsection happens. Politicians may think that by passing corporate welfare laws, they are helping American business be more competitive vs the rest of the world, or maintaining employment for their constituents, or securing a comfy seat in the corporation afterwards. Regular people may also like the idea of helping [some crucial sectors](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2007/04/farm_subsidies.html) like agriculture, but then, of course, there’s plenty of, maybe not campaign donations, but surely [votes themselves](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306919214001523) inolved.

This is not a case of Moloch. There are agents who do want the subsidies, and agents that are not aware of that happening at the scale it does. Is this problem fixable? Maybe. In Europe and the US, farm subsidies run wild, in New Zeland they don’t. An inquiry into that would be interesting.

Read more about the world of cronyism [here.](http://mercatus.org/sites/default/files/Mitchell-web.pdf)

### Congress

People are catching up with reality. Now people’s approval rating of their member of Congress [in the US is 49%](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/the-fix/wp/2014/08/05/a-majority-of-people-dont-like-their-own-congressman-for-the-first-time-ever/). That said, the explanation for that may well be that people like in-groupers more than out-groupers. One from your region is more in-groupy than an abstract assembly of people you’ve never heard of, and who can’t ever agree on anything.

Scott is right again, to a point. This is not fully Mollochian, as the previous case, because there are agents interested in Congressional favours, and approval ratings mean little. The US is far from a civil revolt. People have little incentive to even be aware of the many things that go about in Congress, which is why this happens. Things could be worse: Public Choice paints a [bleaker picture](http://www.adamsmith.org/blog/politics-government/why-we-vote-the-way-we-vote/) than actually observed.

You could say, though, that there should be a way to break this out from within the system. There is: The long-term painful and very difficult way is criticising cronyism year after year until politicians interiorise the anti-cronyism rule. Not all countries are equally corrupt, nor it seems to be increasing at fast paces. The short-term, and also highly difficult Public Choice solution is ban government from being able to do those things.

## III

> A basic principle unites all of the multipolar traps above. In some competition optimizing for X, the opportunity arises to throw some other value under the bus for improved X. Those who take it prosper. Those who don’t take it die out. Eventually, everyone’s relative status is about the same as before, but everyone’s absolute status is worse than before. The process continues until all other values that can be traded off have been – in other words, until human ingenuity cannot possibly figure out a way to make things any worse.

However, his examples don’t quite work like this: I think he’s mixing different problems, as we’ll see with his Las Vegas example later on.

Where are the other values being sacrificed in the Prisonner’s Dilemma, or th Dollar Auction? Even in the rat example, he deviates from the classical Malthusian logic to talk about rats sacrificing other values due to evolutionary pressures. Malthus is just about preferences for more offspring clash with limited resources for a births=deaths equilibrium, nothing else. Same for capitalism: Companies try to please customers, but they also have to please workers, and shareholders, and all within a framework of private property. They have to strike a balance, and make tradeoffs between satisficing ones and satisficing others. Multiple values are served, in varying ratios. This holds even if profit maximizing was a totally accurate description of the market: It’s everywhere and always _constrained_ profit maximizing.

For agriculture, in Scott’s paragraph, it’s more plausible: people were optimising for killing each other, so the good life of the hunter-gatherer was thrown away because agriculture was needed to win that competition.

In general, I don’t see people reduced to subsistance in those examples as they are instantiated in real life, and problems in examples 11-14 are not _that_ terrible.

Regarding utopia, and incentives,

> Just as you can look at an arid terrain and determine what shape a river will one day take by assuming water will obey gravity, so you can look at a civilization and determine what shape its institutions will one day take by assuming people will obey incentives.

That’s an interesting, empirical, claim. If true, the strongest version of it implies that everyone will behave the same given the same incentives, because if that wasn’t the case, there are non-incentive factors that explain the differences. And those exist, as discussed in \[6\] and \[4-5\]. You could take the incentive structure of Finland, shoehorn it into Liberia, and still not get a Nordic paradise.

> But that means that just as the shapes of rivers are not designed for beauty or navigation, but rather an artifact of randomly determined terrain, so institutions will not be designed for prosperity or justice, but rather an artifact of randomly determined initial conditions.

It is the case that intitutions can be designed for justice. Beliefs about what people think it’s just or fair are what, in the end, rule: they set the contours of what’s permissible in society, and civil revolts are what end up happening if violated.

> Just as people can level terrain and build canals, so people can alter the incentive landscape in order to build better institutions. But they can only do so when they are incentivized to do so, which is not always. As a result, some pretty wild tributaries and rapids form in some very strange places.

But people can be incentivised just by the fact of wanting to solve those problems! The contrary is to suppose that we just care about ourselves, and that this reflects into the institutions. To solve the many coordination problems we face, we don’t need to be complete altruists, just have a pinch of other-regarding preferences. And we do have them. Were not for that, not even Hobbesian contractarianism can get off the ground.

### The Las Vegas mystical experience

> Like, by what standard is building gigantic forty-story-high indoor replicas of Venice, Paris, Rome, Egypt, and Camelot side-by-side, filled with albino tigers, in the middle of the most inhospitable desert in North America, a remotely sane use of our civilization’s limited resources?

A reflection often made, by reference not just to casinos, but to everything that’s above basic needs, in general. Is a remotely sane use of resources for me to pay to eat Apfelstrudel, knowing that that money could be used to save other people’s lives? This is basically the idea behind [using dead children as currency](http://www.raikoth.net/deadchild.html): choices we make matter, and doing something for yourself meas not doing something for others, and viceversa.

> And it occurred to me that maybe there is no philosophy on Earth that would endorse the existence of Las Vegas. Even Objectivism, which is usually my go-to philosophy for justifying the excesses of capitalism, at least grounds it in the belief that capitalism improves people’s lives. Henry Ford was virtuous because he allowed lots of otherwise car-less people to obtain cars and so made them better off. What does Vegas do? Promise a bunch of shmucks free money and not give it to them.

One thing is to actively celebrate Las Vegas as something good, and other would be to endorse the destruction of it (or actively avoiding its construction) as something right. For a utilitarian this doesn’t make sense: the right is the good, and the more the better. For for the rest of the world, the difference is important.

And even then, for utilitarians, it doesn’t seem that bad to have Las Vegas: you have lots of people who enjoy wasting money, some people who enjoy winning money, and a few people who enjoy getting rich in the process. Making an utilitarian case against Las Vegas is not trivial. But Scott makes it straight ahead, but this makes sense from his actual worldview:

> So we have all this amazing technological and cognitive energy, the brilliance of the human species, wasted on reciting the lines written by poorly evolved cellular receptors and blind economics, like gods being ordered around by a moron.

As said before, when Scott saw Las Vegas he probably had his vision of a good society superimposed in Las Vegas. They could be exercising our higher faculties of reasoning, or making fine art, having meaninful social relations, or whatever, and instead they are just gambling. I guess that this reaction would be the same if the expected winnings in a casino were less negative: people would be said to be paying for the thrill, or something.

I’m not of the school of thought that defends “Meh, everything is fine as long as people don’t hurt each other”. I see something quite similar to Scott’s vision as the ideal of a good society, worth trying to get close to.

I haven’t had anything like this experience, but the closest was probably reading Tabarrok & Beito’s The Voluntary City \[19\]. There are people who say that since reading Meditations on Moloch, they see perverse incentives everywhere. Since reading that book, I see cooperation everywhere, and ways of getting around perverse incentives.

## IV

But then, things are not that bad, grants he, at last. And discusses some reasons why that is so.

One is **excess resources**: If there are plenty, we can do things other than compete against everyone like music, art, philosophy and love. He cites a brief Hanson post there which point isn’t obvious, or even properly argued. Those non-adaptative things, that moments of sticking out the middle finger to biology, and evolution, are among the most human moments we have. Each symbol drawn in a blackboard by an analytic topologist or a philosopher is a boastful laugh against the forces that shaped us.

Assuming a future of no more technological advancement, where technology is constant, we would need increasing fertility levels (>2) to return to the Malthusian scenario, but if fertility <2, assuming resources will either increase or stagnate relative to now, there won’t be future scarcity, but more abundance.

The second one is **physical limitations**. Slaves aren’t worked to death because they are more profitable alive. Scott says that slavery in Southern US was weird because it was economically inefficient. It wasn’t, according to Robert Fogel, and if we are to believe Pseudoerasmus’ reading of the literature \[18\], that’s the consensus opinion. But the reading of what happened is true: Overworked, mistreated, slaves aren’t as good workers as one who are treated a bit better. Think of a Slaffer’s Curve: Slaves who don’t work don’t produce, and slaves made to work at 100% will exhaust and don’t produce either, so you would have to work them at some point in the middle. Or people can believe slavery is wrong, and stop doing it altogether, even when it’s economically efficient.

The third is utility maximisation

> But many of the most important competitions / optimization processes in modern civilization are optimizing for human values. You win at capitalism partly by satisfying customers’ values. You win at democracy partly by satisfying voters’ values.

Indeed. Part of what I did in the sections above was to plug in the logic of this paragraph into the examples described.

Regarding his Ethiopians with pesticides example, the coffee growers know that Americans don’t want that. For some time, they may be able to get away with it, until some independent lab, or the government, or consumers, find out about the pesticide and its unadvertised toxicity and sue the farmers. Ideally, it would count as a contract violation (people bought “coffee”, and “coffee” means a set of expectations that exclude toxic pesticides), and costs would be imposed to the farmers. If farmers had a low enough temporal preference, they would see that using the pesticide is not good for them. In reality, doing this will be hard due to differences in time preference, and the difficulties of cross-nationally suing poor farmers. But in advanced nations, this problems are easily dealt with.

Regarding the baby boom, it all comes down to what people are willing to endure. You may end up with some companies overworking people, or with some more companies, for the same employment level and maybe the same wages, for a more reasonable wage.

With the robot coffee picker, Ethiopians could do something different, or people would have to value employment as an end in itself, via buying “Fair Employment” coffee that guarantees that it has been picked by hand. However, economically and morally, it is probably better for them to dedicate themselves to other activity, and for us to buy the robot coffee. But if we really cared about the problems, if we valued their employment enough, we would solve it via that Fair Employment coffee.

> Or suppose that there is some important value that is neither a value of the employees or the customers. Maybe the coffee plantations are on the habitat of a rare tropical bird that environmentalist groups want to protect. Maybe they’re on the ancestral burial ground of a tribe different from the one the plantation is employing, and they want it respected in some way. Maybe coffee growing contributes to global warming somehow. As long as it’s not a value that will prevent the average American from buying from them or the average Ethiopian from working for them, under the bus it goes.

That’s what we have property and laws for! With those, things valued by some do not enter into the action plans of others, and externalities are mandated to be internalised. In a previous section, I discussed city pollution, and how that Moloch has been slain. For global warming, there is not a full agreement on what to do about it, but it seems that technological advancement (solar power, more efficient turbines, and, hopefully, fusion energy) is on its way to solve it. Farmers, even if aware of their contribution to GW, know that they contribute just a little. Larger industries are more aware of it, and its easier to monitor them, so the problem is more easily solved that way.

> I know that “capitalists sometimes do bad things” is not exactly an original talking point. But I do want to stress how it’s not equivalent to “capitalists are greedy”. I mean, sometimes they _are_ greedy. But other times they’re just in a sufficiently intense competition where anyone who doesn’t do it will be outcompeted and replaced by people who do. Business practices are set by Moloch, no one else has any choice in the matter.

That could be true if the market was an unconstrained profit maximisation process. But it isn’t: markets are people, and people have values. Business practices are not set by Moloch, they are set by people. People make tradeoffs between different values, and some equilibriums are just socially forbidden.

> And as well understood as the capitalist example is, I think it is less well appreciated that democracy has the same problems. Yes, in theory it’s optimizing for voter happiness which correlates with good policymaking. But as soon as there’s the slightest disconnect between good policymaking and electability, good policymaking _has to_ get thrown under the bus.

In theory, democracy does not optimize for voter happiness. Democracy just implements whatever the ruling set of politicians want to implement. In turn, those are elected based on the preferences of the people who vote them. But people can vote policies that go against their happiness, or more broadly, against their meaningful project pursuing. Sometimes, or even, many times, going against what people vote is what ends up being beneficial. Some argue that the precise reason democracy works is because it isn’t that tightly coupled to people desires, [and elite preferences are overrepresented](http://econlog.econlib.org/archives/2012/09/why_is_democrac.html). And elites are better informed, and less crazy than the rest of the people. Surely there are disconnects between electability and good policymaking: economic policy does not look like as designed by economists in every country, but in general it doesn’t look as designed by the average citizen either.

> For example, ever-increasing prison terms are unfair to inmates and unfair to the society that has to pay for them. Politicans are unwilling to do anything about them because they don’t want to look “soft on crime”, and if a single inmate whom they helped release ever does anything bad (and statistically one of them will have to) it will be all over the airwaves as “Convict released by Congressman’s policies kills family of five, how can the Congressman even sleep at night let alone claim he deserves reelection?”. So even if decreasing prison populations would be good policy – and it is – it will be very difficult to implement.

I agree with this. I don’t think people deserve to suffer for not knowing something they did was wrong. So it is unfair to inmates. People [do want](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/wonk/wp/2015/10/01/heres-how-much-americans-hate-mandatory-minimum-sentences/) to abolish mandatory minimum sentences in the US, and it wouldn’t surprise me this will happen in a few years.

However, I wouldn’t say this outcomes are the result of utility maximization. They are the result of respecting people’s rights and caring about others, and contrained by that, pursue your conception of the good. That in many cases will involve making money, and in quite a lot of those, it will involve trying to make more money than the rest.

Regarding **coordination**, Scott discusses the concept of a garden: everyone bands together under a single authority that ensures the good cooperative situations happen. Governments would be an example of this, says Scott. Governments could end pollution, prisoner dilemmas, workers being harmed, arms races, and a World Government would be able to solve those, for the entire world.

Scott characterises governments are rule patterned violence: agreements plus enforcement. But things different from governments also do this, he reminds us: teachers act like governments by having rules against cheating, limiting actions student can take when competing for grades. Norms among the students can also work as a government: rules (don’t cheat) and punishment (or else we will shun you).

> Social codes, gentlemens’ agreements, industrial guilds, criminal organizations, traditions, friendships, schools, corporations, and religions are all coordinating institutions that keep us out of traps by changing our incentives.
> 
> But these institutions not only incentivize others, but are incentivized themselves. These are large organizations made of lots of people who are competing for jobs, status, prestige, et cetera – there’s no reason they should be immune to the same multipolar traps as everyone else, and indeed they aren’t. Governments can in theory keep corporations, citizens, et cetera out of certain traps, but as we saw above there are many traps that governments themselves can fall into.

The first paragraph is a nice list of the battalions serving us in mercilessly slaying Molochs. The second paragraph reminds us that those institutions can still fell prey to Moloch, including governments. Governments are people, and as such they behave.

> The United States tries to solve the problem by having multiple levels of government, unbreakable constutitional laws, checks and balances between different branches, and a couple of other hacks.
> 
> Saudi Arabia uses a different tactic. They just put one guy in charge of everything.
> 
> This is the much-maligned – I think unfairly – argument in favor of monarchy. A monarch is an unincentivized incentivizer. He _actually_ has the god’s-eye-view and is outside of and above every system. He has permanently won all competitions and is not competing for anything, and therefore he is perfectly free of Moloch and of the incentives that would otherwise channel his incentives into predetermined paths. Aside from a few very theoretical proposals like my [Shining Garden](http://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/06/raikoth-laws-language-and-society/), monarchy is the _only_ system that does this.

Now, this paragraphs are quite interesting. The first one explains the idea of separation of powers and Constitutions: the solution to the who will watch the watchers problem. But it isn’t a real solution. Limiting government power with government via incentives [doesn’t work](http://www.cato-unbound.org/issues/february-2008/limited-government-possible) in theory, altough it somewhat does in practise. You need a power from the outside to contrain it, and ultimately it is what people think is just and fair. That’s Etienne de la Boetie \[20\] great insight. What really holds it together is beliefs, ethics, tradition. And yes, incentives too.

For monarchies, Scott presents the argument for them: Monarchs are outside the system. They escaped Moloch and can do as they wish. He says that besides some highly theoretical proposals like his Shining Garden, ONLY monarchy can do that. I won’t criticise or praise the Shining Garden here, but I’ll point out that monarchs are not truly Moloch free, thing he admits later on. Let’s pull some more paragraphs to finish this section,

> But then instead of following a random incentive structure, we’re following the whim of one guy. Caesar’s Palace Hotel and Casino is a crazy waste of resources, but the actual Gaius Julius Caesar Augustus Germanicus wasn’t exactly the perfect benevolent rational central planner either.

He grants that altough monarchs could, in theory, take the god’s-eye view and solve problems, [this is not a necessity](http://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2006/02/tyler_cowen_on_.html). Precisely because their apparent freedom from incentives, there is little (besides people’s willingness to cope) to stop them from doing what they want. Except other monarchs or countries, that is. Monarchs would also be stuck in Molochian traps vis-a-vis other monarchs, as they are in a symmetrical relation to each other. Scott’s argument for monarchy would work not for your average Habsburg or Bourbon, but for a World Monarch. Unless we recognise, as we should, that you can escape Moloch, or perverse incentives, without a power above. This is also why the Hobbesian logic applied to the world fails: countries aren’t constantly fighting.

A monarchy is not something qualitatively different from one of the organisations described above by Scott (companies, associations…). The difference is that they are usually large. But imagine a small monarchy near a country with really low taxes and regulations. ¿Could the monarchy avoid lowering taxes and regulations to attract capital? If the monarch cares only about money, she will lower them. If the monarch cares about maintaining her garden as it is, she won’t. But same goes for any other organisation.

> The libertarian-authoritarian axis on the Political Compass is a tradeoff between discoordination and tyranny. You can have everything perfectly coordinated by someone with a god’s-eye-view – but then you risk Stalin. And you can be totally free of all central authority – but then you’re stuck in every stupid multipolar trap Moloch can devise.
> 
> The libertarians make a convincing argument for the one side, and the neoreactionaries for the other, but I expect that [like most tradeoffs](http://slatestarcodex.com/2014/03/01/searching-for-one-sided-tradeoffs/) we just have to hold our noses and admit it’s a really hard problem.

Freedom and uncoordination, or coordination but risk of opression. This, to some length, is true. But it’s more like freedom and risk of uncoordination, or coordination but risk of opression. Scott grants the NRx that ideally, their theory would deliver coordination and niceness, but that in practise it may not. To libertarians, the theory is not granted. Not even in theory will coordination will be achieved, let alone in practise. I will return to this later on.

## V

A summary of Scott’s point so far:

> Multipolar traps – races to the bottom – threaten to destroy all human values. They are currently restrained by physical limitations, excess resources, utility maximization, and coordination. \[…\] the most important change in human civilization over time is the change in technology. So the relevant question is how technological changes will affect our tendency to fall into multipolar traps.
> 
> Develop a new robot, and suddenly coffee plantations have “the opportunity” to automate their harvest and fire all the Ethiopian workers. Develop nuclear weapons, and suddenly countries are stuck in an arms race to have enough of them. Polluting the atmosphere to build products quicker wasn’t a problem before they invented the steam engine.
> 
> The limit of multipolar traps as technology approaches infinity is “very bad”.
> 
> Multipolar traps are currently restrained by physical limitations, excess resources, utility maximization, and coordination.

If he mentioned before that there were some factors keeping Moloch at bay, now the problem is that technology can destroy those.

For physical limitations, the slavemaster can feed the slaves Soylent and modafinil, and track them with GPS. As for Malthusian conditions, Scott cites Bostrom: maybe now fertility rates are <2, but people who do have children pass their traits to the newer generations, perhaps increading long run fertility. Or maybe, cultural norms will keep that constrained. He quickly jumps to a world of ems that can quickly reproduce and exhaust all available hardware. If new agents can be very quickly created, physical limits will have expanded and won’t be a contrain anymore.

This just assumes that emulated people will trample over other people’s rights. But they can be constrained, as people right now are.

As for excess resources, with asteroid mining, and constructing worlds in virtual reality, limits to growth lure far in the future. But they are, in theory, there, and Scott reminds us of that. Unless we grow at a lower pace, and develop faster than light propulsion, or something.

As for utility maximization (more like not being fully selfish, actually), Scott presents the everyone out of work because of robots-scenario. So far, [it is not happening](http://www.voxeu.org/article/robots-productivity-and-jobs) except for low-wage jobs. But in theory, there are many jobs that could fall prey to robots. \[21\] There is a way out of this

> (there are some scenarios in which a few capitalists who own the robots may benefit here, but in either case the vast majority are out of luck)

_If_ those capitalists, given the absurdly rich they would be, donated a minuscule fraction of their wealth, perhaps things would be really nice. Cynics would propose a universal basic income, which is coercing them into doing the same thing.

As for religious sects taking over due to increased natality, he notes that many of them quit, so it’s not that problematic.

But still, memes are dangerous, because they spread independetly of their truth value

> The creationism “debate” and global warming “debate” and a host of similar “debates” in today’s society suggest that the phenomenon of memes that propagate independent of their truth value has a pretty strong influence on the political process. Maybe these memes propagate because they appeal to people’s prejudices, maybe because they are simple, maybe because they effectively mark an in-group and an out-group, or maybe for all sorts of different reasons.
> 
> The point is – imagine a country full of bioweapon labs, where people toil day and night to invent new infectious agents. The existence of these labs, and their right to throw whatever they develop in the water supply is protected by law. And the country is also linked by the world’s most perfect mass transit system that every single person uses every day, so that any new pathogen can spread to the entire country instantaneously. You’d expect things to start going bad for that city pretty quickly.
> 
> Well, we have about a zillion think tanks researching new and better forms of propaganda. And we have constitutionally protected freedom of speech. And we have the Internet. So we’re pretty much screwed.
>

## Spotting and annihilating Molochs as a social activity

### The Pantheon of Molochs

What does the metaphor of Moloch _exactly_ refer to?

1.  To ignorance, and inhability to conceive cooperative equilibriums where one benefits more than otherwise
2.  To irrationality, to choosing suboptimal means to achieve goals
3.  To perverse incentives: following ‘rational’ decisions leading to aggregate problems, of varying magnitude
4.  To the imperfections of our biology
5.  A combination of the above

The Prisonner dilemma is a case of 3 (Or 2, depending on your conception of rationality)

The dollar auction is a case of 1: Not knowing ex ante where that game leads to. Some problems of democracy are also due to this.

The Las Vegas example is mostly 4. Is it irrational to play in a casino? Not necessarily, if you care about different things than actually winning. Is it part of a good life? Probably not, in my and Scott’s worldviews.

The Malthusian cases are a combination of 3-4.

Then, the problems described by Scott can be sorted out in two big bins:

1.  Problems that someone knows how to solve
2.  Problems that we don’t know how to solve

The em world and SAI is this second case, and most of what Scott said is the 1st case. Scott begins by building his case from small observations, here and there, of mini Molochs before going full on and connect those type 1 problems to type 2 problems.

If you have in your head lots of things at the same time, it seems problems are bigger than what they really are. Here we have a poem, a vision of Las Vegas, irrationality, ignorance, incentives, actually existing problems, and potential problems. The whole thing and fuzz about Moloch can be cleared up and reduced to the problem of ems and SAI. As said: Moloch is not a big deal now. I won’t solve the em-SAI problem disjunction here, I’ll at least wait until I’ve read Hanson’s book on the topic. Suffice to say that we are quite safe until those problems hit us. If they never come, we’ll be safe forever, seemingly.

### The Politics of Moloch

What prompted this absurdly long (for my standards, I don’t approve of super lengthy texts) piece was a comment [in twitter by Devon Marisa Zuegel](https://twitter.com/devonzuegel/status/679010843481083905). Though Moloch is everywhere, and Scott’s piece has not politics as a main theme, a political reading of it can be made: We need government to keep Moloch at bay. Individual self-interest and competition won’t slay it. Therefore, the reality of Moloch poses a problem for libertarianism, it could be said.

It is sometimes said that a libertarian is someone who has only studied half a semester of economics, where the niceness of markets is explained, but skipped the second semester, where market failures are described. I’ll go further and say that most economists finish their studies there. Further studies lead you to Public Choice, were you learn that, even if market fails, and even if in theory governments can correct market failures, in practise they need not do it, and it may be better, given plausible assumptions, to let the market alone. Finally, the fourth step is to realise that we can escape incentives to some extent and troll naive economic textbooks really hard. We can notice we are in lose-lose situations and devise institutions to solve them, changing our incentives. We can band together to solve a problem. We can sign contracts to bind ourselves credibly. Scott calls many thing like these ‘government’. But this is simplistic. Remember what I said Scott said:

> Scott characterises governments are rule patterned violence: agreements plus enforcement. But things different from governments also do this, he reminds us: teachers act like governments by having rules against cheating, limiting actions student can take when competing for grades. Norms among the students can also work as a government: rules (don’t cheat) and punishment (or else we will shun you).

Governments are not rule patterned violence. It’s not only that they are not only that, but they are something else altogether. Governments are the institutions in control of States: sovereign entities who enjoy the recognition of their legitimacy by a sizable fraction of the people it rules over. As a result, it enjoys political authority, even when most philosophers say such thing is undefensible \[24\]. Governance is what we are looking for, and this one thing governments can do, and almost always do. Me signing a contract with you is governance, and asking a trustworthy third part to punish a defector is governance. Not government. Government is more like omnicomprensive, large, and unchallengeable. Governance is more specific, small, and polycentric. Among these extremes there is a range of possibilities. Right now, it is well known that you can have effective rules in the abscence of government \[25-29\], even if it is to form pirate bands or prison gangs. The bounds of cooperation are set by beliefs about what’s permissible, remember, so not all cooperation is nice. You see this even in settings where people are supposed to fight, like online games, e.g. EVE Online \[30-31\]. Can we translate this to the entire world, to have actually existing extensive cooperation without governments? Maybe \[32\]

Minimal-state libertarianism is far easier to defend. Economic arguments, even when overlooking our cooperative skills, let you justify a government of up to 30-35% of GDP. \[33\]. That’s no minimal government, but around the levels Switzerland is, and Switzerland is one of the model countries libertarians usually refer to. Other examples that meet the target are Hong Kong and Singapore. And this includes the need to assist the poor! If you are more optimistic regarding markets, you can bring down it to 5% of GDP, as recently defended, in Spanish, here \[34\].

We can argue over those arguments. But what is simply not true is that in theory libertarianism doesn’t work. Empirically it may not. Market failures can be solved inside the market itself, in theory, given realistic assumptions about how human being are. This assumptions do not include pure selfishness or perfect competition.

Finally, if there are some more coordination failures remaining, they can be solved by the minimal-State government, or left there to be solved by pure cooperation alone, in the very long run.

### An express debunking of the Non-Libertarian FAQ 3.0, or helping some fishermen

Let’s go back to the fishermen example and finally solve it.

So we have our 1000 firms, each one causing a -1$ loss to others, from an initial gain of $1000 each, so they are making zero. The fishermen get together and lay out their situation. They agree on signing a conditional contract, not too dissimilar from what you can do in Kickstarter: Everyone will pay 300$ for a filter, so that everyone makes $700 a month. The contract will only be binding if everyone signs it, and they go to a court and sign it there so that it becomes binding, and enforceable by an external agency. Now they have changed their incentives: They can choose to do nothing (payoff=0) or sign (expected payoff=700). So they sign. Once they have gone to court, they can either cooperate (expected payoff=700) or can shirk. But now their payoff is not 999, but paying a fine until they cooperate, so now it’s rational to cooperate. Fixed.

But you have used a court! So what? In Libertonia there are courts, what were you expecting? Third-party enforceability is a valuable service.

But what if there’s a stubborn fisherman who demands others to pay him to actually sign the contract. There we run into the question of whether the fishermen actually had the right to pollute the water in the first place. \[35\].

So suppose that the fishermen raise this issue. Most of them argue that since the lake is a common resource, and since it’s in the interest in all of them for it to be clean, they should regard it as a common property, where rules can be imposed to ensure inner coordination. The hypothetical free rider could argue that since he was the first to get to the river, he has the right to pollute, and that others ought to pay him. This could be some sort of Coasean bargain. In this case, if he demands payment P from each other, he would be making 700+999P. Others would be making 700-P. In the cooperative case, total earnings are 700_1000=0.7 megadollars. In the noncooperative case, 700+999P+700_999-999P=0.7 megadollars, same amount, different distribution. P would depend here on a variety of factors, but total gains are the same. The court system would have to deal with cases like this, and resolve disputes, set precedents, trying to ensure good outcomes while respecting rights. No trivial task, but not an impossible one either.

If this is Scott’s core objection to libertarianism, I think he’ll now be a step closer to it. And he’s already really close, if not already into it!

## Conclusion

The point of this essay has been that the Moloch problem is overrated, and that we can overcome it. The living Molochs of today are the dead Molochs of tomorrow’s History books. There remain some “What-if” Molochs that seem difficult to slay: The Em World and an ungood Superintelligence. Until those come in the future, we are mostly safe from Molochlike threats. Why has Scott overestimated the threat? It seems to me that it is because of particularly narrow view of how markets work, and how people behave.

As for political systems, Molochlike problems pose greater dangers for democracy than for capitalism. Capitalism allows for both small, step-by-step solutions and big ones that can be carried out even if other people disagree, within the limits of property rights. The logic of sovereignty bans this. It makes really difficult to experiment with different political organisations that may help solve the problems Scott describes. Within the market, gardens can be built and last. To me, Elua is more a polycentrically governed world coupled with some altruism on our side than a benevolent Superintelligence. Difficult as both are, my solution seems a bit easier.

His speculations are about how an AI could solve our problems. Mine involve us, actual humans, crafting institutions and rules to do so from within the system we live in. We have tools for cooperation, but no guarantee that we will be wise enough to use them. As for Scott’s plan, it involves the solution of two hard problems: Building a SAI and then making it nice.

In a way, the solution to the problem was monarchy at a large scale: to make all of us kings, with walls of rights we recognise each other. But at the same time, we don’t make this wall infinitely high. There are situations where it may not be bad to violate them. Probably in those cases where their definition is not very clear, like in the fishermen case, or cases where the benefit/cost ratio is really high. Ideally, we would end up with a world filled with institutions encompassing resources where problems of this sort tend to happen. Make lakes or rivers common resources for their users, the atmosphere could be regarded as one large common property. Streets, buildings, and cities can be dealt with in the same way. I’m not downplaying the difficulty of that, I assure you.

Will we necessarily cooperate? No. Can we, in theory? Yes. Is it likely that we will? If we can envision and implement adequate rules and institutions, and if we care about others, yes, we will.

Scott has described our enemy. I have described us, and the weapons we have. Now, go and keep working on making things better! Moloch is not going to kill itself!
