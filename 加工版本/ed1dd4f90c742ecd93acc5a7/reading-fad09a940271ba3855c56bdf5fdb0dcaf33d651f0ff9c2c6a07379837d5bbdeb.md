_今年早些时候，我决定花几周时间，弄清自己对 Artificial Superintelligence 带来的 existential risk（ASI xrisk）究竟怎么看。结果，这比我想象的困难得多。经过几个月的阅读、思考和与人交谈，下面将讨论这次探索中产生的几个 observations，包括：_

_-   三个 ASI xrisk persuasion paradoxes，它们使得无论是支持还是反对 ASI xrisk，都 intrinsically 难以提出 strong evidence。缺少这样的 compelling evidence，是人们对 ASI xrisk 存在如此强烈 disagreement 的部分原因；人们往往转而依靠 prior beliefs、self-interest 和 tribal reasoning 来决定自己的 opinions（这可以理解）。
-   这个 alignment dilemma：关心 xrisk 的人是否应该参与 concrete alignment work，因为唯有如此，我们才有希望 build safe systems；还是应该拒绝做这类工作，因为这会助长 accelerating a bad outcome？这是关于许多 AI alignment work 的 accelerationist character 的更广泛讨论的一部分，因此 capabilities / alignment 是一个 false dichotomy。
-   这个 doomsday question：是否存在 recipes for ruin——simple、easily executed、immensely destructive 的 recipes，足以 end humanity，或造成 catastrophic world-changing damage？
-   对于 ASI speeding up scientific discovery，存在哪些 bottlenecks？尤其是：ASI 是否可能 discover existing theories 中 latent 的 emergent phenomena 的新 levels？_

## 引言

下文以访谈形式呈现。这实际上不是一次访谈，而是我与许多人交谈的 idealized distillation。在尝试较为常规的 essay 或 paper 形式、却感到难以处理之后，我选择了这种不寻常的形式；我认为，在大多数关于 ASI xrisk 的讨论中，那些形式所暗示的 confidence 超出了应有程度。访谈似乎能更恰当地融合 evidence、argument 和 opinion。其中一些材料涉及的 background，是熟读 ASI xrisk 相关文献的人已经了解的[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn1)。不过，其中也有我认为值得关注的 novel contributions——例如关于 emergence[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn2) 和三个 xrisk persuasion paradoxes 的讨论。

**“你相信 ASI 会带来 xrisk 吗？”** 是的，我相信。至于这种 risk 有多大，我没有很强的判断，只认为它足够 significant，应该受到极其认真的对待。ASI 很可能既是人类有史以来开发过的最 dangerous 的 technology，也是最 enabling 的 technology[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn3)。下文我将描述自己如此相信的部分理由。坦率地说：我怀疑这样的 arguments 能否改变任何人的想法。不过，这番讨论将为另一个讨论奠定基础：为什么深思熟虑的人对 ASI xrisk 的 opinions 会有如此大的分歧。我们将看到，这部分源于不同的 politics 和 tribal beliefs，但也存在一些 fundamental epistemic reasons，它们 intrinsic 于这个 problem 的 nature。

**“那么，你的 probability of doom 是多少？”** 我认为这个 concept 非常 misleading。人类得到的 outcomes 取决于我们能够作出的 choices。我们可以作出让 doom 在几十年的 timescale 内几乎 inevitable 的 choices——事实上，我们不需要 ASI 就能做到这一点，很可能[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn4)通过其他方式就能实现（nukes、engineered viruses，……）。我们也可以作出让 doom 极其 unlikely 的 choices。关键在于弄清什么可能带来 flourishing，然后去做那些事。在开始经常听到 AI 公司的人 fatalistically 使用“probability of doom”、无视他们的 choices 能够改变 outcomes 这一事实之后，这个术语开始让我感到沮丧。“Probability of doom”是 conceptual hazard 的一个例子[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn5)——仅仅使用这个 concept，就可能导致思考出错。它的主要用途似乎是 marketing：如果广受尊重的人坚定地表示自己的 probability of doom 很 high 或很 low，可能会让其他人停下来思考其中的原因。但我不喜欢有利于 marketing、却不利于 understanding 的 concepts；它们会助长 collective misunderstanding，并且可能最终导致 collective errors in action。

**“真不敢相信，你居然认真看待 ASI xrisk 这些事！”：** 自从在 1980年代还是个青少年时，我就一直（非常低调地！）为此担忧。最近唯一改变的是 urgency。在成长过程中，我大体上对 science 和 technology 极其 optimistic，但也担忧许多 concomitant problems：ozone hole、nuclear weapons、acid rain、climate change、ecosystem collapse、overpopulation、grey goo、peak oil，以及许多其他 risks，包括我今天会称作 ASI xrisk 的问题。其中大多数不是 xrisks，但人们有理由认为它们具有 potentially catastrophic 的影响，会 affecting 数亿乃至数十亿人——至少当时是如此。学校里的许多老师对这类问题非常 pessimistic，我也吸收了其中一些 pessimism。例如，我从小就觉得 nuclear Armageddon 很 likely，尤其是在大约 ~14岁时读过《The Cold and the Dark》之后；这本书讲的是 large-scale nuclear war 的 consequences。

后来，在二十多岁时，我注意到，童年或少年时期担心的许多最可怕的 predictions 似乎并没有成真。有时，这是因为其 timescale 长于一二十年；但在某些情况下，问题已在很大程度上得到处理，或已淡出 collective consciousness。例如，ozone hole 主要通过 Montreal Protocol 得到处理。Acid rain 似乎也已不再那么令人担忧，其中的原因我只了解一部分。但在 public consciousness 中，这些问题已被其他 existential worries 取代；事实上，existential worry 在人类中似乎比人们可能 _a priori_ 猜想的更加 conserved。我渐渐相信，常常存在一种 _hubris in pessimism_：人们很容易把针对某个重大问题的“我\[和我的朋友们\]不知道 solution”，与“不存在 solution”混淆。civilizational problems 往往以事先极少有人预料到的方式得到 solved。

例如，在 1970年代和 1980年代，人们非常害怕“population bomb”，即 world population 看似 unstoppable、很可能 calamitous 的 exponential growth[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn6)。今天，这种曾经非常真实的恐惧已很难回想起来，因为 urbanization、birth control，以及改善后的 food production 和 distribution，已经在很大程度上拆除了 population bomb——事实上，今天许多人更担心 population implosion 的前景。但在 1970年代和 1980年代，许多人对 overpopulation 的 doom 感，与今天的 climate 或 AI pessimists 相似。再举一个例子，过去十年里，climate 和 renewable energy 方面的 progress 让我惊喜，尤其是 solar 和 wind energy、batteries，以及其他 decarbonization 方式的 prices 大幅下降。仍然有大量事情要做，我也知道 climate pessimists 会对我的 optimism 嗤之以鼻；不过，我认为这种 optimism 是正确的。climate change 将会造成（事实上已经造成）considerable impacts，但这些 impacts 的 severe 程度将远低于许多人十年前的预想。

几十年来，我一直希望并相信，类似的意外 progress 会让对 ASI xrisk 的担忧变得 obsolete。因此，我把自己的忧虑放在了一边；真要说的话，我对 AI 的 long-run prospects 非常 optimistic，尽管当时我认为 major progress 很可能还需等待一段时间[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn7)。如今，major progress 已经到来[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn8)。而今天的局面在我看来非常 challenging；问题非但没有得到 solved，反而似乎正在迅速恶化。

**“这并不是支持 ASI xrisk 的 argument！”** 对，确实不是。事实上，我花了相当长时间才明白的一件事是：有非常充分的理由说明，期待一个无论支持_还是_反对 xrisk 的 bulletproof argument，本身就是错误的。稍后我会再谈为什么如此。不过，现在先说几句概括性的看法。我相信人类_能够_ make ASI，而且很可能很快就会 make 它——如果没有 disaster，或没有为 slowdown 作出重大努力，那么就在三十年内，甚至可能早得多。许多有才干的人和许多有权势的人都在竭力推动它。事实上：庞大的 _systems_ 正开始推动它。其中一些人和 systems 的强烈动机，是对 power 和 control 的渴望。还有许多人的强烈动机，是希望为人类作出贡献。他们正确地认为，ASI 将带来巨大的好处，促成重大的 medical advances、materials advances、educational advances，等等。我用了“advances”这个词，它已经有点变成一个 marketing term，但我指的不是 _Nature_\-press-release-style-(usually)-minor-advances。我指的是 polio-vaccine-transforming-millions-of-lives-style-advances，甚至规模更大的 advances。这些 optimists 将 ASI 视为一种很可能产生惊人的 abundance、并让其得到广泛 shared 的 technology，从而 enriching 世界上的_每个人_。

不过，尽管这很美好、值得庆祝，在我看来，那些 advances 很可能也有可怕的阴暗面。在某种意义上，人类的 understanding _始终_是 dual use：真正深入的 understanding，会以一种非常 general 的方式，让 universe 更加 malleable，服从我们的意志。例如，relativity 和 quantum mechanics 的 insights，虽然对现代 molecular biology、medicine、materials、computing 的许多部分，以及其他许多领域至关重要，但它们也帮助促成了 nuclear weapons。我不认为这是偶然：当你极大地 increase 自己对构成 universe 的物质的 understanding 时，这样的 dual uses 几乎 inevitable。

顺便谈谈 short term——未来几年——我预计我们将看到 rapidly improving 的 multi-modal foundation models，它们 mix language、mathematics、images、video、sound、现实世界中的 action，以及许多 specialized sources of data，例如关于 viruses 和 proteins 的 genetic data、来自 particle physics 的 data、来自 vehicles 和 oceans 的 sensor data，等等。这些 models 将“know”世界许多不同方面的大量内容，也将拥有用于 abstract reasoning 的 raw substrate——例如 language 和 mathematics；它们将在这些 domains 之间获得至少一些 transfer，并且会比 GPT-4 这样的 systems 强大得多、得多。这并不意味着它们届时就会是真正的 AGI 或 ASI！几乎肯定还需要其他 ideas；不过，那些 ideas 也可能已经 extant。无论如何，我预计这些 models 作为 discovery 强大的新 technologies 的辅助工具，将会越来越 powerful。此外，我预计，要获得“positive” capabilities，却不同时获得 negative capabilities，将会非常、非常困难。你不可能只 learn quantum mechanics 的“positive” consequences；它们与 negative consequences 是一揽子到来的。RLHF 这样的 Guardrails 将有助于 suppress negative 的部分，但正如我稍后将讨论的，remove 那些 guardrails 也会相对简单。

回到中长期：许多关心 ASI xrisk 的人关注的是 ASI taking over，成为某种人类的 successor species。但即使仅仅把 ASI 当作 tool[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn9) 来看，ASI 也会成为我们 ability to understand 的巨大 accelerant，因而成为我们 power 的巨大 amplifier。对 individuals 和 groups 都会如此。这会带来许多许多非常好的事情。不幸的是，无论 guardrails 有多好，它_也_会带来许多 destructive things。像“是否存在一种 trivially easy-to-follow recipe，可以用来 genocide \[一个 race\]？”或“是否存在一种 trivially easy-to-follow recipe，可以用来 end humanity？”这样的问题，绝不能确定就没有 affirmative answers；人类只是（目前且幸运地）太愚笨，无法回答，而 ASI 却可能回答。

从历史上看，我们一直很擅长 evolving guardrails，以 curb 和 control 强大的新 technologies。这确实是 optimism 的理由。然而，我担心在这个情况下，我们无法 evolve 足以应对这种 increase 的 guardrails。从 1940s 到 1980s 的 nuclear buildup 是一个警示性的 example：回顾 evidence 就能看清，迄今我们只是勉强 escaped large-scale nuclear war——而现在还只是开头！ASI 很可能会以快得多的 timescale，in parallel 地 create 许多这样的 threats，而且让 individuals 和 small groups 更容易获取。intellect 的世界就是能够提供极其 scalable leverage：如果你能 create 一个 artificial John von Neumann，就能 produce 一支由他们组成的大军，其中一些可能会为那些我们实在不愿让其获得这种 capacity 的人工作。许多人喜欢谈论让 ASI systems 变得 safe 和 aligned；姑且不说做到这一点有多难（甚至 sensibly defining 它有多难），看来还必须对今后_所有_ ASI systems 做到这一点。这似乎需要一种 all-seeing surveillance regime，而这条路危机重重。或许，这样的 surveillance regime 不一定只是由 government 或 corporations 针对 populace 来 implemented，也可以通过更加 omnidirectional 的方式 implemented，成为一种 ambient sousveillance[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn10)。

## 关于 real systems 的 practical alignment

**“你怎么看目前正在进行的 practical alignment work——RLHF、Constitutional AI 等等？”**：这些 work 确实在 technically 方面很有意思。将它们与以前的 systems 作 contrast 很有意思，例如 Microsoft 的 Tay，它可以[很容易被弄得](https://www.theverge.com/2016/3/24/11297050/tay-microsoft-chatbot-racist)去做许多糟糕的事情。你也能让 ChatGPT 和 Claude 做糟糕的事情，但你得更费力；对那些 systems 所做的 alignment work 已经 created 了某种程度上 stable 的 guardrails。这类 work 也很引人注目，因为在这个 case 中，safety-oriented 的人做了 detailed technical work 来 improve real systems，有 hard feedback loops，以及明确的 success 和 failure criteria；这不同于许多早期 ASI xrisk work 中常见的 abstract philosophizing。在前一种情况下，improve 你的 ideas 显然容易得多，而在后一种情况下，你更容易欺骗自己。

尽管如此：_practical alignment work 是 extremely accelerationist 的_。如果 ChatGPT 的表现像 Tay，AI 现在仍然只会在《The New York Times》的第 19 页被略微提及。这些 alignment techniques 在 AI 中发挥的作用，有点像用于 control 一枚 nuclear bomb 何时 goes off 的 systems。如果这样的 bombs 只会 at random 地 went off，就没有人会 build nuclear bombs，人类也就不会面临 nuclear threat。Practical alignment work 让今天的 AI systems 对 customers 更具吸引力，作为 building 其他 systems 的 platform 更加 usable，作为 investors 的投资对象更加 profitable，也更能被 governments 接受。net result 就是，practical alignment work 是 accelerationist 的。[Paul Christiano](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/vwu4kegAEZTBtpT6p/thoughts-on-the-impact-of-rlhf-research) 是 RLHF 和 AI safety 这两个领域的 pioneers 之一，他有一篇思考极其深入的文章，讨论鉴于 RLHF 造成的 acceleration，他是否后悔曾 working on RLHF。我钦佩这篇文章的自我反省与诚实，但最终，我和这篇文章的许多评论者一样，认为他只是部分正视了这样一个事实：他的 work 将 considerably hasten ASI，包括 extremely dangerous systems。

过去十年里，我遇到过许多 AI safety 人士，他们说起话来，仿佛“AI capabilities”和“AI safety/alignment” work 是一种 dichotomy。他们谈论想把 capabilities researchers “move”到 alignment。但大多数 concrete alignment work _就是_ capabilities work。这是一种 false dichotomy，也是 conceptual error 如何能够把一个 field 引入歧途的又一个 example。幸运的是，许多 safety 人士现在已经理解了这一点，但我有时仍会看到这种 false dichotomy 误导人们，有时甚至通过糟糕的 funding decisions 造成 systematic effects。

关于 alignment 的第二点是，无论 guardails 有多好，它们都是 intrinsically unstable 的，而且 easily removed。我经常遇到聪明的 AI safety 人士，他们有富于创意的 schemes，希望能让 ASI systems 变得 safe。也许能，也许不能。但 scheme 越 elaborate，局面就越 unstable。如果你有一份魔法汤的食谱，需要 123 种不同的配料，但所有配料都必须按 weight 以 1% 以内的精度混合，而且哪怕一次 deviation 都会让它变得剧毒致命，那么你真的不该烹煮并喝下这碗“安全”的汤。一个帮厨忘了放一根韭葱，然后砰的一下，整个村庄就没了。

在 Stable Diffusion 上，你能看到类似的事情。据我所知，最初的 releases 被做得（某种程度上）safe。但人们当然很快就找到了让它们变得 unsafe 的办法，可以用来 generating 未经当事人同意的 deep fake porn 或 gore images。而且，目前正在进行各式各样的 finetuning AI systems 的 work，包括从 memory 中 remove items、向 memory 中 add items、remove RLHF、poison data 等等。让一个 safe AI system 变得 unsafe，似乎远比 making 一个 safe AI system 容易。这有点像我们打算只喝 100% 的魔法汤，由许多不同 groups 提供，并希望每一碗汤都做得绝对完美。

换句话说：即使我们不知怎的弄清了如何 build 大家都同意是 perfectly aligned 的 AI systems[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn11)，这也不可避免地会产生 non-aligned systems。问题的一部分在于，_AI systems 大多是由 ideas 构成的_。假设最早的 ASI systems 是由 OpenAnthropicDeepSafetyBlobCorp 做出的，而且它们绝对 100% safe（无论这是什么意思）。但其他人随后会用那些 ideas 来 make less safe systems，或是因为对 safe 应该意味着什么持有不同的 ideologies，或是仅仅出于无能。我认为 safe 的东西，极不可能与 Vladimir Putin 认为 safe 的东西相同；然而，如果我知道如何 build ASI systems，那么 Putin 肯定也能 build 这样的 systems。而他很可能会设置非常不同的 guardrails。这甚至不同于 nuclear weapons；对于后者，capital costs 和获取 fissionable materials 的限制，使 enforcement of non-proliferation 显得 plausible。在 AI 中，rapidly improving ideas 和 dropping compute costs 意味着，今天需要 massive resources 才能 build 的 systems，明天花点小钱就能 built。你可以从 GPT-3 这样的 systems 上看到这一点，几年前它们还需要巨额资金和庞大的 teams；现在，小型 open source groups 用不多的 budgets 就能获得 better results。

总而言之：许多人正在尝试弄清_如何_ align systems。即使 successful，这些 efforts 也会：(a) 通过让这样的 systems 对 customers 和 governments 更具吸引力，并让 investors 兴奋，accelerate 它们的 widespread use 和 proliferation；但随后 (b) 又会被一些人 easily circumvented，而他们对“safe”的理解可能与你我非常非常不同。其中会包括怀有恶意的 governments，以及 criminal 或 terrorist organizations。

**“这是否意味着，你反对这类关于 alignment 的 practical work？”** 不！不完全是。相反，我是在指出一个 _alignment dilemma_：你是否应该 participate in practical、concrete alignment work，理由是只有做这样的 work，人类才有机会 build safe systems？还是应该避免 participate in 这样的 work，认为它是在 accelerating 一个几乎肯定是坏的 outcome，而 outcome 变好的 chances 却只有极小的（甚至 non-existent 的）improvement？请注意，这个 dilemma _并不_等同于如今常见的 assertion，即 alignment work 是 intrinsically accelerationist 的。相反，它提出的是另一个虽有 related 却不同的观点：如果你认真看待 ASI xrisk，那么 alignment work 就是一个做也不对、不做也不对的 proposition。

不幸的是，我确实在 alignment dilemma 上左右为难！这是一个非常棘手的 dilemma，因为它根据某个 uncertain future event，分裂了本应是天然 collaborators 的两个 groups。除了关于 collaboration 和 politics 的这一点之外，它还有棘手的 epistemic implications。正如我前面指出的，当你 working on concrete practical problems 时，最容易取得 real progress，因为你在 studying real systems，可以 iteratively test 和 improve 你的 ideas。通过更加 abstract 的 work 来取得 progress 并非不可能——vulnerable world hypothesis[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn12)、existential risk[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn13) 等重要 ideas，就来自对 ASI xrisk 的 abstract work。但眼前的 practical work 是一个更容易取得 intellectual progress 的环境。

**“一些深思熟虑的 open source advocates 认为，对** **AGI 和 ASI 的追求如果公开进行，就会更 safer。** **你认同吗？”：** 其中许多人认为，tech industry 在过去 30 年里以一种 unhealthy 的方式 concentrated power，而 open source 能 mitigates 其中一部分 concentration of power。这有时是对的，但也可能失效：有时 open source systems 会被 large companies co-opted 或 captured，而这可能 protect 或 reinforce 那些 companies 的 power。假设在这里可以避免这种 effect，我当然同意，open source approaches 很可能有助于解决许多关于 AI systems 的 fairness 和 ethics 的重要眼前 concerns。此外，addressing 那些 concerns 是任何通向 alignment 的 long-term work 中必不可少的一部分。然而，不幸的是，从更长期来看，这个 argument 完全站不住脚。在短期内，open source 可能有助于以 healthy、更加 equitable 的方式 redistribute power。从长期来看，问题仅仅在于人类能获得的 power 太多：让它更广泛地可获取，并不能 solve 这个 problem，反而会让它变得更糟。

## ASI xrisk persuasion paradoxes（ASI xrisk 的说服悖论）

**“网上关于 ASI xrisk 的许多讨论似乎质量很低。你认为这是为什么？”** 我会间接回答这个问题。对于 science 和 mathematics 的大多数领域，我喜爱的一点是，nature 有时会迫使你改变对自己深信不疑的 fundamental 事物的看法。十几岁时，我在心理上抗拒 relativity 和 quantum mechanics 的 theories。两者都从 fundamental 层面挑战了我对世界的认识。time dilation 和 quantum indeterminacy 这样的 Ideas 看起来显然是错的！然而，经过许多挣扎，我最终意识到，错的是我对世界的 intuitions。这些并不是我想要得出的 conclusions：它们是被关于世界的许多、许多、许多 facts 所 _forced_ 的；如果我 reject time dilation 和 quantum indeterminacy 这样的 ideas，我就根本无法 explain 那些 facts。当然，这并不意味着 relativity 和 quantum mechanics 就是 physics 的最终定论。但它们至少是重要的踏脚石，帮助我们理解一个严重 violates 我们 basic intuitions 的世界。

我喜爱这种思维上的转变。我认为，这是 science 最非凡的成就之一。而在关于 ASI xrisk 的讨论中，让我沮丧的是，这样的转变发生得如此之少。试想，在 time dilation 这个问题上，如果 physicists 分裂成两个 tribes，一个 tribe 相信 time dilation，另一个不相信，会是什么情形。这就类似于今天的局面：AI experts（以及其他具备相关 expertise 的人）对于 AI systems 是否会在未来几十年里 pose xrisk 这个问题存在巨大分歧。严肃的 physicists 之间，并不存在关于 time dilation（或非常类似的现象）是否正在 occurring 的类似争论；这个问题以惊人的速度得到了明确解决。如今，任何这样的“争论”都属于 cranks 的领域[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn14)。

话虽如此，参与 ASI xrisk 讨论的人似乎确实会在小事上改变看法。但我很少见到有人在大事上改变看法。如果他们最初的 intuition 强烈倾向于“极少的 xrisk”，那么似乎唯一可能改变这种 intuition 的，就是他们的 tribe——如果他们的朋友想法不同——而不是 evidence。如果他们最初的 intuition 是“相当大的 xrisk”，情况也一样[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn15)。这听起来像是在批评那些人，但我的本意并非如此：我认为，面对我们薄弱的 evidence 和 arguments，这往往是合理的反应。换句话说：_我认为，目前支持和反对 ASI xrisk 的 arguments_ _都不太好。_ 这又听起来像是在批评已有工作的质量。我最初也曾（相当傲慢地）认为，事实很可能就是如此。但我后来觉得，这也并不属实。我只是认为，这些 problems 本身就极其困难。这让我想起 foundations of quantum mechanics，以及对 quantum measurement 的理解所面临的（仍在持续的）局面：这些 problems 如此困难，即便能力非凡、知识渊博的人也难以取得进展。

遗憾的是，在 ASI xrisk 这个问题上，结果就是人们并不是根据 evidence 和 argument 的质量来决定立场。相反，许多人似乎是根据 vibe、tribe、power 和 self-interest 作出选择。于是，公共讨论便建立在 persuasion、charisma、overconfidence 和 power 之上。是 Intuition pumps，而非真正的 understanding。拥有一个 plausible story，_并不_等同于拥有一个 compelling、well-tested model。我想，当某个立场建立在真正的 understanding 之上时，通常更容易令人信服，这确实是对的。（无论是支持还是反对 xrisk 的 case，都是如此。）但即便如此，如今这些 arguments 的质量就是没有那么强。

这一切都让我相当灰心。这就是我采用这种非正式的伪访谈形式的原因：正如开头所说，essay 或 paper 似乎暗示了超出合理程度的 confidence。不过，有一点帮助了我的思考，那就是逐渐理解了一些原因：为什么 analyze 支持和反对 xrisk 的 case 本身就很困难。

**“让人难以为支持或反对 xrisk 建立 case 的那些内在原因是什么？”** 有三个 _xrisk persuasion paradoxes_ 造成了这种困难。非常简要地说，它们是：

1.  为 xrisk 提出有力 argument 的最直接方式，是 convincingly describe 一条 detailed concrete pathway to extinction。你越 concretely describe 这些 steps，支持 xrisk 的 case 就越有力。但当然，improving 这种 argument 的任何“progress”，实际上都在 creates xrisk。“这里有 detailed instructions，说明几乎任何人如何都能 easily and inexpensively create 一个 antimatter bomb：\[convincing、verifiable instructions\]”，比起猜测“一个 ASI 也许能想出一种 cheap and easy recipe，几乎任何人都能据此 easily create antimatter bombs”，更能构成支持 xrisk 的 compelling argument。或者，你也许通过补全 ASI 可能必须完成的几个 intermediate steps 来取得“progress”。也许你 show，antimatter 比人们原本 _a priori_ 以为的要稍微容易 make 一些。当然，你应该完全避免提出或研究这样的 arguments。这就像老板让你说明为什么应该解雇你，只不过程度极端得多：这正是表现出 strategic incompetence 的好时候。支持 ASI xrisk 的 case 常常以非常 handwavy 和 incomplete 的方式提出；xrisk 的批评者随后便 dismiss 那些 vague arguments。我最近听到一位 AI investor 抱怨：“doomers 从来不会用 convincing detail 来 describe 事情会如何恶化。”我当然理解他们的挫败感；与此同时，这种 vagueness 却值得庆幸和保留。

2.  任何支持 xrisk 的 sufficiently strong argument，都很可能以 avert xrisk 的方式 alter human actions。矛盾的是，argument 越强，就越可能 avert xrisk。假设在 1930s 末或 1940s 初，有人发现并公布了一个真正 convincing argument，说明 nuclear weapons 会点燃世界的 atmosphere。如果真是如此，那么这些 bombs 被 developed 和 used 的可能性就会小得多。同样，随着我们对 human-caused climate change 的 understanding 不断 improved，它也逐渐引起了 human action 的巨大变化。众多例子之一是，近年来，我们对 renewable energy 的使用通常以每年约 15% 的 rate grown，而 fossil fuel 的 growth rate 每年不超过百分之几，有时还要低得多。这种 relative increase 并不完全源于对 climate 的担忧，但这些担忧无疑帮助推动了 renewables 的 investment 和 progress。

3.  根据 definition，任何我们能够 describe in detail 的 pathway to xrisk，都不 require superhuman intelligence。这个问题的一种变体经常出现在小说中：作者很难令人信服地写出远比自己聪明的角色。我很喜欢 Vernor Vinge 对此的描述：它是让 science fiction 难以创作的普遍障碍，用 Vinge 的话说，就是“一堵横亘在未来的不透明之墙”[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn16)。当然，这堵墙并非完全不透明，因为未来任何 ASI 都会受制于许多我们今天就能预见的 constraints。它们将无法 prove 2+2=5；将无法 violate laws of physics；它们很可能（除非出现非常意外的变化）无法 solve halting problem，也无法 solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time。

更广泛地说：无论对 ASI xrisk 的担忧是 well-founded，还是完全胡说，这三个 obstructions 都 hold。也就是说，它们可以被视为既妨碍提出支持 xrisk 的 good arguments，也妨碍提出反对 xrisk 的 good arguments[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn17)。而这种难以对 ASI xrisk 提出 dispositive argument 的困境，也让我们更清楚地理解，为什么人们如此经常转而依赖 vibe 和 tribe。

（顺便说一句，你也许注意到，persuasion paradox 1 asserts，某些类型的支持 xrisk 的 argument 可能会 _create_ xrisk，而 paradox 2 asserts，支持 xrisk 的 arguments 可能会 _avert_ xrisk。这可能显得 inconsistent，但当然，两者都可以为 true。更广泛的要点是：human action 是 contingent upon 我们的 understanding 的；这种 understanding 的任何变化，都可能改变我们的 actions，进而改变我们的 outcomes。argument 与 actions 和 outcomes 的这种 entangling，使 xrisk 很难 reason about。）

## Recipes for ruin（毁灭配方）

**“好吧，我想我明白你为什么认为，很难得出关于 xrisk 的 strong results。但肯定也** **有充分的 _a priori_ 理由值得怀疑**。**新 technologies 往往伴随着 risks，而 humanity 有时** **很难应对它们，** **但我们通常最终多少还是能做到，尽管** **代价往往过高。** **许多国家已经禁止使用 asbestos 和 fuel 中的 lead 之类的东西；** **我们已经大幅改进了** **应对 pesticides 以及 airline 和 automobile safety 的方式；** **虽然我们在 greenhouse gases 方面的改善（过于）缓慢，** **但我们确实在改善。** **你为什么担忧来自 ASI 的 xrisk？** **让 systems 变得 safe 的同类 approaches，难道在那里就不会奏效吗？”** 是的，这是一个很好的问题。不过，我会间接地切入。我希望你暂时完全忘掉 AI 和 ASI，转而想象自己被带到一个 all-knowing oracle 面前。你可以向这个 oracle 提出这样的问题：

> 你能给我一个 simple、easy-to-follow recipe，让一个聪明的青少年花费 $10,000、用一个月的时间，并且不引起任何 security concerns，就能 build 一个会 destroy 整个世界的 device 吗？

或者：

> 让一个聪明的青少年用一个月的时间，按照一个 easy-to-follow recipe，build 一个会 destroy 整个世界的 device，其 minimal cost 是多少？

这些是 _doomsday questions_，询问的是 _simple recipes for ruin_。我要再次强调，它们与现有的 AI systems，甚至与未来的 ASI systems，都没有直接关系。它们只是关于什么是 physically possible，以及利用今天的 technologies 能够 achieved 什么的问题。不过，我曾与许多人讨论过这样的 doomsday questions，令人惊讶的是，人们对这些问题的 intuitions 差异如此之大，而且这些 intuitions 似乎会影响他们如何看待 ASI xrisk。我稍后会回到它们与 ASI 的联系。不过，首先我想再描述几个要向我们的 oracle 提出的 variant questions：

> 是否存在一种由 1,000 个 protons、neutrons 和 electrons 构成的 arrangement，一旦被 created，就会 destroy 世界？

或者，如果我们想更悲观一些：

> 是否存在 single electron 的一种 state，\[比如说\]，一旦被 created，就会 destroy 世界？

事实上，假设你能以某种方式，把 single electron accelerate 到一万亿、一万亿、一万亿、一万亿、一万亿 electron volts。把它瞄准 Earth 会产生什么 impact？我曾在 Twitter 上[提出这个问题](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1677543571031982081)。虽然许多回复缺乏相关知识，但有几个人具有 physics 方面的相关 expertise，而我惊讶地发现，他们之间并没有达成一致。部分问题在于，这个 energy 远远超过 Planck energy[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn18)，而任何好的回答都有可能需要一种 theory of quantum gravity。不过，似乎几乎可以确定，如果这样的 particle 与 Earth interact，并将其 energy 中任何相当大的 fraction 在 Earth 各处广泛 dissipate，就会造成极大的破坏。

想象一个具有如此 high energy 的 single particle，似乎有点像作弊。因此，你也许可以改为问 oracle：

> 是否存在 single electron 的一种 easily-achievable low-energy state，\[比如说\]，一旦被 created，就可能 destroy 世界？对于 low energy，我们把 limit 设为一百万 electron volts。

现在，你也许会反驳：“哦，你难道不能 prove 那是不可能的吗？如果要造成大量 destruction，肯定需要大量 energy 吧？”事实上，这个 argument 非常不能令人满意。我们马上就会看到原因。首先，让我们 refine 前面的一个问题：

> 是否存在一种由 1,000 个 protons、neutrons 和 electrons 构成的 easily-achievable、low-energy arrangement，一旦被 created，就会 destroy 世界？

这样的 scenario 是 Kurt Vonnegut 的小说《猫的摇篮》的部分设定。他想象了一种 water 的 phase，称为 Ice-9，它具有几个不同寻常的 properties：(1) Ice-9 在普通 room temperature 下是 solid；而且 (2) 它是一种 seed crystal，当它与同样处于 room temperature 的普通 liquid water 接触时，会使普通 water 也 freeze，并 turn into 更多 Ice-9[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn19)。

这两个听起来无害的 properties 是 disastrous 的。这意味着，一旦 Ice-9 与 ocean 接触，它就会使世界上所有 oceans freeze over[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn20)，带来可怕的后果，不仅 wiping out human life，还会 wiping out biosphere 的很大一部分。这将是一场 world-ending catastrophe。而且，它无需非常庞大的 energy sources 就能毁灭世界：相反，它会利用 crystallization process，将一种 form of matter rearrange 成 energy 相近的另一种（远不那么理想的）form of matter。因此，向我们的 oracle 提出的一个更具体的 variant doomsday(-esque) question 是：

> 是否可能 prepare 一种 water 的 phase，使其具有类似 Kurt Vonnegut 小说《猫的摇篮》中 Ice-9 的 properties？

当然，每个读过《猫的摇篮》的人都会想，像 Ice-9 这样的东西是否可能 exist。我偶尔听到的一种 rebuttal[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn21) 是，如果 water 的这种 polymorph 是可能的，那么它应该早已偶然出现。更详细地说：Earth 的 oceans 含有大量四处飞速运动的 water molecules，它们会偶然呈现各种 configurations。如果存在 Ice-9 的 seed crystal，那么它到现在应该早已偶然出现，而 Earth 也应该已被 Ice-9 覆盖。事实并非如此，这就 implies 这种情况是 extremely unlikely 的。

这个 argument 多少让人安心。当然，它意味着，比如说，在未来一千年内的任何时候，Ice-9 都不太可能偶然出现。但它并不能完全让人安心：对于是否可能 _design_ 一种具有所需 properties 的 water molecules 的 configuration，它几乎没有说明什么。这样的 configuration 不必非常 complicated，就可能已经极不可能曾在 Earth 上偶然出现。

事实上，至少有点像 Ice-9 的东西，以前在 Earth 上_确实_出现过。大约 2.4 billion 年前，Earth 的 atmosphere 仅含有 trace amounts 的 oxygen。正是在那时，evolution 偶然 discovered 了现代 photosynthetic pathway，一些 cyanobacteria evolving 出将 CO2 和 sunlight convert 成 energy 和 oxygen 的 ability。这对那些 lifeforms 来说棒极了，为它们提供了好得多的 energy source。但对其他 lifeforms 来说，这很可能是 catastrophic 的，因为它们被 oxygen poisoned，导致了一次 mass extinction。那一定像是一场缓慢的 suffocation；它远慢于 Vonnegut 所描述的 Ice-9 takeover，但其笼罩范围或许更为全面[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn22)。这或许是 grey goo[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn23) 在 Earth 上的第一次胜利。

再一次重申我先前的观点：这些 oracle questions 没有一个与 AI 或 ASI 有任何直接关系。它们都只是关于 laws of physics 和现有 human capabilities 的问题。不过，正如我上面所说，我发现人们对于答案的 intuitions 差异极大。许多人根本无法想象 recipe for ruin 竟然是可能的。但有些人觉得这很容易想象。在觉得容易想象的人中，许多人之所以如此，是因为他们听过这类故事。但一个故事可以令人信服，却并不真实——想想那些使用 faster-than-light mechanisms 的 science fiction 故事，它们表面上看似合理，却会在我们的实际 universe 中造成严重问题[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn24)。因此，仅仅听过一个令人信服的故事，意义并不大。

更让我担忧的是，有一类人似乎经常认为 recipe for ruin 是 plausible 的：富于想象力、对 technology 的长远发展有广博而深刻认识的 scientists。我曾与其中许多人谈论这个问题，相当多的人（并非全部）认为这样的 recipes for ruin 是 plausible 的。这并不那么令人惊讶：他们往往很清楚，matter 的许多 properties 在被 discovery _之前_，会显得多么不可思议、多么 unlikely：从 fire 到 superconductivity；从 steam engine 到 fission chain reactions；从 hot air balloons 到 gravitational wave detectors；如此等等，举不胜举。Humanity 不断 discovering 潜藏在 matter 中的 possibilities，让从前不可能的事情变得容易。想象一下，向一个从未见过 fire 的人描述它——一种 substance，能 consumes 面前的一切，将其变成焦黑的 char；能以闪电般的速度移动；是 exothermic 的，这意味着它能够 self-sustaining；能够 destroy 大如国家的区域。这听起来会很荒谬。事实上，如果 Earth 的 atmosphere 中 oxygen 的 levels 高得多，fire 很可能（至少近似地）就是 recipe for ruin 的一个例子。要造成像 Tokyo 和 Dresden 轰炸中那种可怕、吞噬一切的 firestorms，就会容易得多。我们把 fire 视为理所当然，但它不是 recipe for ruin，似乎主要只是偶然[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn25)；当然，它已经很接近了。

我喜欢这种以 hypothetical oracles 为基础的 framing，原因之一在于，它避开了 ASI xrisk 讨论中常见的一个问题。在这类讨论中，人们往往为 ASIs 设定 Godlike capabilities。这自然且合理地引发了怀疑：“哦，ASI 不可能做到那个。”oracle framing 通过将事情分解成两个步骤，避开了这种回应。关于 recipes for ruin 是否 exist 的问题，是一个关于 laws of physics 和 human technological capabilities 的完全合理的问题。也许你能找到一个 argument，说明为什么不可能 exist 这样的 recipe for ruin。

不过，假设你认为这样的 recipe 的 exist 是 plausible 的。那么，一个自然而来的后续问题就是：一个 ASI 能否 discover 它[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn26)？即使 recipes for ruin exist，也绝不能显然认定 ASI's 就能 discover 它们：目前 human 对 universe 的 understanding，与 complete understanding 之间的 gap，看起来很可能（极其！）大。也许 ASI 能够 radically speed up scientific and technological progress，同时让 recipes for ruin 在可预见的未来仍安全地不为人知。然而，我的 intuition 是，对于 recipes for ruin 的存在，以及一个不久的将来的 ASI 将完全有能力 discovering 它们，我感到极其担忧。

**“ASIs finding recipes for ruin，是你对 xrisk 的主要担忧吗？”** 不，这只是看待这个问题的一个视角。它旨在说明，即使没有 rogue AIs taking over，非常 powerful 且 widely accessible 的 technologies 也可能带来哪些 kinds of dangers。但要想出许多其他 scenarios 也很容易；毫无疑问，你已经见过其中一些。对于 recipes for ruin，你最终会陷入这样一种处境，寄希望于：(a) 这样的 recipes 不 exist；(b) 如果它们确实 exist，ASIs 将无法 develop 它们；(c) 如果它们能够 find 它们，alignment guardrails 将是如此 robust 且 impermeable，以至于 humans 实际上无法 learn 这样的 recipes；(d) 如果 guardrails 可以被 crossed，那么没有任何 human 会想要或有 capability 去 use 它们。不幸的是，我对所有这些都深表怀疑。如果造成 mass destruction 就像从 Walmart 购买 gun 和 ammo（在 US 的大部分地区）一样容易，我想我们的处境会相当糟糕。至少，我想我们会开始看到_多得多_的 surveillance、authoritarianism 和 civil unrest，以及随之而来的 demagogues 的崛起。我指的不是 Trump 那些年在 US 看到的那种。我指的是 Stalin-Gulag-or-worse 风格。而这还是 /optimistic/（！）的 scenario。pessimistic scenario 要糟糕得多。

## ASI 会在多大程度上 speed up science and technology？experiment 何时是 bottleneck？一个 ASI 何时能通过 think 而获得新的 discoveries 和新的 capabilities？

**“那么看来，你的担忧很大程度上在于 ASI 的 ability，即** **speed up science and technology。你认为这会发生到什么程度？”** 确实如此，我的担忧很大程度上就在这里。这让我个人感到（非常）不自在：我职业生涯的大部分时间都在关注如何 speed up science and technology。而现在，我发现自己开始怀疑，我是不是一直站错了队[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn27)。回应你关于 ASI 会在多大程度上 speed up science and technology 的问题：一个有用的 model 是从三个 barriers 来看。有一个 _intellectual barrier_：也许有些 discoveries 之所以 difficult，仅仅是因为即使对一个 ASI 来说，think the right thoughts 也具有挑战性。例如，即使一个 ASI 在 solving NP-hard problems 时也会遇到困难，这似乎是 plausible 的。许多 technical design 的 problems，或 finding good arguments（例如 mathematical proofs）的 problems，都具有这种 character，至少 heuristically 如此：它们很容易被 verify as correct，却很难 find。换句话说：虽然对于几乎任何 problem，一个 ASI 都可能比一个人类具有强大得多的 intellectually capable 程度，它仍可能远远不及一个 omniscient oracle[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn28)。humanity 与 omniscience 之间的 gap 是 vast 的！

第二个 barrier 是 _resource barrier_。尽管那些试图 discover Higgs boson 的人非常 smart，他们也没能利用这些 smarts 来避免 constructing 一个非常 large、非常 expensive 的 particle accelerator。当然，有时你可以 tradeoff resources and intelligence：例如，intelligence 确实使我们能够 build 好得多的 particle detectors，无疑 reducing 了 discovering Higgs 的 cost。也许 intelligence 本可以被用来进一步 speed things up，或者更快地 acquire resources[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn29)。

一个特别值得关注的 barrier 是第三个 barrier，即 _experimental barrier_。人们经常指出，experiment 是 AI-enabled scientific progress 中的一个 bottleneck。过去，一些 xrisk 的 proponents 采取过一种 naive view，认为一个 ASI 通过“仅仅 thinking”就能 radically speed up 所有 scientific discovery。Experienced scientists 随后反驳说，这很荒谬，仅仅非常、非常 smart 并不能 eliminate 对 experiment 的 need。事实上，我认为，对于 experiment 何时会是 bottleneck、何时不会，我们可以说出一些有意思的东西。

**“那么：experiment 何时会是 bottleneck？”** 假设我们拥有一个 perfect theory of everything 和 infinite computational power。即使拥有这些 resources，我们从事（比如）medicine 的 ability 仍会受到令人意外的 constrained。原因在于，我们根本不能仅凭这些 resources 来 deduce human body 的 nature（无论是 in general，还是在一个 specific instance 中）：它在极大程度上 depends on contingent facts：我们的 evolutionary history、origin of life、我们的父母、我们成长的 environment，等等。换句话说：你不能利用 pure theory 来 discover，human beings 是由 DNA、RNA、ribosome 等 built out of 的。这些是我们需要在世界中 observe 的 experimental facts。

确实，我们不断在 human body 中 discovering 此前不知道的完整 _systems_；如果未来我们 discover 许多新的 systems，我不会感到惊讶。从许多方面看，medicine 的 history 可以被看作一段 experimentally developing 越来越好的 model 的 history，这个 model 描述 human beings 如何 work，以及用来 change 这一点的 interventions。你可能会反驳说，只要有 sufficient computational power 和一个 theory of everything，我们至少可以从 pure theory 中 deduce DNA、RNA、ribosome（_等等_）的 _possibility_。在满足某些 assumptions 的前提下，我愿意接受这一点[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn30)。但这并不能 single them out，认定它们就是 human beings 中实际存在的东西。为此，你需要大量 experimental observation[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn31)。

这种 bottleneck 在任何关于世界的 historically contingent facts 对某个 phenomenon 产生 crucially impact 的情形下都 holds。因此，例如，这一 observation 会 affects 我们对 Earth's climate 如何 works 的 understanding，也会 affects 我们对 psychology、astrophysics 以及许多其他 sciences 的 understanding。在每一种情况下，我们都必须进行 observations，看看世界中实际存在什么；无论我们多么 clever，仅仅 think 都是不够的。这并不意味着我们不能 reduce 对 experiments 的 need。但在许多 sciences 中，关于世界的 contingent facts 是 dominate 的。

**“那么，ASI 何时 _会_ 能够通过 think 而获得新的 discoveries？”** 上述情况还有另一面，那就是，可以预期 ASI 会在我们已经拥有 extremely accurate predictive theories 的情形下 excel；contingencies 已经是 known 的，并且已被详细 incorporated into theory。确实，已经有一些案例，人类利用这些 theories 获得了巨大优势，取得了 substantial 的进一步“progress”[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn32)，主要仅通过 thinking（“theory and/or simulation”），或许再辅以少量 experiment：

-   最早的 atomic bombs 在相当大程度上是利用 theory 和 simulation 来 designed 的，其基础是 1930s 期间 developed 的 neutrons 和 nucleus 的 models。Experiment 仍是 required 的，但 theorists 的 thinking 在如此大的程度上 drove 了 bomb 的 design，这很了不起。
-   最早的 hydrogen bombs 更加 heavily relied on theory，部分原因是 atomic bomb 内部的 conditions——它被用来 trigger hydrogen bomb——如此 unusual，以至于难以 study experimentally。确实，在 ENIAC computer 上 carried out 的第一次 calculation 就是 hydrogen bomb 的 theoretical simulation。
-   第一架 stealth fighter 主要通过 theory 和 simulation 来 designed，尽管这也结合了一些 physical experimentation。
-   Bose-Einstein condensation 仅通过 theory 就被 predicted in advance。有人告诉我，在一次研讨会上，“emergence”一词的创始人 Phil Anderson 曾说，emergent phenomena 如此 surprising，以至于不可能 predict in advance。研讨会的一位听众说：“那 Bose-Einstein condensation 呢？”Anderson 立即回道，那不算，因为 Einstein 是个天才[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn33)。
-   LIGO 在惊人的程度上由 theory 和 simulation 所 driven，并 relied upon 它们；仍然需要大量 experimental input 来 characterize 和 suppress noise sources，因为这些 noise sources 事先并未被 fully understood 或 characterized。
-   许多 remarkable phenomena 已经基于 extant theories 被 predicted in advance，包括 lasing、quantum Hall（但不包括 fractional quantum Hall）effect、quantum teleportation、topological quantum computing，以及其他 phenomena。
-   把 computers 内部引人注目的新 phenomena 看作这种 general pattern 的 examples，是一件耐人寻味的事。例如，在我看来，public-key cryptography 就是一个完全通过 theory 获得 scientific discovery 的 instance，它 grounded in 某个 extant theoretical system（在这个案例中，是 Turing machine theory of computation，以及 cryptography 的一些 broad notions）。

当这种 predictive theory 可以开展时，我们很容易说，自己是在一个 engineering regime 中 operating。但这并不对。从 public-key cryptography 的 discovery 中，你可以看到这一点：与其说它是 engineering，不如说它是对一个全新 phenomenon 的 discovery；在某种意义上，这个 phenomenon 隐藏在已有的关于 computation 和 cryptography 的 ideas 之中。或者，考虑这个问题：我们能否仅从 Schroedinger equation 就 predicted liquid water 的 existence——以及 Navier-Stokes equations、turbulence 等 phenomena？在过去 20 年里，从 first principles 出发对 water 的 study，一直是 physics 中一个令人意外地活跃的 research 领域。然而，据我所知，我们甚至还无法 deduce Navier-Stokes equations，更不用说其他值得关注的 phenomena[34](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn34)。我认为，当这样的 emergent phenomena 和 emergent principles 被 discovered 时，这主要是 science（而非 engineering）。在我看来，一个 fundamental question 是：

**“ASI 何时能从 accurate underlying theories 中 deduce emergent phenomena 和 emergent laws？”** 当然，我不知道这个问题的答案。但只要 ASI _能够_ 作出这样的 deductions，就会赋予它 qualitatively new types of power and capability，超出人类目前所拥有的。虽然我无法确定，但我预计这将很常见：我们现有的 theories 中 latent 着许许多多这样的新 types of emergent phenomena，而 ASI 最终会 discover 许多这样的 phenomena。确实，我预计，借助 AlphaFold 2 和类似的 systems，我们已经相当接近这一点了。例如，也许用于 protein design 的 AI-driven systems 正在 discovering 这种 design 的新 principles；甚至，也许它们已经在这样做，而它们的人类创造者却并不知情。

## 结论

ASI xrisk 是 complicated 的，而这些笔记省略了许多重要问题。其中一个问题是 AI 和 ASI 的 positive impact。关于 ASI xrisk 的大多数讨论几乎完全聚焦于事情严重出错的 scenarios。这可以理解，但却是一个错误[35](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn35)。任何 harms 在很大程度上（尽管并非全部）都会是追求那些推动 AI 的 positive goals 所带来的 side effect。当然，我认识的大多数致力于 ASI 的人不只是想在个人层面获得成功，他们也渴望一个更美好、更健康、更安全、更公正的世界[36](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn36)。因此，任何 thorough analysis 都必须深深植根于对 AI 的 positive goals、methods 和 impacts 的理解，植根于一个 Great AI Flourishing for Humanity 的愿景。我已经就此写过[一篇短文](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html)，也有大量笔记，但一份整合起来的阐述会好得多。同时 [exploring multiple scenarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scenario_planning) 的一个好处是，理解 optimistic scenarios 会丰富并改变一个人对 negative scenarios 的理解；反过来也一样。另一个好处是，如果像我这样担忧的人其实不该担忧，那么这样做能更清楚地呈现利害所在：放慢 AI 的发展可能是一项让人类付出极高代价的活动。

和许多人一样，我也被 ChatGPT 和 GPT4 震撼了。尽管 GPT4 还远远达不到 ASI，它却使更好地理解 ASI xrisk 的需要变得迫切。这些笔记是我对 ASI xrisk 的第一次深入探索，写下它们是为了帮助我消化此前的工作，也贡献一些自己的想法。我的进展比预想的更慢，而我至今仍不知道该如何应对 ASI xrisk，这让我感到沮丧。在这里，一个人怎样才能产生 high-leverage impact？一位睿智的朋友兼同事 Catherine Olsson 曾说过，无论做什么，无论你对整体局势有多么 pessimistic，positive progress 都建立在 optimistic plans 之上。即便面对像 ASI xrisk 这样令人望而生畏的挑战，这一点也成立：optimism 不是天真的希望，而是一种用于行动的 constructive framework。以更深入的理解为目标，似乎是迈向这样一种 framework 的有益一步。

_Earlier this year I decided to take a few weeks to figure out what I think about the existential risk from Artificial Superintelligence (ASI xrisk). It turned out to be much more difficult than I thought. After several months of reading, thinking, and talking with people, what follows is a discussion of a few observations arising during this exploration, including:_

_-   Three ASI xrisk persuasion paradoxes, which make it intrinsically difficult to present strong evidence either for or against ASI xrisk. The lack of such compelling evidence is part of the reason there is such strong disagreement about ASI xrisk, with people often (understandably) relying instead on prior beliefs, self-interest, and tribal reasoning to decide their opinions.
-   The alignment dilemma: should someone concerned with xrisk contribute to concrete alignment work, since it's the only way we can hope to build safe systems; or should they refuse to do such work, as contributing to accelerating a bad outcome? Part of a broader discussion of the accelerationist character of much AI alignment work, so capabilities / alignment is a false dichotomy.
-   The doomsday question: are there recipes for ruin -- simple, easily executed, immensely destructive recipes that could end humanity, or wreak catastrophic world-changing damage?
-   What bottlenecks are there on ASI speeding up scientific discovery? And, in particular: is it possible for ASI to discover new levels of emergent phenomena, latent in existing theories?_

## Introduction

What follows is presented in an interview format. It's not actually an interview, but rather an idealized distillation of conversations I've had with many people. I chose this unusual form after struggling with a more conventional essay or paper form; I think such forms imply more confidence than warranted in most discussions about ASI xrisk. An interview seems a more appropriate mix of evidence, argument, and opinion. Some of the material covers background that will be known to people well read on ASI xrisk[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn1). However, there are also novel contributions – for example, the discussion of emergence[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn2) and of the three xrisk persuasion paradoxes – that I believe are of interest.

**"Do you believe there is an xrisk from ASI?"** Yes, I do. I don't have strong feelings about how large that risk is, beyond being significant enough that it should be taken very seriously. ASI is likely to be both the most dangerous and the most enabling technology ever developed by humanity[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn3). In what follows I describe some of my reasons for believing this. I'll be frank: I doubt such arguments will change anyone's mind. However, that discussion will lay the groundwork for a discussion of some reasons why thoughtful people disagree so much in their opinions about ASI xrisk. As we'll see, this is in part due to differing politics and tribal beliefs, but there are also some fundamental epistemic reasons intrinsic to the nature of the problem.

**"So, what's your probability of doom?"** I think the concept is badly misleading. The outcomes humanity gets depend on choices we can make. We can make choices that make doom almost inevitable, on a timescale of decades – indeed, we don't need ASI for that, we can likely[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn4) arrange it in other ways (nukes, engineered viruses, …). We can also make choices that make doom extremely unlikely. The trick is to figure out what's likely to lead to flourishing, and to do those things. The term "probability of doom" began frustrating me after starting to routinely hear people at AI companies use it fatalistically, ignoring the fact that their choices can change the outcomes. "Probability of doom" is an example of a conceptual hazard[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn5) – a case where merely using the concept may lead to mistakes in your thinking. Its main use seems to be as marketing: if widely-respected people say forcefully that they have a high or low probability of doom, that may cause other people to stop and consider why. But I dislike concepts which are good for marketing, but bad for understanding; they foster collective misunderstanding, and are likely to eventually lead to collective errors in action.

**"I can't believe you take this ASI xrisk stuff seriously!":** I have (very lowkey!) worried about it since being a teenager in the 1980s. The only thing that's changed recently is the urgency. Growing up, I was mostly extremely optimistic about science and technology, but also worried about many concomitant problems: the ozone hole, nuclear weapons, acid rain, climate change, ecosystem collapse, overpopulation, grey goo, peak oil, and many other risks, including what I would today call ASI xrisk. Those are mostly not xrisks, but they are (or were) reasonably thought to be potentially catastrophic, affecting hundreds of millions or billions of people. Many of my teachers in school were very pessimistic about issues like these, and I absorbed some of that pessimism. For instance, I grew up thinking nuclear Armageddon was likely, especially after reading the book "The Cold and the Dark" as a ~14-year old, about the consequences of a large-scale nuclear war.

Later, in my twenties, I noticed that many of the direst predictions I worried about as a child or teenager didn't seem to have come true. Sometimes that was because the timescale was longer than a decade or two, but in some cases the issue had been largely addressed, or had faded out of collective consciousness. The ozone hole was, for example, largely addressed by the Montreal Protocol. Acid rain seems to have faded as a concern, for reasons I only partly understand. But such issues have been replaced in public consciousness by other existential worries; indeed, existential worry seems more conserved among humans than one might _a priori_ suspect. I gradually came to believe that there is often a type of _hubris in pessimism_: it's easy to confuse "I \[and my friends\] don't know of a solution" to some big problem with "there is no solution". Often, civilizational problems are solved in ways anticipated by very few people in advance.

As an example, in the 1970s and 1980s there was much fear about "the population bomb", the apparently unstoppable and likely calamitous exponential growth of the world population[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn6). This very real fear is hard to remember today, when the population bomb has been mostly defused by urbanization, birth control, and improved food production and distribution – indeed, many people are today more concerned about the prospect of a population implosion. But in the 1970s and 1980s many people had feelings of doom about overpopulation akin to today's climate or AI pessimists. As another example, over the past decade I've been pleasantly surprised by progress on climate and renewable energy, especially the enormous drop in prices of solar and wind energy, of batteries, and of other modes of decarbonization. There's still a huge amount to be done, and I know climate pessimists will poo-poo my optimism; still, I think the optimism is correct. There will be (indeed, already are) considerable impacts of climate change, but those impacts will be far less severe than many suspected ten years ago.

For decades I hoped and believed a similar kind of unexpected progress would make worries about ASI xrisk obsolete. And so I put aside my concerns; if anything, I was very optimistic about the long-run prospects for AI, though I thought major progress was likely some way off[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn7). Well, major progress is now here[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn8). And today the situation looks to me to be very challenging; rather than the problem being solved, it looks like it's getting rapidly worse.

**"That wasn't an argument for ASI xrisk!"** True, it wasn't. Indeed, one of the things that took me quite a while to understand was that there are very good reasons it's a mistake to expect a bulletproof argument either for _or_ against xrisk. I'll come back to why that is later. I will make some broad remarks now though. I believe that humanity _can_ make ASI, and that we are likely to make it soon – within three decades, perhaps much sooner, absent a disaster or a major effort at slowdown. Many able people and many powerful people are pushing very hard for it. Indeed: enormous _systems_ are starting to push for it. Some of those people and systems are strongly motivated by the desire for power and control. Many are strongly motivated by the desire to contribute to humanity. They correctly view ASI as something which will do tremendous good, leading to major medical advances, materials advances, educational advances, and more. I say "advances", which has come to be something of a marketing term, but I don't mean _Nature_\-press-release-style-(usually)-minor-advances. I mean polio-vaccine-transforming-millions-of-lives-style-advances, or even larger. Such optimists view ASI as a technology likely to produce incredible abundance, shared broadly, and thus enriching _everyone_ in the world.

But while that is wonderful and worth celebrating, those advances seem to me likely to have a terrible dark side. There is a sense in which human understanding is _always_ dual use: genuine depth of understanding makes the universe more malleable to our will in a very general way. For example, while the insights of relativity and quantum mechanics were crucial to much of modern molecular biology, medicine, materials, computing, and in many other areas, they also helped lead to nuclear weapons. I don't think this is an accident: such dual uses are very near inevitable when you greatly increase your understanding of the stuff that makes up the universe.

As an aside on the short term – the next few years – I expect we're going to see rapidly improving multi-modal foundation models which mix language, mathematics, images, video, sound, action in the world, as well as many specialized sources of data, things like genetic data about viruses and proteins, data from particle physics, sensor data from vehicles, from the oceans, and so on. Such models will "know" a tremendous amount about many different aspects of the world, and will also have a raw substrate for abstract reasoning – things like language and mathematics; they will get at least some transfer between these domains, and will be far, far more powerful than systems like GPT-4. This does not mean they will yet be true AGI or ASI! Other ideas will almost certainly be required; it's possible those ideas are, however, already extant. No matter what, I expect such models will be increasingly powerful as aids to the discovery of powerful new technologies. Furthermore, I expect it will be very, very difficult to obtain the "positive" capabilities, without also obtaining the negative. You can't just learn the "positive" consequences of quantum mechanics; they come as a package deal with the negative. Guardrails like RLHF will help suppress the negative, but as I discuss later it will also be relatively simply to remove those guardrails.

Returning to the medium-and-longer-term: many people who care about ASI xrisk are focused on ASI taking over, as some kind of successor species to humanity. But even focusing on ASI purely as a tool[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn9), ASI will act as an enormous accelerant on our ability to understand, and thus will be an enormous amplifier of our power. This will be true both for individuals and for groups. This will result in many, many very good things. Unfortunately, it will _also_ result in many destructive things, no matter how good the guardrails. It is by no means clear that questions like "Is there a trivially easy-to-follow recipe to genocide \[a race\]?" or "Is there a trivially easy-to-follow recipe to end humanity?" don't have affirmative answers, which humanity is merely (currently and fortunately) too stupid to answer, but which an ASI could answer.

Historically, we have been very good at evolving guardrails to curb and control powerful new technologies. That is genuine cause for optimism. However, I worry that we won't be able to evolve guardrails sufficient to the increase in this case. The nuclear buildup from the 1940s through the 1980s is a cautionary example: reviewing the evidence it is clear we have only just barely escaped large-scale nuclear war so far – and it's still early days! It seems likely that ASI will create many such threats, in parallel, on a much faster timescale, and far more accessible to individuals and small groups. The world of intellect simply provides vastly scalable leverage: if you can create one artificial John von Neumann, then you can produce an army of them, some of whom may be working for people we'd really rather not have access to that kind of capacity. Many people like to talk about making ASI systems safe and aligned; quite apart from the difficulty in doing that (or even sensibly defining that) it seems it must be done for _all_ ASI systems, ever. That seems to require an all-seeing surveillance regime, a fraught path. Perhaps such a surveillance regime can be implemented not merely by government or corporations against the populace, but in a much more omnidirectional way, a form of ambient sousveillance[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn10).

## On practical alignment with real systems

**"What do you think about the practical alignment work that's going on – RLHF, Constitutional AI, and so on?"**: The work is certainly technically interesting. It's interesting to contrast to prior systems, like Microsoft's Tay, which could [easily be made](https://www.theverge.com/2016/3/24/11297050/tay-microsoft-chatbot-racist) to do many terrible things. You can make ChatGPT and Claude do terrible things as well, but you have to work harder; the alignment work on those systems has created somewhat stable guardrails. This kind of work is also striking as a case where safety-oriented people have done detailed technical work to improve real systems, with hard feedback loops and clear criteria for success and failure, as opposed to the abstract philosophizing common in much early ASI xrisk work. It's certainly much easier to improve your ideas in the former case, and easier to fool yourself in the latter case.

With all that said: _practical alignment work is extremely accelerationist_. If ChatGPT had behaved like Tay, AI would still be getting minor mentions on page 19 of _The New York Times_. These alignment techniques play a role in AI somewhat like the systems used to control when a nuclear bomb goes off. If such bombs just went off at random, no-one would build nuclear bombs, and there would be no nuclear threat to humanity. Practical alignment work makes today's AI systems far more attractive to customers, far more usable as a platform for building other systems, far more profitable as a target for investors, and far more palatable to governments. The net result is that practical alignment work is accelerationist. There's an extremely thoughtful essay by [Paul Christiano](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/vwu4kegAEZTBtpT6p/thoughts-on-the-impact-of-rlhf-research), one of the pioneers of both RLHF and AI safety, where he addresses the question of whether he regrets working on RLHF, given the acceleration it has caused. I admire the self-reflection and integrity of the essay, but ultimately I think, like many of the commenters on the essay, that he's only partially facing up to the fact that his work will considerably hasten ASI, including extremely dangerous systems.

Over the past decade I've met many AI safety people who speak as though "AI capabilities" and "AI safety/alignment" work is a dichotomy. They talk in terms of wanting to "move" capabilities researchers into alignment. But most concrete alignment work _is_ capabilities work. It's a false dichotomy, and another example of how a conceptual error can lead a field astray. Fortunately, many safety people now understand this, but I still sometimes see the false dichotomy misleading people, sometimes even causing systematic effects through bad funding decisions.

A second point about alignment is that no matter how good the guardails, they are intrinsically unstable, and easily removed. I often meet smart AI safety people who have inventive schemes they hope will make ASI systems safe. Maybe they will, maybe they won't. But the more elaborate the scheme, the more unstable the situation. If you have a magic soup recipe which requires 123 different ingredients, but all must be mixed accurate to within 1% weight, and even a single deviation will make it deadly poisonous, then you really shouldn't cook and eat your "safe" soup. One of the undercooks forgets to put in a leek, and poof, there goes the village.

You see something like this with Stable Diffusion. Initial releases were, I am told, made (somewhat) safe. But, of course, people quickly figured out how to make them unsafe, useful for generating deep fake porn or gore images of non-consenting people. And there's all sorts of work going on finetuning AI systems, including to remove items from memory, to add items into memory, to remove RLHF, to poison data, and so on. Making a safe AI system unsafe seems to be far easier than making a safe AI system. It's a bit as though we're going on a diet of 100% magic soup, provided by a multitude of different groups, and hoping every single soup has been made absolutely perfectly.

Put another way: even if we somehow figure out how to build AI systems that everyone agrees are perfectly aligned[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn11), that will inevitably result in non-aligned systems. Part of the problem is that _AI systems are mostly made up of ideas_. Suppose the first ASI systems are made by OpenAnthropicDeepSafetyBlobCorp, and they are absolutely 100% safe (whatever that means). But those ideas will then be used by other people to make less safe systems, either due to different ideologies about what safe should mean, or through simple incompetence. What I regard as safe is very unlikely to be the same as what Vladimir Putin regards as safe; and yet if I know how to build ASI systems, then Putin must also be able to build such systems. And he's likely to put very different guardrails in. It's not even the same as with nuclear weapons, where capital costs and limited access to fissionable materials makes enforcement of non-proliferation plausible. In AI, rapidly improving ideas and dropping compute costs mean that systems which today require massive resources to build can be built for tuppence tomorrow. You see this with systems like GPT-3, which just a few years ago cost large sums of money and took large teams; now, small open source groups can get better results with modest budgets.

Summing up: a lot of people are trying to figure out _how_ to align systems. Even if successful, such efforts will: (a) accelerate the widespread use and proliferation of such systems, by making them more attractive to customers and governments, and exciting to investors; but then (b) be easily circumvented by people whose idea of "safe" may be very, very different than yours or mine. This will include governments and criminal or terrorist organizations of ill intent.

**"Does this mean you oppose such practical work on alignment?"** No! Not exactly. Rather, I'm pointing out an _alignment dilemma_: do you participate in practical, concrete alignment work, on the grounds that it's only by doing such work that humanity has a chance to build safe systems? Or do you avoid participating in such work, viewing it as accelerating an almost certainly bad outcome, for a very small (or non-existent) improvement in chances the outcome will be good? Note that this dilemma _isn't_ the same as the by-now common assertion that alignment work is intrinsically accelerationist. Rather, it's making a different-albeit-related point, which is that if you take ASI xrisk seriously, then alignment work is a damned-if-you-do-damned-if-you-don't proposition.

Unfortunately, I am genuinely torn on the alignment dilemma! It's a very nasty dilemma, since it divides two groups who ought to be natural collaborators, on the basis of some uncertain future event. And apart from that point about collaboration and politics, it has nasty epistemic implications. It is, as I noted earlier, easiest to make real progress when you're working on concrete practical problems, since you're studying real systems and can iteratively test and improve your ideas. It's not impossible to make progress through more abstract work – there are important ideas like the vulnerable world hypothesis[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn12), existential risk[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn13) and so on, which have come out of the abstract work on ASI xrisk. But immediate practical work is a far easier setting in which to make intellectual progress.

**"Some thoughtful open source advocates believe the pursuit of** **AGI and ASI will be safer if carried out in the open.** **Do you buy that?":** Many of those people argue that the tech industry has concentrated power in an unhealthy way over the past 30 years. And that open source mitigates some of that concentration of power. This is sometimes correct, though it can fail: sometimes open source systems are co-opted or captured by large companies, and this may protect or reinforce the power of those companies. Assuming this effect could be avoided here, I certainly agree that open source approaches might well help with many important immediate concerns about the fairness and ethics of AI systems. Furthermore, addressing those concerns is an essential part of any long-term work toward alignment. Unfortunately, though, this argument breaks down completely over the longer term. In the short term, open source may help redistribute power in healthy, more equitable ways. Over the long term the problem is simply too much power available to human beings: making it more widely available won't solve the problem, it will make it worse.

## ASI xrisk persuasion paradoxes

**"A lot of online discussion of ASI xrisk seems of very low quality. Why do you think that is?"** I'll answer that indirectly. Something I love about most parts of science and mathematics is that nature sometimes forces you to change your mind about fundamental things that you really believe. When I was a teenager my mind recoiled at the theories of relativity and quantum mechanics. Both challenged my sense of the world in fundamental ways. Ideas like time dilation and quantum indeterminacy seemed obviously wrong! And yet I eventually realized, after much wrestling, that it was my intuitions about the world that were wrong. These weren't conclusions I wanted to come to: they were _forced_, by many, many, many facts about the world, facts that I simply cannot explain if I reject ideas like time dilation and quantum indeterminacy. This doesn't mean relativity and quantum mechanics are the last word in physics, of course. But they are at the very least important stepping stones to making sense of a world that wildly violates our basic intuitions.

I love this kind of change in thinking. I think it's one of the most remarkable achievements of science. And something I find depressing in discussions of ASI xrisk is how little it's happening. Imagine if, with time dilation, physicists had split into two tribes, with one tribe believing in time dilation, and the other not. It'd be analogous to the situation today, where AI experts (and others with relevant expertise) disagree enormously on the question of whether AI systems pose xrisk over the next few decades. There is no similar debate amongst serious physicists about whether time dilation (or something very similar) is occurring; that question was decisively settled with remarkable speed. Any "debate" today is the realm of cranks[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn14).

With that said, people in the discussion of ASI xrisk do seem to change their mind about small things. But I see few people changing their mind about big things. If their initial intuition is strongly "very little xrisk", then the only thing that seems likely to change that intuition is their tribe – if their friends think differently – not evidence. Ditto if their initial intuition is "considerable xrisk"[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn15). This sounds like a critique of those people, but it's not meant that way: I think it's an often-sensible response to the weakness of our evidence and arguments. Put another way: _I think the current arguments both_ _for and against ASI xrisk aren't very good._ That, in turns, sounds like a critique of the quality of work done. That's (rather snottily) what I initially thought was likely to be true. But I've come to think that's not true, either. I just think the problems are intrinsically extremely difficult. It reminds me of the (ongoing) situation with the foundations of quantum mechanics, and the understanding of quantum measurement, where the problems are so difficult that even extraordinarily capable and well-informed people have trouble making progress.

Unfortunately, in the case of ASI xrisk the result is that people aren't deciding their positions due to the quality of the evidence and argument. Instead, many seem to be choosing based on vibe, tribe, power, and self-interest. And so you end up with a public conversation based on persuasion and charisma and overconfidence and power. Intuition pumps, not genuine understanding. Having a plausible story is _not_ the same as having a compelling, well-tested model. I suppose it's true that it's often easier to be convincing when a position is grounded in genuine understanding. (This would be true for either the case for or against xrisk.) But still: the quality of the arguments today just isn't that strong.

I've found this all quite discouraging. It's why I've used this informal pseudo-interview form: as I said at the start, an essay or paper seems to imply more confidence than warranted. However, one thing that has helped my thinking is gradually understanding some reasons it's intrinsically hard to analyze the case for and against xrisk.

**"What are those intrinsic reasons it's hard to make a case either for or against xrisk?"** There are three _xrisk persuasion paradoxes_ that make it difficult. Very briefly, these are:

1.  The most direct way to make a strong argument for xrisk is to convincingly describe a detailed concrete pathway to extinction. The more concretely you describe the steps, the better the case for xrisk. But of course, any "progress" in improving such an argument actually creates xrisk. "Here's detailed instructions for how almost anyone can easily and inexpensively create an antimatter bomb: \[convincing, verifiable instructions\]" makes a more compelling argument for xrisk than speculating that: "An ASI might come up with a cheap and easy recipe by which almost anyone can easily create antimatter bombs." Or perhaps you make "progress" by filling in a few of the intermediate steps an ASI might have to do. Maybe you show that antimatter is a little easier to make that one might _a priori_ have thought. Of course, you should avoid making such arguments entirely, or working on it. It's a much more extreme version of your boss asking you to make a case for why you should be fired: that's a very good time to exhibit strategic incompetence. The case for ASI xrisk is often made in very handwavy and incomplete ways; critics of xrisk then dismiss those vague arguments. I recently heard an AI investor complain: "The doomers never describe in convincing detail how things will go bad". I certainly understand their frustration; at the same time, that vagueness is something to celebrate and preserve.

2.  Any sufficiently strong argument for xrisk will likely alter human actions in ways that avert xrisk. The stronger the argument, paradoxically, the more likely it is to avert xrisk. Suppose that in the late 1930s or early 1940s someone had found and publicized a truly convincing argument that nuclear weapons would set the world's atmosphere on fire. If that was the case then the bombs would have been much less likely to have been developed and used. Similarly, as our understanding of human-caused climate change has improved it has gradually caused enormous changes in human action. As one of many examples, in recent years our use of renewable energy has typically grown at a rate of about 15% per year, whereas the rate of fossil fuel growth is no more than a few percent per year, and sometimes much less. That relative increase is not due entirely to climate fears, but those fears have certainly helped drive investment and progress in renewables.

3.  By definition, any pathway to xrisk which we can describe in detail doesn't require superhuman intelligence. A variation of this problem shows up often in fiction: it is difficult for authors to convincingly write characters who are far smarter than the author. I love Vernor Vinge's description of this as a general barrier making it hard to write science fiction, "an opaque wall across the future" in Vinge's terms[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn16). Of course, it's not an entirely opaque wall, in that any future ASI will be subject to many constraints we can anticipate today. They won't be able to prove that 2+2=5; they won't be able to violate the laws of physics; they likely (absent very unexpected changes) won't be able to solve the halting problem or to solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time.

More broadly: these three obstructions hold regardless of whether concerns about ASI xrisk are well-founded or total baloney. That is, they can be regarded as obstructions to both making good arguments for and good arguments against xrisk[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn17). And that difficulty in making a dispositive argument about ASI xrisk makes it clearer why people so often fall back instead on vibe and tribe.

(Incidentally, you may note that persuasion paradox 1 asserts that certain types of argument for xrisk may _create_ xrisk, while paradox 2 asserts that arguments for xrisk may _avert_ xrisk. This may seem inconsistent, but of course both can be true. The broader point: human action is contingent upon our understanding; any change to that understanding may change our actions, and thus our outcomes. This entangling of argument with actions and outcomes makes xrisk difficult to reason about.)

## Recipes for ruin

**"Okay, I think I see why you believe it's difficult to obtain strong results about xrisk. But surely there** **are strong _a priori_ reasons to doubt**. **New technologies often carry risks, and humanity sometimes** **struggles to deal with them,** **but we usually more or less get there in the end, albeit** **often with a too-high cost.** **Many countries have banned things like asbestos and lead in fuel;** **we've greatly improved how** **we deal with pesticides and airline and automobile safety;** **while we're (too) slowly improving on greenhouse gases,** **we are genuinely improving.** **Why are you concerned about xrisk from ASI?** **Won't the same sorts of approaches to making systems safe work there?"** Yeah, it's an excellent question. I'll approach it indirectly however. I want you to forget about AI and ASI entirely for a bit, and imagine instead that you have been placed in front of an all-knowing oracle. And you get to ask the oracle questions like:

> Can you give me a simple, easy-to-follow recipe, where with $10,000, a month's work, and without arousing any security concerns, a bright teenager can build a device that will destroy the entire world?

Or:

> What is the minimal cost where, with a month's work, and using an easy-to-follow recipe, a bright teenager can build a device that will destroy the entire world?

These are _doomsday questions_, asking for _simple recipes for ruin_. I emphasize again that they have nothing directly to do with existing AI systems, or even with future ASI systems. They are simply questions about what is physically possible, and what can be achieved with today's technologies. However, I've talked over such doomsday questions with many people, and it's surprising how much people's intuitions about such questions vary, and how those intuitions seem to influence how they think about ASI xrisk. I'll come back to the ASI connection later. First, though, I want to describe a few more variant questions for our oracle:

> Is there an arrangement of 1,000 protons, neutrons, and electrons that, if created, would destroy the world?

Or, if we want to be even more pessimistic:

> Is there a state of a single electron \[say\], that, if created, would destroy the world?

In fact, suppose you were somehow able to accelerate a single electron to a trillion, trillion, trillion, trillion, trillion electron volts. What would be the impact of aiming it at the Earth? I [asked this question](https://twitter.com/michael_nielsen/status/1677543571031982081) on Twitter. While many of the replies were poorly informed, a few people had relevant expertise in physics, and I was surprised to see that there wasn't agreement between them. Part of the issue is that this energy is far past the Planck energy[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn18), and it's plausible that any good answer would require a theory of quantum gravity. However, it seems almost certain that if such a particle were to interact with the Earth, and dissipate any substantial fraction of its energy widely through the Earth, it would be enormously disruptive.

It seems a bit like cheating to imagine a single particle of such high energy. So you might ask the oracle instead:

> Is there an easily-achievable low-energy state of a single electron \[say\], that, if created, could destroy the world? By low energy let us set the limit at a million electron volts.

Now, you might argue, "oh, can't you prove that's not possible? Surely if you're going to set off a lot of destruction you need a great deal of energy?" In fact, that argument is deeply unsatisfactory. We'll see why in a moment. First, let's refine one of our earlier questions:

> Is there an easily-achievable, low-energy arrangement of 1,000 protons, neutrons, and electrons that, if created, would destroy the world?

A scenario like this was part of the premise of Kurt Vonnegut's novel "Cat's Cradle". He imagined a phase of water, dubbed Ice-9, which had a few unusual properties: (1) Ice-9 is solid at ordinary room temperature; and (2) it's a type of seed crystal, which, when it comes into contact with ordinary liquid water, also at room temperature, causes the ordinary water to also freeze and turn into more Ice-9[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn19).

That innocuous-sounding pair of properties is disastrous. It means that once Ice-9 comes into contact with the ocean it will cause all the world's oceans to freeze over[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn20), with horrendous consequences, wiping out not just human life but much of the biosphere. It would be a world-ending catastrophe. And it would have ended the world with no need for very large energy sources: rather, it would be using the crystallization process to rearrange one form of matter into another (much less desirable) form of matter of similar energy. So a more specific variant doomsday(-esque) question for our oracle is:

> Is it possible to prepare a phase of water with properties like Ice-9 in Kurt Vonnegut's novel "Cat's Cradle"?

Of course, everyone who reads "Cat's Cradle" wonders whether something like Ice-9 can exist. A rebuttal which I've heard occasionally[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn21) is that if such a polymorph of water were possible, then it would already have occurred by chance. In more detail: the Earth's oceans contain lots of water molecules whizzing around, and taking on all sorts of configurations by chance. If there was a seed crystal for Ice-9, it would have occurred by chance by now, and the Earth would be covered in Ice-9. The fact it isn't implies this is extremely unlikely.

This argument is somewhat reassuring. Certainly, it means it's unlikely Ice-9 will occur by chance any time in the next thousand years, say. But it's not entirely reassuring: it says very little about whether or not it's possible to _design_ a configuration of water molecules with the required properties. Such a configuration wouldn't need to be very complicated before it was extremely unlikely to have ever occurred by chance on Earth.

In fact, something at least a little like Ice-9 _has_ occurred on Earth before. About 2.4 billion years ago the Earth's atmosphere contained just trace amounts of oxygen. It was at that time that evolution accidentally discovered the modern photosynthetic pathway, with some cyanobacteria evolving the ability to convert CO2 and sunlight into energy and oxygen. This was terrific for those lifeforms, giving them a much improved energy source. But it was very likely catastrophic for other lifeforms, who were poisoned by the oxygen, causing a mass extinction. It must have seemed like a slow suffocation; it was much slower than the takeover by Ice-9 described by Vonnegut, but it was perhaps even more enveloping[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn22). It was perhaps the first victory by a grey goo[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn23) on Earth.

Reiterating my earlier point yet again: none of these oracle questions has anything directly to do with AI or ASI. They're all just questions about the laws of physics, and existing human capabilities. Still, as I said above I find that people differ very strongly in their intuitions about the answers. Many people just can't conceive that a recipe for ruin is even possible. But some find it quite easy. Many of those who find it easy do so because they've heard stories in this vein. But a story can be compelling without being true – think of all the science fiction stories that use faster-than-light mechanisms which have a surface plausibility, but which would cause severe problems in our actual universe[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn24). So hearing a compelling story on its own means little.

What I find more concerning is one group who often seem to think a recipe for ruin is plausible: imaginative scientists with a broad and deep view of the long run of technology. I've spoken with many about this, and quite a few (not all) find such recipes for ruin plausible. It's not so surprising: they tend to understand well how miraculous and unlikely many properties of matter would have seemed _before_ their discovery: from fire to superconductivity; from the steam engine to fission chain reactions; from hot air balloons to gravitational wave detectors; and so on, and on, and on. Humanity keeps discovering possibilities latent within matter, which make easy what was formerly impossible. Imagine describing fire to someone who had never seen it – a substance that consumes all before it, turning it into blackened char; can move with lightning speed; is exothermic, which means it can be self-sustaining; can destroy areas the size of countries. It would sound ludicrous. Indeed, if the Earth's atmosphere had much higher levels of oxygen, fire might well (at least approximately) be an example of a recipe for ruin. If would become far easier to achieve horrendous, all-consuming firestorms like those seen in the bombings of Tokyo and Dresden. We take fire for granted, yet it seems to mostly be chance[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn25) that it is not a recipe for ruin; certainly, it is adjacent.

One reason I like this framing in terms of hypothetical oracles is that it avoids a problem common in discussions of ASI xrisk. Often, in such discussions, people posit Godlike capabilities for ASIs. This naturally and reasonably triggers skepticism: "Oh, ASI won't be able to do that". The oracle framing avoids that response, by breaking things down into two steps. The question about whether recipes for ruin exist is a perfectly reasonable question about the laws of physics and human technological capabilities. Maybe you can find an argument for why no such recipe for ruin can exist.

However, suppose you regard it as plausible that such a recipe exists. Then a natural follow-on question is: could an ASI discover it[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn26)? It is by no means obvious that even if recipes for ruin exist then ASI's will be able to discover them: the gap between current human understanding of the universe and a complete understanding seems likely to be (extremely!) large. It may be that ASI can radically speed up scientific and technological progress, while leaving recipes for ruin safely unknown for the foreseeable future. However, my intuition is to be extremely concerned that recipes for ruin exist, and that a near-future ASI will be perfectly capable of discovering them.

**"Are ASIs finding recipes for ruin the main concern you have about xrisk?"** No, that's just one lens on the problem. It's meant to be illustrative of the kinds of dangers that very powerful and widely accessible technologies could pose, even without rogue AIs taking over. But it's easy to conjure up many other scenarios, too; no doubt you've seen some. In the case of recipes for ruin, you end up in a situation where you're hoping that: (a) such recipes don't exist; (b) if they do exist, ASIs won't be able to develop them; (c) if they can find them, the alignment guardrails will be so robust and impermeable that humans in practice won't be able to learn such recipes; (d) if the guardrails can be crossed, then no human will want or have the capability to use them. Unfortunately, I have strong doubts about all these. If it were as easy to cause mass destruction as it is to buy a gun and ammo from Walmart (in much of the US), I think we'd be in quite a bad state. At the very least, I think we'd start to see a _lot_ more surveillance and authoritarianism and civil unrest, and the concomitant rise of demagogues. I don't mean of the kind seen in the US during the Trump years. I mean Stalin-Gulag-or-worse style. And that's the /optimistic/(!) scenario. The pessimistic scenario is far worse.

## To what extent will ASI speed up science and technology? When is experiment a bottleneck? When can an ASI think its way to new discoveries and new capabilities?

**"It seems, then, that much of your concern lies in the ability of ASI to** **speed up science and technology. How much do you think that will happen?"** It's true, that is where much of my concern is. It's (very) personally uncomfortable: I've spent much of my career concerned with how to speed up science and technology. And now find myself wondering if I've been playing on the wrong team[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn27). In response to your question about how much ASI will speed up science and technology: a useful model is in terms of three barriers. There's an _intellectual barrier_: maybe some discoveries are difficult simply because it is challenging for even an ASI to think the right thoughts. It seems plausible, for example, that even an ASI will have trouble solving NP-hard problems. Many problems of technical design or of finding good arguments (e.g., mathematical proofs) have this character, at least heuristically: they are easy to verify as correct, but hard to find. Put another way: while an ASI may be immensely more intellectually capable than a human for almost any problem, it may still fall vastly short of an omniscient oracle[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn28). The gap between humanity and omniscience is vast!

The second barrier is a _resource barrier_. Although the people trying to discover the Higgs boson were very smart, they weren't able to use those smarts to avoid constructing a very large, very expensive particle accelerator. Of course, sometimes you can tradeoff resources and intelligence: intelligence did (for example) enable us to build much better particle detectors, no doubt reducing the cost of discovering the Higgs. And perhaps intelligence could have been used to speed things up more, or perhaps to acquire the resources faster[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn29).

A particularly interesting barrier is the third barrier, the _experimental barrier_. People often point out that experiment is a bottleneck in AI-enabled scientific progress. Historically, some proponents of xrisk have adopted a naive view, in which by "just thinking" an ASI could radically speed up all scientific discovery. Experienced scientists then retort that this is ridiculous, that just being really, really smart doesn't eliminate the need for experiment. In fact, I think we can say something interesting about when experiment will be a bottleneck and when it won't be.

**"So: when will experiment be a bottleneck?"** Suppose we had a perfect theory of everything and infinite computational power. Even given those resources, our ability to do (say) medicine would be surprisingly constrained. The reason is that we simply can't deduce the nature of the human body (either in general, or in a specific instance) using those resources alone: it depends a tremendous amount on contingent facts: of our evolutionary history, of the origin of life, of our parents, of the environment we grew up in, and so on. Put another way: you can't use pure theory to discover that human beings are built out of DNA, RNA, the ribosome, and so on. Those are experimental facts we need to observe in the world.

Indeed, we keep discovering entire _systems_ in the human body that we were previously unaware of; I won't be surprised if we discover many new systems in the future. In many ways, the history of medicine can be viewed as a history of experimentally developing a better and better model of how human beings work, and of interventions to change that. You might object that we could at least deduce the _possibility_ of DNA, RNA, the ribosome (_etc_) from pure theory, given sufficient computational power and a theory of everything. I'm willing to buy that, subject to certain assumptions[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn30). But that wouldn't single them out as what is actually going on in human beings. For that, you need a lot of experimental observation[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn31).

This kind of bottleneck holds in any situation where historically contingent facts about the world crucially impact a phenomenon. So, for instance, this observation affects our understanding of the way the Earth's climate works, of psychology, astrophysics, and many other sciences. In each case we must make observations to see what is actually out there in the world, it isn't sufficient to just think, no matter how clever we are. This doesn't mean we can't reduce the need for experiments. But in many sciences contingent facts about the world dominate.

**"So, when _will_ ASI be able to think its way to new discoveries?"** There's a flipside to the above, which is that ASI can be expected to excel in situations where we already have extremely accurate predictive theories; the contingencies are already known and incorporated into the theory, in detail. Indeed, there are already cases where humanity has used such theories to great advantage to make substantial further "progress"[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn32), mostly through thinking ("theory and/or simulation") alone, perhaps augmented with a little experiment:

-   The first atomic bombs were designed in considerable part using theory and simulation, built on models of neutrons and the nucleus which had been developed in the 1930s. Experiment was still required, but it's remarkable the extent to which theorists thinking drove the design of the bomb.
-   The first hydrogen bombs relied even more heavily on theory, in part because the conditions inside an atomic bomb – used to trigger the hydrogen bomb – were so unusual that they were difficult to study experimentally. Indeed, the very first calculation carried out on the ENIAC computer was a theoretical simulation of the hydrogen bomb.
-   The first stealth fighter was designed principally through theory and simulation, though it was done in conjunction with some physical experimentation.
-   Bose-Einstein condensation was predicted in advance through theory alone. I am told that in a seminar Phil Anderson, originator of the term "emergence", once said that emergent phenomena were so surprising as to be impossible to predict in advance. Someone in the audience at the seminar said "what about Bose-Einstein condensation?" Anderson shot back that that didn't count, since Einstein was a genius[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn33).
-   LIGO was driven by and relied upon theory and simulation to a staggering extent; a lot of experimental input was still necessary to characterize and suppress the noise sources, which were not fully understood or characterized in advance.
-   Many remarkable phenomena have been predicted in advance based on extant theories, including lasing, the quantum Hall (but not fractional quantum Hall) effect, quantum teleportation, topological quantum computing, and others.
-   It's intriguing to think of striking new phenomena within computers as examples of this general pattern. Something like, for example, public-key cryptography seems to me an instance of a scientific discovery made entirely through theory, grounded in some extant theoretical system (in that case, the Turing machine theory of computation, as well as some broad notions of cryptography).

It's tempting to say that we are operating in an engineering regime when this kind of predictive theory can be done. But that's not right. You see this with the discovery of public-key cryptography: it's not so much engineering as the discovery of an entirely new phenomenon, in some sense lying hidden within existing ideas about computation and cryptography. Or consider the question of whether we could have predicted the existence of liquid water – and phenomena like the Navier-Stokes equations, turbulence, and so on – from the Schroedinger equation alone? Over the last 20 years the study of water from first principles has been a surprisingly active area of research in physics. And yet, as far as I know, we are not yet at the point where we can even deduce the Navier-Stokes equations, much less other phenomena of interest[34](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn34). I believe it is principally science (not engineering) when such emergent phenomena and emergent principles are being discovered. It seems to me that a fundamental question is:

**"When can ASI deduce emergent phenomena and emergent laws, from accurate underlying theories?"** Of course, I don't know the answer to this question. But to the extent that ASI _can_ make such deductions, it will give it qualitatively new types of power and capability, beyond what humans presently have. And although I cannot know for sure, I expect this will be common: that there are many, many such new types of emergent phenomena latent in our existing theories, and ASI will eventually discover many such phenomena. Indeed, I expect we're already rather close to this with AlphaFold 2 and similar systems. Perhaps, for example, AI-driven systems for protein design are discovering new principles for such design; indeed, perhaps they're even doing it unknown to their human creators.

## Conclusion

ASI xrisk is complicated, and these notes omit many important issues. One such issue is the positive impact of AI and ASI. Most discussions of ASI xrisk focus almost entirely on scenarios in which things go badly wrong. This is understandable, but a mistake[35](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn35). Any harms will largely (though not entirely) be a side effect of pursuing the positive goals motivating AI. Certainly, most of the people I know working toward ASI don't just want to be personally successful, they also desire a better, healthier, safer, more just world[36](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/index.html#fn36). And so any thorough analysis must be deeply grounded in an understanding of the positive goals, methods, and impacts of AI, a vision of a Great AI Flourishing for Humanity. I've written [one short essay](https://michaelnotebook.com/mc2023/index.html) on this, and have extensive notes, but an integrated presentation would be far better. One benefit of simultaneously [exploring multiple scenarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scenario_planning) is that understanding optimistic scenarios enriches and changes one's understanding of negative scenarios; and vice versa. Another benefit is that it makes the stakes clearer if worriers (like me) are wrong to worry: slowing AI down may be a very high-cost-to-humanity activity.

Like many, I was stunned by ChatGPT and GPT4. While GPT4 falls well short of ASI, it makes urgent the need to better understand ASI xrisk. These notes are my first in-depth exploration of ASI xrisk, written to help me metabolize prior work, and to contribute a few ideas of my own. I've made progress more slowly than I expected, and am frustrated at still not knowing what to do about ASI xrisk. How can one have high-leverage impact here? A wise friend and colleague, Catherine Olsson, once remarked that in any endeavor, no matter how pessimistic you may be about the overall situation, positive progress is founded in optimistic plans. This is true even when tackling challenges as daunting as ASI xrisk: optimism not as naive hope, but as a constructive framework for action. Aiming at a deeper understanding seems a helpful step toward such a framework.

28.  A staple of discussions of ASI and the Singularity is, of course, bootstrapping computing devices, each generation able to rapidly design far more capable successors (or extensions) to itself.
