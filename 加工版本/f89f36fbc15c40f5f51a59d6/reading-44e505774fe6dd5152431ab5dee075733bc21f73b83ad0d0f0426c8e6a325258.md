# 第95百分位并没有那么好｜95%-ile isn't that good

---

## 正文

能 Reaching 95%-ile 并不怎么令人惊叹，因为这并没有那么难。我觉得这是我最容易招来嘲笑的想法之一。直白地说出来时，它听起来很 elitist，这也无济于事。但我觉得恰恰相反：大多数人在大多数事情上都能变得（relatively）good。

请注意，我说的 95%-ile，是指在 participate 的人中达到 95%-ile，而不是在所有人中（对于许多 activities，只要去做，你在所有人中就已经是 99%-ile 或更高了）。我指的也不是在 practice regularly 的人中达到 95%-ile。那个“[奇怪的小窍门](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_weird_trick_advertisements)”是，对于许多 activities，在 practice 的人中达到大约 10%-ile，就能让你在 participate 的人中达到大约 90%-ile 或 99%-ile。

这篇文章会谈具体的事情，因为[我见过的相关讨论全都停留在抽象层面](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/926493613097439232)，这让它们变成了 Rorschach tests。例如，Scott Adams 有篇被广泛引用的文章，声称做 generalist 比做 specialist 更好，因为要变得“extraordinary”，你要么必须在一件事上成为“the best”，要么必须在两件事上达到 75%-ile。如果这严格成立，做 generalist 当然会更好，但这显然是夸张；即使不是“the best”，你也可以从 specialized skill 中获得很大的价值；由于按原文写出的 precise claim 显然很荒唐，而文章其余部分又都是含糊的 handwaving，讨论不可避免地会沦为各自陈述自己的 prior beliefs，基本无视文章的内容。

就我个人而言，在我参与过的每一项能得到粗略 percentile ranking 的 activity 中，处于 95%-ile 的人都在不断 make mistakes，而这些 mistakes 看起来应该很容易 observe 和 correct。“Real world” activities 通常无法 reduced 成 percentile rating，但达到看起来相近的 level of proficiency，似乎也同样容易。

我们先详细看看 Overwatch（一个 video game），因为这项 activity 我很熟悉，而且很容易获得 ranking information，也很容易 observe 正在发生什么；然后我们会看看一些“real world” examples，在其中也能 observe 到相同的 phenomena，不过我们无法获得 real world examples 的 ranking information[1](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:S)。

### Overwatch（守望先锋）

在 Overwatch 的 90%-ile 和 95%-ile ranks，绝大多数 players 几乎一直都在 make basic game losing mistakes。这些都是 simple mistakes，例如在 match timer 耗尽时 standing next to the objective，而不是 on top of the objective，把 probable victory 变成 certain defeat。如果你想了解足够多的 specific mistakes 的细节，以便自行判断一个 mistake 是否算“basic”，请看附带的脚注[2](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:O)。

我们可能预期这种情况会发生，原因有这些：

1.  人们不想 win，或者不在乎 winning
2.  人们 understand 自己的 mistakes，但还没有投入足够时间来 fix 它们
3.  人们是 untalented 的
4.  人们不 understand 如何 spot 自己的 mistakes 并 fix 它们

在 Overwatch 的 lower ranks，你可能会见到很多属于 (1) 的人，他们似乎不在乎 winning；但到了 30%-ile，你经常能看到人们以各种方式表明自己想 win，例如对那些被认为不在乎 victory 或者 unskilled 的 players 大喊大叫，抱怨他们认为 make mistakes、妨碍自己的 team winning 的人，等等[3](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:A)。除了偶尔出现的 troll，当人们因为 losing 而极其愤怒时，认为他们通常是在努力 win，并非不合理。

(2)，没有投入足够时间来 fix 自己的 mistakes，在某个时候会适用于所有正在 improving 的 players；但如果你看看 50%-ile 的 median time played，就会发现 stably ranked 在那里的人已经投入了数百小时（而 higher ranks 的 median time played 更高）。考虑到我们讨论的 mistakes 有多么 simple，对大多数 players 来说，情况不可能是没有投入足够时间。

在 Overwatch forums，low-ranked Overwatch players 经常抱怨自己就是不 talented，而且永远不可能 get better。不论采用怎样的 practice regimen，大多数人可能都没有在 professional league 中 play 的 talent；但如果只需 fixing 像“没有意识到自己应该 stand on the objective”这样的 mistakes 就能达到 95%-ile，那么达到 95%-ile 其实并不需要很多 talent。

虽然 (4)，人们不 understanding 如何 spot 和 fix 自己的 mistakes，并不是仅剩的 possible explanation[4](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:D)，但我相信，对大多数 players 而言，这是 most likely explanation。大多数因为卡在某个 rank、可能直到 95%-ile 或 99%-ile 而表达沮丧的 players，似乎都没有意识到，他们可以通过 observing 自己的 gameplay，或让别人看看自己的 gameplay，来 drastically improve。

这其中有件令人好奇的事：Overwatch 让 spot basic mistakes 变得很容易（相比大多数其他 activities）。你被 killed 之后，game 会从 killed 你的 player 的 viewpoint 展示你是怎样 died 的，让你看到是什么导致了你的 death。Overwatch 还会 records 整场 game，让你观看这场 game 的 replay，从而弄清发生了什么，以及为什么 game 最终 won 或 lost。在许多其他 games 中，你得先设置 recording software，才能 view a replay。

如果你读 Overwatch forums，就会看到不断有人发帖，内容基本上是：“我简直要沮丧死了！我已经 played 这个 game 1200 小时了，却仍然 ranked 10%-ile，\[一些因 player 而异的 Overwatch 特有内容\]。”总会有其他用户回复类似这样的话：“我们没法从你的文字判断问题出在哪里，请发一段你的 gameplay 的 video。”当原帖作者用自己 play 的 recording 回复时，人们就会给出有用的 feedback；只要认真对待，这些 feedback 就会立即让这个 player 变得好得多。如果你持续关注这些求助的人，往往很快就能看到他们在高得多的 rank 再次寻求 feedback（例如，从 10%-ile 升到 40%-ile）。看到这些建议奏效当然很好，但遗憾的是，那么多 players 都没意识到，观看自己的 recordings，或发布 recordings 来寻求 feedback，本可以让自己少经历 1198 小时的沮丧。

Overwatch players（包括已经深入 95%-ile 及以上的人）似乎普遍会：

-   想要 improve
-   不去 get feedback
-   明明 getting feedback 就能让 improving quickly 变得容易，却仍然 Improve slowly

Overwatch 提供了 tools，使 get feedback 变得 relatively 容易；但那些强烈表达自己渴望 improve 的人，却没有利用这些 tools。

### 现实生活

我的经验是，其他 games 也类似，而且我认为“现实生活”中的 activities 也没有那么不同，尽管存在一些 complications。

一个 complication 是，现实生活中的 activities 往往没有一个 single、one-dimensional 的 objective 可供 optimize。另一个是，即便针对一个 specific、well defined 的 goal，人们对于什么能让一个人在现实生活中的 activity 上做到 good，往往也缺乏理解（与 games 和 sports 相比）。

带有 rating systems 的 Games 很容易 optimize：你的 meta-goal 可以是获得 high rating，而这通常可以通过 fixing 上述那些 mistakes 来 increasing 你的 win rate，从而 achieved，比如没有意识到应该站到 objective 上。对于任何一个具体的 mistake，你甚至可以合理估计它对你的 win rate 的 impact，进而估计对你的 rating 的 impact。

在现实生活中，如果你想成为（例如）“一个 good speaker”，这可能意味着你想进行 informative talks，帮助人们 learn；也可能意味着你想进行 entertaining talks，让人们喜欢；或者你想在享有盛誉的 conferences 上进行 keynotes；或者你想受邀进行 talks，每次出场获得 $50k。这些都是不同的 objectives，achieving 它们需要不同的 strategies，而且对于某个具体的 mistake（例如，在一场 20 minute talk 中花 8 minutes 介绍自己），它对你的 goal 意味着什么并不清楚。

另一个让 games（至少是 mainstream 的那些）容易 optimize 的因素是，它们往往有很多爱好者，曾近乎痴迷地尝试弄清什么是 effective。这意味着，如果你想 improve，除非你想跻身世界顶尖，否则只需弄清哪些 resources 对其他人有用，选一个来阅读或观看，然后 practice。例如，如果你想在 bridge 或 spades 这样的 trick-taking card game 中达到 99%-ile（在所有 players 中，而不是在“有 masterpoints 的 ACBL players”或“经常参加 North American Bridge Championships 的人”这样的 subgroups 中），你可以通过以下方式做到：

-   learning 这个 game 的 basics
-   reading [一本关于 cardplay 的 beginner book](https://amzn.to/2sUtmsk)
-   practicing applying 这些 material

如果你想成为一个 good speaker，而且心中对“一个 good speaker”有 specific definition，仍然没有一条显而易见的前进道路。Great speakers 会给出直接 contradictory 的 advice（例如，避免 focusing on presentation skills 与 practice presentation skills）。近乎痴迷地尝试 improve 并弄清什么有用的人相对较少，这导致缺乏用于 improving 的 rigorous curricula。不过，这也意味着，以 percentile 而言，improve 很容易，因为[根本在尝试 improve 的人相对较少](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1442945072144678914)。

尽管有上述所有 caveats，我仍然相信，与 games 或 sports 相比，在现实生活中的 activities 上变得 relatively good 更容易，因为人们在大多数现实生活中的 activities 上投入的 delibrate practice 太少了。举个例子，如果你是当地的 table tennis 高手，能 beat 当地酒吧里的每个路人，当你向某人挑战一局，而对方说“当然，你的 rating 是多少？”时，你就知道自己要被狠狠收拾了，而且对方很可能拿着鞋刷打都能 beat 你（顺便说一句，这件事确实发生在我的一个朋友身上）。你可能是 99%-ile，但一个没有 talent、却花时间 practice 过 basics 的人，会有让你无法 return 的 serve，也能 kill 当地酒吧高手能够 consitently hit 的任何 shot。在大多数现实生活中的 activities 中，几乎没有人投入的 delibrate practice 能达到每周去当地 table tennis club practices 两小时的人的水平，更不用说 top pro 那样每天可能认真 train 四小时的人了。

举两个具体的例子，从 2013 到 2017，我帮助 [Leah](http://blog.leahhanson.us/speaking.html) 准备 talks。她最初几次 practice talks 的水平，大致与你随便走进一场大型 tech conference 的 talk 时预期的差不多。在她开始 speaking 的最初几年里，她为每次 public talk 都做了大约 30 次 practice runs，其中一半我都观看了并给出了 feedback。她的第一次 public talk（在我看来）就远高于大型、广受认可的 tech conference 上 talk 的 average 水平，此后她的 talks 越来越好，直到她在 2017 停止 speaking。

正如我们上面讨论过的，这比 game ratings 更 subjective，而且没有办法真正 determine 一个 percentile，但如果你看看大多数人如何准备 talks，Leah 能够 above average 就不太令人意外了。在她最早参加 speaking 的某场 conference 的前一晚，我们与另一位 speaker 聊天，对方提到自己还没有完成 talk，手头只有十五分钟的 material（但那是一场四十分钟的 talk）。对方正试图弄清如何填满剩余时间。这种准备方式并不少见，而这样准备出来的 talks，绝大多数都不怎么样。

大多数人认为，为一场 talk 做 30 次 practice runs 很荒唐，practice 的量简直到了痴迷的程度，但我认为 Gary Bernhardt 说得对：如果你向 300 人的 audience 进行一场 30-minute talk，那就是 150 person-hours 用于观看你的 talk，所以花 15 hours practicing 并非明显不合理（而且 30 次 practice runs 很可能不到 15 hours，因为有些 runs 可以提前结束，和/或反复 practice 有问题的 sections）。有一点值得注意：这种在进行 talk 时被认为痴迷的 practice 水平，与一个水平中等的 table tennis club player 花在 practicing 上的时间相比，仍然相形见绌。

如果你学过 pedagogy，你可能会说，我给 Leah 的帮助实在很差。众所周知，让 laypeople 自行摸索如何 improve，是 learn 某样东西最糟糕的方式之一；good instruction 更 effective，而让 skilled coach 或 teacher 提供 one-on-one instruction 则更加 effective[5](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:C)。这 100% 正确，我的帮助确实很差。不过，大多数人不会为一场 talk practice 超过几次，许多人甚至一次都不会 practice（我没有很好的 data 来 proving 这一点，这是对我参加过的 conferences 上的 speakers 进行非正式询问所得的结果）。因此，与大多数 speakers 相比，Leah 的 30 次 practice runs 是极其大量的 practice，即便我们使用的是最糟糕的 improvement techniques 之一，也取得了 relatively good 的 outcome。

我的 writing 是另一个例子。我不打算拿自己与任何其他人 compare，但在我写这个 blog 的最初几年里，我的 writing improved dramatically，仅仅因为我花了一点精力来获取和采纳 feedback。

Leah 几乎读过每篇 post 的一两份 drafts，并给我 feedback。最初几篇 posts 的时候，由于我们两人都对 writing 一无所知，很难 identifying 究竟哪里有问题。如果我写出了 awkward prose 或 confusing narrative structure，我们能指着它说“那看起来不对”，却无法 describe 哪里不对，也提不出 fix。这就像在 spellcheck 出现之前，你 misspelled 一个 word，能看出哪里不对，但想出的每个 permutation 都同样不对。

我对此的 fix 是聘请一位我欣赏其 writing 的 professional editor，并告诉对方：“我不在意 spelling 和 grammar fixes，我的 writing 存在一些我不理解的 fundamental problems，请向我解释它们是什么”[6](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:F)。我认为，这比我帮助 Leah 准备 talks 更 effective，因为我们请来了一个基本上相当于 professional coach 的人。我的 editor 给我们的帮助之一，是提供了一套可以用于讨论 structural problems 的 vocabulary，就像 [design patterns](https://amzn.to/2GPPmYE) 为人们提供了一套用于谈论 OO design 的 vocabulary 一样。

### 回到这个博客的常规话题：programming

Programming 与上面的现实生活例子相似：不可能 assign 一个 rating，或 calculate percentiles，或做诸如此类的事情，但通过获得关于自己所做之事的 feedback，仍然可能不费太多力气，就相对于过去的自己取得 significant improvements。

例如，[这是 Michael Malis 做过的一件事](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/926492239081197569)：

> 我发现的一个极其 useful 的 exercise，是观看自己 program。一周里，我都让一个 program 在 background 中 running，records 我的 screen。到周末，我会看前一周的几个片段。通常，我会看那些感觉 complete 某项 task 所花时间远超应有时间的时段。观看时，我会特别留意时间究竟花在了哪里，并弄清楚自己本可以怎样做得更好。第一次这样做时，我真的很惊讶，原来自己的时间都花在了这些地方。
> 
> 例如，以前 writing code 时，我会先为一个新 feature 把所有 code 都 write 好，再把所有 code 一起 test。这样 testing code 时，我必须先 isolate 出 bug 在哪个 function 里，然后再 debug 那个单独的 function。看过自己 writing code 的录像后，我意识到，implementing 这个 feature 的 total time 中，约有四分之一都花在了 tracking down 这些 bugs 究竟在哪些 functions 里！这对我而言完全不明显，如果没有给自己录像，我就不会发现。既然现在知道自己在 isolating 出 bugs 在哪个 function 里这件事上花了这么多时间，我现在会在 write 每个 function 时就 test 它，以确保它们 work。这让我 write code 快了很多，因为它 dramatically reduces 了 debug 我的 code 所需的时间。

过去，我花过时间弄清楚自己 code 时，时间都去了哪里，基本上看到了与 Overwatch 中相同的情况，只不过我不是不断犯 game-losing mistakes，而是不断做一些毫无意义地浪费时间的事情。仅仅改掉其中一些坏习惯，可能就已经让我获得了至少 2x 的 productivity increase；这很容易 measure，因为 fixing 这些问题基本上就是把浪费的时间夺回来。例如，我注意到，如果需要等两分钟时去网上看点东西，自己就会分心 N minutes，所以我确保始终保留一个有用工作的 queue，来填补空闲时间（如果我正在做的事情非常 latency sensitive，不想 task switch，我就什么也不做，直到等待结束）。

这里需要注意的一点是，真正 track 自己正在做什么很重要，而不只是猜测自己正在做什么。当我 recorded 人们实际做的事情，再把它与他们以为自己在做的事情 compare 时，两者往往很不一样。没有 metrics 或 tracing 就 operate 一个 complex software system，通常会被认为很荒唐；但没有 metrics 或 tracing 就管理自己，却很正常，尽管你远比自己所处理的 software 更 complex，也更难理解。

[Jonathan Tang 曾指出，choosing the right problem dominates execution speed](https://danluu.com/hn-comments/#what-makes-engineers-productive-https-news-ycombinator-com-item-id-5496914)。我并不反对这一点，[但 doubling execution speed 仍然是一个不错的收获，而且 independent of selecting the right problem to work on](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/)，我也不认为关于如何 choose the right problem 的讨论能够在抽象层面得到有效描述，而要举例所必需的上下文，会比本文中已经过长的 Overwatch 例子长得多；也许我会另写一篇文章，专门谈这件事。

总之，对我来说，写这样一篇文章有点奇怪，因为我觉得，从文化上说，我们在美国有点过于在意 productivity，尤其是在我最近住过的地方（NYC & SF）。但在个人层面，工作或做家务时更高的 productivity，不一定非要转化成更多工作或家务，也可以转化成更多休假时间，或者更多做任何你所珍视之事的时间。

至于 Overwatch 这样的 games，我不认为 improving 是一种 moral imperative；在 50%-ile、10%-ile 或任何 rank 上玩得开心，都没什么不对。但在我玩过的每一款带有 rating 和／或 league/tournament system 的 game 中，都有很多人即使没怎么花力气 improving，也会在 lose 时非常沮丧、不开心。如果是这样，为什么不花一点力气 improving，让自己少花一点时间生闷气呢？

### 一些用于 improving 的 meta-techniques

-   获得 feedback 并 practice
    -   理想情况下来自 expert coach；但如果没有，也可以来自 layperson，甚至你自己（如果你有某种方式对自己所做的事情进行 recording/tracing）
-   采用 Guided exercises，或带有 solutions 的 exercises
    -   这在关于“老” games 的书中很容易找到，例如 chess 或 Bridge。
    -   对于特定 areas，你往往能找到包含这些内容的系列书籍，例如在 math 中，Springer Undergraduate Mathematics Series (SUMS) 的书通常有带 solutions 的 problems

当然，这些并不是什么新奇的想法，例如 Kotov 在 70s 出版的系列书籍 Think like a Grandmaster、Play Like a Grandmaster、Train Like a Grandmaster 就讲过这些相同的想法，因为这些是一些最显而易见的 improve 方法。

### 附录：其他最容易招来嘲笑的想法

下面是我写过的、在发表时遭到最广泛嘲笑的想法：

-   [在时髦的 tech companies，programmers 挣到这样的收入并不罕见 $350k/yr or more](https://danluu.com/startup-tradeoffs/) (2015, stated number was $当时为 250k/yr)
-   [Monorepos 可以是 reasonable 的](https://danluu.com/monorepo/) (2015)
-   [我们应该预期会看到多得多的 CPU bugs](https://danluu.com/cpu-bugs/) (2016)
-   [Markets 与 discrimination 并不是 incompatible 的](https://danluu.com/tech-discrimination/) (2014)
-   [Computers 在某些方面正变得 slower](https://danluu.com/input-lag/) (2017)
-   [关于 types 的 benefit 的 Empirical evidence 几乎不存在](https://danluu.com/empirical-pl/) (2014)
-   撰写关于某个 subject、却避免使用 domain-specific terminology 的 technical posts，是 reasonable 的

我那些关于 compensation 的文章，享有一种令人尴尬的殊荣：它们最常同时因为显而易见到毫无意义，以及错得可笑而受到指责。我怀疑，它们也是对人们产生最大 aggregate impact 的文章——已经有两位数的人告诉我，其中一篇 compensation 文章改变了他们的人生，而他们现在每年比过去多挣 $x00,000/yr，因为他们知道自己有可能找到薪水高得多的工作。我甚至怀疑，在那些因为了解到可以多挣很多钱而作出重大改变的人中，向我反馈的人还不到 10%。

2015 年，我写第一篇关于 compensation 的文章时，更多人嘲笑我写了显然错误的东西，而不是写了显而易见的东西，但过去几年情况反过来了。当 Twitter 的某个角落，或远在 HN/reddit 圈子之外的某个网络论坛碰巧看到我的文章时，我偶尔仍会因为写错了而遭到嘲笑，但“显然错误”与“显而易见”的 ratio，可能已经从 20:1 变成了 1:5。

自 2015 年以来，人们对 monorepos 的看法也发生了类似变化。除了一些大公司里的人，以前那些紧跟潮流的人普遍认为 monorepos 显然很蠢，但这确实已经改变了。变化没有人们对 compensation 的看法那么大，但已经足以让我现在遇到坚定的 anti-monorepo-er 时感到有点惊讶。

尽管人们对 CPU bugs 的看法花了更长时间才转变，但那可能是上面列出的文章中，如今遭到嘲笑最少的一篇。

2.  我刚开始 playing Overwatch 时（也就是我做那个 experiment 的时候），最后得到的 rated 略高于 50%-ile（对 Overwatch players 来说，那就是 Plat——本文将使用 percentiles，而不是 ranks，以免让非 Overwatch players 也得学习这些 ranks 的含义）。人们普遍认为，而且这很可能属实，在 Overwatch 中 playing 主要 ranked game mode 的人，on average 比只 playing unranked modes 的人更好，因此我的 actual percentile 很可能比 50%-ile 稍高，而本文列出的所有“true” percentiles 都高于 nominal percentiles。

    在略高于 50%-ile 的水平，你经常会看到以下情况：

    -   Supports（healers）会对一个处于 full health 的人 heal（这不起任何作用），而他们身旁的 teammate 正在 dying，随后 dies
    -   Players 不会注意到有人直接走到 team 后面，逐个 kills 人，直到整个 team 都被 killed
    -   Players 会 shoot 一个 enemy，直到只需再一 shot 就能 kill 这个 enemy，然后却 switch 到另一个 target，让那个 1-health enemy heal 回 full health，之后才再次 shooting 那个 enemy
    -   在 dying 后，players 不会等待自己的 team respawn，而是直接跑进 enemy team，与他们 fight 1v6。这会在整场 game 中反复发生（这款 game 被 designed 为 6v6，但在低于 95%-ile 的 ranks 中，一方 team 的一个人 dies 后，就很少能看到 6v6 engagement）
    -   Players 显然完全不知道 character abilities 有什么作用，甚至包括他们正在 playing 的 character
    -   Players 会选择 risk 非常 high、但 reward 很 low 的 plays（对 Overwatch players 来说，一个典型 example 就是 game 在 2CP defense 开局时，Rein 去尝试 meme pin，这在 50%-ile 非常常见，在 95%-ile 则很少见，因为觉得这个 move 是好主意的 players，其 decision making 往往普遍 poor）。
    -   人们的 aim 会很 terrible，在只需 hit 某人一次就能 kill 对方时，却会连续 miss 四五次 shots
    -   如果一个 flanking enemy threatens 一个无法 escape 的 healer，以及一个有 escape ability 的 non-healer，那个 non-healer 很可能会使用自己的 ability 逃跑，留下 healer 等死，尽管他们只要在被 healed 的同时 attacked，就能轻易 kill 那个 flanker 并 save 自己的 healer。

    只要你的 gameplay 有一个 aspect 仅仅是 bad，而不是 atrocious，就足以达到 50%-ile。对我来说，那是我的 teamwork；对其他人来说，则是他们 gameplay 的其他部分。我之所以说自己的 teamwork 是 bad，而不是 good，甚至算不上 mediocre，是因为我基本上不知道该如何 play 这款 game，不知道各个 characters 的 strengths、weaknesses 和 abilities 是什么，所以根本不可能与自己的 team coordinate effectively。我也不知道 game modes 实际上是如何 worked 的（例如，什么情况下 game 会 end in a tie，什么情况下会进入另一个 round），所以我基本上就是 randomly 四处游荡，只是更倾向于待在我能找到的最大一群 teammates 附近。这就已经是 above average 了。

    你可以说，一个人在这款 game 中相当 good，因为他们是 above average。但从 non-relative 的意义上说，略微 above average 其实相当 bad——当两个 teammates 正通过 voice comms 大喊“\[enemy\] 在我们后面！”时，一个人却没注意到自己的整个 team 正在被从后方 killed，很难说这样的人不算 bad。

    在 game 内，game 的 goal 就是 win。game modes 各不相同，但对于主要的 ranked game，它们都会涉及某种 objective，你必须待在其上或附近。以下情况非常常见：round timer 正在倒数到 zero，如果你的 team 没有人 touches objective，你的 team 就 guaranteed to lose；但如果有人能够 touch objective 而不 die instantly，你的 team 就可能 win（这会使 game 进入 overtime，直到双方 teams 都停止 touching objective 后不久才结束）。一个相当常见的 concrete example 是，enemy team 有四个 players 在 objective 上，而你的 team 有两个 players 在 objective 附近，一个 tank 和一个 support/healer。你的 team 的另外四个 players 已经 died，正从 spawn 返回。他们已经足够近，如果你能 touch objective 且不 instantly die，他们就能赶到，并且很可能 take objective，从而 win；但如果你在 taking objective 后立刻 die，他们就来不及赶到，那样你们就会 lose。

    如果你在 90%-ile 到 95%-ile playing 那个 support/healer，这场 game 几乎总会这样结束：tank 会朝 objective 移动，被 shot，然后决定不想 take damage，于是从 objective 退开。作为 support，你的 health pool 很 small，如果 touch objective，就会 die instantly，因为对方 team 会 shoot 你。由于你若不向 objective 靠近，你的 team 就 guaranteed to lose，所以为了有任何 winning 的机会，你不得不这么做。在你被 killed 后，tank 要么会走上 objective 然后 die，要么会朝 objective 走过去，却在时间耗尽前未能赶到。无论哪种情况，你们大概都会 lose。

    如果 tank 尽到自己的职责，在你 died 之前走上 objective，你就可以 heal 那个 tank 足够长的时间，让你的 team 的其他人赶到，你们也就很可能 win。enemy team 如果 coordinated，就可以绕过或穿过 tank 来 kill 你，但他们不会这样做——任何知道这么做会让自己 win 这场 game，并且 aim 足够好、能够成功付诸行动的人，都不可能不最终进入更高的 rank）。而你 team 中那个假想的 tank，如果知道在这种情况下，自己的职责是为 support absorb damage，而不是反过来，就不会在 95%-ile 停留太久，因为他们会 win 太多 games，升到更高的 rank。

    另一个绝大多数 90%-ile 到 95%-ile players 都会处理错误的 basic situation，是你处于 offense，正等待自己的 team respawn，以便能作为一个 group attack。即使在 90%-ile，也许仍有 1/4 到 1/3 的 players 不会这么做，而是直接冲向 enemy team；不过，会有足够多的 players 意识到 1v6 不是个好主意，因此你经常会有 5v6 或 6v6 fights，而不是像在 50%-ile 看到的那样，接连不断地进行 1v6 和 2v6 fights。不管怎样，在为了形成一次 5v6 而等待 team respawn 时，很可能会有一个 player，虽然意识到自己不该直接冲进 enemy team 中间 1v6，却决定应该尝试以 long-ranged attacks 对 enemy team 进行 1v6。即使 enemy 有多个具备 instant-kill long range attacks 的 snipers，人们仍会这么做，而不是安全地躲在墙后。即使自己 playing 的 character 并不是 sniper，需要 hit enemy 2-3 次才能 get a kill，人们也会对着多个 snipers 这么做，结果自己极有可能无法 get a kill，却承担了很大的 dying risk。对 Overwatch players 来说，即使自己有 full ult charge，而对方 team 没有，人们也会这样做，把一个本该对己方有 advantage 的 situation（你的 team 的 ults 已 ready，而对方 team 已经 used ults），_充其量_变成一个 neutral situation（双方 teams 都有 ults），最坏则会瞬间 losing 这场 fight。

## 原文

Reaching 95%-ile isn't very impressive because it's not that hard to do. I think this is one of my most ridiculable ideas. It doesn't help that, when stated nakedly, that sounds elitist. But I think it's just the opposite: most people can become (relatively) good at most things.

Note that when I say 95%-ile, I mean 95%-ile among people who participate, not all people (for many activities, just doing it at all makes you 99%-ile or above across all people). I'm also not referring to 95%-ile among people who practice regularly. The "[one weird trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_weird_trick_advertisements)" is that, for a lot of activities, being something like 10%-ile among people who practice can make you something like 90%-ile or 99%-ile among people who participate.

This post is going to refer to specifics since [the discussions I've seen about this are all in the abstract](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/926493613097439232), which turns them into Rorschach tests. For example, Scott Adams has a widely cited post claiming that it's better to be a generalist than a specialist because, to become "extraordinary", you have to either be "the best" at one thing or 75%-ile at two things. If that were strictly true, it would surely be better to be a generalist, but that's of course exaggeration and it's possible to get a lot of value out of a specialized skill without being "the best"; since the precise claim, as written, is obviously silly and the rest of the post is vague handwaving, discussions will inevitably devolve into people stating their prior beliefs and basically ignoring the content of the post.

Personally, in every activity I've participated in where it's possible to get a rough percentile ranking, people who are 95%-ile constantly make mistakes that seem like they should be easy to observe and correct. "Real world" activities typically can't be reduced to a percentile rating, but achieving what appears to be a similar level of proficiency seems similarly easy.

We'll start by looking at Overwatch (a video game) in detail because it's an activity I'm familiar with where it's easy to get ranking information and observe what's happening, and then we'll look at some "real world" examples where we can observe the same phenomena, although we won't be able to get ranking information for real world examples[1](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:S).

### Overwatch

At 90%-ile and 95%-ile ranks in Overwatch, the vast majority of players will pretty much constantly make basic game losing mistakes. These are simple mistakes like standing next to the objective instead of on top of the objective while the match timer runs out, turning a probable victory into a certain defeat. See the attached footnote if you want enough detail about specific mistakes that you can decide for yourself if a mistake is "basic" or not[2](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:O).

Some reasons we might expect this to happen are:

1.  People don't want to win or don't care about winning
2.  People understand their mistakes but haven't put in enough time to fix them
3.  People are untalented
4.  People don't understand how to spot their mistakes and fix them

In Overwatch, you may see a lot of (1), people who don’t seem to care about winning, at lower ranks, but by the time you get to 30%-ile, it's common to see people indicate their desire to win in various ways, such as yelling at players who are perceived as uncaring about victory or unskilled, complaining about people who they perceive to make mistakes that prevented their team from winning, etc.[3](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:A). Other than the occasional troll, it's not unreasonable to think that people are generally trying to win when they're severely angered by losing.

(2), not having put in time enough to fix their mistakes will, at some point, apply to all players who are improving, but if you look at the median time played at 50%-ile, people who are stably ranked there have put in hundreds of hours (and the median time played at higher ranks is higher). Given how simple the mistakes we're discussing are, not having put in enough time cannot be the case for most players.

A common complaint among low-ranked Overwatch players in Overwatch forums is that they're just not talented and can never get better. Most people probably don't have the talent to play in a professional league regardless of their practice regimen, but when you can get to 95%-ile by fixing mistakes like "not realizing that you should stand on the objective", you don't really need a lot of talent to get to 95%-ile.

While (4), people not understanding how to spot and fix their mistakes, isn't the only other possible explanation[4](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:D), I believe it's the most likely explanation for most players. Most players who express frustration that they're stuck at a rank up to maybe 95%-ile or 99%-ile don't seem to realize that they could drastically improve by observing their own gameplay or having someone else look at their gameplay.

One thing that's curious about this is that Overwatch makes it easy to spot basic mistakes (compared to most other activities). After you're killed, the game shows you how you died from the viewpoint of the player who killed you, allowing you to see what led to your death. Overwatch also records the entire game and lets you watch a replay of the game, allowing you to figure out what happened and why the game was won or lost. In many other games, you'd have to set up recording software to be able to view a replay.

If you read Overwatch forums, you'll see a regular stream of posts that are basically "I'm SOOOOOO FRUSTRATED! I've played this game for 1200 hours and I'm still ranked 10%-ile, \[some Overwatch specific stuff that will vary from player to player\]". Another user will inevitably respond with something like "we can't tell what's wrong from your text, please post a video of your gameplay". In the cases where the original poster responds with a recording of their play, people will post helpful feedback that will immediately make the player much better if they take it seriously. If you follow these people who ask for help, you'll often see them ask for feedback at a much higher rank (e.g., moving from 10%-ile to 40%-ile) shortly afterwards. It's nice to see that the advice works, but it's unfortunate that so many players don't realize that watching their own recordings or posting recordings for feedback could have saved 1198 hours of frustration.

It appears to be common for Overwatch players (well into 95%-ile and above) to:

-   Want to improve
-   Not get feedback
-   Improve slowly when getting feedback would make improving quickly easy

Overwatch provides the tools to make it relatively easy to get feedback, but people who very strongly express a desire to improve don't avail themselves of these tools.

### Real life

My experience is that other games are similar and I think that "real life" activities aren't so different, although there are some complications.

One complication is that real life activities tend not to have a single, one-dimensional, objective to optimize for. Another is that what makes someone good at a real life activity tends to be poorly understood (by comparison to games and sports) even in relation to a specific, well defined, goal.

Games with rating systems are easy to optimize for: your meta-goal can be to get a high rating, which can typically be achieved by increasing your win rate by fixing the kinds of mistakes described above, like not realizing that you should step onto the objective. For any particular mistake, you can even make a reasonable guess at the impact on your win rate and therefore the impact on your rating.

In real life, if you want to be (for example) "a good speaker", that might mean that you want to give informative talks that help people learn or that you want to give entertaining talks that people enjoy or that you want to give keynotes at prestigious conferences or that you want to be asked to give talks for $50k an appearance. Those are all different objectives, with different strategies for achieving them and for some particular mistake (e.g., spending 8 minutes on introducing yourself during a 20 minute talk), it's unclear what that means with respect to your goal.

Another thing that makes games, at least mainstream ones, easy to optimize for is that they tend to have a lot of aficionados who have obsessively tried to figure out what's effective. This means that if you want to improve, unless you're trying to be among the top in the world, you can simply figure out what resources have worked for other people, pick one up, read/watch it, and then practice. For example, if you want to be 99%-ile in a trick-taking card game like bridge or spades (among all players, not subgroups like "ACBL players with masterpoints" or "people who regularly attend North American Bridge Championships"), you can do this by:

-   learning the basics of the game
-   reading [a beginner book on cardplay](https://amzn.to/2sUtmsk)
-   practicing applying the material

If you want to become a good speaker and you have a specific definition of “a good speaker” in mind, there still isn't an obvious path forward. Great speakers will give directly contradictory advice (e.g., avoid focusing on presentation skills vs. practice presentation skills). Relatively few people obsessively try to improve and figure out what works, which results in a lack of rigorous curricula for improving. However, this also means that it's easy to improve in percentile terms since [relatively few people are trying to improve at all](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/1442945072144678914).

Despite all of the caveats above, my belief is that it's easier to become relatively good at real life activities relative to games or sports because there's so little delibrate practice put into most real life activities. Just for example, if you're a local table tennis hotshot who can beat every rando at a local bar, when you challenge someone to a game and they say "sure, what's your rating?" you know you're in for a shellacking by someone who can probably beat you while playing with a shoe brush (an actual feat that happened to a friend of mine, BTW). You're probably 99%-ile, but someone with no talent who's put in the time to practice the basics is going to have a serve that you can't return as well as be able to kill any shot a local bar expert is able to consitently hit. In most real life activities, there's almost no one who puts in a level of delibrate practice equivalent to someone who goes down to their local table tennis club and practices two hours a week, let alone someone like a top pro, who might seriously train for four hours a day.

To give a couple of concrete examples, I helped [Leah](http://blog.leahhanson.us/speaking.html) prepare for talks from 2013 to 2017. The first couple practice talks she gave were about the same as you'd expect if you walked into a random talk at a large tech conference. For the first couple years she was speaking, she did something like 30 or so practice runs for each public talk, of which I watched and gave feedback on half. Her first public talk was (IMO) well above average for a talk at a large, well regarded, tech conference and her talks got better from there until she stopped speaking in 2017.

As we discussed above, this is more subjective than game ratings and there's no way to really determine a percentile, but if you look at how most people prepare for talks, it's not too surprising that Leah was above average. At one of the first conferences she spoke at, the night before the conference, we talked to another speaker who mentioned that they hadn't finished their talk yet and only had fifteen minutes of material (for a forty minute talk). They were trying to figure out how to fill the rest of the time. That kind of preparation isn't unusual and the vast majority of talks prepared like that aren't great.

Most people consider doing 30 practice runs for a talk to be absurd, a totally obsessive amount of practice, but I think Gary Bernhardt has it right when he says that, if you're giving a 30-minute talk to a 300 person audience, that's 150 person-hours watching your talk, so it's not obviously unreasonable to spend 15 hours practicing (and 30 practice runs will probably be less than 15 hours since you can cut a number of the runs short and/or repeatedly practice problem sections). One thing to note that this level of practice, considered obessive when giving a talk, still pales in comparison to the amount of time a middling table tennis club player will spend practicing.

If you've studied pedagogy, you might say that the help I gave to Leah was incredibly lame. It's known that having laypeople try to figure out how to improve among themselves is among the worst possible ways to learn something, good instruction is more effective and having a skilled coach or teacher give one-on-one instruction is more effective still[5](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:C). That's 100% true, my help was incredibly lame. However, most people aren't going to practice a talk more than a couple times and many won't even practice a single time (I don't have great data proving this, this is from informally polling speakers at conferences I've attended). This makes Leah's 30 practice runs an extraordinary amount of practice compared to most speakers, which resulted in a relatively good outcome even though we were using one of the worst possible techniques for improvement.

My writing is another example. I'm not going to compare myself to anyone else, but my writing improved dramatically the first couple of years I wrote this blog just because I spent a little bit of effort on getting and taking feedback.

Leah read one or two drafts of almost every post and gave me feedback. On the first posts, since neither one of us knew anything about writing, we had a hard time identifying what was wrong. If I had some awkward prose or confusing narrative structure, we'd be able to point at it and say "that looks wrong" without being able to describe what was wrong or suggest a fix. It was like, in the era before spellcheck, when you misspelled a word and could tell that something was wrong, but every permutation you came up with was just as wrong.

My fix for that was to hire a professional editor whose writing I respected with the instructions "I don't care about spelling and grammar fixes, there are fundamental problems with my writing that I don't understand, please explain to me what they are"[6](https://danluu.com/p95-skill/#fn:F). I think this was more effective than my helping Leah with talks because we got someone who's basically a professional coach involved. An example of something my editor helped us with was giving us a vocabulary we could use to discuss structural problems, the way [design patterns](https://amzn.to/2GPPmYE) gave people a vocabulary to talk about OO design.

### Back to this blog's regularly scheduled topic: programming

Programming is similar to the real life examples above in that it's impossible to assign a rating or calculate percentiles or anything like that, but it is still possible to make significant improvements relative to your former self without too much effort by getting feedback on what you're doing.

For example, [here's one thing Michael Malis did](https://twitter.com/danluu/status/926492239081197569):

> One incredibly useful exercise I’ve found is to watch myself program. Throughout the week, I have a program running in the background that records my screen. At the end of the week, I’ll watch a few segments from the previous week. Usually I will watch the times that felt like it took a lot longer to complete some task than it should have. While watching them, I’ll pay attention to specifically where the time went and figure out what I could have done better. When I first did this, I was really surprised at where all of my time was going.
> 
> For example, previously when writing code, I would write all my code for a new feature up front and then test all of the code collectively. When testing code this way, I would have to isolate which function the bug was in and then debug that individual function. After watching a recording of myself writing code, I realized I was spending about a quarter of the total time implementing the feature tracking down which functions the bugs were in! This was completely non-obvious to me and I wouldn’t have found it out without recording myself. Now that I’m aware that I spent so much time isolating which function a bugs are in, I now test each function as I write it to make sure they work. This allows me to write code a lot faster as it dramatically reduces the amount of time it takes to debug my code.

In the past, I've spent time figuring out where time is going when I code and basically saw the same thing as in Overwatch, except instead of constantly making game-losing mistakes, I was constantly doing pointlessly time-losing things. Just getting rid of some of those bad habits has probably been at least a 2x productivity increase for me, pretty easy to measure since fixing these is basically just clawing back wasted time. For example, I noticed how I'd get distracted for N minutes if I read something on the internet when I needed to wait for two minutes, so I made sure to keep a queue of useful work to fill dead time (and if I was working on something very latency sensitive where I didn't want to task switch, I'd do nothing until I was done waiting).

One thing to note here is that it's important to actually track what you're doing and not just guess at what you're doing. When I've recorded what people do and compare it to what they think they're doing, these are often quite different. It would generally be considered absurd to operate a complex software system without metrics or tracing, but it's normal to operate yourself without metrics or tracing, even though you're much more complex and harder to understand than the software you work on.

[Jonathan Tang has noted that choosing the right problem dominates execution speed](https://danluu.com/hn-comments/#what-makes-engineers-productive-https-news-ycombinator-com-item-id-5496914). I don't disagree with that, [but doubling execution speed is still decent win that's independent of selecting the right problem to work on](https://danluu.com/productivity-velocity/) and I don't think that discussing how to choose the right problem can be effectively described in the abstract and the context necessary to give examples would be much longer than the already too long Overwatch examples in this post, maybe I'll write another post that's just about that.

Anyway, this is sort of an odd post for me to write since I think that culturally, we care a bit too much about productivity in the U.S., especially in places I've lived recently (NYC & SF). But at a personal level, higher productivity doing work or chores doesn't have to be converted into more work or chores, it can also be converted into more vacation time or more time doing whatever you value.

And for games like Overwatch, I don't think improving is a moral imperative; there's nothing wrong with having fun at 50%-ile or 10%-ile or any rank. But in every game I've played with a rating and/or league/tournament system, a lot of people get really upset and unhappy when they lose even when they haven't put much effort into improving. If that's the case, why not put a little bit of effort into improving and spend a little bit less time being upset?

### Some meta-techniques for improving

-   Get feedback and practice
    -   Ideally from an expert coach but, if not, this can be from a layperson or even yourself (if you have some way of recording/tracing what you're doing)
-   Guided exercises or exercises with solutions
    -   This is very easy to find in books for "old" games, like chess or Bridge.
    -   For particular areas, you can often find series of books that have these, e.g., in math, books in the Springer Undergraduate Mathematics Series (SUMS) tend to have problems with solutions

Of course, these aren't novel ideas, e.g., Kotov's series of books from the 70s, Think like a Grandmaster, Play Like a Grandmaster, Train Like a Grandmaster covered these same ideas because these are some of the most obvious ways to improve.

2.  When I first started playing Overwatch (which is when I did that experiment), I ended up getting rated slightly above 50%-ile (for Overwatch players, that was in Plat -- this post is going to use percentiles and not ranks to avoid making non-Overwatch players have to learn what the ranks mean). It's generally believed and probably true that people who play the main ranked game mode in Overwatch are, on average, better than people who only play unranked modes, so it's likely that my actual percentile was somewhat higher than 50%-ile and that all "true" percentiles listed in this post are higher than the nominal percentiles.

    Some things you'll regularly see at slightly above 50%-ile are:

    -   Supports (healers) will heal someone who's at full health (which does nothing) while a teammate who's next to them is dying and then dies
    -   Players will not notice someone who walks directly behind the team and kills people one at a time until the entire team is killed
    -   Players will shoot an enemy until only one more shot is required to kill the enemy and then switch to a different target, letting the 1-health enemy heal back to full health before shooting at that enemy again
    -   After dying, players will not wait for their team to respawn and will, instead, run directly into the enemy team to fight them 1v6. This will repeat for the entire game (the game is designed to be 6v6, but in ranks below 95%-ile, it's rare to see a 6v6 engagement after one person on one team dies)
    -   Players will clearly have no idea what character abilities do, including for the character they're playing
    -   Players go for very high risk but low reward plays (for Overwatch players, a classic example of this is Rein going for a meme pin when the game opens on 2CP defense, very common at 50%-ile, rare at 95%-ile since players who think this move is a good idea tend to have generally poor decision making).
    -   People will have terrible aim and will miss four or five shots in a row when all they need to do is hit someone once to kill them
    -   If a single flanking enemy threatens a healer who can't escape plus a non-healer with an escape ability, the non-healer will probably use their ability to run away, leaving the healer to die, even though they could easily kill the flanker and save their healer if they just attacked while being healed.

    Having just one aspect of your gameplay be merely bad instead of atrocious is enough to get to 50%-ile. For me, that was my teamwork, for others, it's other parts of their gameplay. The reason I'd say that my teamwork was bad and not good or even mediocre was that I basically didn't know how to play the game, didn't know what any of the characters’ strengths, weaknesses, and abilities are, so I couldn't possibly coordinate effectively with my team. I also didn't know how the game modes actually worked (e.g., under what circumstances the game will end in a tie vs. going into another round), so I was basically wandering around randomly with a preference towards staying near the largest group of teammates I could find. That's above average.

    You could say that someone is pretty good at the game since they're above average. But in a non-relative sense, being slightly above average is quite bad -- it's hard to argue that someone who doesn't notice their entire team being killed from behind while two teammates are yelling "\[enemy\] behind us!" over voice comms isn't bad.

    After playing a bit more, I ended up with what looks like a "true" rank of about 90%-ile when I'm using a character I know how to use. Due to volatility in ranking as well as matchmaking, I played in games as high as 98%-ile. My aim and dodging were still atrocious. Relative to my rank, my aim was actually worse than when I was playing in 50%-ile games since my opponents were much better and I was only a little bit better. In 90%-ile, two copies of myself would probably lose fighting against most people 2v1 in the open. I would also usually lose a fight if the opponent was in the open and I was behind cover such that only 10% of my character was exposed, so my aim was arguably more than 10x worse than median at my rank.

    My "trick" for getting to 90%-ile despite being a 1/10th aimer was learning how the game worked and playing in a way that maximized the probability of winning (to the best of my ability), as opposed to playing the game like it's an [FFA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deathmatch) game where your goal is to get kills as quickly as possible. It takes a bit more context to describe what this means in 90%-ile, so I'll only provide a couple examples, but these are representative of mistakes the vast majority of 90%-ile players are making all of the time (with the exception of a few players who have grossly defective aim, like myself, who make up for their poor aim by playing better than average for the rank in other ways).

    Within the game, the goal of the game is to win. There are different game modes, but for the mainline ranked game, they all will involve some kind of objective that you have to be on or near. It's very common to get into a situation where the round timer is ticking down to zero and your team is guaranteed to lose if no one on your team touches the objective but your team may win if someone can touch the objective and not die instantly (which will cause the game to go into overtime until shortly after both teams stop touching the objective). A concrete example of this that happens somewhat regularly is, the enemy team has four players on the objective while your team has two players near the objective, one tank and one support/healer. The other four players on your team died and are coming back from spawn. They're close enough that if you can touch the objective and not instantly die, they'll arrive and probably take the objective for the win, but they won't get there in time if you die immediately after taking the objective, in which case you'll lose.

    If you're playing the support/healer at 90%-ile to 95%-ile, this game will almost always end as follows: the tank will move towards the objective, get shot, decide they don't want to take damage, and then back off from the objective. As a support, you have a small health pool and will die instantly if you touch the objective because the other team will shoot you. Since your team is guaranteed to lose if you don't move up to the objective, you're forced to do so to have any chance of winning. After you're killed, the tank will either move onto the objective and die or walk towards the objective but not get there before time runs out. Either way, you'll probably lose.

    If the tank did their job and moved onto the objective before you died, you could heal the tank for long enough that the rest of your team will arrive and you'll probably win. The enemy team, if they were coordinated, could walk around or through the tank to kill you, but they won't do that -- anyone who knows that will cause them to win the game and can aim well enough to successfully follow through can't help but end up in a higher rank). And the hypothetical tank on your team who knows that it's their job to absorb damage for their support in that situation and not vice versa won't stay at 95%-ile very long because they'll win too many games and move up to a higher rank.

    Another basic situation that the vast majority of 90%-ile to 95%-ile players will get wrong is when you're on offense, waiting for your team to respawn so you can attack as a group. Even at 90%-ile, maybe 1/4 to 1/3 of players won't do this and will just run directly at the enemy team, but enough players will realize that 1v6 isn't a good idea that you'll often 5v6 or 6v6 fights instead of the constant 1v6 and 2v6 fights you see at 50%-ile. Anyway, while waiting for the team to respawn in order to get a 5v6, it's very likely one player who realizes that they shouldn't just run into the middle of the enemy team 1v6 will decide they should try to hit the enemy team with long-ranged attacks 1v6. People will do this instead of hiding in safety behind a wall even when the enemy has multiple snipers with instant-kill long range attacks. People will even do this against multiple snipers when they're playing a character that isn't a sniper and needs to hit the enemy 2-3 times to get a kill, making it overwhelmingly likely that they won't get a kill while taking a significant risk of dying themselves. For Overwatch players, people will also do this when they have full ult charge and the other team doesn't, turning a situation that should be to your advantage (your team has ults ready and the other team has used ults), into a neutral situation (both teams have ults) _at best_, and instantly losing the fight at worst.

    If you ever read an Overwatch forum, whether that's one of the reddit forums or the official Blizzard forums, a common complaint is "why are my teammates so bad? I'm at \[90%-ile to 95%-ile rank\], but all my teammates are doing obviously stupid game-losing things all the time, like \[an example above\]". The answer is, of course, that the person asking the question is also doing obviously stupid game-losing things all the time because anyone who doesn't constantly make major blunders wins too much to stay at 95%-ile. This also applies to me.

    People will argue that players at this rank _should_ be good because they're better than 95% of other players, which makes them relatively good. But non-relatively, it's hard to argue that someone who doesn't realize that you should step on the objective to probably win the game instead of not touching the objective for a sure loss is good. One of the most basic things about Overwatch is that it's an objective-based game, but the majority of players at 90%-ile to 95%-ile don't play that way.

    For anyone who isn't well into the 99%-ile, reviewing recorded games will reveal game-losing mistakes all the time. For myself, usually ranked 90%-ile or so, watching a recorded game will reveal tens of game losing mistakes in a close game (which is maybe 30% of losses, the other 70% are blowouts where there isn't a single simple mistake that decides the game).

    It's generally not too hard to fix these since the mistakes are like the example above: simple enough that once you see that you're making the mistake, the fix is straightforward because the mistake is straightforward.

3.  There are probably some people who just want to be angry at their teammates. Due to how infrequently you get matched with the same players, it's hard to see this in the main rated game mode, but I think you can sometimes see this when Overwatch sometimes runs mini-rated modes.

    Mini-rated modes have a much smaller playerbase than the main rated mode, which has two notable side effects: players with a much wider variety of skill levels will be thrown into the same game and you'll see the same players over and over again if you play multiple games.

    Since you ended up matched with the same players repeatedly, you'll see players make the same mistakes and cause themselves to lose in the same way and then have the same tantrum and blame their teammates in the same way game after game.

    You'll also see tantrums and teammate blaming in the normal rated game mode, but when you see it, you generally can't tell if the person who's having a tantrum is just having a bad day or if it's some other one-off occurrence since, unless you're ranked very high or very low (where there's a smaller pool of closely rated players), you don't run into the same players all that frequently. But when you see a set of players in 15-20 games over the course of a few weeks and you see them lose the game for the same reason a double digit number of times followed by the exact same tantrum, you might start to suspect that some fraction of those people really want to be angry and that the main thing they're getting out of playing the game is a source of anger. You might also wonder about this from how some people use social media, but that's a topic for another post.

4.  For example, there will also be players who have some kind of disability that prevents them from improving, but at the levels we're talking about, 99%-ile or below, that will be relatively rare (certainly well under 50%, and I think it's not unreasonable to guess that it's well under 10% of people who choose to play the game). IIRC, there's at least one player who's in the top 500 who's deaf (this is severely disadvantageous since sound cues give a lot of fine-grained positional information that cannot be obtained in any other way), at least one legally blind player who's 99%-ile, and multiple players with physical impairments that prevent them from having fine-grained control of a mouse, i.e., who are basically incapable of aiming, who are 99%-ile.

    There are also other kinds of reasons people might not improve. For example, Kevin Burke has noted that when he coaches youth basketball, some children don't want to do drills that they think make them look foolish (e.g., avoiding learning to dribble with their off hand even during drills where everyone is dribbling poorly because they're using their off hand). When I spent a lot of time in a climbing gym with a world class coach who would regularly send a bunch of kids to nationals and some to worlds, I'd observe the same thing in his classes -- kids, even ones who are nationally or internationally competitive, would sometimes avoid doing things because they were afraid it would make them look foolish to their peers. The coach's solution in those cases was to deliberately make the kid look extremely foolish and tell them that it's better to look stupid now than at nationals.
