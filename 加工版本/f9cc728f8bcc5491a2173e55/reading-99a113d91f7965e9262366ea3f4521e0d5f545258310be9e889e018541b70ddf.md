# On being welcoming

---

## 正文

_注意：这大概不该是你阅读的第一篇关于 effective altruism 的文章。它会给你留下相当 biased 的印象！如果你此前还没读过相关内容，不妨先读读我整理的那份[阅读清单](http://www.benkuhn.net/ea-reading)。_

> effective altruist movement 中有个笑话，说其中有“各种各样的人——mathematicians、economists、philosophers，_以及_ computer scientists”
> 
> —Scott Alexander

> 我们是 entrepreneurs 和 economists。是 CEOs 和 scientists。是 Students 和 philanthropists。
> 
> —[effectivealtruism.org](http://www.effectivealtruism.org/)

我遇到过的、年龄超过 50 且活跃于 EA community 的人，恰好只有一个。（就是 Peter Singer。）

我认识的 political conservatives 大概有两个。non-philosophy liberal arts majors 有三个。artist 一个。lawyer 一个。journalists 两个。活跃于 politics 的人一个。teacher 一个。在 [25 largest US occupations](http://www.careerinfonet.org/oview3.asp?level=overall&id=1&nodeid=5) 中任职的人，总共大概有五个。

而且我知道，自己因此错过了很多东西。那三个 liberal arts majors 是我最喜欢一起谈论 effective altruism 的人中的几位——因为我猜不到他们会说什么！对于大多数关于 effective altruism 的谈话，我已经知道 utilitarian-computer-programmer 会给出什么回答了（我自己就是这样的人）。相比之下，那些学过 history、English 或 social studies 的朋友，读过我没读过的东西，也用我没用过的方式思考过。吸收他们看待世界的方式，能让我以新的方式思考问题，而 Utilitarian Computer Programmer #1,001 就无法给我这些。

我上面列出的 numbers 相当难看，但按理说，要改善这些 numbers 应该不难。比如，[GiveWell 的 staff](http://www.givewell.org/about/people) 中大约有一半拥有 liberal arts degrees（尽管他们大多数人与更广泛的 EA community 来往不多）。排名前 25 的 occupations 占 workforce 的三分之一——150 million 人中有 50 million 人。那么，到底是怎么回事？为什么在引起那些与我们不同的人的兴趣这件事上，我们做得如此糟糕？

* * *

> 虽然我或许能理解，但我绝不可能去 town hall meeting 谈论“Utilitarians”。话还没说完，就会有人一记重拳打在我的喉咙上。
> 
> —Facebook 发帖者

> 很遗憾，我在 EA Global 感受不到欢迎。令人难过的是，去年的 EA conference 也没让我感到多受欢迎。
> 
> 参会申请里确实有下面这个问题；让我觉得 elitist、exclusive 又陌生的事情有很多，这只是其中之一：
> 
> “请 Describe 一个你曾 hacked 的 non-technical system，以及你是如何 hacked 它的。”
> 
> —Facebook 发帖者

没有人是故意让别人感到不受欢迎。但_光这样还不够_。即使我们没有有意识地 exclude 别人，我们仍会习惯性地使用 in-group jargon，把谈话引向自己最喜欢的话题，让新来的人在 meetups 上安静地坐在角落里。我们会谈论 low-socioeconomic-status 的人如何“不是 valuable recruits”，或者举办以烧烤为主题的 EA meetups，又或者在一年一度的大型聚会的广告中，[只让 tech CEOs 和 Peter Singer](http://www.eaglobal.org/#speakers-page) 出镜。

我绝不是说，做这些事的人是坏人，抱着糟糕的想法。事实上，问题恰恰在于，他们_并不是_。除非我们仔细考虑别人会如何理解我们——不只是那些与我们想法相近的人，也包括那些思考方式与我们截然不同的人——否则这类事情自然而然就会发生。

> 你也看得出来，我不是 philosophy student（这也是我很少在这里发帖的原因之一）。
> 
> —Facebook 发帖者

> 就我自己初识 EA 的经历而言，刚接触时，“EA community”给我的感觉非常 exclusive，而且 hostile。因为，尽管我当时基本上已经是个 consequentialist，也已经希望在世界上产生 high positive impact，但我还没有想透那些我遇到的大多数“EAs”早已花了几个月乃至几年思考的问题；即使某件事我一听到、稍作考虑就觉得有道理，我仍然觉得自己因为此前没有想到它而受到了负面的评判，而我之所以没有想到，仅仅是因为截至那时，我生活中的种种境况恰好从未给过我任何契机，让我去考虑那件具体的事。
> 
> —EA Forum 发帖者

* * *

好吧，那么我们究竟能_做_些什么？下面是一些建议，并不 exhaustive。

-   去关心别人的经历。不要试图说服他们“成为 EAer”；试着让_他们_来说服_你_相信某件事。弄清楚他们的 perspective 能为 effective altruism 增添什么，而不是 effective altruism 应该从他们身上减去什么。

-   试着思考 effective altruism 的不同部分。很多人喜欢钻研 philosophical 的细枝末节，或者兴致勃勃地讨论 QALY weightings，但还有大片我们相对很少探索的谈话领域，对 outsiders 而言，那些话题会相对更有趣。

-   专门为 outreach 多花些心力。一个人如果是某个 group 唯一的 representative，往往会感到不自在，也容易有不好的经历，_即使他们受到的对待与其他任何人都一样。_而且，如果不专门花些心力，也更不容易接触到他们，因为大多数人的朋友都与自己相似。这意味着，仅仅为了抵消其他所有人都是 utilitarian computer programmer 所带来的 distorting effect，多花些心力就很有帮助。

-   举个具体例子，假设你正在组织一场 meetup。向那些你认为能为 effective altruism 的讨论带来新 perspective 的人发出特别邀请。请其他常来参加 meetup 的人也这样做。然后，请这些受到特别邀请的人下次邀请_他们的_朋友。如果活动由一位原本会被视为 outsider 的人主持，就尤其有帮助。

-   大家到场后，留意谈话的走向，确保它没有逐渐滑向某个让人觉得无聊的 attractor。（我和任何人一样喜欢钻研 QALY weighting 的细枝末节，但我宁愿不把这种喜好强加给别人！）如果有些新来的人似乎没有参与到大群体的谈话中，分成更小的 groups 可能会有帮助，因为这样能让人不那么紧张。

-   如果你要向一群人介绍什么，试着从一个 outsider 的角度来看待它（最好是从几个 outsiders 的角度）。他们会觉得格格不入吗？尽量不要使用太多 in-group jargon，也不要把这个 group 呈现得完全 homogeneous。你不需要撒谎或歪曲事实——但如果你发现自己陷入两难，要么夸大其词，要么让活动显得 exclusionary，那就想想下次该怎样解决这个问题。

## 原文

_Note: this should probably not be the first thing you read about effective altruism. It’ll give you a pretty biased impression! If that’s you, try something from the [reading list](http://www.benkuhn.net/ea-reading) I compiled instead._

> the joke in the effective altruist movement that it contains “all kinds of people—mathematicians, economists, philosophers, _and_ computer scientists”
> 
> —Scott Alexander

> We’re entrepreneurs and economists. CEOs and scientists. Students and philanthropists.
> 
> —[effectivealtruism.org](http://www.effectivealtruism.org/)

I’ve met exactly one person over 50 who’s active in the EA community. (It’s Peter Singer.)

I know maybe two political conservatives. Three non-philosophy liberal arts majors. One artist. One lawyer. Two journalists. One person who’s active in politics. One teacher. Maybe five people total in the [25 largest US occupations](http://www.careerinfonet.org/oview3.asp?level=overall&id=1&nodeid=5).

And I know I’m missing out because of it. Those three liberal arts majors are some of my favorite people to talk to about effective altruism—because they’re not predictable! I already know the utilitarian-computer-programmer answer to most conversations about effective altruism (being one myself). Whereas my friends who have studied history or English or social studies have read things I haven’t and thought in ways I haven’t. Absorbing their view of the world gives me new ways to think about things in ways that Utilitarian Computer Programmer #1,001 just can’t.

The numbers I listed above are pretty abysmal, but you’d think it wouldn’t be hard to improve them. For instance, about half of [GiveWell’s staff](http://www.givewell.org/about/people) have liberal arts degrees (though most of them don’t engage that much with the broader EA community). The top 25 occupations make up a third of the workforce—50 out of 150 million. So what gives? Why are we doing so abysmally at piquing the interest of anyone different from us?

* * *

> Though I may be able to understand it, there’s no way I can go to a town hall meeting talking about “Utilitarians”. Somebody would thunder punch me in the throat mid-sentence.
> 
> —Facebook poster

> I’m sorry to say that I don’t feel welcome at EA Global. And sadly, I didn’t feel very welcome at the EA conference last year either.
> 
> This actual question on the application to attend is one of many things that has stuck me as elitist, exclusive, and unfamiliar:
> 
> “Describe some non-technical system you’ve hacked and how you hacked it.”
> 
> —Facebook poster

Nobody is trying to make people feel unwelcome. But _that’s not enough_. Even if we don’t consciously exclude people, we fall into patterns of in-group jargon, we steer the conversation towards our favorite subjects, we let the newbies sit quietly in a corner at meetups. We talk about how low-socioeconomic-status people “aren’t valuable recruits” or have barbecue-themed EA meetups or have [only tech CEOs and Peter Singer](http://www.eaglobal.org/#speakers-page) featured in the advertisement for our annual mega-meetup.

I emphatically don’t mean that the people who did these things were bad people with bad ideas. In fact, the problem is precisely that they _weren’t_. This is the kind of thing that happens by default, unless we think carefully about how we’ll be interpreted—not just by people who think like us, but by people who think very differently.

> As you can tell, I’m not a philosophy student (one of the reasons I rarely post in this space).
> 
> —Facebook poster

> Speaking from my own introduction to EA, at first touch the feel of “the EA community” was very exclusive and hostile, because even though I was already essentially a consequentialist and already wanted to have a high positive impact in the world, I hadn’t thought through what most of the “EAs” I was meeting had spent months or years thinking about already, and even if something made sense as soon as I heard it and considered it, I felt negatively judged for not having already thought of it just because I hadn’t happened to have previously had any prompting to consider that specific thing from any of the conditions of my life up until then.
> 
> —EA Forum poster

* * *

Okay, so what can we actually _do_? Here are some non-exhaustive suggestions.

-   Get interested in other people’s experiences. Don’t try to convince them to “become an EAer;” try to get _them_ to convince _you_ of something. Figure out how their perspective can add to effective altruism, not what effective altruism should subtract from them.

-   Experiment with thinking about different parts of effective altruism. Lots of people love getting into the philosophical weeds or geeking out about QALY weightings, but there are vast expanses of conversational territory that we leave relatively unexplored that would be comparatively more interesting to outsiders.

-   Make special outreach efforts. People who are the only representative of some group tend to feel uncomfortable and have bad experiences, _even if they’re treated the same as anyone else._ They’re also less likely to be reached without special effort, since most people’s friends are similar to them. That means it helps to put in extra effort just to counter the distorting effect of everyone else being a utilitarian computer programmer.

-   For a concrete example, suppose you’re running a meetup. Extend special invitations to people you think could add a new perspective to a discussion about effective altruism. Ask other meetup regulars to do the same. Then ask the specially-invited people to invite _their_ friends the next time. It particularly helps if one of the people who would otherwise be perceived as an outsider is hosting the event.

-   Once people are there, keep tabs on the conversation to make sure it’s not drifting off into an attractor that bores people. (I love getting into the weeds of QALY weighting as much as anyone, but I’d rather not impose this view on other people!) If there are new folks who don’t seem engaged in the large group, it might help to break into smaller groups, as these can be less intimidating.

-   If you’re presenting something to a group, try to look at it as an outsider would (or even better, several outsiders). Would they feel out of place? Try not to use too much in-group jargon or present the group as totally homogeneous. You don’t need to lie or distort things—but if you find yourself caught between stretching the truth or making the event feel exclusionary, think of ways to fix that next time.
