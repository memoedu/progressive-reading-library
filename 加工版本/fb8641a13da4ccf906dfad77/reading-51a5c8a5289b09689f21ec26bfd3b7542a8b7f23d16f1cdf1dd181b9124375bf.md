# 因 friction 而死

---

## 正文

[是时候 build 了](https://a16z.com/2020/04/18/its-time-to-build/)，Marc Andreessen 写道。这篇文章以 Covid pandemic 为背景。文章的第一部分讨论了对 pandemics 的 preparation，

> 问题的一部分显然在于 foresight，在于 imagination 的失败。但问题的另一部分，在于我们事先没有 _do_ 的事情，以及我们现在仍未能做到的事情。而这是 action 的失败，具体来说，就是我们普遍缺乏 _build_ 的能力。

除了未能 plan 或 build，还可以再加上一项：未能 _maintain_。

2006 年，在 Gov. Schwarzenegger 执政下，California 已经制定了一项 [plan](https://revealnews.org/article/california-created-a-massive-medical-reserve-with-acute-care-beds-ventilators-and-n95-masks-and-let-it-collapse/)，用来 address 像当前这样的 pandemics；而且这不只是一个 plan，所需的 equipment 实际上已经 manufactured，随后也已 purchased。

> 据前 emergency management officials 和 state budget records，该州当时 [tax revenue 充裕](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-may-13-me-budget13-story.html)，很快就向 mobile hospital program 和一项相关的 Health Surge Capacity Initiative 投入了超过 \$200 million，以 stockpile medicines 和 medical gear，供 outbreaks of infectious disease 时使用。

但后来

> 然而，这项雄心勃勃的举措——在该州如今面对 new coronavirus 时，本来会至关重要——却碰了壁：一场严酷的 recession、state revenues 的 free fall，以及在 2011 年上台的、注重财政的 Democratic governor Jerry Brown 的 administration；他就任时面对的是 \$26 billion 的 deficit。
> 
> 于是，那一年，该州 cut off 了用于 store 和 maintain supplies 的 stockpile 以及 mobile hospitals 的资金。这些 hospitals 甚至还没用过，就已被 defunded。
> 
> 前 health officials 表示，许多 medical equipment 被交给了 local hospitals 和 health agencies，其中包括 ventilators，这些 critical life-saving tools 在当前的 pandemic 中正处于 short supply。但这些 equipment 虽然被 donated，却没有附带任何用于 maintain 它们的 funding。那些 respirators 被任由 expire，也没有被 replaced。

现在，人们或许会问，可以做些什么来避免这一整类错误。诚然，成为一个 prepper society 并不是最 efficient 的事情，但如果可以预见，你将遭遇影响所有人的 impredictable shocks（至于具体何时发生、发生什么，则无法预见），那么你大概就需要那些 emergency plans。因此，working assumption 应该是：下一场 global pandemic 肯定会发生；未知的是会是什么 virus、它会从哪里来，但它一定会来。我们会有充足的时间去 plan、build 和 prepare，但那些 preparations 必须随着时间推移持续得到 maintained。

## 因 friction 而死

Finance 是文章涉及的另一个话题；US government 可以接受所有人的付款，却无法轻松向所有人 pay，这一事实大概与 US 的 tax 地狱有些共同之处；至少在 UK，大多数人不必考虑 taxes，反正 government 已经知道关于你的所有必要信息，所以 taxes 会替你缴好。US 的情况也是一样——这就是为什么会有 tax fraud：他们毫无道理地 double checking 你的工作。我在 UK 曾经被多征了 tax。正如那个梗所说，你绝对想不到接下来发生了什么；我只需要在一个设计得很-好-的 website 上 log-in，在一个 box 里填入我的 bank account details，两天后就收到了 refund。就这么简单。不需要 complex forms，不需要任何 paperwork、打电话，或诸如此类的事情。

Venmo——以及程度较轻的 Plaid——也都是失败的症状，它们是针对问题的 patches，而不是针对 root cause 的 fixes；它们是围绕一个 broken system 展开的、-值得欢迎的- innovations，却不是对 system 本身的 fix。它们确实有存在的必要，这意味着 underlying systems 不足以满足对 fast payments 或 easily accessible APIs 的 demand。在 UK，Monzo 或 Starling 这样的新一波 banks，以及到了现在这个阶段大概所有其他 banks，都 provide 与 Venmo 相同的 functionality，只不过 payment 是真实的（它会 instantly 到达一个真正的 bank account）。那么，为什么在 US 不能进行 instantaneous [payments](https://www.mercatus.org/bridge/podcasts/11112019/george-selgin-past-present-and-future-real-time-payments-system) 呢？对 banks 来说，原因不会让你意外：原来，创办一家新 bank 并不像创办千篇一律的 SaaS business 那么容易。即使不走向 [fully free banking](https://www.alt-m.org/2015/04/28/what-you-should-know-about-free-banking-history/)，也可以做得更好；UK 在 fintech 方面处于领先地位，同样有充分的理由；他们不仅制定了 favorable regulations，还就创办 bank 所需遵循的 steps 提供了良好的 guidance 和 [explainers](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/new-bank/new-bank-start-up-unit-guide.pdf?la=en&hash=30E5AF0EAEB35E5E857C7187416AA5A46DDA4117)，或设立了 [regulatory sandbox](https://www2.deloitte.com/content/dam/Deloitte/uk/Documents/financial-services/deloitte-uk-fca-regulatory-sandbox-project-innovate-finance-journey.pdf)，帮助 banks 起步，而不必经历 onerous approval processes[1](https://nintil.com/on-building/#however)。当你 reduce 从 idea 走向 product 的 friction 时，你就会得到更多 products；你既可以增强人们 build 的意愿，也可以让 building 变得更容易；但改变意愿比改变 incentives 更难。

> 你并不只是在 pandemic 中，或更广泛的 healthcare 中，看到这种沾沾自喜、这种对 status quo 的满足以及不愿 build 的态度。在 Western life 的方方面面，尤其是 American life 的方方面面，你都能看到它。
> 
> 你能在 housing 以及我们 cities 的 physical footprint 中看到它。在 economic potential 不断涌现的 cities 中，我们根本无法 build 足够的 housing——这导致 San Francisco 等地的 housing prices 疯狂飙升，让普通人几乎不可能搬进去，获得未来的 jobs。我们甚至也已经无法 build cities 本身了。当 HBO 的《Westworld》的制片人想要描绘未来的 American city 时，他们没有去 Seattle、Los Angeles 或 Austin 拍摄——他们去了 Singapore。我们所有最好的 cities 都应该拥有熠熠生辉的 skyscrapers 和壮观的 living environments，其水平应该远远超过现在；它们在哪里？

我不会说这是沾沾自喜。确实存在一些自满，不过我不知道其中有多少自鸣得意。在 Apple 出现之前，并没有对 smartphones 的 demand；或者，在 Superhuman 出现之前，并没有对 fast email apps 的 demand。直到你看到 screens 上的 90 Hz refresh rates，你才知道自己想要它。在未来到来之前，人们并没有对未来的 demand。

在 housing 方面，也不是自满的问题。就拿 San Francisco 来说。为什么 San Francisco 在 skypscraper building 方面没有 outcompeting Singapore？因为 build 是 illegal 的。好吧，但为什么？毕竟，[San franciscoans](https://sf.curbed.com/2019/2/13/18223595/chamber-commerce-citybeat-2019-poll-housing) 确实想要更多 housing，并不只是那些在 twitter 上为此抱怨的 tech bros。一旦 build 那些熠熠生辉的 skyscrapers [成为可能](https://nintil.com/building-skyscrapers-and-spending-on-major-projects/)，US 与其他 countries 相比，表现并不差；在这里，实在没有太大必要对自己的国家大加鞭挞。所以，这里并不是 real state developers 懒惰而自满，而是在这个例子中，San Francisco 的 political structures 被一个 politically organized minority 所 captured，而他们不想要 density 和 skyscrapers。这不是不 building 的问题，而是从一开始就不允许 building 的问题：politics 和 regulation。当 politics 不再那么成问题时，你就会得到 [nice neighbourhoods as a service](https://culdesac.com/)。事实上，放眼按年份 sorted 的 world's tallest buildings 的 list，US 在 2019 和 2020 年的 entries 数量是[相当可观的](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings)。不过，接下来可以说的是，与亚洲的同类建筑相比，US skyscrapers 看起来平淡无奇、缺乏感染力。这是为什么？US designs 的 build 成本大概更低，但它们并不 provide 相同的 aesthetic experience。在酷炫的 buildings 出现在你的 city 之前，人们对它们并没有 demand，但到了那时已经太晚了。

起初，我能想象到对这篇文章的一种反应：聚焦 skyscrapers 或许是误入歧途；尽管它们作为 unique works of art 有某种 intrinsic appeal——skyscrapers 如此 expensive 的一个原因在于，每一座往往都是 artisanal、one of a kind 的作品，而不是 mass-market product——人们对它们有 demand，是因为它们让 cities 能够实现 economies of scale，而后者之所以存在，又是因为我们推动 remote work 的力度还不够。因此，正确的理想不该是 skyscrapers，而应该是宜人的 walkable cities 和 work from home；虽然这些对倾向于未来主义的人来说不那么有吸引力，却是人们真正会更喜欢的生活。不过，这种 critique 并不恰当：有了 skyscrapers 和 faster transportation，city 中的生活就变得可以与住在拥有宽敞 garden 的 spacious house 中的生活兼容，因此你可以灵活决定自己想住在哪里。随着 transportation technologies 的进步，[tradeoffs](https://nintil.com/progress-in-progress/) 就不再那么成问题了。

## Education（教育）

> 你在 education 中也能看到这一点。没错，我们有 top-end universities，但它们的 capacity 只能 teach 美国每年新增的 4 million 名 18 岁人群中 microscopic percentage 的人，或全世界每年新增的 120 million 名 18 岁人群中 microscopic percentage 的人。为什么不 educate 每一名 18 岁的人？这难道不是我们能做的最重要的事吗？为什么不 build 数量多得多的 universities，或把我们已有的 universities 大幅 scale？K-12 education 中上一次 major innovation 是 Montessori，它可以追溯到 1960s；此后 50 年来，我们一直在做 education research，却始终没有达到 practical deployment；为什么不利用我们现在知道的一切，build 更多出色的 K-12 schools？我们知道 one-to-one tutoring 可以 reliably increase education outcomes，幅度达到 two standard deviations（即 Bloom two-sigma effect）；我们有 internet；为什么我们还没有 built systems，把每一位 young learner 与一位年龄更大的 tutor 进行 match，以 dramatically improve student success？

对于为什么不做我们已经学到的所有事情，一个回答是，其中真正 works 的很少。我写过一篇更长的 research essay，讨论 [Bloom's two sigma](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/) 以及总体上的 education research，从中可以得出的 conclusion 是，researching 更好的 learn 方式很难。Bloom's two sigma 充其量更像是 Bloom's one sigma[2](https://nintil.com/on-building/#kids)，不过，用 software-based methods 来 complement 甚至 replace teachers 仍有希望。为什么 large public education systems 还没有将这些 systems 进行 deployed at scale，仍是一个谜；对它们来说，developing software tutoring 是一项 one-off cost——所 taught 的 content 几乎不变——因此，这可能要么与不知道这些 systems 是可行的有关，要么与 system 自身内部的 opposition 有关（又是 politics）。

对更多 universities 的强调也放错了重点；那些 top end universities 在很大程度上是为已经 skilled 的人服务的 [accreditation systems](https://nintil.com/notes-on-the-case-against-education/)。当然，你可以 scale up Harvard，让它 educate 多 1000x 的人，但我怀疑，这是否能让 expanded set of alumni 产出的 patents、companies 或 research 增加到接近 1000x。

## Atoms，而非 bits

> 你在 manufacturing 中也能看到这一点。与 conventional wisdom 相反，American manufacturing output 比以往任何时候都 higher，但为什么如此多的 manufacturing 被 offshored 到 manual labor 更 cheaper 的地方？我们知道如何 build highly automated factories。我们知道，为了 design、build 和 operate 那些 factories，我们会 create 数量庞大的 higher paying jobs。我们知道——而且此刻正在亲身经历！——relying on offshore manufacturing 来生产 key goods 的 strategic problem。为什么我们不在全国各地 building Elon Musk 的“alien dreadnoughts”——giant、gleaming、state of the art 的 factories，以 highest possible quality 和 lowest possible cost，producing 每一种可以想象的 product？

一个原因是，即便可以 build highly automated factories，在 Vietnam 的 Taiwanese 也同样可以，而且还能 leveraging 更 cheaper 的 workforce；software 正在 eating 整个世界，而不只是 US，technology 也会 diffuses across borders。此外，相对于某个给定的 technological development 水平，你[并不总是想要更多 automation，](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2018/apr/16/elon-musk-humans-robots-slow-down-tesla-model-3-production)目前，在许多 industrial tasks 中，employ human workers 更 efficient，因此 workforce 的 cost 仍会在 location decisions 中发挥作用。再说，forcing reshoring of manufacturing 的 costs and benefits 就摆在那里；假设你有一份 national pandemic stockpile，随时可以在需要时 deployed。这就能为你争取足够的时间，让 international supply chains 自行理顺，而不必始终 own manufacturing capability。[3](https://nintil.com/on-building/#wang)

> 你在 transportation 中也能看到这一点。supersonic aircraft 在哪里？数以 millions 计的 delivery drones 在哪里？high speed trains、高架的 monorails、hyperloops，还有，对，flying cars，都在哪里？

Supersonic aircrafts 正在慢慢到来。最先是价格昂贵、面向 business-class 的 Boom Aero planes，希望将来还会有由 [SABRE](https://www.reactionengines.co.uk/beyond-possible/sabre) powered 的 hypersonic economy class airliners。其他一切也都可以这样说。hyperloops 和 [flying cars](https://lilium.com/) 正在被 built，而且，如果有人特别担心 West 的话，它们并不是在 Asia 被 built 的。在我看来，比起不加限定地呼吁 building，更需要的是 building 得 _faster_。

> 事实上，我认为，building 就是我们 reboot American dream 的方式。我们以 huge quantities 来 build 的东西，比如 computers 和 TVs，其 price 会 rapidly drop。我们没有这样做的东西，比如 housing、schools 和 hospitals，其 price 则会 skyrocket。American dream 是什么？是拥有自己的家，并能养活一家人的机会。我们需要打破 housing、education 和 healthcare 那些 rapidly escalating price curves，确保每个美国人都能实现这个梦想，而唯一的办法就是 build。

这是一个关键 insight，也是我在自己的 skyscrapers essay 中提出的一点，但不应如此乐观：确实可以通过 mass-manufacturing 来 bring down manufacturing 的 cost 和 time requirements，这也能 applied 到 [housing](https://factoryos.com/) 上，但如果你还想让 designs 是 gleaming 且 unique 的，那就别指望 returns to scale 和 learning by doing 了。其他几项也同样 problematic；building physical spaces 本身不是问题（除非它是一家 [hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_need)）；问题在于，以 _affordable_ 的方式 provision _high quality_ services（education 和 healthcare）。按 risk-adjusted outcomes 来看，US 的 healthcare system 可能是世界上最好的（也就是说，如果你患有 cancer，在 US 的 recovering odds 比在 UK 更好），而 top universities 也同样在 US。当然，这也是一个出了名的 unaffordable system。无论打算如何 solve，它的 core 都不会是 software，而是 politics。在目前的 incentives 和 regulatory landscape 下，能在 US _build_ 一个 affordable healthcare system 吗？大概不能，不过，如果我错了，那就更好了。你 _just_ _even_ 能 open 一家 hospital 并 provide super cheap healthcare 吗？

## 那个 P-word

> 我预料这篇 essay 会成为 criticism 的对象。这是我给 critics 的一个 modest proposal。与其 attacking 我关于该 build 什么的 ideas，不如 conceive 你们自己的！你们认为我们应该 build 什么？我极有可能会同意你们。

虽然我无意让这成为对 Andreessen 的 essay 的 critique；事实上我赞同它的精神；但我认为更有意思的 question 是：为什么东西没有被 built？为什么东西没有比现在 built 得更快？每个 sector 中起作用的 mechanistic forces 是什么？我的 thesis 是，mentality of improvement——用 [Anton Howes](https://antonhowes.substack.com/p/age-of-invention-higher-perfection) 的 terms 来说——并没有消失；而是从 zero 走向 curing cancer 的 frictions 增加了。

但究竟应该 built 什么呢。看看任何一个已经存在很久的 organization。随着它们年岁渐长，organizations 会 degrade in function，并停止对 resources 进行 most efficient use，从而使它们的 replacement 成为 necessity[4](https://nintil.com/on-building/#value)，这是一条 law of nature 吗？以全世界的 space launch sector 为例。几十年来，我们 launched rockets，然后把它们扔掉；当然，曾经有一些 [proof of concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-X)，表明避免这种 engineering 形式的 economic masochism 是 achievable 的，但 reusing rockets 这个 idea 直到 [SpaceX](https://www.quora.com/What-is-SpaceXs-market-share) 加入后才真正成了气候。并不是 industry 中的 insiders，也不是哪个特定的人在 demanding reusable rockets；它曾是一个无人想到的 possibility——就像 smartphone——直到某个不知从哪里冒出来的 [techbro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk) 让它成为现实。还有什么像 space launch industry 一样？大多数 airplanes 都由 Boeing 和 Airbus made，而且看起来它们并没有在被 replaced。大多数 major drug companies 都已经超过一百岁了。

最近有一些讨论，围绕一篇呼吁 cutting off 给 [WHO](https://thedispatch.com/p/suspending-who-funding-should-be) 的 funding 的文章。我的 [回应](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1250697207797473281) 是，也许我们需要摆脱它，再建一个。这句话听起来不可思议。它可是 _THE WORLD_ 的 Health Organization，名字里就写着呢。但 providing worldwide coordination for health 的不一定非得是那个特定的 organization。其他一切也一样，仅仅因为它一直在那里，并不意味着它会或应该永远留在那里，更不意味着它之所以存在，是因为它 proper、good 且 efficient。

这就把我们带到了 politics 问题。自然，如果接受 organizations 就像 [Javascript frameworks](https://blog.revillweb.com/why-javascript-churn-is-a-good-thing-607b0d53018c) 一样需要 constant replacement 这个 idea，就难免注意到 governments 根本没有被 disrupted，除了偶尔发生的 revolution 或 war，而这些在世界上 civilised 的地区几乎不存在。因此，对 politics 的失望以及拒绝参与 politics，似乎成为 Silicon Valley 的 ethos 的一部分，也就不足为奇了；这导致人们尝试寻找绕开它的 solutions，比如 [seasteading](https://www.seasteading.org/) 或 charter [cities](https://www.pronomos.vc/knowledge)（希望它们能展示什么是可能的，并在更大的 polities 中 spark change！）。但就像一个 individual philantrophist 独自 funding scientist，不如影响 NIH 如何 allocates 自己规模大得多的 funding 那样 effective，绕开 politics 也不如参与这场游戏 effective。话虽如此，作为一个年轻聪明的人，你会怎么做：在 Google [Rest and vest](https://www.businessinsider.com/rest-and-vest-millionaire-engineers-who-barely-work-silicon-valley-2017-7?r=US&IR=T)，还是参与 slow moving、lowly paid 的 think tank 游戏？这根本不用想。但这也是一个 prisoners dilemma：你和处境相似的 techbros 从 long term 来看，最终都会更穷。¯\\_(ツ)_/¯

## 更好的 regulation 可能是什么样

![](https://nintil.com/images/2020-04-19-on-building/xvent-aov-smoke-vent-roof-light-ce-mark-1080x675.jpg)

如果你住在 Europe[5](https://nintil.com/on-building/#underwriter)，你大概见过 [CE logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CE_marking) 。但你知道要把它放在一个 product 上需要做什么吗？人们可能以为，这涉及一个漫长的 process，需要向 government agency 发送 product 的 samples，而这些 samples 必须 conform to 荒唐地 exact 的 requirements，规定的是怎么做，而不是做什么[6](https://nintil.com/on-building/#supersonic)。但不一定如此，在最简单的情况下，只需要 asserting 你 conform to 某个已公布的 standard（或者，如果你愿意为它与 official standard 一样 safe 这一点作辩护，你也可以自行制定 standard），把 marking 贴在你的 product 上，就完事了。之后，governments 会对 market 进行 sampling，检查 products 是否真的 conform；如果没有，就会强制进行一次 costly recall。这个 mechanism 把 low friction（想 sell 就可以直接 sell）与 conform to standards 的 incentives（如果弄错了，你确实要付出高昂代价）结合起来。这对 riskless products 来说都很好，但即使是更 complex 的 products，比如涉及 medical equipment equipment 的，你也可以让一家称为 notified body 的 private certification agency——[从各家相互 competing 的机构中选一家](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/notified-bodies-for-medical-devices/notified-bodies-for-medical-devices)\- 为你进行 testing 和 assessment。实际上，这就引入了 regulatory competition，而不是只有一家 regulatory agency。虽然这个 system 不 apply 于 automotive、aerospace 或 pharmaceutical sectors——但确实 apply 于 EU 的 medical devices——想象一下，如果在 US 有 FDA 的 competitors（由一个 Meta-FDA regulated）；例如，人们可以废除 Phase I/II/III trial system，改为根据 case-by-case 的情况采用 relaxed requirements。任何关于 accelerating drug-to-market 的 proposal 都可以尝试。人们必须仔细 design meta-regulations，以便协调 incentives，balance safety 和 innovation；例如，如果这些 regulators certify 了 unsafe drug，你可以让它们承担 liable 的责任，同时强制它们持有与得到 certified 的 drugs 的 volume 成 proportion 的 insurance policy。你可以创办一家 predictions-market-based agency，利用 market 来 judge 正在考虑 approval 的东西的 effectiveness，诸如此类。

因此，迫切需要 building 的，是能够 enable 并 encourage builders 的 systems（institutions、regulations）。那句名言说的是“Build，他们就会来”，但我们也可以说“Regulate wisely，他们就会 build”。

恰好，[Collison questions](https://patrickcollison.com/questions) 中有一个是

> **对于没有 natural way to die 的 systems，你如何 ensure adequate replacement rate？**
> 
> Schumpeter 主张，capitalism 的问题不在于 capital 如何被 allocated 给 _existing_ structures，而在于 structures 如何被 created 和 destroyed。Systems 和 institutions 不可避免地会变得陈旧，或变得不那么 effective，但也会非常努力地 survive。（另见：[institutional imperative](http://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)、Berliner 对“[invisible foot](https://www.amazon.com/Innovation-Decision-Soviet-Industry/dp/0262520524)”的 commentary，以及 Herbert Kaufman 的[短书](https://www.amazon.com/Government-Organizations-Immortal-Herbert-Kaufman/dp/0815748396)。）Bankruptcy 会处理 senescent businesses。但对于并不 naturally subject to extinction processes 的 systems 和 institutions，我们如何获得 sufficient replacement rate？

## 原文

[It's time to build](https://a16z.com/2020/04/18/its-time-to-build/), writes Marc Andreessen. The piece is framed in the context of the Covid pandemic. The first part of the essay is about preparation for pandemics,

> Part of the problem is clearly foresight, a failure of imagination. But the other part of the problem is what we didn’t _do_ in advance, and what we’re failing to do now. And that is a failure of action, and specifically our widespread inability to _build_.

There is one more that can be added, besides failing to plan or build: failing to _maintain_.

In 2006, California, under Gov. Schwarzenegger had a [plan](https://revealnews.org/article/california-created-a-massive-medical-reserve-with-acute-care-beds-ventilators-and-n95-masks-and-let-it-collapse/) in place to address pandemics like the current one, and it wasn't just a plan, the equipment required was actually manufactured and subsequently purchased.

> The state, [flush with tax revenue](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-may-13-me-budget13-story.html), soon sunk more than $200 million into the mobile hospital program and a related Health Surge Capacity Initiative to stockpile medicines and medical gear for use in outbreaks of infectious disease, according to former emergency management officials and state budget records.

But then

> But the ambitious effort, which would have been vital as the state confronts the new coronavirus today, hit a wall: a brutal recession, a free fall in state revenues – and in 2011, the administration of a fiscally minded Democratic governor, Jerry Brown, who came into office facing a $26 billion deficit.
> 
> And so, that year, the state cut off the money to store and maintain the stockpile of supplies and the mobile hospitals. The hospitals were defunded before they’d ever been used.
> 
> Much of the medical equipment – including the ventilators, critical life-saving tools that are in short supply in the current pandemic – was given to local hospitals and health agencies, former health officials said. But the equipment was donated without any funding to maintain them. The respirators were allowed to expire without being replaced.

Now one may wonder what can be done to avoid this entire class of mistakes. Sure enough, becoming a prepper society is not the most efficient thing there is, but if you are going to predictably have impredictable shocks (As to the when and what exactly) that affect everyone, you probably need those emergency plans. So the working assumption should be that there will be a next global pandemic for sure, what is unknown is what virus and where it will come from, but it will come. There will be plenty of time to plan and build and prepare, but those preparations have to be maintained over time.

## Death by friction

Finance is another of the topics touched on by the article; the fact that the US government can be paid by everyone but not easily pay everyone probably shares something with the US tax hell; in at least the UK there is no need to think about taxes for most people the government knows everything they need to know about you anyway so they are paid for you. In the US it is the same case - that's why tax fraud is a thing, they are double checking your work for no good reason. In the UK I was once taxed in excess. As the meme goes, you won't believe what happened next; I just had to log-in in a -nicely- designed website, write my bank account details in a box and two days later I was refunded. Just like that. No need for complex forms, any paperwork, phone calls, or anything of the sort.

Venmo -and to a lesser extent, Plaid- are symptoms of failure too, they are patches to problems, not fixes to their root cause, they are -welcome- innovations around a broken system but not a fix for the system itself. The real need for their existence means that the underlying systems are insufficient to meet the demand for fast payments or easily accessible APIs. In the UK the new wave of banks like Monzo or Starling, and probably all the other ones at this stage, provide the same functionality as Venmo, only that the payment is real (It lands in a real bank account instantly). Now why can't you do instantaneous [payments](https://www.mercatus.org/bridge/podcasts/11112019/george-selgin-past-present-and-future-real-time-payments-system) in the US? For banks, the story won't surprise you: Turns out that starting a new bank is not as easy as your cookie-cutter SaaS business. Without going to [fully free banking](https://www.alt-m.org/2015/04/28/what-you-should-know-about-free-banking-history/) you can do better; again the UK is the leader at fintech for a good reason; not only they crafted favorable regulations but also issued good guidance and [explainers](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/new-bank/new-bank-start-up-unit-guide.pdf?la=en&hash=30E5AF0EAEB35E5E857C7187416AA5A46DDA4117) of the steps one needs to follow to start a bank or set up a [regulatory sandbox](https://www2.deloitte.com/content/dam/Deloitte/uk/Documents/financial-services/deloitte-uk-fca-regulatory-sandbox-project-innovate-finance-journey.pdf) to help banks get started without having to go through onerous approval processes[1](https://nintil.com/on-building/#however). When you reduce friction to go from idea to product you get more products, you can either increase the desire to build or you can make building easier; but changing desires is harder than incentives.

> You don’t just see this smug complacency, this satisfaction with the status quo and the unwillingness to build, in the pandemic, or in healthcare generally. You see it throughout Western life, and specifically throughout American life.
> 
> You see it in housing and the physical footprint of our cities. We can’t build nearly enough housing in our cities with surging economic potential — which results in crazily skyrocketing housing prices in places like San Francisco, making it nearly impossible for regular people to move in and take the jobs of the future. We also can’t build the cities themselves anymore. When the producers of HBO’s “Westworld” wanted to portray the American city of the future, they didn’t film in Seattle or Los Angeles or Austin — they went to Singapore. We should have gleaming skyscrapers and spectacular living environments in all our best cities at levels way beyond what we have now; where are they?

I wouldn't say it is smug complacency. There is some complacency, to be sure, though I don't know how smug it is. There is no demand for smartphones unless Apple shows up; or there is no demand for fast email apps until Superhuman shows up. Once you see 90 Hz refresh rates on screens you didn't know you wanted it. There is no demand for the future until the future is here.

In housing it's not complacency either. Take San Francisco. Why isn't San Francisco outcompeting Singapore at skypscraper building? It's illegal to build. Okay, but why? After all [San franciscoans](https://sf.curbed.com/2019/2/13/18223595/chamber-commerce-citybeat-2019-poll-housing) do want more housing, it's not just the tech bros who rant on twitter about it. Once [it is possible](https://nintil.com/building-skyscrapers-and-spending-on-major-projects/) to build those gleaming skyscrapers, the US does not do bad compared to other countries, the need for national flagellation here is rather moderate. So here it's not that the real state developers are lazy and complacent, it's that the political structures of, in this case, San Francisco, are captured by a politically organized minority who does not want density and skyscrapers. That's not a problem of not building, but of not allowing building in the first place: politics and regulation. When politics is less of an issue you get [nice neighbourhoods as a service](https://culdesac.com/). In fact looking more broadly the list of the world's tallest buildings, sorted by year has a [respectable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings) number of entries for the US in 2019 and 2020. What one can then say though is that the US skyscrapers look bland and uninspiring compared to their Asian counterparts. Why is that? The US designs are probably cheaper to build, but they don't provide the same aesthetic experience. There is no demand for cool buildings until they are in your city but by then it is too late.

At first a reaction I can imagine to the piece may be that focus on skyscrapers may be misguided; while they have some intrinsic appeal as unique works of art - a reason skyscrapers are so expensive is that each one tends to be an artisanal, one of a kind piece, not a mass-market product - they are demanded because they enable economies of scale in cities but in turn those exist because we are not pushing hard enough for remote work. The right ideal would thus be not skyscrapers but nice walkable cities and work from home, which while they appeal less to the futuristically inclined, is what people actually would prefer. However that critique would be misplaced: With skyscrapers and faster transportation, life in the city becomes compatible with life in a spacious house with an ample garden, so you can fluidly decide where you want to live. With progress in transportation technologies [tradeoffs](https://nintil.com/progress-in-progress/) become less of an issue.

## Education

> You see it in education. We have top-end universities, yes, but with the capacity to teach only a microscopic percentage of the 4 million new 18 year olds in the U.S. each year, or the 120 million new 18 year olds in the world each year. Why not educate every 18 year old? Isn’t that the most important thing we can possibly do? Why not build a far larger number of universities, or scale the ones we have way up? The last major innovation in K-12 education was Montessori, which traces back to the 1960s; we’ve been doing education research that’s never reached practical deployment for 50 years since; why not build a lot more great K-12 schools using everything we now know? We know one-to-one tutoring can reliably increase education outcomes by two standard deviations (the Bloom two-sigma effect); we have the internet; why haven’t we built systems to match every young learner with an older tutor to dramatically improve student success?

One answer to why not do all the things we have learned is because very little works. I wrote a longer research essay on [Bloom's two sigma](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/) and education research in general and the conclusion one gets from it is that researching better ways to learn is hard. Bloom's two sigma is more like Bloom's one sigma[2](https://nintil.com/on-building/#kids), at best, though there is promise in software-based methods to complement or even replace teachers. It remains a puzzle why large public education systems have not deployed these systems at scale; for them developing software tutoring is a one-off cost - the content of what is taught changes very little -, so presumably this may have to do with either lack of knowledge that these systems are possible, or opposition from within the system itself (again, politics).

The emphasis on more universities is also misplaced; those top end universities are to a large extent [accreditation systems](https://nintil.com/notes-on-the-case-against-education/) for those already skilled. Sure you can scale up Harvard to educate 1000x more people but I doubt that will yield anywhere near 1000x more patents, companies, or research coming out of the expanded set of alumni.

## Atoms, not bits

> You see it in manufacturing. Contrary to conventional wisdom, American manufacturing output is higher than ever, but why has so much manufacturing been offshored to places with cheaper manual labor? We know how to build highly automated factories. We know the enormous number of higher paying jobs we would create to design and build and operate those factories. We know — and we’re experiencing right now! — the strategic problem of relying on offshore manufacturing of key goods. Why aren’t we building Elon Musk’s “alien dreadnoughts” — giant, gleaming, state of the art factories producing every conceivable kind of product, at the highest possible quality and lowest possible cost — all throughout our country?

A reason is that even when it's possible to build highly automated factories, so can the Taiwanese in Vietnam, leveraging the cheaper workforce too; software is eating the world, not just the US, and technology diffuses across borders. Plus you [don't always want more automation,](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2018/apr/16/elon-musk-humans-robots-slow-down-tesla-model-3-production) relative to a given level of technological development; right now it is more efficient to employ human workers in many industrial tasks, hence the cost of the workforce continues to play a role in location decisions. And then again; the costs and benefits of forcing reshoring of manufacturing are there; suppose you have a national pandemic stockpile, ready to be deployed when needed. That buys you enough time for international supply chains to sort themselves out, without having to own the manufacturing capability the entire time. [3](https://nintil.com/on-building/#wang)

> You see it in transportation. Where are the supersonic aircraft? Where are the millions of delivery drones? Where are the high speed trains, the soaring monorails, the hyperloops, and yes, the flying cars?

Supersonic aircrafts are slowly getting here. First by means of business-class expensive Boom Aero planes and hopefully in the future [SABRE](https://www.reactionengines.co.uk/beyond-possible/sabre) powered hypersonic economy class airliners. The same can be said about everything else. The hyperloops and the [flying cars](https://lilium.com/) are being built, and not in Asia, if one specifically worries about the West. More than a unqualified call for building; as I see it there is more of a need for building _faster_.

> In fact, I think building is how we reboot the American dream. The things we build in huge quantities, like computers and TVs, drop rapidly in price. The things we don’t, like housing, schools, and hospitals, skyrocket in price. What’s the American dream? The opportunity to have a home of your own, and a family you can provide for. We need to break the rapidly escalating price curves for housing, education, and healthcare, to make sure that every American can realize the dream, and the only way to do that is to build.

This is a key insight, and a point I make in my skyscrapers essay but one shouldn't be that optimistic: It is true that one can bring down the cost and time requirements of manufacturing by means of mass-manufacturing, and this can be applied to [housing](https://factoryos.com/) too but if you also want the designs to be gleaming and unique, forget about returns to scale and learning by doing. The other items are equally problematic; building the physical spaces themselves is not the problem (Unless it is a [hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_need)); the problem is the _affordable_ provision of _high quality_ services (education, and healthcare). The US has probably the best healthcare system in the world by risk-adjusted outcomes (ie. if you have cancer you have better odds of recovering in the US than in the UK), and the top universities are equally the US. It is of course a notoriously unaffordable system. Regardless of what plan one has to solve it is not going to have software at its core but politics. Can one _build_ an affordable healthcare system in the US given the current incentives and regulatory landscape? Probably not, though the better if I'm wrong. Can you _just_ _even_ open a hospital and provide super cheap healthcare?

## The P-word

> I expect this essay to be the target of criticism. Here’s a modest proposal to my critics. Instead of attacking my ideas of what to build, conceive your own! What do you think we should build? There’s an excellent chance I’ll agree with you.

While I don't intend this to be a critique of Andreessen's essay; in fact I agree with its spirit; I think the more interesting question is Why aren't thing being built? Why aren't things being built faster than they are? What are the mechanistic forces at play in each sector?. My thesis is that the mentality of improvement - to put it in [Anton Howes](https://antonhowes.substack.com/p/age-of-invention-higher-perfection)' terms- has not been lost; rather the frictions in going from zero to curing cancer have increased.

But what should be built. Look at any organization that has been around for a long time. Is it a law of nature that, as they age, organizations degrade in function and stop making the most efficient use of resources, making their replacement a necessity[4](https://nintil.com/on-building/#value)? Take the entire world's space launch sector. For decades we launched rockets and threw them away; sure there were [proof of concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-X) that showed that avoiding this engineering form of economic masochism was achievable but the idea of reusing rockets didn't really became a thing until [SpaceX](https://www.quora.com/What-is-SpaceXs-market-share) came in. It wasn't the insiders in the industry, or it wasn't anyone in particular demanding reusable rockets, it was an unthinked possibility -like the smartphone- until some random [techbro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk) made it real. What's like the space launch industry? Most airplanes are made by Boeing and Airbus and it doesn't look like they are getting replaced. Most major drug companies are over a century old.

There was some recent discussion about an article that called for cutting off funding to the [WHO](https://thedispatch.com/p/suspending-who-funding-should-be). My [response](https://twitter.com/ArtirKel/status/1250697207797473281) is that maybe we need to get rid of it and make another one. That sentence sounds unthinkable. It's _THE WORLD_'s Health Organization, right in the title. But it doesn't have to be that specific organization providing worldwide coordination for health. The same is true for everything else, just because it has been there forever doesn't mean it will or should stay there forever, let alone the reason for it being there is that is is proper, good, and efficient.

Which brings us to the politics issue. Naturally if one buys the idea that just like [Javascript frameworks](https://blog.revillweb.com/why-javascript-churn-is-a-good-thing-607b0d53018c) organizations need constant replacement one can't help but notice that governments are not disrupted at all, save the occasional revolution or war which are virtually nonexistant in civilised areas of the globe. So it's no surprise that frustration with and refusal to engage in, politics, seems part of Silicon Valley's ethos, which leads to attempts to seek solutions that work around it like [seasteading](https://www.seasteading.org/) or charter [cities](https://www.pronomos.vc/knowledge) (Hopefully those will exemplify of what's possible and spark change in larger polities!). But just like an individual philantrophist funding scientist on their own is less effective than affecting the way the NIH allocates its own vastly greater funding, working around politics is less effective than playing the game. That said as a young smart individual what will you do: [Rest and vest](https://www.businessinsider.com/rest-and-vest-millionaire-engineers-who-barely-work-silicon-valley-2017-7?r=US&IR=T) at Google, or play the slow moving lowly paid think tank game? It's a no brainer. But it's also a prisoners dilemma: You and your similarly situated techbros will end up poorer long term. ¯\\_(ツ)_/¯

## What better regulation could look like

![](https://nintil.com/images/2020-04-19-on-building/xvent-aov-smoke-vent-roof-light-ce-mark-1080x675.jpg)

If you live in Europe[5](https://nintil.com/on-building/#underwriter) you have probably seen the [CE logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CE_marking) . But do you know what it takes to get it on a product? One might think that it involves a lengthy process involving sending a government agency samples of the product that have to conform to absurdly exact requirements about how rather than what[6](https://nintil.com/on-building/#supersonic). But not necessarily, it involves in the simplest case just asserting that you conform to a published standard (Or you can make up your own standard if you are willing to defend that it is as safe as the official one), slapping the marking on your product, and that's it. Afterwards, governments will be sampling the market to see if the products actually conform and if they don't they will force a costly recall. This mechanism couples both low friction (You can just sell if you want) with incentives to conform to the standards (If you get it wrong, you do pay a hefty price). This is all good for riskless products, but even for more complex ones, involving, say, medical equipment equipment, you can have a private certification agency known as a notified body -[among various competing ones](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/notified-bodies-for-medical-devices/notified-bodies-for-medical-devices)\- do the testing and assessment for you. In effects this introduces regulatory competition instead of having a single regulatory agency. While this system does not apply in the automotive, aerospace or pharmaceutical sectors -but it does for medical devices in the EU-, imagine if you had, in the US, FDA competitors (regulated by a Meta-FDA); one could for example scrap the Phase I/II/III trial system and have relaxed requirements on a case-by-case basis. Any proposal on accelerating drug-to-market could be tried. One would have to design the meta-regulations carefully so as to line up incentives to balance safety and innovation; for example you could make these regulators liable if they certify an unsafe drug, while forcing them to have an insurance policy in proportion to the volume of drugs that gets certified. You could start a predictions-market-based agency that uses the market to judge the effectiveness of what is considering for approval, you name it.

Hence what urgently needs building is systems (institutions, regulations) that enable and encourage builders. The quote goes "Build and they will come", but we may say "Regulate wisely and they will build" as well.

Appropriately, one of the [Collison questions](https://patrickcollison.com/questions) is

> **How do you ensure an adequate replacement rate in systems that have no natural way to die?**
> 
> Schumpeter claimed that the problem of capitalism is not how capital is allocated to _existing_ structures but how structures are created and destroyed. Systems and institutions inevitably get stale or become less effective but also work very hard to survive. (See also: the [institutional imperative](http://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html), Berliner's commentary on the "[invisible foot](https://www.amazon.com/Innovation-Decision-Soviet-Industry/dp/0262520524)", and Herbert Kaufman's [short book](https://www.amazon.com/Government-Organizations-Immortal-Herbert-Kaufman/dp/0815748396).) Bankruptcy takes care of senescent businesses. But how do we get a sufficient replacement rate in systems and institutions that aren't naturally subject to extinction processes?
