## 入门材料

先读这三篇文章中的一篇（不是全部），就能大致了解像 effective altruist 一样思考意味着什么——主要是 treating all lives equally，以及努力去做 helps 得 _most_ 的事情（而不只是做某件 helps 的事情）。

-   [_Efficient Charity: Do Unto Others_](http://www.effective-altruism.com/efficient-charity-do-unto-others/) 是一篇介绍 basic EA ideas 的出色入门文章，作者的文笔非常棒。正是这篇文章让我开始认真思考 effective altruism。（1,800 词）

    > 想象一下，你正准备在预算有限的情况下，出发穿越北极，进行一场危险的探险。杂货店里那位头发花白的老探矿者遗憾地摇摇头：你买不起所有需要的东西，只能买最必需的物品，然后盼着自己走运。但什么才是必需的？如果买最暖和的派克大衣就意味着买不起睡袋，你还应该买吗？你是否应该多带一周的食物以防万一，哪怕这意味着不带步枪？或者，你能不能买下步枪，不带食物，靠打猎解决晚餐？

-   如果你更喜欢视频，[Peter Singer 的 TED 演讲](https://www.ted.com/talks/peter_singer_the_why_and_how_of_effective_altruism) 是另一个介绍 EA 的经典入门材料。（17 分钟）

    > 我想知道，你们当中有多少人在看着\[一个幼儿被车辆碾过的画面\]时，刚才对自己说：“我不会那样做。我会停下来帮忙。”如果你有过这个念头，请举手。

    > 正如我所想，你们大多数人都这样想过。而且我相信你们。我确信你们说得对。不过，在过于赞许自己之前，先看看这个。UNICEF 报告说，2011 年，有 6.9 million 名不满五岁的儿童死于 preventable、poverty-related diseases。UNICEF 认为这是好消息，因为这个数字一直在从 1990 年的 12 million 稳步下降。这确实是好事。但即便如此，6.9 million 仍然意味着每天有 19,000 名儿童死去。我们没有在街上从他们身边走过，这真的重要吗？他们离我们很远，这真的重要吗？我不认为这确实构成了 morally relevant difference。他们不在我们眼前，当然，还有他们属于不同的 nationality 或 race，这些在我看来都不是 morally relevant。真正重要的是，我们能否 reduce 那个 death toll？我们能否 save 每天死去的那 19,000 名儿童中的一些？

-   Wikipedia 也有一篇不错的[总体介绍](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism)，不过比前两篇更枯燥一些。第一句话包含了我最喜欢的、用一句话描述 effective altruism 的表述。（2,300 词）

    > Effective altruism 是一种 philosophy 和 social movement，它 applies evidence 和 reason，来 working out 最 effective 的 ways，以 improve the world。Effective altruists 会 consider 所有 causes 和 actions，然后以能 brings about 最大 positive impact 的方式 act。正是这种 broad evidence-based approach，使 effective altruism 区别于 traditional altruism 或 charity。Effective altruism 有时涉及 taking actions，而这些 actions 不那么 intuitive 或 emotionally salient。philosopher Peter Singer 是 effective altruism 的一位著名 supporter。

## 关于 personal choices

如果被推得过于极端，effective altruism 可能显得苛求得令人难以承受。当钱本可以在别处 objectively do much more good 时，我们怎么能 justify 在自己身上花任何钱呢？对于这个问题，目前还没有什么现成的好答案——每个人都在努力弄清楚该在哪里划线，或该用什么 standard 要求自己。不过，读一读其他人的 personal strategies，以及他们对这个问题的看法，确实能带来很多启发。

-   Julia Wise 的优秀文章 [_Cheerfully_](http://www.effective-altruism.com/cheerfully/) 讨论了作者在 trading off 自己的 welfare 与他人的 welfare 时的挣扎。这篇文章没有辜负作者的名字。围绕这个话题，Julia 的整个博客 [Giving Gladly](http://www.givinggladly.com/) 都很值得一读。（500 词）

    > 我年轻一些的时候，意识到自己是否选择 donate，意味着另一个人 living 与 dying 之间的 difference，这让我受到极大的震动。很多 decisions 开始显得极其明显地 wrong。
    > 
    > 我记得自己告诉爸爸，我已经决定，对我来说，生孩子会是 immoral 的，因为孩子会把我太多的时间和钱从 better causes 上挪走。“听起来，这种生活方式不会让你开心，”他说。
    > 
    > “我的 happiness 不是重点，”我告诉他。

-   Jeff Kauffman（Julia 的丈夫）也写过[他和 Julia 如何 budget 他们的钱](http://www.jefftk.com/p/keeping-choices-donation-neutral)，以免每次在自己身上花钱都感到焦虑。Jeff 和 Julia 会 donate 他们 combined income 的大约 50%，所以，对于如何让 giving 在 psychologically 对他们行得通，他们已经摸索出了不少心得。（500 词）

    > 对我来说，关键是让大多数 choices 保持 donation neutral。每当有钱进账，我就把它 divide 成“要 give 给最 effective charity 的钱”和“可以随自己心意花的钱”。如何 divide，是一个艰难而令人苦恼的 choice，但这个 choice 我每年只需要做一次。这样，当我决定买什么东西时（袜子、房租、手机、乐器、食物），我知道这笔钱无论如何都不会被 given away，因此不必因为在与人们的 lives 进行 making tradeoffs 而不断感到内疚。

-   Bernadette Young 写过一篇出色的文章，讨论如何 reconciling effective altruism 与 [parenthood](http://www.effective-altruism.com/parenthood-and-effective-altruism/)；后者是一个人可能做出的 costliest personal decisions 之一。这是一个典范，展示了如何在努力过 effective life 的背景下，对待其他同样艰难的 decisions。（3,000 词）

    > 成为父母这一 choice，是我们能够做出的最重大、最具 binding 性的 life decisions 之一。如果你想在自己人生的重大 decisions 中 incorporate effective altruism 的 principles，这个 decision 可能会如何展开？在考虑 parenthood 的 costs 和吸引力时，我将论证，生孩子确实可以与这些 principles 保持 consistent。我还会解释，为什么我认为 EA movement 应该对把 parenthood 作为 EA life 一部分的想法持开放态度。

-   去年 12 月的 giving season，GiveWell 的员工共同发表了一篇关于[他们的 personal donation plans](http://blog.givewell.org/2013/12/12/staff-members-personal-donations/) 的文章。作为 effective altruists 的 thought processes 的一个例子，它很有启发性。（4,100 词）

    > 然而，完全不 giving to charity（或 give 给雇用我的 organization），我会觉得不妥。一般来说，我努力按照自己希望别人采取的方式行事：我努力在“standard” generosity 和 ethics 上做得很好，再把自己更 personal、debatable、potentially-biased 的 agenda 叠加在这个基础上，而不是用它取代这个基础。我不会偷钱去 give 给我们的 top charities；我不会为了给 GiveWell 的工作省时间，就缺席重要的家庭活动（即便这个活动对我没什么意义）；同样地，我希望每年把自己钱财中相当大的一部分 give 出去，而且这种方式 (a) aims at helping the less fortunate，(b) 不会被误认为是 aiming to benefit myself or those in my circle。此外，纯粹为了 helping others 而开支票，也是我希望自己一直保持实践的事情。

## 关于像 effective altruist 一样思考

在我看来，effective altruism 有四个主要 principles：

-   **Maximization:** 努力做到 _the most_ good，而不只是 _some_ good（并且接受不同事物会带来不同 amounts of good）。

-   **Cause-neutrality:** 愿意根据任何 cause 有多 effective 来 consider 它，而不是根据对某个 cause 已有的“passion”或“connection”来 giving。

-   **Egalitarianism:** 对所有 sentient beings 的 well-being 都 equally 地 valuing。

-   **Consequentialism:** 将你的 actions 的 _all_ consequences 都 taking into account，而不只是 direct consequences。例如，如果你不接受 nonprofit 的那份工作，他们是否就会 hired 其他人来代替你？

尽管这些 ideas 看起来 intuitive 而且 obvious，要充分消化它们却很难！所以，看看其他人沿着这些 ideas 走到了哪里，会有所帮助。

-   GiveWell 有一份 [charity evaluation questions 的清单](http://www.givewell.org/charity-evaluation-questions)，对其 process 的探讨非常深入。（很难统计字数；随意看看，略读即可）

-   想看 GiveWell 的 process 的一个 example application，可以看看他们[关于 GiveDirectly 的 report](http://www.givewell.org/international/top-charities/give-directly)，这是他们目前的 top-rated charity。不过，最好别全部读完——实在太长了！（26,600 词）

## 关于 cause comparison

cause prioritization 的 fundamental question 是：我们如何 compare 两个看似 disparate 的 causes 所带来的 good 有多少？给一个家庭提供 malaria-preventing bednet，与给一个孩子提供能够 prevent parasitic worm infections 的 drugs 相比，我们应该如何 value 两者？

-   在 global health 中，一个有助于回答这个问题的 idea 是 QALY，也就是“Quality-Adjusted Life Year”，你可以[在 Wikipedia 上](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QALY)阅读相关介绍。QALY 是一种 unit，用来 measures 患上一种 typical disease 在 on average 意义上有多 bad。尽管 QALYs 确实是一种 flawed assessment tool，但作为进行困难的 cross-cause comparisons 的初次尝试，它们很有启发性。（1,000 词）

-   当然，事情并不像 estimating 每种 intervention 的 dollars-per-QALY 然后 comparing 那么简单——现实世界要复杂得多。GiveWell 的博文 [_Some Considerations Against More Investment in Cost-Effectiveness Estimates_](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/11/04/some-considerations-against-more-investment-in-cost-effectiveness-estimates/) 列出了这种 approach 的一些 problems。（1,100 词；可略读）

    > 简而言之，我们 argue，目前可用的最佳 cost-effectiveness estimates，尽管背后有极其强大的团队和 funding，却同时存在以下 problematic 特征：extremely simplified（ignoring 重要但 difficult-to-quantify 的 factors）、extremely sensitive（assumptions 的 small changes 可能 lead to figures 的 huge changes），以及没有经过 reality-checked（large flaws 可能多年都 unchecked——而且 unnoticed）。我们认为，同时 improve 这三方面在 conceptually 上很困难：improving 前两方面很可能需要大幅增加 complexity，而这反过来会削弱外部人士 understand 和 reality-check estimates 的能力。考虑到为了 creating 我们目前看到的这些 problematic estimates，已经 invested 了如此多的 resources，我们不确定能否用 reasonable resources 来 created 真正 reliable 的 estimates——甚至不确定究竟能否做到。

-   要了解弄清什么 works、什么不 works 有多难，可以看看一些令人意外的 examples：The High-Impact Network 制作了一份[关于 charitable effectiveness 的 quiz](http://www.thehighimpactnetwork.org/modules/charity-assessment)。即便只是猜测这些 charities 产生了 _positive_ 还是 _negative_ effects，都相当困难，更不用说它们有多 cost-effective 了！（2,300 词）

    > **Program description（项目描述）.** “在 1970s，New Jersey（USA）一所 prison 中 serving life sentences 的 inmates 开始了一项 program，试图通过‘scare’或 deter，让 at-risk 或 delinquent children 避免未来走上 crime 的道路。这项名为‘Scared Straight’的 program，其 main component 是由 inmates 对前来参观 prison facility 的 juveniles 进行 aggressive presentation。这种 presentation 描绘了 adult prisons 中的生活，而且经常包含夸大的 rape 和 murder 故事……这项 program 获得了大量 favorable media attention，很快就在全国超过 30 个 jurisdictions 中 replicated。”
    > 
    > **Decide（作出判断）.** 你认为 Scared Straight 产生的是 (a) good effect（即 decreased the rate of juvenile crime）、(b) bad effect（即 increased the rate of juvenile crime），还是 (c) 没有 effect？请自己想出答案。

## 关于一些 effective altruists 喜欢的 specific causes

想获得非常宽泛的概览，可以阅读 Luke Muehlhauser 的 [Four Focus Areas of Effective Altruism](http://www.effective-altruism.com/four-focus-areas-effective-altruism/)，其中概述了许多人认为尤其可能 effective 的 broad areas：reducing poverty、growing the EA movement、improving the prospects of the far future，以及 improving the lives of nonhuman animals。在这些领域之内，还有不少更 concrete 的 areas 和看起来 promising 的 specific things to do。（900 词）

-   GiveWell 有一份 [GiveWell Labs Priority Causes](http://blog.givewell.org/2012/05/09/givewell-labs-update-and-priority-causes/) 清单，涵盖了他们对 global health 和 nutrition、funding scientific research、meta-research，以及 global catastrophic risks 的关注。你可以通过阅读 GiveWell 网站上的其他各种资料，进一步了解这些内容。（1,500 词）

-   80,000 Hours 是一个 EA organization，它有一篇介绍 causes 的[稍长文章](http://80000hours.org/blog/300-which-cause-is-most-effective--300)，按 importance、tractability 和 uncrowdedness 给出了 ratings。内容与前文有些重叠，也有一些更深入的探讨。（7,000 词；可略读）

-   相对于其 importance，投入的工作似乎明显不足的一个 area，是对 _global catastrophic risks_ 的 research——这类 low-probability event 可能 seriously damage human civilization。这里的经典参考资料是 Future of Humanity institute 的 Nick Bostrom 所著的 [_Global Catastrophic Risks_](http://smile.amazon.com/Global-Catastrophic-Risks-Nick-Bostrom-ebook/dp/B003WE9D7M/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1408554899&sr=8-1&keywords=global+catastrophic+risks)；不过，如果想读短一些的内容，可以试试 [Wikipedia](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_catastrophic_risks)。（5,500 词；可略读）

-   另一个受到欢迎的 area 是 reducing nonhuman animals 的 suffering，它们的生活往往相当糟糕，尤其是 factory farms 中的那些。有一个名为 Animal Charity Evaluators 的 organization，试图制作一份 GiveWell-style 的 top animal-welfare charities 清单；[他们的 FAQ](http://www.animalcharityevaluators.org/about/faq/) 是了解 reducing animal suffering 的一个很好的信息来源。（2,300 词；可略读）

    > 我们当然相信，addressing global poverty 和其他 human-centric causes 有很大的 value。不过，考虑到我们的 goal 是 most efficiently reduce the largest amount of suffering，有非常 compelling 的理由将重点放在 animals 上。Animals 的数量远远 outnumber humans。举例来说，全球每年有近 60 billion 只 animals 为了 food 而被 bred 和 killed；相比之下，截至 December 2013，整个 human population 是 7.13 billion。此外，众多 sources（undercover videos、standard agricultural practices）都表明，animals 所 endure 的 suffering——往往 caused by human self-interest——极其巨大。通过 educating 人们如何 best advocate for animals，我们为让最多的 sentient creatures 获得 largest possible reduction of suffering 作出贡献。

## 关于决定如何度过你的一生

-   80,000 Hours 通过 coaching 和 research，帮助人们借助自己的 careers 来 do the most good。他们有一篇[篇幅庞大的 research 文章](http://80000hours.org/blog/314-in-which-career-can-you-make-the-most-difference)，按照许多 criteria 对多种不同的 careers 进行 compares。（7,000 词；可略读）

    > 许多接受我们 coaching 的人，起初对自己能做什么几乎没有头绪。他们的问题只是：“我有哪些 options？”对于已经有几个不错的 options 可供考虑的人，最常问的问题之一是：“我是不是漏掉了什么好的 options？”这份 list 的目的就是帮助回答这些问题。

-   若想看看稍有不同的 perspective，GiveWell 的 Holden Karnofsky 曾举行过一次[关于 altruistic career choice 的 conference call](http://www.givewell.org/altruistic-career-choice)，我觉得很有帮助——它更多谈的是如何拥有 effective career 的 heuristics，而不是像上面那篇 80k 文章那样提供具体建议。该页面链接的 transcript 很长，但如果你也有时间，值得一读。（页面上有 300 词，另有一份 15,000 词的通话 transcript）

    > “我应该把自己的 time/talents ‘give’ 到哪里？”这个问题可以 broken down 为“我应该如何将自己的 time/talents develop 成具有 great value 的东西？”以及“我应该如何 select 合适的 organization/cause/area，将那种 value apply 到其中？”对于像这次通话的参与者这样处于相对 early-career 阶段的人，我认为前一个问题值得更多关注（不过也不是完全不考虑第二个问题）。因此，我鼓励大家格外重视这类问题：“我能在哪些事情上 succeed？”以及“我怎样才能 improve 自己的 abilities、knowledge、network，以及整体 degree of success？”我相信，不同的人适合在非常不同的事情上、以非常不同的方式 succeed，而 greater success 意味着在 having impact 方面有显著更好的 options。

## Introductory materials

Start with one (not all) of these three articles for a good overview of what it means to think like an effective altruist—mainly about treating all lives equally, and trying to do what helps the _most_ (rather than just something that helps).

-   [_Efficient Charity: Do Unto Others_](http://www.effective-altruism.com/efficient-charity-do-unto-others/) is a fantastic primer on basic EA ideas by a really awesome writer. This is the essay that got me seriously thinking about effective altruism. (1,800 words)

    > Imagine you are setting out on a dangerous expedition through the Arctic on a limited budget. The grizzled old prospector at the general store shakes his head sadly: you can’t afford everything you need; you’ll just have to purchase the bare essentials and hope you get lucky. But what is essential? Should you buy the warmest parka, if it means you can’t afford a sleeping bag? Should you bring an extra week’s food, just in case, even if it means going without a rifle? Or can you buy the rifle, leave the food, and hunt for your dinner?

-   If you prefer videos, [Peter Singer’s TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/peter_singer_the_why_and_how_of_effective_altruism) is another canonical introduction to EA. (17 minutes)

    > I wonder how many of you, looking at \[footage of a toddler being run over\], said to yourselves just now, “I would not have done that. I would have stopped to help.” Raise your hands if that thought occurred to you.

    > As I thought, that’s most of you. And I believe you. I’m sure you’re right. But before you give yourself too much credit, look at this. UNICEF reports that in 2011, 6.9 million children under five died from preventable, poverty-related diseases. UNICEF thinks that that’s good news because the figure has been steadily coming down from 12 million in 1990. That is good. But still, 6.9 million is 19,000 children dying every day. Does it really matter that we’re not walking past them in the street? Does it really matter that they’re far away? I don’t think it does make a morally relevant difference. The fact that they’re not right in front of us, the fact, of course, that they’re of a different nationality or race, none of that seems morally relevant to me. What is really important is, can we reduce that death toll? Can we save some of those 19,000 children dying every day?

-   Wikipedia also has a good [general overview](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism), albeit drier than the previous two. The first sentence includes my favorite one-sentence description of effective altruism. (2,300 words)

    > Effective altruism is a philosophy and social movement which applies evidence and reason to working out the most effective ways to improve the world. Effective altruists consider all causes and actions, and then act in the way that brings about the greatest positive impact. It is this broad evidence-based approach that distinguishes effective altruism from traditional altruism or charity. Effective altruism sometimes involves taking actions that are less intuitive or emotionally salient. The philosopher Peter Singer is a notable supporter of effective altruism.

## On personal choices

If taken too extremely, effective altruism can seem incredibly demanding. How can we justify spending anything at all on ourselves when the money could objectively do much more good elsewhere? There’s no good canned answer to this question yet—everyone struggles with figuring out where to draw the line, or what standard to hold themselves to. But reading about other people’s personal strategies and takes on the question can be really informative.

-   Julia Wise’s excellent essay [_Cheerfully_](http://www.effective-altruism.com/cheerfully/) discusses the author’s struggles with trading off her own welfare against others’. It lives up to the author’s name. Julia’s entire blog, [Giving Gladly](http://www.givinggladly.com/), is well worth reading on this topic. (500 words)

    > When I was younger, I was extremely struck by the realization that my choice to donate or not meant the difference between someone else’s living and dying. A lot of decisions started to look very starkly wrong.
    > 
    > I remember telling my dad that I had decided it would be immoral for me to have children, because they would take too much of my time and money away from better causes. “It doesn’t sound like this lifestyle is going to make you happy,” he said.
    > 
    > “My happiness is not the point,” I told him.

-   Jeff Kauffman (Julia’s husband) has also written about [how he and Julia budget their money](http://www.jefftk.com/p/keeping-choices-donation-neutral) so that they don’t feel angst about every time they spend money on themselves. Jeff and Julia donate about 50% of their combined income, so they’ve figured out a lot about how to make giving work for them psychologically. (500 words)

    > For me the key is to make most choices donation neutral. As money comes in I divide it into “money to give to the most effective charity” and “money to spend as I wish”. How to divide it is a hard and distressing choice, but it’s one I only have to make once a year. Then when deciding to buy something (socks, rent, phone, instruments, food) I know it’s money that isn’t getting given away regardless, so I don’t have to feel constantly guilty about making tradeoffs with people’s lives.

-   Bernadette Young wrote an excellent piece on reconciling effective altruism with [parenthood](http://www.effective-altruism.com/parenthood-and-effective-altruism/), one of the costliest personal decisions one can make. It’s a paradigm example of treating other such difficult decisions in the context of trying to live an effective life. (3,000 words)

    > The choice to become a parent is one of the biggest and most binding life decisions we can make. How might that decision go if you want to incorporate principles of effective altruism in the big decisions of your life? In considering the costs and attractions of parenthood, I will make the case that having children can indeed be consistent with those principles. I’ll also explain why I think the EA movement should be open to the idea of parenthood as part of an EA life.

-   Last December in giving season, GiveWell’s staff co-published a post on [their personal donation plans](http://blog.givewell.org/2013/12/12/staff-members-personal-donations/). It’s very informative as an example of effective altruists’ thought processes. (4,100 words)

    > Yet it wouldn’t feel right to skip giving to charity altogether (or to give to the organization that employs me). In general, I try to behave as I would like others to behave: I try to perform very well on “standard” generosity and ethics, and overlay my more personal, debatable, potentially-biased agenda on top of that rather than in replacement of it. I wouldn’t steal money to give it to our top charities; I wouldn’t skip an important family event (even one that had little meaning for me) in order to save time for GiveWell work; and along the same lines, I want to give some substantial portion of my money each year in a way that (a) aims at helping the less fortunate (b) can’t be confused with aiming to benefit myself or those in my circle. In addition, cutting a check for the sole purpose of helping others is something I want to stay practiced at.

## On thinking like an effective altruist

In my view, there are four main principles of effective altruism:

-   **Maximization:** trying to do _the most_ good rather than just _some_ good (and accepting that different things do different amounts of good).

-   **Cause-neutrality:** being willing to consider any cause based on how effective it is, rather than giving based on pre-existing “passion” or “connection” to a cause.

-   **Egalitarianism:** valuing the well-being of all sentient beings equally.

-   **Consequentialism:** taking into account _all_ the consequences of your actions, not just the direct consequences. For example, if you don’t take that job with a nonprofit, would they have just hired someone else instead?

Although these ideas seem intuitive and obvious, they’re hard to digest fully! So it helps to look at where other people have gone with them.

-   GiveWell has a [list of charity evaluation questions](http://www.givewell.org/charity-evaluation-questions) that go really in-depth in their process. (hard to get a word count; just poke around and skim)

-   For an example application of GiveWell’s process, check out their [report on GiveDirectly](http://www.givewell.org/international/top-charities/give-directly), their current top-rated charity. But probably don’t read all of it—it’s really long! (26,600 words)

## On cause comparison

The fundamental question of cause prioritization is: how can we compare how much good is done by two disparate-seeming causes? How much should we value giving one family a malaria-preventing bednet, versus giving one child drugs that prevent parasitic worm infections?

-   One idea that helps answer this question in global health is the QALY, or “Quality-Adjusted Life Year”, which you can read about [on Wikipedia](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QALY). A QALY is a unit that measures how bad it is, on average, to be afflicted with a typical disease. While QALYs are certainly a flawed assessment tool, they are an instructive first stab at making difficult cross-cause comparisons. (1,000 words)

-   Of course, it’s not as simple as estimating the dollars-per-QALY of every intervention and then comparing—the real world is much more complex than that. GiveWell’s blog post [_Some Considerations Against More Investment in Cost-Effectiveness Estimates_](http://blog.givewell.org/2011/11/04/some-considerations-against-more-investment-in-cost-effectiveness-estimates/) lays out some of the problems with this approach. (1,100 words; skimmable)

    > In a nutshell, we argue that the best currently available cost-effectiveness estimates – despite having extremely strong teams and funding behind them – have the problematic combination of being extremely simplified (ignoring important but difficult-to-quantify factors), extremely sensitive (small changes in assumptions can lead to huge changes in the figures), and not reality-checked (large flaws can persist unchecked – and unnoticed – for years). We believe it is conceptually difficult to improve on all three of these at once: improving on the first two is likely to require substantially greater complexity, which in turn will worsen the ability of outsiders to understand and reality-check estimates. Given the level of resources that have been invested in creating the problematic estimates we see now, we’re not sure that really reliable estimates can be created using reasonable resources – or, perhaps, at all.

-   For some surprising examples of how hard it is to figure out what works and what doesn’t, The High-Impact Network made a [quiz on charitable effectiveness](http://www.thehighimpactnetwork.org/modules/charity-assessment). It’s quite hard to even guess whether these charities had _positive_ or _negative_ effects, let alone how cost-effective they are! (2,300 words)

    > **Program description.** “In the 1970s, inmates serving life sentences at a New Jersey (USA) prison began a program to ‘scare’ or deter at-risk or delinquent children from a future life of crime. The program, known as ‘Scared Straight’, featured as its main component an aggressive presentation by inmates to juveniles visiting the prison facility. The presentation depicted life in adult prisons, and often included exaggerated stories of rape and murder… The program received considerable and favorable media attention and was soon replicated in over 30 jurisdictions nationwide.”
    > 
    > **Decide.** Do you think that Scared Straight had a (a) good effect (i.e., decreased the rate of juvenile crime), (b) bad effect (i.e., increased the rate of juvenile crime), or (c) had no effect? Come up with your answer on your own.

## On specific causes that some effective altruists like

For a very broad overview, Luke Muehlhauser’s [Four Focus Areas of Effective Altruism](http://www.effective-altruism.com/four-focus-areas-effective-altruism/) outlines broad areas that a lot of people think are especially likely to be effective: reducing poverty, growing the EA movement, improving the prospects of the far future, and improving the lives of nonhuman animals. Within this, there are a number of more concrete areas and specific things to do that seem promising. (900 words)

-   GiveWell has a list of [GiveWell Labs Priority Causes](http://blog.givewell.org/2012/05/09/givewell-labs-update-and-priority-causes/) which covers their interest in global health and nutrition; funding scientific research; meta-research; and global catastrophic risks. You can get more info on these by reading various other materials on GiveWell’s site. (1,500 words)

-   80,000 Hours, an EA organization, has a [somewhat longer write-up](http://80000hours.org/blog/300-which-cause-is-most-effective--300) of causes with ratings for importance, tractability, and uncrowdedness. Some overlap with the previous, and some more depth. (7,000 words; skimmable)

-   One area that seems definitely under-worked-on compared to its importance is research on _global catastrophic risks_—the kind of low-probability event that might seriously damage human civilization. The classic reference here is the book [_Global Catastrophic Risks_](http://smile.amazon.com/Global-Catastrophic-Risks-Nick-Bostrom-ebook/dp/B003WE9D7M/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1408554899&sr=8-1&keywords=global+catastrophic+risks) by Nick Bostrom of the Future of Humanity institute, but for a shorter read, try [Wikipedia](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_catastrophic_risks). (5,500 words; skimmable)

-   Another popular area is reducing the suffering of nonhuman animals, who tend to live pretty bad lives, especially those on factory farms. There’s an organization called Animal Charity Evaluators that tries to do a GiveWell-style list of top animal-welfare charities, and [their FAQ](http://www.animalcharityevaluators.org/about/faq/) is a good source of info on reducing animal suffering. (2,300 words; skimmable)

    > We certainly believe there is great value in addressing global poverty and other human-centric causes. Nevertheless, given that our goal is to most efficiently reduce the largest amount of suffering, there is a very compelling case to focus on animals. Animals vastly outnumber humans. To illustrate, almost 60 billion animals are bred and killed for food each year worldwide, compared to the entire human population of 7.13 billion as of December 2013. Furthermore, as evidenced by numerous sources (undercover videos, standard agricultural practices), the suffering that animals endure – often caused by human self-interest – is enormous. By educating people about how they can best advocate for animals, we contribute to the largest possible reduction of suffering for the largest number of sentient creatures.

## On deciding what to do with your life

-   80,000 Hours coaching and research to help people do the most good with their careers. They have a [monster research post](http://80000hours.org/blog/314-in-which-career-can-you-make-the-most-difference) that compares a bunch of different careers on many criteria. (7,000 words; skimmable)

    > Lots of people who receive our coaching start with little idea about what they could do. Their question is just, ‘What are my options?’ For people who already have a couple of good options on the table, one of the most common questions asked is, ‘Have I overlooked something good?’ The purpose of this list is to help answer these questions.

-   For a somewhat different perspective, Holden Karnofsky of GiveWell had a [conference call on altruistic career choice](http://www.givewell.org/altruistic-career-choice) that I found quite helpful—more in terms of heuristics for having an effective career than specific suggestions like the 80k post above. The transcript linked from the page is long, but worth it if you have the time as well. (300 words on the page, plus a 15,000-word transcript of the call)

    > The question of “Where should I ‘give’ my time/talents?” can be broken down into “How should I develop my time/talents into something that has great value?” and “How should I select the right organization/cause/area to apply that value to?” For relatively early-career people such as the call participants, I believe the former question deserves more focus (though not entirely to the exclusion of the second question). As such, I encouraged people to place a lot of weight on questions like “What can I succeed at?” and “How can I improve my abilities, knowledge, network, and overall degree of success?” I believe that different people are cut out to succeed in very different things and in very different ways, and that greater success means dramatically better options for having impact.
