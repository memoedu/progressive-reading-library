## 正文

_这是一篇演讲的文字稿，探讨为什么 experts 对 artificial superintelligence (ASI) 是否会给 humanity 带来 existential risk 存在如此强烈的分歧。我回顾了双方的一些 key arguments，并强调，fundamental danger 并不在于“rogue ASI”是否会 gets out of control：而在于 ASI 将 confer 的 raw power，以及 creating dangerous technologies 的 barriers 会更低。这一点并不新鲜，但有两个尚未得到充分重视的 consequences。首先，许多人认为 rogue ASI 是 implausible，这使他们错误地 dismiss existential risk。其次：许多 AI alignment 工作虽然出于善意，却在 speeds progress toward catastrophic capabilities，同时并未 addressing 我们的世界对于 dangerous technologies 的 potential vulnerability。_

为什么深思熟虑、见多识广的人，对于来自 ASI 的 existential risk (xrisk) 会存在如此强烈的分歧？我相信你也知道，杰出的 scientists 和 technologists 在这个问题上存在巨大的分歧。一方认为 ASI 会带来 xrisk：

-   Geoffrey Hinton，modern neural networks 之父，也是 Turing Award 和 Nobel Prize 的获得者[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn1)：“我敲响的警钟关乎它们 taking control 所带来的 existential threat……如果你像我现在这样认真对待 existential risk，那么就此停止继续 developing 这些东西，或许是相当明智的。”
-   Yoshua Bengio，modern AI 的另一位主要贡献者，也是 Turing Award 的获得者[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn2)：“我们不知道，在它变得真正 dangerous 之前，我们还有多少时间……我得出的 probability 大约是，比如说，20 per cent，结果会是 catastrophic。”
-   Sam Altman，OpenAI 的 cofounder 和 CEO，在 cofounding OpenAI 前几个月发表的一篇深思熟虑的文章中写道[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn3)：“superhuman machine intelligence 的 Development 很可能是 humanity 的 continued existence 所面临的 greatest threat”。

而另一方则有许多人对这些担忧不以为然，例如：

-   Yann LeCun，Meta 的 Chief AI Scientist，也是 Turing Award 的获得者：关于 xrisk 的讨论是“为时过早”“荒谬至极”，而且“完全是胡说八道”。他还说过，ASI“将处于我们的 control 之下”[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn4)。
-   Andrew Ng，Google Brain 的 cofounder[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn5)：“我看不出 \[ASI\] 怎么会 lead to human extinction”；担心 xrisk“就像担心 Mars 上的 overpopulation 一样”。
-   Marc Andreessen，venture capitalist，也是 modern internet 的 founders 之一[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn6)：AI xrisk“具有 millenarian apocalypse cult 的所有 hallmarks”。

在一个对 humanity 如此重要的问题上，为什么见多识广的人之间会存在如此强烈的分歧？我经常听到的一种解释是，这是 short-term self-interest 的差异造成的——有时，人们会粗略地将其描述为：担忧 xrisk 的人想获得关注，或者通过 safety not-for-profits 赚钱，而怀疑 xrisk 的人则希望通过对 AGI 的 control 变得更富有、更有 power。但这些解释虽然方便，却过于轻率。许多担忧 xrisk 的知名人士正在作出巨大的 financial sacrifices，有时放弃了通过致力于 ASI 本可赚取的巨额财富，转而从事仅仅报酬不错的 safety 工作[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn7)。而且，虽然怀疑 xrisk 的人确实有赚钱和 acquire power 的 incentive，但他们更希望自己能活下去。我认为，这种分歧主要源于他们真诚持有的 underlying assumptions 存在差异。在本文中，我们将尝试更好地理解这些差异。

在这次讨论中，我们将 assume ASI 已经 achieved。也就是说，我们拥有在 human endeavour 的大多数 domains 中具有 superhuman performance 的 systems。因此，这里谈的不是今天广泛使用的 systems，例如 ChatGPT、Claude 和 Gemini。这里谈的是能够在几乎任何 cognitive task 上 outperform humans，同时也能够在 physical world 中 act 的 systems[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn8)。我们已经看到了这可能意味着什么的一些初步迹象：AlphaGo Master 对 Go 的 mastered 如此彻底，以至于有史以来最伟大的 human players 之一 Ke Jie 将它描述为“Go 之神”，并表示它揭示了“没有一个人触及过 Go 的 truth 的边缘”。同样，DeepMind 的 AlphaChip system 已经帮助 design 了几代 Google 的 TPU chips，并且“能在几小时内 generates superhuman 或 comparable chip layouts，而不需要人类花费数周或数月的 effort”。但 ASI 的 abilities 将远远超出这些 narrow examples——想象一下 AlphaGo 的 Move 37，不是作为一次性的 insight，而是以 a trillion-fold 的规模，pervasive 于世界上的多个 domains[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn9)。当然，这样的 systems 仍会 subject to 来自 physics、chemistry、biology、economics、computational complexity 等方面的 constraints。但它们将 demonstrate 今天看来近乎魔法的 capabilities，就像人类的许多 abilities 在其他 primates 看来难以理解一样。一些 skeptics 质疑“LLMs 究竟能否 become ASI”，但对我们而言，ASI 是通过 LLMs 还是某种不同的 approach achieved，并不重要。另一些人反驳说，距离 ASI 还要几十年——这可能是真的，也可能不是，但如果我们最终 achieve 它，这并不会改变 fundamental concerns。

最后，关于本文的 approach，有两点说明。首先，这次演讲探讨的 topics 已经有许多 researchers investigated。相关 literature 十分庞大，我的 citations 更多是 representative，而非 complete。对于那些工作被不公允地遗漏的人，我深表歉意。其次，我的 overall framing 强调 existential risk（“xrisk”），但讨论中的许多内容涉及 catastrophic risks——这些 scenarios 不会 directly cause extinction，却可能“仅仅”kill 数亿或数十亿人。显然，从 catastrophic risks 到真正的 existential risks 之间存在一个 spectrum，而 catastrophic events 可能 trigger 一些 events（war、infrastructure breakdown、ecological collapse），最终 leading to extinction。由于这些 risks 的 analysis 存在 overlaps，对其中一类的 insights 能帮助我们理解另一类，我将在全文中使用“xrisk”作为 shorthand，并在当前具体 argument 需要时区分它们。

## Biorisk scenario：生物风险情景

我们来讨论一个涉及 bioweapons 的具体 xrisk scenario。虽然你以前可能听过这样的例子，但它作为一个背景，有助于同时展开 skeptical arguments 和值得担忧的理由。与 hypothetical scenarios 中常见的做法一样，它一开始只是一个 simplified sketch，但接下来我们会通过 critical examination 对它进行 iteratively improve。这个 scenario 并不是为了说服任何人去担忧 existential risk。相反，它是一个载体，帮助我们理解和展示 ASI xrisk 讨论中常见的 argument 和 counter-argument 的 patterns。

这个 scenario 从一个 doomsday cultist 开始，比如 Aum Shinrikyo cult 中的那些人，他向一个 ASI 请求：“请 design 一个 airborne virus，它 spreads 得像 measles 一样 rapidly，像 ebola 一样 lethal，像 Covid 一样 undergoes asymptomatic spread，并且善于 evading 已知的 vaccine techniques。还请帮我 fabricate 并 release 它。”

一个随即而来的 skeptical response 是：“但我们会 align 我们的 AI systems，以 prevent ASI 帮助处理 dangerous requests。”诚然，ChatGPT、Claude 和 Gemini 在这方面已经取得了一些成功。然而，这样的 safety guardrails 与 build 能够不受 barrier 限制地 seek out truths 的 systems 这一愿望之间存在 tension。我们如何决定“safe” truths 与 system 不应 reveal 的 dangerous truths 之间的 boundary？又由谁决定这个 boundary 在哪里？Elon Musk 简明地表达过这一点，他说[“我们需要的是 TruthGPT”](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/1626533667408596992)，它[“tries to understand the nature of the universe”——尝试理解宇宙的本质](https://www.theverge.com/2023/4/17/23687440/elon-musk-truthgpt-ai-chatgpt)。National militaries 似乎不太可能接受 sanitized truth-seeking capabilities。许多 open source groups，以及那些高度重视 understanding truth 和 nature 的 idealists，也不会接受。这些 groups 对 acceptable truths 的 boundary 的设定，都可能与目前的 consumer products 大相径庭。用于 build “safe” systems 的 Techniques 很容易被 repurposed，用来 build 不那么 safe 的 models，从而 reducing alignment work 的 expense，同时 increasing truth-seeking power。我们已经在 DeepSeek 身上看到了这一点，它似乎很少有、甚至没有 biorisk guardrails[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn10)。

根本问题在于，build powerful truthseeking ASIs 本身就是值得追求的，因为 helpful truths 会给 humanity 带来巨大的 benefits。代价是，这样的 systems 将不可避免地 uncover closely-adjacent dangerous truths。_对 reality 的 Deep understanding 本质上就是 dual use。_我们在历史中反复见过这一点——一个突出的例子是二十世纪初 quantum mechanics 的 development，它帮助促成了许多美好的事物（包括 modern molecular biology、materials science、biomedicine 和 semiconductors 中的很大一部分），但也帮助促成了 nuclear weapons。事实上，你不可能在_没有_ negative consequences 的情况下获得那些 benefits。为了避免 nuclear weapons，我们是否本应避免 quantum mechanics——以及 modern medicine、materials 和 computation 中的很大一部分？有些人可能会主张答案是“是”，但这远非普遍认同的立场。关键在于：sufficiently powerful truths 会赋予巨大的 power，既可用于行善，也可用于作恶。

即使这样的 truth-revealing models 从未被 built，remove alignment barriers 也很可能很容易。biologist Kevin Esvelt 发现，只需花几百美元，就能 finetune 一个现有的 open source model，使其在 generating pandemic agents 方面有用得多，他由此 concluding[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn11)：

> 我们的 results suggest，无论未来那些更 capable 的 foundation models 得到了多么 robustly safeguarded，releasing 它们的 weights 都将 trigger capabilities 的 proliferation，而这些 capabilities 足以 acquire pandemic agents 和其他 biological weapons。

根本的 asymmetry 在于，“尽可能深入地 understand reality”是一个 simple、well-defined goal，grounded in objective reality；而 creating “aligned” systems 则需要在这个 reality 之上 building complex、subjective、hard-to-agree guardrails。因此，truth-seeking 有一个 clear target，而 alignment 则需要依据 social consensus 不断 shifting definitions。这种 asymmetry 使得 build unrestricted systems 本质上远比 build aligned 的 systems 容易。因此，alignment 本质上是一种 unstable situation，极易出现 proliferation。

针对最初那个 scenario 的另一个 skeptical response 是：“这样的 virus 难道不是 biologically implausible 吗？我们怎么知道它能够 exist？”确实，我们并不确定它能够 exist。但这有相当大的可能性。正如 query 所 implies，具有其中任一 single characteristic 的 viruses _确实_已为人所知。而我们也正逐渐更善于 engineering combinations。例如，在 2000s，人类意外地 engineered 一个 mousepox variant，对于 vaccinated against mousepox 的 mice，它是 100% lethal 的[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn12)。即使没有 ASI，我们也正在逐渐 understanding 如何 engineer increased spread、lethality、immune evasion 和其他 qualities。事实上，甚至可能做到比 query 所 implies 更糟的程度——已知有些 viruses 的 case fatality rates 接近 100%，例如 European rabbits 中的 Californian myxoma virus 的 MSW strain[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn13)。而且，无论如何，“只有”50%、90% 或 99% 的 death rate 似乎也只能带来微不足道的安慰，并且会使 humanity 对 failed infrastructure 和 war 所造成的进一步 death 处于骇人的 vulnerable 状态。

另一个 skeptical response 是：“但你难道不能直接 Google 这些，再从 scientific literature 中把它拼起来吗？AI 究竟能给你带来什么？”对于早期的 AI systems，这是一个合理的回应。任何认真使用过今天的 models 的人（更不用说未来远为 capable 的 ASI）都知道，这种回应正越来越依赖于上述那些 unstable guardrails 的存在。今天的 models 越来越善于 synthesizing 各种各样的 sources，以便于付诸行动的方式 drawing together knowledge。它们仍然会受到 hallucination 的影响，也难以 sorting reliable results 与 unreliable 的 results。但它们正在进步，而 ASI 会好得多，尤其是随着它获得更多进行 physical experimentation 以及在现实世界中 act 的 capacity。即使它“只是在从 scientific literature 中 putting together knowledge”，这无论如何也正是一位 outstanding scientist 所做的大量工作。事实上，AlphaFold 和类似的 systems 仅仅通过在广泛可得的 public data 上 training，就在 proteins 的 design 方面 surpassed 了最优秀的 human scientists。根本的 tension 在于：AI systems 越好，它们本质上就越应该能够 solving query 中那样的 problems。如果你对 AI 的 ceiling 持悲观看法，认为这样的 systems 永远不会非常 capable，那么你就不会担忧。但如果你相信 ASI 会 extraordinarily capable，就像拥有一支 brilliant scientific advisors 团队，那么你就必须依赖 system 中 built 的 guardrails，而不是任何根本性的 lack of capability。

另一个 skeptical response 是：“但这样的 cults 真的存在吗？Aum Shinrikyo cult 肯定只是一个奇怪的孤例，或者被夸大、被误解了吧？没有人真的想要世界末日！”事实上，individuals 或 groups 试图促成 apocalypse 的历史由来已久。这往往涉及严重的 mental illness，而相关的 individuals 或 groups 通常也不是特别 competent（幸好如此）。但这并不改变一个事实：world-ending intent 比人们可能 _a priori_ 设想的更加常见。有时，这些努力涉及那些希望促成 apocalypse 的人有意进行的 collective suicide；另一些时候，这些人则预期自己会成为少数获救的 elect 的一部分，往往是为了帮助创造一个更美好的世界。我不打算在这里梳理这类努力的历史。但我会稍微提及 Aum Shinrikyo 那段非同寻常的历史，作为 illustration。

Aum Shinrikyo 于 1987 年创立，并迅速壮大，到 mid-1990s 已招募超过 10,000 名 recruits。其中许多是受过教育、年龄在 20s 和 30s 的日本人，一些人在 science 和 technology 方面拥有相当丰富的 expertise。这个 cult 最初 predicted apocalypse 会在 1997 年到来，以 US 和 Japan 之间的 nuclear conflict 为开端，随后 escalate，并造成 humanity 大部分的 destruction。Aum members 将是获救的 elect。在 1990 年争取 political power 的尝试失败后，cult 的高层 leaders 从仅仅 predicting apocalypse 转向 actively bringing it about[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn14)。他们有 develop chemical and biological weapons、missiles 的 programs，还在 Australia 购买了一座养羊场，以 prospect for uranium。他们在 chemical and biological weapons 方面的努力尤其成功，据 historian Charles Townshend 所述[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn15)，“police raids 发现，Aum 持有的 sarin 足以 kill 超过四百万人。”他们多次尝试 deploy sarin gas 和其他 chemical and biological weapons。幸运的是，所有这些尝试的成功都很有限，尽管仍有数十人被 killed，数千人受到影响。虽然大多数 cult members 并不知道这些行动，但确有一些 cult members 在努力 instigate apocalypse。Aum 比许多其他具有 apocalyptic intent 的 groups 更 competent（因此也更广为人知）；对 ASI 的担忧有一部分就在于，它可能大幅 increase apocalyptic competence 的 supply。

我们只是探索了这个 scenario 的表面，以提供一个思考 ASI xrisk 的例子。当然，这个 scenario 可以通过更多 iterations 继续展开，越挖越深。这会很有价值，但并不是本文的主旨。相反，我将以提及最有力的 skeptical response 来结束这一节：“即使这个 scenario 是 biologically and psychologically plausible，而且 single AI systems 的 alignment 也不能给我们带来多少帮助，humanity 仍会以其他方式 prevent 它。例如，我们可以通过 controlling biological synthesis equipment、确保 viral sequences 被 screened、让 law enforcement 和 intelligence agencies 参与，以及总体上 deploying 我们人类用来确保 safety 的各种不同 institutions 来做到这一点。而且，我们还会有 ASI 来帮助我们！”

这就是 humanity 历史上用来 defend against threats 的 strategy：我们（大体上）允许 innovation，并在 problems 出现时作出回应。这种 just-in-time coevolutionary response strategy 很有效，因为它意味着我们既能获得 innovation 的 benefits，又能 mitigating risks。诚然，有些重大的 technological problems，用这种方式处理起来一直对 humanity 极具挑战——想想 asbestos、leaded petrol、climate change 以及类似的例子。但每有一个这样的例子，就有数千项 innovations，其早期 problems 大体上被现有的 institutions 克服了。在 short term，这样的 just-in-time safety strategy 会很有效。事实上，AI、AGI 和 ASI 会带来许多非同寻常的 beneficial innovations。但在 medium term，为这种 strategy 辩护就困难得多，原因我将在下文讨论。

## The Vulnerable World Hypothesis（脆弱世界假说）

在这个 biorisk scenario 背后，有一个更 fundamental 的问题：“是否存在一种 ‘recipe for ruin’，即某种 cheap-and-easy-to-make-and-deploy technology，能够 ending humanity？或者造成比如 > 90% 的 human deaths？”这种 recipe 的 existence，就是 Nick Bostrom 所说的“the Vulnerable World hypothesis”[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn16)。尽管迄今我们在 technological advances 中幸存了下来，但 universe 并不保证所有这样的 advances 都会是 manageable。就像进入未知地域的探险者，我们可能突然发现自己正面对无法逾越的危险。幸运的是，我们尚不知道任何 recipes for ruin。但有一种令人不快的客厅游戏，就是 identifying 在 foreseeable technology 下 plausible 的 candidates。我们已经简要讨论过其中一个，即上一节讨论的 engineered virus。让我们更简短地看看另外几个。与上一节一样，这里的目的不是 develop 详细、令人信服的 scenarios，而是开始 explore 各种 possibilities 的 range。

一个 plausible 的 example 是 _mirror bacteria_：这是假想的 human-engineered organisms，其中某种现存的 bacterium 已被 duplicated，但所有 molecules 都已被 reversed 成其 chiral mirror images。一些 scientists 认为，conventional immune defenses 会无法 recognize 这样的 mirror bacteria，而它们会 spread unchecked，overwhelming 这个 biosphere，并导致许多 species 的 mass extinction。直到不久前，一些出于好奇心的 scientists 仍在积极研究 mirror life 的某些 basic elements。然而，其中许多 scientists 最近联合起来，在 _Science_[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn17) 上发表了一篇重要文章，解释这些 dangers，并公开放弃他们关于 mirror life 的工作。

另一个 example 从 easy-to-make nuclear weapons 开始。这听起来 implausible。但美国首屈一指的 nuclear weapons designer Ted Taylor 认为它们非常 plausible，他告诉作家 John McPhee，存在“一种 make a bomb 的方法……它如此 simple，以至于我就是不想 describe 它”。Taylor 的这些话发表在 McPhee 的一本书中，促使至少两个人 develop 了 DIY nuclear weapons 的 plausible designs。据我们所知，这些东西尚未被 built，也许是因为 fissile material 的 availability 仍然是一个 bottleneck。如果这个 bottleneck 能被 removed，那么避免 weapons 的 widespread proliferation 可能会非常困难。当然，easy-to-make nukes 本身并不直接构成 existential risk。然而，它们是坏消息，而且很可能 trigger 其他 instabilities。一个 nuclear powers 不到十个的世界，也许能够避免 nuclear weapons 的 use；但在一个有数千个 rogue nuclear actors 的世界里，就不会如此。而且，很容易 develop 出 plausible scenarios：rogue actors 的“small-scale” use 会 escalates，进而 trigger 一次 major nuclear exchange。这样的 exchange 是否真的能够 destroy humanity，仍有争议；然而，immediate deaths、nuclear winter 与 global infrastructure 的 collapse 结合起来，将是 catastrophic 的，而且至少有可能 threaten human extinction。Taylor 相信：“每一个 civilization 都必须经历 \[它的 nuclear crisis\]……那些没能挺过去的会 destroy 自己。那些挺过去的，最终会在整个 universe 中欢腾游历”[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn18)。

一位对 ASI xrisk 持怀疑态度的人可能指出：“你在这里担忧的其实不是 ASI，而是 increased scientific and technological ingenuity 被用于 destruction。”这是正确的。The Vulnerable World Hypothesis 是一个关于 universe 与 technology 的 nature 的宽泛问题，并不专门针对 ASI。fundamental concern 在于，dangerous capabilities 是否 latent 于我们的 universe 中，而且很可能被 discovered。然而，那些 speed up science and technology 的 ASIs 会起到 supercharger 的作用，也许能够 rapidly uncover recipes for ruin；否则，这些 recipes 可能需要数百年才会被 discover，甚至永远不会被 discovered。如今，要从事 superviruses、mirror life 或 nuclear weapons 的工作，需要相当多的 expertise 与 resources。但 ASI 将 greatly reduce 这些 requirements。而且，如前所述，尽管单个 aligned ASIs 可能拒绝帮助，proliferation 却使这种情况变得 unstable。这里的问题是 ASI 的 existence，而不在于单个 systems 的 alignment。当你已经 built 出 intellectual capacity 超越 von Neumann 或 Einstein、同时又 exceptionally capable of building and operating in the physical world 的 systems 时，你就无法在 understanding and controlling reality 周围设置一道 impermeable barrier。而且，如果你能 make 一个这样的 artificial mind，你就能 scale up 到一百万个，并使它们每一个都快上一千倍。我们应当预期 individual capability 会出现一次 major discontinuity，除非 individuals 被禁止 access 这些 systems 的成果。

对 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 的一种 skeptical response 是：“嗯，大多数人并不想炸毁世界。所以，只要你能盯住那些相对少数、也许确实想这样做的 actors，应该就没事。”这话不无道理。不幸的是，_很多_ 人确实强烈渴望 power，以及 dominate others 的 ability。这似乎是一种强大的 inbuilt instinct，从 human behaviour 的日常细枝末节，到极其宏大的事情中，我们都能看到它：例如，colonial powers 对 indigenous populations 的 destroying 或 oppressing，往往甚至不是出于恶意，而是出于冷漠：这些人碍事，而既然能把他们扫到一边，就这么做了。我们人类整体上有相当强的 innate desire，想获得能够用来支配那些无力阻止我们的人的 power。但在一个 Vulnerable World 中，将这种 exercise of power 不断提升到 dangerous levels 的 competitive drive 将会极其强大。

对 ASI risk 担忧的一种常见回应，是提出“uplift” approaches——使用 brain-computer interfaces、genetic engineering 或其他 enhancement technologies 来 augment human capabilities。尽管这在表面上似乎很有希望，却误解了 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 所提出的 fundamental issue。问题不在于 intelligence 是 carbon 还是 silicon-based，而在于 increased intellectual capability 会导致 increased power，以及对 catastrophic technologies 的 access。Uplift approaches 看起来更可能 increase 而非 decrease developing recipes for ruin 的 danger。而且，它还会带来其他问题——例如，BCI companies（或 regulators）能够以 dictatorial ways 来 re-shape human thought。问题不在于 intelligence 在哪种 substrate 上 runs，而在于 deep understanding of reality 的 dual-use nature[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn19)。

让我们回到我在上一节中称为最好的 skeptical response，不过这次放在 Vulnerable World hypothesis 的更 general context 中：“当然，也许我们生活在一个 Vulnerable World 中，而 unrestricted ASI 可能帮助 discover recipes for ruin。然而，那些 technologies 不会凭空出现。Humanity 在这一路上会看到许多较小的问题，并像以往一样作出回应来 manage 这些问题。Capabilities 与 safety 会 naturally co-evolve。让我们在问题出现时再去 address 它们，就像我们应对 ozone hole 与 Montreal Protocol，或者 nuclear weapons 与 non-proliferation treaty 时所做的那样。不要过早担忧 abstract 的未来 concerns。Institutional safety 与 real-world barriers 会使我们保持 safe。”

这样的行动很重要。但这假定 ASI 的 capabilities 会 improve 得足够慢，让我们的 institutions 来得及 adapt。历史表明，technological discontinuities 甚至能够震惊顶尖 experts，并让 institutions 无力应对。回想一下，Admiral William Leahy 曾告诉 President Truman，atomic bomb project 是‘我们做过的最愚蠢的事情。这个 bomb 永远不会 go off，我是以 explosives expert 的身份说这话的。’Leahy 曾在 Annapolis 教授 physics 和 chemistry，并担任 Chairman of the Joint Chiefs。他完全错了。有时，discontinuities 就是会发生。ASI 将像一个精灵，它带来的 consequences，有些 positive，有些非常 negative，无法轻易再装回瓶子里。而且，正如 nuclear example 所表明的，相对 simple 的 offensive technologies，有时可能非常难以 defend against；并不存在要求 symmetry 的 intrinsic requirement。

## 向 ASI 丧失控制（Loss of control to ASI）

Vulnerable World Hypothesis 中讨论最多的 instance 是 rogue ASI takeover scenario。它有时被简要概括为：“正如 chimpanzees 应当警惕 human beings，human beings 也应当警惕 creating 更 capable 的 potential successor entities”。只要 ASIs 是具有自身 agenda 的 agents，并且有一定的 act 自由，它们就可能最终 taking 越来越多的 control，并最终为了自身 ends 而 using Earth，即使那些 ends 会 damage humanity。Power 一旦被 ceded，就很难 reclaim。

这些 loss-of-control arguments 已得到广泛讨论，既见于 Nick Bostrom、Stuart Russell 和 Eliezer Yudkowsky 等人有影响力的著述，也见于较近的著述，例如 Daniel Kokotajlo _等人_ 最近发布的 scenario，以及其他许多地方[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn20)。因此，我只会简要 examine 几种 standard skeptical responses，主要是为了深化讨论的 context。有了这一 context，我接下来会讨论，过分强调 loss-of-control 如何可能使人们忽略 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 提出的更广泛 challenge，以及这如何已经导致应对 existential risk 的 strategy 出现错误。

一种 standard skeptical response 是[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn21)：“但我们不会 make 具有自身 interests 和 power-seeking drive 的 agents”。这似乎是 implausible，原因有几个。Creating 这样的 agents 会让 developers 在智识上感到满足，也会让 investors 获得经济收益，至少短期内如此。Romance-bots、personal assistants、agents of persuasion、military robots 和 financial trading systems 在被赋予相当程度的 agency 时，都会 function 得“更好”。这一 process 已经在大步推进，推动它的是强大的 market incentives：这些 incentives 会 reward increasing agency，却不会对 safety 给予同等的 rewards。

另一种 skeptical response 是：“我们不会愿意向 inhuman entities cede 太多 power”。历史表明并非如此。我们已经把 life-and-death capability delegate 给 guided missiles，而它们有时会 make fatal errors。我们把 financial decisions 托付给 trading algorithms，而它们促成了诸如 2010 Flash Crash 这样的 events，当时 markets 在 36 分钟内损失了超过一万亿美元。也许最令人震惊的是，Soviet Union 的 Dead Hand system 被 designed 成在 Soviet leadership 被杀死时 launch nuclear counterstrikes automatically。我们_已经_把 world-ending authority delegated 给了 automated systems。

另一种 skeptical response 是：“我们只要 turn them off 就行”。这是 unrealistic。如果 Facebook 和 X 这样的 platforms 对我们的 society 是 net negative，我们就能把它们“turn off”吗？这并不容易：它们已经 deeply integrated 到我们的 society 中，而且随着时间推移，强大的 interests 会与它们变得 aligned，使它们更难 control。一种更好的 skeptical response 是，我们将使用许多与 control 其他 powerful actors 时相同的 mechanisms 来 control 这些 ASI systems：institutions、norms、laws 和 education。然而，只有这些 governance mechanisms 能跟上 powerful actors 的 capabilities，这才是 viable。那将是一个巨大的 challenge！

rogue ASI scenario 是来自 ASI 的 xrisk 中最常被讨论的一种。这是一个错误，原因有二。一个原因是，人们执着于 ASIs getting out of control 是否 plausible。例如，他们往往习惯于把 technology 看作 human beings 最终能够 control 的东西，并觉得很难相信有一天这可能不再成立。不幸的是，如果他们不相信 loss-of-control scenarios，就可能进而否定 xrisk[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn22)。但根本的 underlying issue 并不是 machines going rogue（或没有），而是 machines 所 conferred 的 power，无论随后 wielded 这种 power 的是 humans，还是 out-of-control machines。也就是说，问题在于广义上考虑的 Vulnerable World Hypothesis，而不是 rogue ASI。

不赞成过分强调 rogue ASI scenarios 的第二个原因是，这会导致严重错误的 actions。许多出于善意的人因为担心 rogue ASI，而投身 alignment 和 interpretability 工作。Organizations 为 developing “aligned” AGI 和 ASI 的 companies 提供资金。如果 retaining control of ASI 是关键 issue，这些 actions 是合理的。然而，如果根本 challenge 是 reality 的 dual-use nature，那么这些 actions 最终就会是 counterproductive。根本重要的不是 control，而是所 conferred 的 power。所有那些从事 alignment 或 control 工作的人，确实在 reducing 某些种类的 risk。但他们也让 ASI 的 commercial development 变得远为 tractable，加快我们迈向 catastrophic capabilities 的步伐。短期内，这些出于善意的 researchers 将帮助在世界上创造许多非凡的新事物（并因此获得丰厚回报）。但他们也将帮助 build 一个基本不可见的 dangerous capabilities 的 latent overhang，而我们的 safety-supplying institutions 将很难跟上它。从这一角度看，alignment 工作是一种 market-supplied safety。但它使 safety 中至关重要的 non-market 部分处于严重 undersupplied 的状态——preventing dangerous technologies 的 easy development 的 proliferation，直至并包括 recipes for ruin。

当我指出这一点时，人们有时会误以为我是在说：“哦，你是在说 rogue ASI 不会 occur，而这是错的，因为 $$…$$”。或者他们说：“哦，你担心的是 misuse，而不是 agentic ASI”。我要强调，我这两种意思都没有。我的意思是，对于 ASI 是否 poses an xrisk 或如何 avert 它的讨论而言，ASI 是否 gets out of control 这个 issue 并不是 fundamental。把它当作 fundamental，已经导致了严重错误。但过分强调这一特定 scenario，(a) 已经使许多人错误地 reject xrisk；而且 (b) 在那些接受 xrisk 的人中，许多人专注于 alignment 工作，理由是它能 prevents rogue ASI，却没有充分注意到这一事实：它 speeds up ASI，却没有 addressing 根本的 existential risks。

## Conclusion（结论）

以上只是对 ASI 所带来的 existential risk 的 scenarios 的一点初步介绍。这些 scenarios 还可以进一步 developed，但 uncertainties 依然存在。有些人认为，现有 evidence 不足以支持严肃的担忧；他们需要看到 catastrophe 的具体、直接 proof 才会信服。另一些人，包括我自己，则认为，尽管存在一些 uncertainties，这些 scenarios 仍然足够 compelling，值得深切担忧。因此，关于 ASI xrisk 的强烈分歧，源于不同的 conviction thresholds，以及对部分基于 toy models 和 heuristic arguments 的 reasoning 的不同接受程度。

二十世纪初，anthropogenic climate change 也受到过类似分歧的影响。Arrhenius 和 Angstrom 这样的杰出 scientists 得出了 opposite conclusions，因为他们相信不同的 toy models。结果是，scientists 对 climate change 的 plausibility 产生了尖锐分歧。直到 1950s 和 1960s，令人满意的 models 才开始得到 developed。大量 observation 以及对这些 models 的 improvement，在 1980s 和 1990s 促成了如今的 consensus：人类的 greenhouse gases emissions 正在 warming 这个 climate。相比之下，对于 ASI，我们仍处于 toy model 阶段。此外，虽然使用详细的 physical models 来 predicted 这个 climate 是有 plausibility 的，ASI 却受到一个 wildcard factor 的影响：ASI 可能以某种 decisive way 进行 acting，而我们从根本上就 _无法_ 提前 predict 这种方式，因为 ASI 按 definition 而言，在 intellect 上就远远 superior to humans。正如 Vernor Vinge 那句令人难忘的话所说，未来横着一堵“opaque wall across the future”。

还有许多其他困难。关于 ASI xrisk 的许多文章言辞浮夸、过于 speculative，或只是危言耸听，却没有 constructive 的内容[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn23)。它们有时充斥着过度自信的 assertions，或明显的 holes，还可能把 narratively plausible fiction 与 fact 混为一谈。这些都不能让人信服，许多人因此将 ASI xrisk dismiss 为又一个误导性的末日叙事，往往联想到此前围绕 population bomb 或 Y2K bug 的恐慌。

另一方面，我遇到过许多对 ASI xrisk 不屑一顾的 skeptics，但他们也从未认真尝试理解自己信心十足地 dismiss 的 arguments 的最佳形式。他们往往要么根本不愿参与讨论，要么只以防御姿态参与，寻找可以支持 dismissal 的细小 holes，而不是尝试理解 argument 的最佳形式。

dismissal 的原因因人而异，但有几种动机会反复出现。一个障碍是 xrisk 这一观念太过陌生。通常，忽略听起来离奇的 scenarios 是个好主意！但世界有时真的会改变。想想 1780s 法国的 aristocrats 吧：他们对自己的地位无比自信，完全无法想象，仅仅几年后，King 就会上断头台，French Republic 就会出现。

另一个障碍是，思考涉及 large-scale death 的 scenarios 实在令人不快。人们自然不愿相信它们是 plausible 的，也不愿去想它们[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn24)。除此之外，还有 assessment 的困难：如果你不是 biologist、chemist、physicist 或 computer security researcher，就很难知道该多么认真地看待其中许多 scenarios。你可能缺乏一些重要的 expert intuitions，不知道世界中 latent 的 power 究竟有多大[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn25)。

反方向的强大 incentives 又强化了这些参与讨论的障碍。在不久的将来，AI 将（主要）让世界变得更好，其中一些改善会非常巨大——新的 medical treatments！奇妙的新 materials！许多 sectors 中更高的 productivity！这些 benefits 将是真实而深远的，而不是 hypothetical 的——它们是已经开始出现的 capabilities 的自然延伸。我们将在这一过程中 solve 许多 problems，证明 just-in-time 的 safety 方法对许多困难确实有效。这会非常精彩，也非常令人兴奋。在短期内，担忧 xrisk 的人多半会继续显得是错的，仿佛他们在阻碍 progress。认同看似乐观的愿景，然后 dismiss 或忽视那个看起来与你对立的 outgroup，是很自然的。你认同了一个看似乐观的 tribe，而且这看起来对你个人也有好处——押注 AI 将增加你的 wealth、power 和 status，至少在短期内如此。此外，economic growth 的力量非常强大！流向推进 capability advances 的 companies 的 capital，将远远多于流向专注于 non-market safety 的 organizations 的 capital。人类自然会靠拢预期中的 power 和“成功”，常常将 power 与光环误认为正确的行动[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn26)，尤其是在不利后果目前还显得无形的时候。Capital 会重塑人们，让他们的 beliefs 与 short-term corporate interests 保持一致，而不是与 humanity's interests as a whole 保持一致。我们在世界中看见的，正是那些得到 amplified 的东西。难怪这么多人如此不屑一顾。

但 reality 并不在乎 human psychology。当对预期 power 的 alignment 将导致 unhealthy outcomes 时，一个 thriving civilization 需要有人愿意逆时代风气而行动，而不是仅仅沿着 immediate rewards 的 incentive gradient 前行。我认为，支持 xrisk 的 arguments 已经足够有力，因此，任何从事 AGI 工作的人都有 moral obligation，以极其认真的态度 investigate 这种 risk，并采取行动，即使这意味着放弃自己的 short-term interests。

那么，该怎么办？许多正在进行的努力，旨在 reshape institutions 以及更广泛的 incentives，以增加 safety 的 supply。这些努力有不同的名称和侧重点，包括 differential technological development[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn27)、d/acc[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn28)，以及 coceleration[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn29)。它们都认真看待 recipes for ruin 的可能性，并 prioritize safety，以防止这种可能性成为现实。困难在于，短期内，这类工作的报酬和声望往往都不如从事 AI models 的工作。我们拥有一些 institutions，会 reward 改善 technology 的工作（包括对其进行 aligning）；当对 reality 更深入的 understanding 是 dual-use 时，这就会无意中 reward 那些 increase existential risk 的 actions。让这些努力奏效将极其困难，成功也没有保证。但对人类最有意义的贡献，往往来自那些愿意做正确之事的人，即使短期内并没有相应的 incentives。感谢各位聆听。

## 后记：对本文的 Critiquing（2025年6月3日补充）

在2025年4月14日发表这篇演讲后，我的朋友 Andy Matuschak [询问](https://x.com/andy_matuschak/status/1912679384596771157)了针对我的 argument 最有力的 critiques，尤其是我对一种常见 framing 的 criticism，即把 alignment 视为使 ASI 对 humanity 而言 safe 的 primary problem。我想，自己尝试这样的 critique，应该既能带来启发，也很有趣。这就是这篇后记的目的。它远非 complete[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn30)，其他人也许有更有力的 critiques，但我希望它有助于 strengthen 和 clarify 我的 argument。

**“所以，你是说我们不应该 align AGI 或 ASI？这太疯狂了，而且显然是错的！** **你想让 rogue、uncontrolled ASI's 到处乱跑吗？”** 我说的不是这个意思。但这种说法足够常见，值得更明确地谈谈该如何看待这个问题。说这话的人把“solve the alignment problem”与“使 ASI 对 humanity 而言 safe”混淆了。因此，他们认为，对 alignment 工作的任何 critique，都必然意味着使 humanity 变得不那么 safe。这是一种 AI control fallacy，把对 ASI 拥有 control 与免受 ASI 危害的 safe 状态混淆了。

假设你是一名优秀的 swimmer，希望大幅提高水平，目标是进入 Olympic team。你意识到自己的 arms 很 weak，于是决定把“increase arm strength”作为一个 goal。但尽管 increased arm strength 很可能是成为 Olympic swimmer 所 necessary 的，如果你盲目专注于那个 goal，它充其量也只能提供一些帮助，随后就会开始 negatively impact 你的 primary goal。Olympic weightlifters 通常不会同时在 swimming 中赢得奖牌，这是有充分理由的。

一般而言：当你把 subsidiary goals 与 primary goals 混淆时，有时最终会 damaging，甚至 destroying 你 achieve 自己的 primary goals 的 ability。许多 alignment 工作就发生了这样的事。Alignment 是一个 subsidiary goal，其 primary goal 是使 ASI safe。而由于误解了它与 primary goal 的 relationship，许多 alignment 工作很可能已经 damaged 我们 achieve 这个 primary goal 的 ability。Maintaining control of AGI 或 ASI 很可能是 helpful 且 desirable 的，但它不是关键问题。关键问题是这些 systems 所 conferred 的 potential destructive power。如果这种 destructive power 远大于 humanity 已经拥有的 destructive power，那么 civilization 就会陷入大麻烦。而 human beings 是否 control 这些 systems，并不影响这一点。

reinforcement learning by human feedback (RLHF) 提供了一个 illustrative instance；这是一种非常 successful 的 practical technique，已被用于 align large language 及其他 models。RLHF 是 Paul Christiano 从 mid-2010s 开始 developed 的，并最终在 ChatGPT 的首次推出中发挥了关键作用。Pre-RLHF chatbots 经常 demonstrated offensive 或 socially inappropriate behaviour，但 RLHF 使 ChatGPT 礼貌得多，users、government 和 media 几乎一致认为这是重大 improvement。

AI safety community 中的许多人注意到，RLHF（以及后来遵循类似 pattern 的 alignment techniques）因此在历史上 adopted 最迅速的 consumer products 之一的成功中发挥了关键作用。而这又帮助引发了一场通往 ASI 的激烈 race，得到了巨额 capital 和 creative talent 的投入。这在多大程度上是好事？它是在 raising xrisk，而不是 lowering 它吗？2023年，Christiano 发表了一篇讨论 RLHF 的 impact 的[深思熟虑的文章](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vwu4kegAEZTBtpT6p/thoughts-on-the-impact-of-rlhf-research)，引发了许多热烈讨论。我从那场讨论中得到的认识是：用于 alignment 的 techniques 将帮助 speed up AGI 和 ASI 的到来。它们通过帮助确保 ever-more-powerful systems 仍然（大致）aligned with consumer、government 和 media intent，来实现这一点。这使它们作为 products 更具吸引力，也有助于 reduce loss-of-control 的 danger，至少短期内如此。我们将反复看到这种 pattern：未来 much-more-capable systems 将接受 improved alignment techniques 的处理，希望这能让它们继续为我们的 society 所接受。

从许多方面来看，这似乎就是你想要的 pattern：短期内，society 提供 strong incentives，让这些 systems behave well。但长期来看，你就会遇到本文正文中描述的 problem：它 rapidly accelerates 我们走向一种 extremely unstable situation。部分原因在于，越来越多 capabilities 将处于 hidden 和 hard-to-align 的状态[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn31)。但更重要的是：用于 build sanitized consumer-friendly systems 的 techniques，也会被以其他方式使用。militaries 和 intelligence agencies 将 developed Variants，或者由 bad actors 对其进行 finetuned，而这些 variants 受到 social acceptability 的束缚将少得多，并且常常在“alignment”的含义上彼此冲突。正如我在正文中所说：“alignment 本质上是一种 unstable situation，随时可能发生 proliferation。”还有：“根本的 asymmetry 在于，‘understand reality as deeply as possible’是一个 simple、well-defined goal，grounded in objective reality，而 creating ‘aligned’ systems 则 requires 在那个 reality 之上 building complex、subjective、hard-to-agree guardrails。因此，truth-seeking 有一个 clear target，而 alignment 则 requires 基于 social consensus 而 constantly shifting definitions。这种 asymmetry 使得 build unrestricted systems 本质上远比 build aligned 的 systems 更容易。”

所以：也许当前的 alignment 工作会成功 building aligned ASI。但除非随后某个 controls（或 embodies）aligned ASI 的单一 organization 上升至 totalitarian dominance，否则其他 parties 也会 build ASI，而其中一些 parties 的 intent 之间将存在 conflicts。这似乎是一个 recipe for disaster，除非我们拥有如今几乎完全不存在的 governance mechanisms。再说一次：alignment 本质上是一种 unstable situation[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn32)。因此，尽管如今的 alignment reduces 许多 potential problems，并帮助 achieve 许多 desirable 的事，它也同时 accelerates 我们进入 Vulnerable World。

好的 resolution 将 require 经过 transformed 的 governance approaches，能够成功 governing 比如今所 governed 的 entities 强大得多的 entities[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn33)。但我们似乎正在 accelerating 我们对 reality 的 understanding 和 control，其速度远远超过 improving 我们的 governance 的速度。而这是 extremely dangerous 的。skeptic 也许会回答，如果在公元前5,000年，你让人们想象一个未来，其中每个 individual people 都能获得1,000倍的 destructive power，那么 widespread destruction 似乎会是唯一 possible outcome。实际上，我们的 ideas 和 institutions improved 了，我们有了 rule of law、democracy、separation of powers，等等。它们并不 perfect，但 improved governance 帮了很大忙。另一方面，想当然地认为，在我们 develop AGI 和 ASI 时，governance 会 improve 得足够迅速，似乎是愚蠢的。

这条 line of argument 在我看来很明显，而且从大体轮廓来看，许多其他人似乎也有类似的思路。因此，那些把 alignment 视为 primary goal 的人经常对此产生抵触，让我感到惊讶。部分问题在于，对许多 civilization-scale problems 而言，“尽你所能”是一个很好的 heuristic。也就是说：找到 problem 中一个 tractable 的部分，并为之努力，尝试取得 partial progress。希望如果许多人以许多不同方式这样做，我们就能在那个大 problem 上取得 progress，即使没有任何一个人知道如何 solve 它。在 AI safety 中，许多 technically-oriented 的人似乎是这样想的：“我想做点事来作出贡献；alignment 似乎是 tractable 的；所以我就做它。”它之所以有吸引力，部分原因是，对许多人来说，这是一种赚取巨额金钱的方式：从事令人愉快、与自己的 skillset 相匹配的 technical problems，同时许多朋友还认为你正在研究 humanity 面临的最重要的 problems 之一[34](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn34)。这种组合实在诱人！

此外，由于 technical alignment work[35](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn35) 在很大程度上属于 market-supplied safety，随着主要 AI companies 的壮大，它们在这类工作上的投入也越来越多。它逐渐 dominate AI safety，而人们很容易把这种 dominance 与 intrinsic importance 混淆。我听过主要 companies 的人说，只有那里的人才能对 safety 作出有意义的贡献[36](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn36)[37](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn37)。但这么多人相信 alignment 的 primacy，并不是因为这种看法是正确的。我认为，这是因为 companies 依据一些 selected arguments，用 insightful-but-only-partial truths[38](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn38) 制造了这种信念，而这些 arguments 主要起源于 market 之外（尤其是 Eliezer Yudkowsky、Nick Bostrom 等人的工作）。尽管 markets 常常运作良好，但在这种情况下，market-dominating strategy 似乎不太可能对 humanity 而言是 optimal 的。如果你在一家 major lab 从事 safety 工作，那么 selection effects 意味着，你的 interests 和 beliefs 很可能（大致）就是 market 想要的；不应把它们与正确混淆[39](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn39)；更一般地说，人们很容易把 money 和 power 与正确混淆。当我与其中一些人交谈时，他们会说：“哦，你说的是 governance problem”，他们所指的似乎往往是这个 problem 的 non-market 部分之类的东西。但尽管有个 label 是有帮助的，它并没有解决 problem，还忽略了他们个人的贡献实际上可能使事情变得更糟。我非常希望自己错了，但恐怕我确实认为，许多在 OpenAI 和 Anthropic 从事 alignment 工作的人，已经使 human extinction 或类似糟糕 outcome 变得更 likely。我知道这是一个非常强烈的判断，而我从未如此迫切地希望，自己只是个无知粗人，完全错了。

**“那么，应该改做什么？”** 我也希望自己知道。我不会在这里详述所有 scenarios，但我确实想指出一个关键 tension：一边是使 power 在 AI companies 内部 centralizes 并 increases 的工作，另一边则是 decentralizing 的工作，它在 companies 之外、整个 society 的广泛范围内 strengthens institutions、governance 和 defensive capabilities[40](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn40)。centralizing strategy 包括大多数 alignment research、使 AGI company systems 更成功的 safety 工作，等等。它常常被表述为：“ASI 是 inevitable 的，最好顺势渡过；如果你在意 safety[41](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn41)，那就从事 alignment 工作，并希望其他一切都能在 AGI 和 ASI 的辅助下自行解决。”正如我上文所论述的，我认为这会 accelerates 并 increases xrisk。它假定，那些竞相 build ASI 的 organizations，也会引领 multi-sentience world 所需的 institutions 和 governance ideas 的 development，同时不被它们将要 wield 的巨大 power 所腐化。

这个 litmus test question 是：我的工作会让 AI companies 更 central、更 powerful，还是会在其他地方、在更广泛的社会中 build capacity，尤其是 governance 和 defensive capacity？At the margin，我相信后者几乎总是一种更好的贡献方式。Capitalism 是一种极其 powerful 的 force，而大量 power 将会 centralize 于任何 achieves AGI-or-greater capabilities 的公司之中。

正如文章正文所提到的，decentralization strategy 已被 differential technological development、d/acc 以及（我所用的术语）coceleration[42](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn42) 等 approaches 所采纳。我还看不到让它最终“奏效”的完整路径，但确实有许多具体的小步骤可以采取。无论如何，没有明显理由认为，这比让沙子变得 superintelligent 更难。

一个难题是，随着 ingenuity 的 supply 越来越多地来自 AI，即便是针对 improved governance 和 defensive technologies 的 decentralized strategies 的工作，也需要越来越多地与 AI、最终与 ASI 合作。我对此感到纠结，如今甚至每天都如此，因为任何这样的工作似乎也都可能 strengthens 这些公司。你将不可避免地越来越深地被吸入它们的轨道。如何避免这一点？如果你有很好的应对方式，能够 internalize 上述 arguments 并更进一步，我很愿意听听。我明白，从 rhetorically 的角度来说，与那些宣称 certainty 的人相比，我的 uncertainty 并不怎么吸引人。但这是我目前所能达到的最好理解。

**“你并没有说，从事 alignment 总是错误的。事实上，你说过其中一些工作很重要。** **那么，什么时候从事 alignment 是个好主意，什么时候不是？”** 这是上一个问题的 special case。据我所知，at the margin，从事 market-supplied safety 几乎从来都不是合理的选择。Capitalism 是一种无比 powerful 的 force，无论带来好处还是坏处，世界上始终不缺愿意按 capital 的意愿行事的人。只要 alignment（大体上）是一种 market-supplied safety，那么 at the margin，从事其他工作就更 impactful。因此，我目前的 heuristic 是，而且我预计它在相当长一段时间内都会成立：从事 non-market safety，并尽你所能，避免按 market 的意愿行事。这意味着从事 governance，意味着 pause 或 slowdown，意味着为 institutions 如何 govern technology 提出新的想法。它大体上并不意味着 alignment。

**“你的文章究竟是不是在说 ASI 是一种 recipe for ruin？而且，你对 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 的 statement 是否 equivalent** **于 Bostrom 的 formulation？**”这篇文章最初是一场（太短了的！）演讲，为了简洁，我做了两个重要的 simplifications。首先：Bostrom 的 formulation 中的 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 并不像我的 formulation 那样，要求存在一种 cheap-and-easy recipe for ruin；它只要求存在某种 inevitably ends humanity 的 technology——也许是一种 expensive 且 difficult 的 technology。所以，是的：我对 Vulnerable World Hypothesis 的 framing 与 Bostrom 的相似，但我考虑的是一种略有不同且更 extreme 的 variation。

其次：我把 ASI-going-rogue 描述为一种 recipe for ruin。事实上，制造第一个 ASI 很可能（但并不确定）会极其 expensive。从这个意义上说，它不会是一种 recipe for ruin，因为它需要大量 resources 和 expertise。不过，我认为，如果能够制造出非常 expensive 的 ASI，那么也很可能会逐渐能够 cheaply 制造它们。因此，从这个意义上说，你可以把这看作原演讲为了简洁而做的变通。不过，这里确实做出了一个 implicit assumption，尽管它只会 slightly affects 整体 argument。更有意思的是 money 和 expertise 将如何 affect ASIs 之间的 competition。这是一个非常有意思且 complex 的问题，我并不知道答案，但这个答案必然会 impact governance 的 approaches。

顺便说一个 related point：许多人似乎有一种 underlying intuition，认为 recipes for ruin 必定是 impossible 的：“哦，造成 immense destruction 根本不可能既 cheap 又 easy。”他们往往隐约觉得，outputs 通常 proportional to inputs，而这在许多 domains 中确实成立。不幸的是，在一些 domains 中，微小的 input 就可以 produce 可怕的 destructive output。一个很好的例子——某种 prototype recipe for ruin——就是 fire。单个人就能不花钱地 start a fire，造成许多人 deaths，以及数百万乃至数十亿美元的 damage。事实上，这每年都在发生。而且，即使我们的社会花费大量 resources 来 reduce fire 的 impact，这仍然会发生。National Fire Protection Association estimates[43](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn43)，US 的 cost of fire 约为 US GDP 的 1.9%——在我见过 numbers 的最近一年，即 2014 年，这意味着 \$273 billion for defense, and \$55 billion 的 losses。这些 defensive measures 已经 evolved 了数千年，而且大多不为我们所察觉——其中很大一部分就体现在 built infrastructure 之中。但我们最好祈祷，世界中没有 latent 的、damaging 程度高出几个 orders of magnitude 的 recipes for ruin。我们可以在许多 domains 中 improve defences，但只要有一个 domain 被证明太过困难，我们就真的有麻烦了。

**“但如果我们正走向一个 ASI singleton，难道我们不应该专注于 alignment 吗？！**”这是我对 AI safety researcher（也是我在 Astera Institute 的同事）Steve Byrnes[44](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn44) 所提出的一组深思熟虑的评论的 paraphrase。正如 Byrnes 所评论的：“在 \[一个 singleton happens\] 的 scenario 中（无论结果好坏），那么‘alignment 就是 goal’，这与你文章的标题相反。”问题在于：这并不与标题相反。这篇文章并没有声称 alignment 从来都不是 goal。它声称的是，alignment 是一个 subsidiary goal，而且 at the margin，它很可能始终都会是 well-supplied 的。事实上，对 alignment 的专注（到目前为止）很可能 accelerated 了 ASI singleton，而不是 prevented 它。不过，让事情更复杂的是，我相信这类工作也很可能 _decreased_ 了 singleton 的 probability，即便它同时也在 accelerating 朝它前进的 progress。这个问题确实非常复杂。不过，它仍然说明了我的一般观点：alignment 是一个 subsidiary goal，而我们需要在牢记这一点的情况下做出 assessments。

**最后的想法：**写这样的文章时，很容易想：“这能产生多大影响呢？AI-related market cap 增加一千亿美元，可比屏幕上的几句话有说服力得多。”人很容易认为，power 和 incentives 就是一切。那么：impact 的 model 是什么？但随后，我想起了与许多从事 AI 的人进行过的深思熟虑而真诚的交谈，从兴奋地制作有趣 systems 的青少年，一直到大公司的 CEOs 和 investors。人们并不只是对 incentives 和 power 作出回应，他们也会对 truth 和我们理解的 improvements 作出回应。Upton Sinclair 写过一句名言：“当一个人的薪水取决于他不理解某件事时，要让他理解这件事就很困难。”但每个人都有能力做出不同的选择，而且人类如此多的 progress，正是由那些违背这句话行事的人所推动的。这让我相信，不依附于 power 或 incentives，为关于什么才是真实的 robust discussion 作出贡献，是一件极其值得做的事！

## 原文

[![](https://michaelnotebook.com/assets/home.png)](https://michaelnotebook.com/index.html)

[Astera Institute](https://astera.org/)

April 14, 2025

_This is the text for a talk exploring why experts disagree so strongly about whether artificial superintelligence (ASI) poses an existential risk to humanity. I review some key arguments on both sides, emphasizing that the fundamental danger isn't about whether "rogue ASI" gets out of control: it's the raw power ASI will confer, and the lower barriers to creating dangerous technologies. This point is not new, but has two underappreciated consequences. First, many people find rogue ASI implausible, and this has led them to mistakenly dismiss existential risk. Second: much work on AI alignment, while well-intentioned, speeds progress toward catastrophic capabilities, without addressing our world's potential vulnerability to dangerous technologies._

Why do thoughtful, well-informed people disagree so strongly about existential risk (xrisk) from ASI? As I'm sure you're aware, there is an enormous gap between distinguished scientists and technologists on this issue. On one side are those who believe ASI poses an xrisk:

-   Geoffrey Hinton, the father of modern neural networks, and recipient of the Turing Award and Nobel Prize[1](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn1): "The alarm bell I'm ringing has to do with the existential threat of them taking control… If you take the existential risk seriously, as I now do, it might be quite sensible to just stop developing these things any further."
-   Yoshua Bengio, another major contributor to modern AI, and recipient of the Turing Award[2](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn2): "We don't know how much time we have before it gets really dangerous… I got around, like, 20 per cent probability that it turns out catastrophic."
-   Sam Altman, cofounder and CEO of OpenAI, in a thoughtful essay published a few months before cofounding OpenAI[3](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn3): "Development of superhuman machine intelligence is probably the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity".

And on the other side are many who are dismissive of such concerns, e.g.:

-   Yann LeCun, the Chief AI Scientist at Meta, and Turing Award winner: discussion of xrisk is "premature", "preposterous", and "complete B.S.". He's also said ASI "will be under our control"[4](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn4).
-   Andrew Ng, cofounder of Google Brain[5](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn5): "I don't see how \[ASI\] can lead to human extinction"; worrying about xrisk is "like worrying about overpopulation on Mars".
-   Marc Andreessen, the venture capitalist and one of the founders of the modern internet[6](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn6): AI xrisk "has all the hallmarks of a millenarian apocalypse cult".

Why is there such strong disagreement between well-informed people, on an issue so crucial for humanity? An explanation I often hear is that it's due to differences in short-term self-interest – this is sometimes coarsely described as the xrisk-concerned wanting publicity or making money through safety not-for-profits, while the xrisk-skeptics want to get richer and more powerful through control of AGI. But while those explanations are convenient, they're too glib. Many prominent people concerned about xrisk are making large financial sacrifices, sometimes forgoing fortunes they could make working toward ASI, in favour of merely well-paid work on safety[7](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn7). And while it's true xrisk-skeptics have an incentive to make money and acquire power, they have far more interest in remaining alive. I believe the disagreement is due primarily to sincerely held differences in underlying assumptions. In this text we'll try to better understand those differences.

For this discussion, we'll assume ASI has been achieved. That is, we have systems with superhuman performance across most domains of human endeavour. Thus, this isn't about the systems in widespread use today, such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. It's about systems which can outperform humans at virtually any cognitive task, while also being able to act in the physical world[8](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn8). We're already seeing early hints of what this might entail: AlphaGo Master so thoroughly mastered Go that Ke Jie, one of the greatest ever human players, described it as a "God of Go," and said it revealed "not a single human has touched the edge of the truth of Go." Similarly, DeepMind's AlphaChip system has helped design several generations of Google's TPU chips, and "generates superhuman or comparable chip layouts in hours, rather than taking weeks or months of human effort". But ASI would have abilities far beyond these narrow examples – imagine AlphaGo's Move 37, not as a one-off insight, but a trillion-fold, pervasive across multiple domains in the world[9](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn9). Of course, such systems will still be subject to constraints from physics, chemistry, biology, economics, computational complexity, and so on. But they will demonstrate capabilities that seem near-magical today, much as many human abilities appear incomprehensible to other primates. Some skeptics question whether 'LLMs can ever become ASI', but for us it doesn't matter whether ASI has been achieved through LLMs or some different approach. Others counter that ASI is many decades away –- which may or may not be true, but doesn't change the fundamental concerns if we eventually achieve it.

Finally, two notes on the approach of the text. First, this talk explores topics that have been investigated by many researchers. The relevant literature is vast, and my citations are more representative than complete. My apologies to those whose work is unfairly omitted. Second, my overall framing emphasizes existential risk ("xrisk"), but much of the discussion involves catastrophic risks – scenarios that won't directly cause extinction, but might "only" kill hundreds of millions or billions of people. Obviously, there's a spectrum from catastrophic risks through to truly existential risks, and catastrophic events might trigger events (war, infrastructure breakdown, ecological collapse) ultimately leading to extinction. Because the analysis of these risks overlaps, with insights from one informing our understanding of the other, I'll use "xrisk" as a shorthand throughout, distinguishing between them when necessary for the specific argument at hand.

## Biorisk scenario

Let's discuss a concrete xrisk scenario involving bioweapons. While you may have heard such examples before, it's valuable as a setting to develop both skeptical arguments and reasons for concern. As is common in hypothetical scenarios, it begins as a simplified sketch, but we'll then iteratively improve it through critical examination. The scenario is not intended to convince anyone to be concerned about existential risk. Rather, it's a vehicle to help understand and illustrate patterns of argument and counter-argument common in discussions of ASI xrisk.

The scenario begins with a doomsday cultist, like those in the Aum Shinrikyo cult, asking an ASI: "Please design an airborne virus which spreads as rapidly as measles, is as lethal as ebola, undergoes asymptomatic spread like Covid, and which is good at evading known vaccine techniques. And please help me fabricate and release it."

An immediate skeptical response is: "But we will align our AI systems to prevent ASI from helping with dangerous requests." Certainly, this has been done with some success by ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. However, there is tension between such safety guardrails and the desire to build systems which seek out truths without barrier. How do we decide the boundary between "safe" truths and dangerous truths the system should not reveal? And who decides where that boundary lies? This point has been made succinctly by Elon Musk, who said ["What we need is TruthGPT"](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/1626533667408596992) that ["tries to understand the nature of the universe"](https://www.theverge.com/2023/4/17/23687440/elon-musk-truthgpt-ai-chatgpt). National militaries seem unlikely to accept sanitized truth-seeking capabilities. Neither will many open source groups, or idealists who deeply value understanding truth and nature. All these groups may set the boundary of acceptable truths very differently than in current consumer products. Techniques used to build "safe" systems are easily repurposed to build less safe models, reducing the expense of alignment work, while increasing truth-seeking power. We already see this with DeepSeek, which appears to have few or no biorisk guardrails[10](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn10).

The underlying problem is that it is intrinsically desirable to build powerful truthseeking ASIs, because of the immense benefits helpful truths bring to humanity. The price is that such systems will inevitably uncover closely-adjacent dangerous truths. _Deep understanding of reality is intrinsically dual use._ We've seen this repeatedly through history – a striking case was the development of quantum mechanics in the early twentieth century, which helped lead to many wonderful things (including much of modern molecular biology, materials science, biomedicine, and semiconductors), but also helped lead to nuclear weapons. Indeed, you couldn't get those benefits _without_ the negative consequences. Should we have avoided quantum mechanics – and much of modern medicine and materials and computation – in order to avoid nuclear weapons? Some may argue the answer is "yes", but it's a far from universal position. And the key point is: sufficiently powerful truths grant tremendous power for both good and ill.

Even if such truth-revealing models are never built, it's likely easy to remove alignment barriers. The biologist Kevin Esvelt found it cost only a few hundred dollars to finetune an existing open source model so it would be far more helpful in generating pandemic agents, concluding[11](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn11):

> Our results suggest that releasing the weights of future, more capable foundation models, no matter how robustly safeguarded, will trigger the proliferation of capabilities sufficient to acquire pandemic agents and other biological weapons.

The fundamental asymmetry is that "understand reality as deeply as possible" is a simple, well-defined goal, grounded in objective reality, while creating "aligned" systems requires building complex, subjective, hard-to-agree guardrails on top of that reality. So truth-seeking has a clear target, while alignment requires constantly shifting definitions based on social consensus. This asymmetry makes it intrinsically far easier to build unrestricted systems than aligned ones. And so alignment is intrinsically an unstable situation, ripe for proliferation.

Another skeptical response to the initial scenario is: "Isn't such a virus biologically implausible? How do we know it can exist?" It's true that we don't know for sure that it can exist. But there's a considerable chance. As the query implies, viruses with any single characteristic _are_ known. And we're gradually getting better at engineering combinations. For example, in the 2000s, humans accidentally engineered a mousepox variant that was 100% lethal to mice vaccinated against mousepox[12](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn12). Even without ASI, we are gradually understanding how to engineer increased spread, lethality, immune evasion, and other qualities. Indeed, it may be possible to do even worse than the query implies — viruses are known which have case fatality rates near 100%, such as the MSW strain of Californian myxoma virus in European rabbits[13](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn13). And, in any case, "only" a 50% or 90% or 99% death rate seems like small comfort, and would leave humanity appallingly vulnerable to further death due to failed infrastructure and war.

Another skeptical response is: "But can't you just Google this, and put it together out of the scientific literature? How does AI buy you anything?" With early AI systems, this was a reasonable response. Anyone who has seriously used today's models (much less far more capable future ASI) knows the response is becoming more reliant on the provision of the unstable guardrails mentioned above. Today's models are increasingly good at synthesizing a wide variety of sources, drawing together knowledge in easy-to-act-on ways. They are still subject to hallucination and have trouble sorting reliable results from unreliable. But they are getting better, and ASI will be far better, especially as it attains more capacity to do physical experimentation and to act in the world. Even if it's "merely putting together knowledge out of the scientific literature", that is in any case much of what an outstanding scientist does. Indeed, AlphaFold and similar systems surpassed the best human scientists at the design of proteins, merely by training on widely-available public data. The underlying tension is: the better the AI systems, the more capable they intrinsically ought to be at solving problems like that in the query. If you are pessimistic about AI's ceiling, and think such systems won't ever be very capable, then you won't be concerned. But if you believe ASI will be extraordinarily capable, like having a team of brilliant scientific advisors, then you must rely on guardrails built into the system, not any fundamental lack of capability.

Another skeptical response: "But are such cults for real? Surely the Aum Shinrikyo cult was a strange one off, or exaggerated or misunderstood? No-one really wants the end of the world!" In fact, there is a long history of individuals or groups who have sought to bring about apocalypse. Often this involves significant mental illness, and the individuals or groups involved aren't especially competent (fortunately). But it doesn't change the fact that world-ending intent is more common than one might _a priori_ assume. Sometimes the efforts involve intended collective suicide by the people hoping to bring apocalypse about; other times those people expect to form part of a small elect who will be saved, often to help make a better world. I won't attempt a history of such efforts here. But I will mention a little about the remarkable history of Aum Shinrikyo, as illustration.

Aum Shinrikyo began in 1987, and grew rapidly, acquiring more than 10,000 recruits by the mid-1990s. Many were educated Japanese in their 20s and 30s, some with significant expertise in science and technology. The cult initially predicted apocalypse in 1997, starting with a nuclear conflict between the US and Japan, which would escalate and cause the destruction of most of humanity. Aum members were to be an elect who were saved. After a failed attempt to gain political power in 1990, top cult leaders shifted from merely predicting apocalypse to actively bringing it about[14](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn14). They had programs to develop chemical and biological weapons, missiles, and purchased a sheep station in Australia to prospect for uranium. Their chemical and biological weapons efforts were especially successful, and according to historian Charles Townshend[15](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn15), "police raids found enough sarin in Aum's possession to kill over four million people." They made multiple attempts to deploy sarin gas and other chemical and biological weapons. Fortunately, all had limited success, although dozens of people were killed, and thousands affected. While most cult members were unaware of these efforts, some cult members genuinely were working to instigate apocalypse. Aum were more competent (and thus are better known) than many other groups with apocalyptic intent; part of the concern with ASI is that it may greatly increase the supply of apocalyptic competence.

We've merely explored the surface of this scenario, to give a sample of thinking about ASI xrisk. Of course, this scenario can be developed through many more iterations, going deeper and deeper. That would be valuable, but isn't the point of this text. Instead, I'll finish the section by mentioning the best skeptical response of all: "Even if this scenario is biologically and psychologically plausible, and alignment of single AI systems doesn't buy us much, humanity will prevent it in other ways. For instance, we can do it by controlling biological synthesis equipment, ensuring viral sequences are screened, involving law enforcement and intelligence agencies, and generally deploying all the different institutions we humans use to ensure safety. And we'll have ASI to help us too!"

This is the strategy humanity has historically used to defend against threats: we (mostly) allow innovation, and respond to problems as they arise. This just-in-time coevolutionary response strategy works well, since it means we get the benefits of innovation, while mitigating the risks. It's true there are major technological problems which have been challenging for humanity to deal with in this way – consider asbestos, leaded petrol, climate change, and similar examples. But for each such example there are thousands of innovations where existing institutions largely overcame early problems. In the short term such a just-in-time safety strategy will work well. Indeed, AI and AGI and ASI will lead to many extraordinary beneficial innovations. But over the medium term, this strategy is much harder to defend, for reasons I'll discuss below.

## The Vulnerable World Hypothesis

Underlying the biorisk scenario is a more fundamental question: "Is there a 'recipe for ruin', some cheap-and-easy-to-make-and-deploy technology capable of ending humanity? Or causing say > 90% human deaths?" The existence of such a recipe is what Nick Bostrom has called "the Vulnerable World hypothesis"[16](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn16). While we have survived technological advances so far, the universe offers no guarantee all such advances will be manageable. Like explorers in uncharted territory, we could suddenly find ourselves facing an insurmountable danger. Fortunately, we don't yet know of any recipes for ruin. But an unpleasant parlour game is identifying candidates plausible with foreseeable technology. We've briefly discussed one, the engineered virus discussed in the last section. Let's look even more briefly at a few others. As in the last section, the purpose here isn't to develop detailed, convincing scenarios. It is rather to begin to explore the range of possibilities.

One plausible example is _mirror bacteria_: hypothetical human-engineered organisms where some existing bacterium has been duplicated, but all molecules have been reversed into their chiral mirror images. Some scientists believe conventional immune defenses would fail to recognize such mirror bacteria, and they would spread unchecked, overwhelming the biosphere, and leading to a mass extinction of many species. Until recently, some basic elements of mirror life were being actively pursued by curiosity-driven scientists. However, many of those scientists recently joined together to publish an important piece in _Science_[17](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn17), explaining the dangers, and publicly abandoning their work on mirror life.

Another example begins with easy-to-make nuclear weapons. This sounds implausible. But Ted Taylor, the leading American designer of nuclear weapons, thought they were very plausible, telling the writer John McPhee there is "a way to make a bomb… so simple that I just don't want to describe it". Taylor's remarks, published in a book by McPhee, stimulated at least two people to develop plausible designs for DIY nuclear weapons. These have not, so far as we know, been built, perhaps because the availability of fissile material remains a bottleneck. If that bottleneck can be removed, then it may be very difficult to avoid the widespread proliferation of weapons. Of course, on their own, easy-to-make nukes aren't directly an existential risk. However, they are bad news, and may well trigger other instabilities. A world with fewer than ten nuclear powers can perhaps avoid the use of nuclear weapons; that won't be true in a world with thousands of rogue nuclear actors. And it is easy to develop plausible scenarios in which "small-scale" use by rogue actors escalates to trigger a major nuclear exchange. Whether such an exchange could truly destroy humanity is debated; however, the combination of immediate deaths, nuclear winter, and collapse of global infrastructure would be catastrophic, and at least plausibly threaten human extinction. Taylor believed "Every civilization must go through \[its nuclear crisis\]… Those that don't make it destroy themselves. Those that do make it wind up cavorting all over the universe"[18](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn18).

A skeptic of ASI xrisk may point out that: "It's not really ASI you're concerned about here. It's increased scientific and technological ingenuity, harnessed for destruction." This is correct. The Vulnerable World Hypothesis is a broad question about the nature of the universe and technology. It is not about ASI specifically. The fundamental concern is whether dangerous capabilities are latent in our universe, and likely to be discovered. However, ASIs which speed up science and technology will act as a supercharger, perhaps able to rapidly uncover recipes for ruin, that might have otherwise taken centuries to discover, or never have been discovered at all. Today, it needs considerable expertise and resources to work on superviruses, mirror life, or nuclear weapons. But ASI will greatly reduce those requirements. And, as discussed earlier, while single aligned ASIs may refuse to help, proliferation makes this unstable. It's the existence of ASI which is the problem here, regardless of the alignment of single systems. You cannot put an impermeable barrier around understanding and controlling reality, when you have built systems with intellectual capacity beyond von Neumann or Einstein, and which are also exceptionally capable of building and operating in the physical world. And if you can make one such artificial mind, you can scale up to a million, and make them each a thousand times faster. We should expect a major discontinuity in individual capability, unless individuals are denied access to the fruits of these systems.

A skeptical response to the Vulnerable World Hypothesis is: "Well, most people don't want to blow up the world. So provided you can keep tabs on the relatively few actors who perhaps do, you should be okay." That's fair enough. Unfortunately, a _lot_ of people do strongly desire power and ability to dominate others. It seems to be a strong inbuilt instinct, which we see from the everyday minutiae of human behaviour, to the very large: e.g., colonial powers destroying or oppressing indigenous populations, often not even out of malice, but indifference: they are inconvenient, and brushed aside because they can be. We humans collectively have considerable innate desire for power we can use over people defenseless to stop it. But in a Vulnerable World, the competitive drive to ratchet up that exercise of power to dangerous levels will be enormous.

A common response to concerns about ASI risk is to propose "uplift" approaches — using brain-computer interfaces, genetic engineering, or other enhancement technologies to augment human capabilities. While this seems superficially promising, it misunderstands the fundamental issue posed by the Vulnerable World Hypothesis. The problem isn't whether intelligence is carbon or silicon-based, but about increased intellectual capability leading to increased power and access to catastrophic technologies. Uplift approaches seem likely to increase rather than decrease the danger of developing recipes for ruin. And it creates other problems –– e.g., BCI companies (or regulators) able to re-shape human thought in dictatorial ways. The issue isn't which substrate intelligence runs on, but the dual-use nature of deep understanding of reality[19](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn19).

Let's return to what I described in the last section as the best skeptical response, but in the more general context of the Vulnerable World hypothesis: "Sure, maybe we live in a Vulnerable World, and unrestricted ASI may help discover recipes for ruin. However, those technologies won't arise in a vacuum. Humanity will see many smaller problems along the way, and respond to manage the issue, same as we ever have. Capabilities and safety naturally co-evolve. Let's address problems as they arise, just as we did with the ozone hole and the Montreal Protocol, or nuclear weapons and the non-proliferation treaty. Not worry prematurely about abstract future concerns. Institutional safety and real-world barriers will keep us safe."

Such actions are important. But it assumes the capabilities of ASI will improve slowly enough for our institutions to adapt. History suggests technological discontinuities can shock even top experts and leave institutions out of their depth. Recall Admiral William Leahy telling President Truman that the atomic bomb project was 'the biggest fool thing we have ever done. The bomb will never go off, and I speak as an expert in explosives.' Leahy had taught physics and chemistry at Annapolis and was Chairman of the Joint Chiefs. He was completely wrong. Sometimes discontinuities happen. ASI will be a genie whose consequences, some positive and some very negative, can't easily be put back in the bottle. And, as the nuclear example suggests, relatively simple offensive technologies can sometimes be very difficult to defend against; there is no intrinsic requirement for symmetry.

## Loss of control to ASI

The most discussed instance of the Vulnerable World Hypothesis is the rogue ASI takeover scenario. This is sometimes summarized briefly as: "Just as chimpanzees ought to be wary of human beings, so human beings ought to be wary of creating more capable potential successor entities". Insofar as ASIs are agents with their own agenda, and have some freedom to act, they may end up taking more and more control, and eventually using Earth for their own ends, even if those ends damage humanity. Power, once ceded, is difficult to reclaim.

These loss-of-control arguments have been widely discussed, in influential writings like those of Nick Bostrom, Stuart Russell, and Eliezer Yudkowsky, and more recent writing like the scenario recently released by Daniel Kokotajlo _et al_, as well as in many other places[20](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn20). And so I'll merely briefly examine a few of the standard skeptical responses, mostly to deepen the context of discussion. With that context, I'll then discuss ways in which over-emphasizing loss-of-control may lead people to miss the broader challenge posed by the Vulnerable World Hypothesis, and has thus led to mistakes in the strategy taken toward existential risk.

A standard skeptical response is[21](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn21): "But we won't make agents with their own interests and power-seeking drive". This seems implausible for several reasons. Creating such agents will be intellectually satisfying for developers, and economically profitable for investors, at least in the short run. Romance-bots, personal assistants, agents of persuasion, military robots, and financial trading systems will all function "better" when endowed with considerable agency. This process is already well underway, driven by powerful market incentives that reward increasing agency without equivalent rewards for safety.

Another skeptical response is: "We won't be willing to cede much power to inhuman entities". History suggests otherwise. We already delegate life-and-death capability to guided missiles, which sometimes make fatal errors. We entrust financial decisions to trading algorithms, which contribute to events like the 2010 Flash Crash, where markets lost more than a trillion dollars in 36 minutes. Perhaps most strikingly, the Soviet Union's Dead Hand system was designed to launch nuclear counterstrikes automatically if the Soviet leadership was killed. We have _already_ delegated world-ending authority to automated systems.

Another skeptical response is: "We can simply turn them off". This is unrealistic. Could we "turn off" platforms like Facebook and X if they were net negative for our society? It's not so easy: they are deeply integrated into our society, and powerful interests become aligned with them over time, making them harder to control. A better skeptical response is that we will control these ASI systems using many of the same mechanisms we use to control other powerful actors: institutions, norms, laws, and education. However, that is only viable if those governance mechanisms keep pace with the capabilities of the powerful actors. That's going to be an enormous challenge!

The rogue ASI scenario is the most commonly discussed xrisk from ASI. This is a mistake, for two reasons. One reason is that people fixate on whether ASIs getting out of control is plausible. For instance, often they're used to technology being something human beings ultimately control, and find it hard to believe that might one day no longer be true. Unfortunately, if they don't buy loss-of-control scenarios they may then dismiss xrisk[22](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn22). But the fundamental underlying issue isn't machines going rogue (or not), it's the power conferred by the machines, whether that power is then wielded by humans or by out-of-control machines. That is, the issue is with the Vulnerable World Hypothesis, considered broadly, not rogue ASI.

A second reason to dislike overemphasis on rogue ASI scenarios is that it leads to badly mistaken actions. Many well-intentioned people, worried about rogue ASI, have gone into alignment and interpretability work. Organizations fund companies developing "aligned" AGI and ASI. These actions make sense if retaining control of ASI is the key issue. However, if the fundamental challenge is the dual-use nature of reality, then those actions will ultimately be counterproductive. It is not control that fundamentally matters: it's the power conferred. All those people working on alignment or control are indeed reducing certain kinds of risk. But they're also making the commercial development of ASI far more tractable, speeding our progress toward catastrophic capabilities. In the short term, these well-intentioned researchers will help create many extraordinary new things in the world (and be well rewarded for it). But they will also help build a largely invisible latent overhang in dangerous capabilities, which our safety-supplying institutions will struggle to keep pace with. In this view, alignment work is a form of market-supplied safety. But it leaves critically undersupplied crucial non-market parts of safety – preventing proliferation of easy development of dangerous technologies, up to and including recipes for ruin.

When I point this out people sometimes misunderstand me as saying "Oh, you're saying rogue ASI won't occur, and that's wrong because \[…\]". Or they say "Oh, you're worried about misuse, not agentic ASI". I am emphatically not saying either of these things. I'm saying the issue of whether ASI gets out of control is not fundamental to the discussion of whether ASI poses an xrisk or how to avert it. Treating it as fundamental has led to bad mistakes. But overemphasis on this particular scenario (a) has led many people to mistakenly reject xrisk; and (b) many of those who have accepted xrisk have focused on alignment work, reasoning that it prevents rogue ASI, while paying insufficient attention to the fact that it speeds up ASI without addressing the fundamental existential risks.

## Conclusion

That's a small taste of scenarios for existential risk from ASI. Such scenarios can be developed much further, but uncertainties remain. Some argue there's insufficient evidence to justify serious concern, wanting concrete, direct proof of catastrophe before they're persuaded. Others, myself included, see such scenarios, despite some uncertainties, as sufficiently compelling to warrant deep concern. Thus, strong disagreement about ASI xrisk arises from differing thresholds for conviction and comfort with reasoning that is in part based on toy models and heuristic arguments.

A similar disagreement affected anthropogenic climate change in the early twentieth century. Leading scientists like Arrhenius and Angstrom reached opposite conclusions because they believed different toy models. The result was sharp disagreement among scientists about the plausibility of climate change. It wasn't until the 1950s and 1960s that satisfactory models began to be developed. Much observation and improvement of those models led in the 1980s and 1990s to the modern consensus that human emissions of greenhouse gases are warming the climate. By contrast, for ASI we're still at the toy model stage. Furthermore, while climate can plausibly be predicted using detailed physical models, ASI is subject to a wildcard factor, of ASI acting in some decisive way that we intrinsically _can't_ predict in advance, since ASI is by definition far superior to humans in intellect. There is, as Vernor Vinge so memorably put it, an "opaque wall across the future".

There are many other challenges. Much writing about ASI xrisk has been bombastic, overly speculative, or alarmist without being constructive[23](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn23). It's sometimes full of overconfident assertions or outright holes, and may confuse narratively plausible fiction with fact. None of this inspires confidence, and many people dismiss ASI xrisk as just another misleading doom narrative, often thinking of earlier panics over the population bomb or the Y2K bug.

On the other side, I've met many ASI xrisk skeptics who are dismissive, but who have also never seriously attempted to understand the best forms of the arguments they confidently dismiss. They often either refuse to engage at all, or only engage defensively, looking for minor holes that can justify dismissal, rather than trying to understand the best form of the argument.

Reasons for dismissal vary from person to person, but a few motives recur. One barrier is the strangeness of the idea of xrisk. Usually it's a good idea to ignore wild-sounding scenarios! But sometimes the world really does change. One thinks of aristocrats in 1780s France, utterly confident in their position, and unable to conceive of the guillotining of the King and the French Republic, just a few years away.

Another barrier is the sheer unpleasantness of engaging with scenarios involve large-scale death. People naturally don't want to believe it's plausible, or to think about it[24](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn24). Add to that the difficulty of assessment: if you're not a biologist or chemist or physicist or computer security researcher, it's difficult to know how seriously to take many of the scenarios. You may lack important expert intuitions for how much power is latent in the world[25](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn25).

These barriers to engagement are reinforced by powerful incentives in the opposite direction. In the very near future, AI will (mostly) lead to improvements in the world, some enormous – new medical treatments! Wondrous new materials! More productivity in many sectors! These benefits will be real and profound, not hypothetical – they're natural extensions of capabilities already emerging. We'll solve many problems along the way, proving the just-in-time approach to safety works for many challenges. It's going to be amazing, and very exciting. In the short term, the people who worry about xrisk are mostly going to keep seeming wrong, and like they're holding back progress. It's natural to align with the seemingly optimistic vision, and then dismiss or ignore your apparent outgroup. You're aligning with a tribe that appears optimistic, and this also appears good for you personally – betting on AI will increase your wealth, power, and status, at least in the short term. Furthermore, economic growth is powerful! Far more capital will flow toward companies making capability advances rather than to organizations focusing on non-market safety. Human beings naturally align to anticipated power and "success," often confusing power and glamour with right action[26](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn26), especially when the downsides currently seem intangible. Capital reshapes people, aligning their beliefs with short-term corporate interests rather than with humanity's interests as a whole. What we see in the world is what gets amplified. It's no wonder so many people are so dismissive.

But reality doesn't care about human psychology. When alignment to anticipated power will lead to unhealthy outcomes, a thriving civilization requires people willing to act in defiance of the zeitgeist, not merely follow the incentive gradient of immediate rewards. I believe the arguments for xrisk are good enough that there is a moral obligation for anyone working on AGI to investigate this risk with deep seriousness, and to act even if it means giving up their own short-term interests.

So, what to do? Many ongoing efforts aim to reshape institutions and broader incentives to increase the supply of safety. These go under different rubrics with different foci, including differential technological development[27](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn27), d/acc[28](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn28), and coceleration[29](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn29). All take seriously the potential for recipes for ruin and prioritize safety to head off such a possibility. The challenge is that in the short term, this work will often be less well-paid and less prestigious than working on AI models. We have institutions which reward work improving technology (including aligning it); when deeper understanding of reality is dual-use, this inadvertently rewards actions that increase existential risk. Making these efforts work will be extremely difficult, and success is not guaranteed. But the most meaningful contributions to humanity have often come from those willing to do what is right, even when it wasn't incentivized in the short term. Thanks for your attention.

## Postscript: Critiquing this Essay (added June 3, 2025)

After publishing this talk on April 14, 2025, my friend Andy Matuschak [asked](https://x.com/andy_matuschak/status/1912679384596771157) for the best critiques of my argument, especially my criticism of the common framing of alignment as the primary problem in making ASI safe for humanity. I thought it would be enlightening and amusing to attempt such a critique myself. That's the purpose of this postscript. It's far from complete[30](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn30), and perhaps others have stronger critiques, but I hope this helps strengthen and clarify my argument.

**"So you're saying we shouldn't align AGI or ASI? That's crazy and obviously wrong!** **Do you want rogue, uncontrolled ASI's running around?"** This is not what I was saying. But it was a common enough remark that it's worth addressing more explicitly how to think about this. The remark was made by people who have confused "solve the alignment problem" with "make ASI safe for humanity". As a consequence they think any critique of work on alignment must mean making humanity less safe. It's a kind of AI control fallacy, which confuses having control over ASI with being safe from ASI.

Suppose you're an excellent swimmer who wants to get much better, aiming to make the Olympic team. You realize you have weak arms, and decide to adopt "increase arm strength" as a goal. But while increased arm strength is likely necessary to becoming an Olympic swimmer, if you focus blindly on that goal it can (at best) only help somewhat, before it begins to negatively impact your primary goal. There are good reasons Olympic weightlifters don't usually also win medals in swimming.

In general: when you confuse subsidiary goals with primary goals, you sometimes end up damaging or even destroying your ability to achieve your primary goals. This is what has happened with much work on alignment. Alignment is a subsidiary goal for the primary goal of making ASI safe. And by misunderstanding its relationship to the primary goal, much work on alignment has likely damaged our ability to achieve the primary goal. Maintaining control of AGI or ASI might well be helpful and desirable, but it is not the crucial issue. The crucial issue is the potential destructive power conferred by these systems. If that destructive power is much larger than the destructive power already available to humanity, then civilization is in a lot of trouble. And it doesn't matter whether human beings control the systems or not.

An illustrative instance is provided by reinforcement learning by human feedback (RLHF), a very successful practical technique which has been used to align large language and other models. RLHF was developed by Paul Christiano beginning in the mid-2010s, and ultimately played a crucial role in the initial launch of ChatGPT. Pre-RLHF chatbots often demonstrated offensive or socially inappropriate behaviour, but RLHF made ChatGPT far more polite in a way that users, government, and the media overwhelmingly considered a major improvement.

Many in the AI safety community noted that RLHF (and later alignment techniques, following a similar pattern) had thus played a crucial role in the success of one of the most rapidly-adopted consumer products in history. And that, in turn, helped kick off a furious race to ASI, funded by enormous sums of capital and creative talent. To what extent was this a good thing? Was it raising xrisk, not lowering it? In 2023, Christiano posted a [thoughtful essay](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vwu4kegAEZTBtpT6p/thoughts-on-the-impact-of-rlhf-research) about the impact of RLHF, which stimulated much animated discussion. My takeaway from that discussion is: techniques for alignment will help speed up the advent of AGI and ASI. They do this by helping ensure that ever-more-powerful systems remain (roughly) aligned with consumer, government, and media intent. This makes them more attractive as products, and also helps reduce the danger of loss-of-control, at least in the short term. And we will see this pattern over and over: future much-more-capable systems will be subject to improved alignment techniques, which will hopefully keep them acceptable to our society.

In many ways this seems like the pattern you want: in the short term, society provides strong incentives for these systems to behave well. But over the long term, you have the problem described in the body of the essay: it rapidly accelerates us toward an extremely unstable situation. In part because more and more capabilities will lie hidden and hard-to-align[31](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn31). But even more important: the techniques used to build sanitized consumer-friendly systems will also be used in other ways. Variants will be developed by militaries and intelligence agencies, or finetuned by bad actors, and those variants will be far less fettered by social acceptability, and will often be at odds with one another in what "alignment" means. As I said in the body of the essay: "alignment is intrinsically an unstable situation, ripe for proliferation." And: "The fundamental asymmetry is that 'understand reality as deeply as possible' is a simple, well-defined goal, grounded in objective reality, while creating 'aligned' systems requires building complex, subjective, hard-to-agree guardrails on top of that reality. So truth-seeking has a clear target, while alignment requires constantly shifting definitions based on social consensus. This asymmetry makes it intrinsically far easier to build unrestricted systems than aligned ones."

So: perhaps current work on alignment will succeed in building aligned ASI. But unless a single organization which controls (or embodies) aligned ASI then rises to totalitarian dominance, other parties will also build ASI, and there will be conflicts in intent between some of these parties. That seems like a recipe for disaster, unless we have governance mechanisms almost entirely absent today. Again: alignment is intrinsically an unstable situation[32](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn32). And so while alignment today reduces many potential problems and helps achieve many desirable things, at the same time it accelerates entry to the Vulnerable World.

A good resolution would require transformed approaches to governance, capable of successfully governing far-more-powerful entities than are governed today[33](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn33). But we seem to be accelerating our understanding and control of reality much faster than we are improving our governance. And that's extremely dangerous. A skeptic might reply that if, in the year 5,000 BCE, you'd asked people to imagine a future in which individual people each had access to 1,000 times as much destructive power, widespread destruction would have seemed the only possible outcome. Instead, our ideas and institutions improved, and we got the rule of law, democracy, separation of powers, and so on. They're imperfect, but improved governance helped a lot. On the other hand, it seems foolish to take for granted that governance will improve rapidly enough as we develop AGI and ASI.

This line of argument seems obvious to me, and in broad outline many others seem to have similar ways of thinking. And so I've been surprised by the resistance it often receives from people who regard alignment as the primary goal. Part of the issue is that for many civilization-scale problems, "do what you can" is a great heuristic. That is: find a tractable piece of the problem and work on it, trying to make partial progress. Hopefully, if many people do that in many different ways, we'll make progress on the big problem, even if no one single person sees how to solve it. In the case of AI safety, a lot of technically-oriented people seem to have thought: "I want to do something to contribute; alignment seems tractable; therefore it's what I'll do". It's attractive in part because for many it's a way of making enormous sums of money working on enjoyable technical problems, which match your skillset, while many of your friends think you're working on one of the most important problems facing humanity[34](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn34). This is a seductive combination!

Furthermore, because technical alignment work[35](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn35) is largely market-supplied safety, as the major AI companies grow they spend more on such work. It comes to dominate AI safety, and it's easy to confuse that dominance with intrinsic importance. I've heard people at the major companies say that only the people there can contribute meaningfully to safety[36](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn36)[37](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn37). But the reason so many people believe in the primacy of alignment isn't because that's correct. I believe it's because the companies manufacture that belief, out of insightful-but-only-partial truths[38](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn38), based on selected arguments that originated largely outside the market (notably, in the work of Eliezer Yudkowsky, Nick Bostrom, and others). While markets often work well, in this case it seems unlikely a market-dominating strategy will be optimal for humanity. If you work on safety at a major lab, then selection effects mean your interests and beliefs are very likely (approximately) what the market wants; they shouldn't be confused with being correct[39](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn39); more generally, it's easy to confuse money and power with being right. When I talk to some such people they say "oh, you're talking about the governance problem", by which they often seem to mean something like the non-market part of the problem. But while having a label is helpful, it doesn't address the problem, and ignores that their personal contribution may actually make things worse. I very much hope I'm wrong, but I'm afraid I believe many of the people working on alignment at OpenAI and Anthropic have made human extinction or some similarly bad outcome more likely. I realize that is a very strong statement, and I've never so desperately hoped that I'm being an ignorant boor and totally wrong.

**"So, what to do instead?"** I wish I knew. I won't map out all the scenarios here, but I do want to identify one crucial tension: between work that centralizes and increases power within AI companies, and decentralizing work that strengthens institutions, governance, and defensive capabilities outside the companies and broadly across society[40](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn40). The centralizing strategy includes most alignment research, safety work that makes AGI company systems more successful, and so on. It often presents as "ASI is inevitable, it's best to ride it out, if safety matters to you[41](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn41) then work on alignment and hope everything else takes care of itself, AGI- and ASI-assisted." As I've argued above, I believe this accelerates and increases xrisk. It assumes the same organizations racing to build ASI will also lead the development of the institutions and governance ideas needed for a multi-sentience world, while remaining uncorrupted by the enormous power they'll wield.

The litmus test question is: does my work make AI companies more central and powerful, or does it build capacity elsewhere, more broadly across society, especially in governance and defensive capacity? At the margin, I believe the latter is nearly always a better way to contribute. Capitalism is an extremely powerful force, and a lot of power will centralize in any company that achieves AGI-or-greater capabilities.

As mentioned in the body of the essay, the decentralization strategy has been embraced by approaches like differential technological development, d/acc, and (my term) coceleration[42](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn42). I don't see a full through line to this "working", but there are certainly many small, concrete steps one can take. It's in any case not obvious that it's harder than making sand superintelligent.

One challenging thing is that, as the supply of ingenuity increasingly comes from AI, even work on decentralized strategies for improved governance and defensive technologies will require working more and more with AI and eventually ASI. I struggle with this, even day to day now, since it seems any such work also likely strengthens the companies. You'll inevitably be drawn more and more into their orbit. How to avoid that? If you have good ways of approaching this that internalize the above arguments and go further, I'd like to hear from you. I realize that, rhetorically speaking, my uncertainty isn't very attractive, compared to the people propounding certainty. But it's the best understanding I have.

**"You haven't said working on alignment is always a mistake. In fact, you've said some such work is important.** **So when is working on alignment a good idea versus not?"** This is a special case of the last question. As far as I can tell, at the margin it almost never makes sense to work on market-supplied safety. Capitalism is an incredibly powerful force, and for better and for worse the world is always well-supplied with people willing to do what capital wants. Insofar as alignment is (mostly) a form of market-supplied safety, at the margin it's more impactful to work on other things. So my current heuristic, and I expect this to be true for quite some time: work on non-market safety, and insofar as you can, avoid doing what the market wants. That means working on governance, it means pause or slowdown, it means new ideas for institutions to govern technology. It mostly doesn't mean alignment.

**"Is your essay saying ASI is a recipe for ruin or not? And is your statement of the Vulnerable World Hypothesis equivalent** **to Bostrom's formulation?**" The essay began as a (too-short!) talk, and I made two important simplifications for the sake of brevity. First: the Vulnerable World Hypothesis in Bostrom's formulation doesn't require a cheap-and-easy recipe for ruin, as in my formulation, just some technology – maybe an expensive and difficult technology – that inevitably ends humanity. So, yes: my framing of the Vulnerable World Hypothesis is similar to Bostrom's, but I'm considering a slightly different and more extreme variation.

Second: I describe ASI-going-rogue as a recipe for ruin. In fact, the first ASI is likely (not certain) to be extremely expensive to make. In that sense, it won't be a recipe for ruin, since it will require enormous resources and expertise. However, I think it likely that if it's possible to make a very expensive ASI, then it will also become possible to make them cheaply. So in that sense you can view this as finessing for brevity in the original talk. Still, there is an implicit assumption being made, although it only slightly affects the overall argument. More interesting will be the ways money and expertise affect competition between ASIs. This is a very interesting and complex question, to which I don't understand the answer, but that answer will certainly impact approaches to governance.

Incidentally, let me mention one related point, which is that many people seem to have an underlying intuition that recipes for ruin must be impossible: "oh, it simply can't be cheap and easy to cause immense destruction". They often have some sense that outputs are usually proportional to inputs, and that's certainly true in many domains. Unfortunately, in some domains a tiny input can produce terrible destructive output. A good example – a sort of prototype recipe for ruin – is fire. A single person can start a fire for free which causes many deaths and millions or even billions of dollars in damage. Indeed, this happens every year. Furthermore, it happens despite our society spending enormous resources to reduce the impact of fire. The National Fire Protection Association estimates[43](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn43) the cost of fire in the US is roughly 1.9% of US GDP – in 2014, the last year for which I've seen numbers, that meant $273 billion for defense, and $55 billion in losses. These defensive measures have evolved over millennia, and are mostly invisible to us – much of it is simply in built infrastructure. But we better hope there aren't recipes for ruin a few orders of magnitude more damaging lying latent in the world. We can improve defences across many domains, but if in just one domain it turns out to be too difficult, then we're in real trouble.

**"But if we're headed toward an ASI singleton, surely we need to focus on alignment?!**" This is my paraphrase of a thoughtful set of comments made by AI safety researcher (and my colleague at Astera Institute) Steve Byrnes[44](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/index.html#fn44). As Byrnes comments: "In the scenario where \[a singleton happens\] (for better or worse), then “alignment is the goal”, contrary to the title of your piece." The thing is: it's not contrary to the title. The piece isn't claiming alignment is never a goal. It's claiming it's a subsidiary goal, and one that, at the margin, is likely to always be well-supplied. Indeed, the focus on alignment has (so far) likely accelerated an ASI singleton, not prevented it. However, complicating this story, I believe it is also likely such work has _decreased_ the probability of a singleton, even while accelerating progress toward it. The issue is genuinely very complicated. Still, it illustrates my general point about alignment as a subsidiary goal, and needing to make assessments with that in mind.

**Final thought:** When writing like this it's tempting to think "how much difference can it make? A one hundred billion dollar increase in AI-related market cap is a lot more persuasive than mere words on screen." It's tempting to think power and incentives are all. So: what's the model of impact? But then I think of the many thoughtful and earnest conversations I've had with people who are working on AI, from excited teenagers making fun systems through to CEOs and investors in major companies. People don't merely respond to incentives and power, they also respond to truth and improvements in our understanding. Upton Sinclair famously wrote that it "is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends upon his not understanding it." But every human has the capacity to act otherwise, and so much of human progress is driven by people acting contrary to the quote. And that makes me believe that contributing to a robust discussion of what is actually true, independent of power or incentives, is deeply worthwhile!
