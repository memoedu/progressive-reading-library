# Mini Blog Post 18: How to teach things well — Neel Nanda

---

## 正文

Introduction（引言）

这是一篇讨论如何做好 teaching 的博客文章。我主要会关注涉及 preparation 和 structure 的 teaching 形式，例如 talks 和 tutoring，不过这些 ideas 可以相当广泛地 transfer。我认为，teaching 和 explaining ideas 是一种_极其_重要的 skill，而[大多数人](https://www.cam.ac.uk/)都不太擅长。我花了很多时间 practicing teaching ideas，也觉得自己找到了一些重要且行之有效的 ideas 和 approaches。我下周要做一次 talk，因此会先着重讨论如何做好 talks，同时尝试勾勒 teaching 的 underlying concepts 和 high-level ideas。然后再谈谈这些内容如何 transfer 到 tutoring 等 contexts，以及具体针对 maths 或 applied rationality 的 teaching——这些是我实际拥有 teaching experience 的主要领域。

说明：我主要关心的是 teaching concepts 和 ideas，以及向那些真心想要 learn、愿意参与其中的人 teaching，因此我的建议也会相应地有所侧重。

我认为，即使你不打算花很多时间 teaching，思考什么是好的 teaching 也很有用——learning 和 teaching 是同一个 process 的两个侧面。我发现，即使身为 student，understanding 好的 teaching 是什么样子，也往往能弥补一个糟糕的 teacher 的许多不足！

Framing（框架）

本文的 key insight 是，好的 teaching 要求你[be deliberate](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/be-deliberate)，并牢记目的：**learning 是一个 information compression 的 process**。当你 learning 新东西时，你本质上是在 receive 一股新的 information。但 human cognition 并不是靠把大量 information 原样 storing 下来运作的。student 会 takes in 这股 information stream，从中 extracts out 重要的 ideas，将其 converts 为 _concepts_，再把它们 stores 在自己的 mind 中。这是一个关键的 distinction，因为它表明，teacher 的工作_不是_向 student 提供 information，而是**让 student understand 正确的 concepts**。Conveying information 只有作为实现这一目标的手段才有用。

实际上，仅仅提供一股 information，往往也能奏效！好的 students 已经 learned 了将 information streams 接收过来并 converting 为 concepts 的 skill。这通常是 implicitly 发生的：student 会 absorb 和 memorise 大量 data，随着时间推移，这些内容会 automatically 在他们脑中 forms 为 concepts 和 ideas。但这需要_大量_的 cognitive labour。好的 teacher 会尽可能承担这些工作，让 student 能将自己的 cognitive labour focus 在重要的事情上。

我在这里的 underlying model 是，我们每个人的 mind 中都有一张由 concepts 构成的网，也就是我们的 **knowledge graph**。它是我们 understand 的所有 concepts、所有 existing knowledge 和 intuitions connected 在一起形成的集合。当你能把某样东西 convert 为 concepts，并**将它 connect 到你的 existing understanding** 时，你就 learned 了它。这意味着不仅要 understanding 这个 concept 本身，还要 understanding 它在 bigger picture 中的位置、在哪里 use 它，等等。

最后这一点是关键——如果 student 离开时对那些 ideas in the abstract 有了很好的 understanding，却不知道什么时候该再次想起这些 ideas，那就并不比什么都没 learned 更好。我们是在有相关事物 triggers 时才会 call on 自己的 knowledge，因此，要让一节 lesson 有用，你就_必须_在 student 的 mind 中 build 那些 connections 和 triggers。

需要牢记的一个关键 distinction，是 ideas 属于 **legible** 还是 **tacit**。legible idea 是 concrete、很容易用 unambiguous words 表达出来的东西，例如如何做 integration by parts。而 tacit knowledge 则更 fuzzier、更 intuitive，例如 recognising 究竟哪些 kinds of integrals 一开始就该用 integration by parts——本质上就是你希望 student 拥有的 intuitions。这个 distinction 值得牢记，因为 legible knowledge 要容易 convey _得多_，但你的目标往往是 convey tacit knowledge（至少应该如此！）。要做好 conveying tacit knowledge，并在不 losing 关键 nuance 的前提下让它尽可能 legible，需要很多 skill。而且，不同的 techniques 对这两类 knowledge 的效果也不同。我对 Cambridge maths course 的许多不满，就在于它极端地 focus 于 legible knowledge，而非 tacit knowledge——也就是 underlying intuitions 和 motivations。

如何 teach

teaching 时有两个关键问题，任何好的 teaching 建议都必须考虑到：

-   用于 convey information 的 ways 有限

    -   我正在 teaching 的 ideas，是以 implicit concepts 的形式 stored 在我的 mind 中的，但为了 convey 它们，我必须将它们 translate 成 language。这些 language 会根据我的全部 implicit knowledge 和 worldview，maps 到_我_脑中的 ideas；但进入 student 脑中时，却会根据_他们的_ implicit knowledge 和 worldview 来 translates。这常常会 creates errors

    -   而将 concepts converting 为 language 是 _inherently lossy_ 的，ideas 包含许多难以 capture 的 tacit nuance

    -   本质上，words 只能 convey legible knowledge，而我需要想办法 hack 这一点，以 convey tacit knowledge。或者，想办法找到 alternate information channels

    -   另一种办法是，我需要有 error checking mechanisms，以便在自己未能很好地 convey 某些内容时_察觉_到。

-   [Typical Mind Fallacy](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense)（典型心智谬误）

    -   learning 的一个_关键_部分，是将你听到的 knowledge 与脑中已有的 ideas combining 起来。

    -   但我只能 access _我_脑中的内容，而不能 access student 脑中的内容。而且，by definition，两者就是不同的——我已经 understand 了自己正在 teaching 的内容！

    -   这对我的 teach 能力是一个沉重打击，我需要时刻意识到这一点，并尝试针对 students 脑中的内容，以及他们如何 receive 我所说的话，build models。

以下是我用来应对这些问题的一些最重要的 tools：

-   High-level picture（高层次全貌）

    -   student 的 knowledge graph 是 _big_ 的。因此，首先要做、也最重要的部分，是 identifying 他们应该把这些 ideas add 到 graph 的哪个部分

    -   因此，你应该_始终_ highlight 这在 bigger picture 中的位置。我们目前试图 answer 哪些 questions？这个 idea 到底为什么 interesting？student 可以在哪里 use 它？它有哪些 limitations？

    -   在 introducing 一个新的 idea 时，这_始终_应该是首先做的事

-   Prioritising information（确定信息的优先级）

    -   Learning 就是 information compression。这从根本上意味着 student 需要 paying selective attention。在 learning 新东西时，[Pareto Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle) 总是 applies——80% 的 importance 在于 20% 的 ideas。

    -   Identifying 这 20% 是相当大的 cognitive labour，因为它并非一目了然。他们需要对_所有内容_ pay attention，然后再 filter。

    -   但是，“这里什么 matters”这个 question，属于我已经拥有的 tacit knowledge！这场 talk 对 student 来说需要很大的 labour，对我却很容易。因此，teacher 能做的最重要的事，就是 highlight 什么 matters、什么不 matters，告诉 student 应该在哪里 focus 他们的 attention。

    -   在实践中，你应该_始终_说“这真的、真的很 important”，或“这些只是 fiddly details”，或“这算是一个 niche edge case”等等。想把这种话说得太频繁，是_极其困难_的。

        -   还要给 important 的内容更多_时间_。如果你讲了一个 important point，就把它写下来，再用框圈起来。Explain 它_为什么_ important，以及它在 bigger picture 中的位置。给出另一种 explanation，或者一个 example。

        -   如果你把 teaching 看作 information _transfer_，就很容易忽略这一点——在这种看法下，你的目标是把所有东西都告诉 student，然后指望他们自己弄清楚该对什么 pay attention。

    -   另一个 tool：经常进行 summaries，highlighting 上一节的 key points。

        -   这也有助于 highlight _connections_。有些 ideas 比其他 ideas 更容易融入他们的 knowledge graph，而指出 connections，可以借助容易建立的 connections，让难建立的 connections 变得容易一些

    -   我很赞同这条建议：“先说你准备告诉他们什么，告诉他们，再说你刚刚告诉了他们什么”，我认为这是 implement 这一 principle 的好方法

    -   这背后的 fundamental principle 是，students 只会 retain 所听内容中极小的一部分。如果你做一场一小时的 talk，他们也许只会 retain 几分钟的 content。这是 learning process 的一个 fundamental fact，而你对此唯一能做的，就是 _control_ 他们 retain 什么。把他们的 attention Focus 到 important 的部分

        -   这种 mindset 帮助我 _identify_ 什么是 important 的。设一个 5 分钟的计时器，把所有你希望大家 retain 的 important 内容写下来。这些就是你的 talk 应该围绕其进行 structured 的 key points！

        -   你说的其他所有内容，都应该旨在帮助这些 key points 更好地 stick——highlight 它们之间以及它们与 existing knowledge 之间的 connections，确保良好的 information transfer，并 convey 这些 key points 背后的 tacit knowledge

    -   prioritisation 的另一个 key skill 是 _cutting things_。如果某一部分是 irrelevant 的，或者既无聊又 fiddly，就 cut 掉！不能把所有有趣的东西都讲出来令人痛苦，但你的时间有限——如果你不 actively prioritise，你并不是在 avoiding trade-offs，而只是在把 control 交给“你留到最后的东西”

        -   听过我的 talk 的人都知道，我还没有 internalise 这个教训

    -   如果你只从这篇文章中 retain 一个 point，那就让它是这一点——它_极其_ important，也是 good teacher 与 mediocre teacher 之间的主要区别

-   理解 pre-requisites

    -   Typical Mind Fallacy 的一个 consequence 是，你特别容易忘记，你的 students 并没有你拥有的全部 context！这表现为，人们在 teaching ideas 时没有提供 pre-requisites。

        -   背后的 idea 是：你 teach 的 ideas 位于_你的_ knowledge graph 中，并且 built upon 已有的 ideas——这些就是 pre-requisites。你需要弄清楚 students 是否

    -   一个常见的后续错误，是理解了 pre-requisites，然后试图把它们全部 explain！

        -   这里一个好的 framing 是 [inferential distance](https://wiki.lesswrong.com/wiki/Inferential_distance#:~:text=Inferential%20distance%20is%20a%20gap,person%20trying%20to%20understand%20it.)。一个新 idea 的 inferential distance，就是一个人在能够 understand 这个新 idea 之前，必须 understand 的新 concepts 的 steps 数。例如，要向一个小孩 teach quadratic formula，他们首先需要 understand polynomials 这个 idea，而为此又需要 understand algebra——这就是三个 inferential steps。

        -   一条通用的经验法则：_绝不_ teach 超过 2 inferential steps 的东西。刚 learned 的 idea 是 shaky 的，而且还没有建立良好的 connections。把新的 ideas anchor 到新的 ideas 上，是非常、非常困难的。

    -   这_真的很难做好_。Pre-requisites 要求你对别人的 mind 有一个良好的 model。一个通常有用的 technique，是找一位来自你的 target demographic 的人做一次 practice run，并请他们标出所有令他们 confused 的内容。

    -   此外，对于 groups 来说，这可能是个 intractable problem，因为每个人都有不同的 prior knowledge。你需要在头脑中清楚地知道，这场 talk 是面向谁的。

-   Students 应该 learn actively，而不是 passively

    -   student _真的_很容易只是 passively 坐在一股 stream of information 中，却什么都没有吸收。这无法达成你的任何一个目标，因为要 form connections、compress information 并将其 connect 到自己的 knowledge graph，他们就需要投入_一些_ cognitive labour。

    -   鼓励这一点的好方法包括：explicitly 告诉他们这很 important，提供 exercises 并留出思考它们的时间，定期向 audience 提出 questions，并给他们一些思考的时间。

        -   Question asking 有一种 failure mode，就是大多数人不会主动回答，或者会稍微走神——对于这个问题，我没有很好的 solutions

    -   在较小的场合，这要容易处理得_多_，我不擅长在 talks 中处理它。我的主要 solution 就是保持吸引力，并把 pace 把握好。

        -   人们常常会走神，因此安排定期休息，以及设置这样的 check-points：“如果你刚才没跟上，我们要换 topic 了，所以没关系”，可以帮助改善这种情况

            -   即使是短短的 30s-120s 休息也会有帮助！鼓励大家站起来伸伸懒腰。

            -   休息从来不会_让人觉得_ important，但它真的、真的很有帮助

-   给出 examples

    -   Examples 是把东西 teach 好的一种_极其_ powerful 的 tool，而人们很少充分使用它们。我从来没有上过一堂 examples 过多的 class（相信我，我试过）

        -   “你可以 teach 一堂没有 content、只有 examples 的 class；但你不能 teach 一堂只有 content、没有 examples 的 class”

    -   为什么 examples 很棒：

        -   Examples 是 resolve lossy information transfer 的绝佳方法——它们是一种与平常完全不同的 channel of communication。即使没有别的作用，它们也能充当 error check

        -   Examples 是 transfer tacit knowledge 的好方法，而且不一定需要使其变得 legible——这就是 build intuition 的含义

        -   Examples 可以帮助把内容放进 bigger picture，可以 motivate 这些 ideas，并 locate 它们在 student 的 knowledge graph 中的位置

        -   给 student 提供一批 motivating examples 后，他们往往可以通过从 examples 中 generalising，自己 _generate_ 这些 ideas

        -   Examples 可以 bridge 从“understanding abstract 的 knowledge”到“understanding 应该在哪里 _use_ 这些 ideas，以及它们应该在何处出现”之间的 gap

    -   如何 use 它们？

        -   在讲一个 point 时，我常常会给一个 micro-example，为它提供 context——例如，“有时 straight lines 不足以 model data，例如遇到 quadratic 时”。这应该快速且轻松，example 应该让 students 立即理解，只有 0 inferential steps

            -   （真不敢相信，我写到第 18 篇文章才给出第一个 nested example :( ）

        -   Examples 很适合放在开头，用来 motivate 内容，并展示我们试图 answer 的 questions。一个好方法是先给出一个 example，然后在我们把这个 example generalise 成 concept 的过程中，不断回过头来引用它

        -   在 introducing 一个 complex idea 后，完整讲解一个长 example，并 illustrate 这个 example 的哪些部分 embody 了这个 complex idea

        -   用 examples 来 illustrate 一个 idea 的 importance 和 relateability——例如，如果在向 students explaining 如何思考良好的 planning，就给一个 student 因错过 deadline 而陷入危机的 example——每个人都能对此感同身受

    -   Examples 包含_大量_ information，因此 information prioritisation 这个 idea 在这里尤其 applies——告诉 students 应该对 example 中的什么 pay attention，以及它为什么 interesting

-   Visual information 与 diagrams

    -   tacit knowledge 常常会在我的头脑中 manifests 为一幅实实在在的图像——把它画出来！

    -   这是另一个很好的 alternate communication channel

    -   我们的 visual memory 和 processing 往往远胜于我们在 language 方面的能力——这对澄清 confusing 和 complex 的部分很有帮助

-   Pacing（节奏安排）

    -   我常常犯的一个错误，是给一个 section 分配的时间，恰好只是我把它讲出来所需的时间。我把 ideas convert 成 words，然后只是把自己想出的内容读一遍。

        -   这就是把 teaching 看成提供 information stream 的 fallacy！应该分配时间让人们 process 和 compress information，而 hard 的部分需要更多时间，easy 的部分需要更少时间

    -   仅凭直觉很_难_把握好这一点——当你 understand 一个 idea 时，它就让人觉得 easy！

    -   一个好窍门：做一份 high-level summary，然后按 difficulty 给每个 section 打分，满分为 5。之后，明确地给那些 sections 更多时间——例如，加入更多 intuitions，多讲几次 key ideas，给出更多 examples

        -   注意——pacing 并不是指你说话的速度，而是你给不同 ideas 分配的时间！

        -   注意，difficulty =/= importance。如果某一部分是 hard 的，但不 important，就 cut 掉。或者给一个简短的 overview，explain 那个 important idea，然后说“不要纠结 details”

    -   另一个窍门——explicitly 告诉 students 哪些 ideas 是 important 的、值得 paying attention，哪些不是

    -   如果在做 practice run（你完全应该做），就定期向 test audience 确认 pacing 是否合适——**世界的默认状态就是，你会把 pacing 搞错**

    -   把 pacing 把握好是 _key_ 的——人们会在 slow、easy 的 sections 走神，在 fast、hard 的 sections 跟丢。作为 teacher，你的工作是让尽可能多的人持续以 optimum rate absorbing information。

    -   很难对内容给出 accurate time estimates——我的窍门是在最后安排一个 bonus section，必要时可以 cut 掉，并根据我已有的笔记和 intuitions，当场调整 pace

-   理解一个 question 背后的 _mindset_

    -   当有人 asks a question 时，默认反应就是 answer 它。这没有做到 deliberate！student asks a question，是因为他们对某件事感到 confused，而你的目标是 resolve 那个 confusion——直接 answering the question 只是达成目标的手段。

        -   这是一个 important distinction，因为 questions 往往很_奇怪_。它们可能是 confused 的，或者不太说得通，或者在问 unimportant 的事情。对我来说，这表现为 student 的 mind [说不通](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense)。这很容易让人感到沮丧，或者干脆直接 answer 那个愚蠢的 question。但这样是 ineffective 的。

            -   一个相关的 effect——有人 asks 的 question，并不是他们_真正_想问的 question。例如，一个 student 在大学开放日问“你读了多少门 A-Levels？”，而他们真正关心的是“_我_需要读多少门 A-Levels 才能被录取”

        -   你的目标应该是 **understand 那种让这个 question 显得合理的 state of mind**——一旦做到了，你往往就可以直接 resolve 那个 confusion，或者 answer 他们真正关心的 question。

            -   可以这样做：asking clarifying questions，尝试 answer 后再说“这 answer 了你的 question 吗？”，就他们_真正_在问什么给出几种 interpretations，并询问是否有哪一种符合他们的想法，把 question paraphrasing 给他们听，等等。

            -   这里要留意的主要 trigger，是一丝 confusion——那个 question 让人觉得有点不对劲，或来得莫名其妙，有什么地方不太说得通。

            -   你需要在这样做和继续 talk 之间谨慎平衡——试着判断，是否有很多人都存在相同的 confusion；如果没有，就继续往下讲

    -   这样做常常能 uncover **Pedagogical Content-Knowledge**，也就是人们 misunderstand 你所 teach 的 ideas 的常见方式。收集这些内容非常有价值，因为这样你以后就可以 recognise 它们，并直接 dissolve 它们。

-   Illusion of transparency（透明度错觉）

    -   typical mind fallacy 的一个 consequence——你很容易_以为_自己已经 clearly communicated knowledge，而实际上远远没有。因此，你需要付出大量努力，保持 grounded 和 calibrated——因为在你看来，一个 confused 的 audience 往往并不会_让你觉得_是 confused 的 audience。

    -   提出 questions！尤其是那些能 highlight key ideas 的 questions，例如“如何做 easy 的事情 X”，或者“这里的 key idea 是什么？”

    -   进行 hand-polls——请大家用举手来 indicate 自己的 understanding：如果 clear，就把手举高；如果不那么 clear，就举低一些。这是一个好 technique，因为大多数人确实会这样做，不像问“有人有什么 questions 吗？”或者“大家觉得这说得通吗？”

-   寻求 feedback 并 iterate

    -   Teaching 是_困难_的。你根本上是在试图通过 lossy 和 low bandwidth 的 communication channels，把 tacit knowledge convey 到一个你只能极有限地接触到的陌生 mind 中。**世界的默认状态就是，你很不擅长这件事**

    -   solution 是，定期请求 specific、actionable 的 feedback 和 calibration，并真正投入切实的努力，据此 updating！Feedback 是你能更好地 understand 一个 student 的 mind 的主要方式之一。

1-on-1 Teaching（一对一教学）

练习 tutoring 和一对一 explaining 往往会更有价值！我觉得，对大多数 students 来说，做 tutoring 是很值得投入时间的事——挺有趣，报酬也不错，而且你会在 explaining ideas 方面进步_非常_多。而在交谈中清楚地 explain 一个 idea 的能力，是一种适用范围_惊人地_广的 skill——我在日常生活中一直都会用到它。

主要区别是，让他们保持 active 要容易得多，也更容易恰当地 adapt pace 和 difficulty。基本上，把我那篇讨论如何[从交谈中 learn](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations) 的文章里的所有 ideas 反过来用就行了

-   _关键_ technique 是请 student 向你 paraphrase 你刚才说过的内容

    -   这会迫使他们保持 active，并 process information

    -   它能 identifies errors，并帮助你 correct 它们

        -   往往你接着就能 recognise th

    -   它有助于为他们 mind 中正在发生的事 build 一个 model

    -   它帮助你 calibrate difficulty 和 pacing

    -   如果你是 tutor，却不让 student 这样做，我觉得你就错过了一个_很大_的、不费什么力气就能得到的好处

        -   向朋友 explaining ideas 时，这也特别有效，不过可能显得有点不礼貌

-   让 student 告诉你，你刚才所说内容中的 key points/ideas 是什么

-   让 student generate examples，尤其是 typical examples

-   在这里，understanding question 背后的 mindset _更加重要_。他们提出 question 时，你都应该_始终_这样做，尤其是当他们似乎不满意你的回答时。

Maths 的 Teaching

-   人们很容易 neglect tacit information——也就是 intuitions、underlying concepts、motivations。这_糟透了_。要把 maths 教好，最重要的部分之一就是 convey 这个 high-level overview

-   每个 proof 都可以被 heavily compressed。大多数 proofs 都有一些 key ideas，接下来就是 repeatedly doing the obvious thing。“Repeatedly doing the obvious thing” 必然会在 student 的 mind 中被 compressed，所以你根本不必把它说出来，_只需_给出 key ideas

-   Examples，尤其是 motivating examples，是_极其_重要的。如果脑海中没有一个 clear motivating example，要 learn 一个新 concept 真的非常、非常难。

    -   Examples 能很好地 teach tacit knowledge——它们 illustrate 你能做什么、不能做什么，以及你为什么要在意这些 ideas

-   在 learning rigour 一段时间后，你最终会拥有 [post-formal](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/theres-more-to-mathematics-than-rigour-and-proofs/) intuitions：这时，你大多会 ignore rigour，而是 think intuitively，但必要时也能转入 rigour。maths 中的大部分 cognitive labour 都在于达到这一点，而好的 teacher 会尽可能多地提供 post-formal intuition

-   Maths，尤其是 pure maths，往往是在 formalising 一个 intuition。Probability 是对 uncertainty 的 formal study。Groups 是对 symmetries 的 formal study。Topology 是对 continuous deformations 的 formal study（也就是不会 rip 或 glue 的那些变化）。指出这一点至关重要

    -   找到这些的一个好办法，是留意这个 topic 让你新近能够 answer 哪些 questions。这是为内容提供 motivation 的好办法！

-   Diagrams _棒极了_

-   往往，你刚开始能够 answer 某一类 question 时，依靠的是大量 tacit knowledge，而大家期望你通过 examples 自己领会这些。通常，其中的 80% 可以用一个 explicit algorithm 来 captured——这是让 tacit knowledge 变得 legible 的好办法。

Applied Rationality/Life Advoce 的 Teaching

-   相比 explicit，这些内容涉及 tacit knowledge 的程度要大_得多_，所以应该采用 workshop format，并把重点放在 exercises 上

    -   要 Emphasise，所有内容都是高度 personal 和 subjective 的——所有 ideas 都应该根据你的 mind 和你的处境来 adapted

    -   让大家两人一组，是促使他们真正做 exercises 的有效办法！

-   理想情况下，把 tacit knowledge 提炼为一个 rough algorithm，并交替进行 explaining steps 和让 students practice 这些 steps

-   class 的 impact 主要在于 students retaining key insights 和 mental habits——也就是从内在感受上，“我应该 apply 这个 idea 的时候”是什么样的。_这_就是他们会 retain 的那一小部分 information。因此，你的职责是把 idea 提炼为这些 habits，把这一点 explicitly 说出来，并通过 structure 这个 class 来 reinforce 它们

-   很大一部分 impact 来自 students 在 class 之后 taking action——这很难！你需要 emphasise 这一点，并 minimise barriers

    -   留出时间，让 students 为 apply 这些 ideas 的方式 generates lists，以及这些 ideas 如何与_他们_的日常生活 relevant

    -   留出时间，让他们为 class 之后要采取的 actions 设置提醒

-   让它_感觉_ actionable——人们很容易觉得某个 idea 很重要，却又觉得它是 abstract 的。比如，觉得 [prioritisation](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation) 是个好 idea，却始终没有真正去做。

    -   要 Emphasise 这个 idea 如何 fits into 日常生活和日常 problems

    -   给出_大量_如何 use 它的 examples——这可以 form connections，比如“哦！我从没想过还能把它用在那件事上”

        -   这就 conveys 了关于_何时_ use 这个 idea 的 tacit knowledge！

-   要 Emphasise relatability 和 importance——给出没有 applied 这个 technique 而导致糟糕处境的 examples，让人产生 visceral 的感受，并觉得 relatable

Conclusion（结语）

如果你打算将来 teaching 某些内容，希望这些想法对你有用！但即便没有这个打算，我也觉得这些 skills 能非常好地 transfer 到日常生活中的 explaining。而且，思考 teaching 能让你成为有效得多的 learner。

我发现，作为 student，我常常可以通过提出恰当的 questions，帮助 teacher 教得更有效——问他们哪些 information 最重要，通过 paraphrasing back 来 checking 自己的 understanding 是否 correct，以及请他们讲讲 motivations 和 higher-level picture。“某些东西没能很好地 fitting into 我的 knowledge graph”这种感觉，可以变得相当 visceral。而意识到 students 的哪些 habits 会 hinder 他们 learning，比如保持 passive 而不是 active，以及不尝试自己进行 information compression，也能帮助我 recognise _我自己_什么时候没能做到这些！

## 原文

Introduction

This is a blog post on how to teach things well. I’ll mostly be focusing on forms of teaching that involve preparation and structure, like talks and tutoring, but these ideas transfer pretty broadly. I think teaching and explaining ideas is an _incredibly_ important skill, and one that [most people](https://www.cam.ac.uk/) aren’t great at. I’ve spent a lot of time practicing teaching ideas, and I think I’ve found a bunch of important ideas and approaches that work well. I’m giving a talk next week, so I’ll initially focus on how to give good talks, but try to outline the underlying concepts and high-level ideas of teaching. And then talk about how these can transfer to contexts like tutoring, and to teaching specifically maths or applied rationality - the main areas I have actual teaching experience with.

Note: I mostly care about teaching concepts and ideas, and teaching things to people who genuinely want to learn and be there, so my advice will focus accordingly.

I think it’s useful to think about good teaching even if you don’t intend to spend much time teaching - learning and teaching are flip sides of the same process. I’ve found that even when in the role of a student, understanding what good teaching looks like can often fix a lot of the shortcomings of a bad teacher!

Framing

The key insight of this post is that good teaching requires you to [be deliberate](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/be-deliberate), and keep the purpose in mind: **learning is a process of information compression**. When you’re learning something new, you essentially receive a stream of new information. But human cognition doesn’t work by just storing a flood of information as is. The student takes in the information stream, extracts out the important ideas, converts it to _concepts_, and stores those in their mind. This is a key distinction, because it shows that the job of a teacher is _not_ to give the student information, it’s to **get the student to understand the right concepts**. Conveying information is only useful as a means to an end to this goal.

In practice, it often works to just give a stream of information! Good students have learned the skill of taking streams of information and converting it to concepts. Often this happens implicitly, they student will absorb and memorise a lot of data, and over time this forms into concepts and ideas in their head automatically. But this is a _major_ amount of cognitive labour. And a good teacher will try to do as much it as possible, to let the student focus their cognitive labour on the important things.

My underlying model here is that we all have a web of concepts in our minds, our **knowledge graph**. The collection of all the concepts we understand, all of our existing knowledge and intuitions, connected together. And you have learned something when you can convert it to concepts and **connect it to your existing understanding**. This means not just understanding the concept itself, but understanding where it fits into the bigger picture, where to use it, etc.

The final part there is key - if the student leaves with a good understanding of the ideas in the abstract, but no idea when to think about the ideas again, it’s no better than if they’d learned nothing at all. We call on our knowledge when something related triggers, so in order for a lesson to be useful, you _need_ to build those connections and triggers in the student’s mind.

A key distinction to bear in mind is ideas being **legible** vs **tacit**. A legible idea is something concrete that can easily be put into unambiguous words, eg how to do integration by parts. While tacit knowledge is something fuzzier and intuitive, eg recognising the kinds of integrals where you’d use integration by parts in the first place - essentially the intuitions you want the student to have. This is a good distinction to bear in mind, because legible knowledge is _much_ easier to convey, but often your goal is to convey tacit knowledge (at least, it should be!). And there’s a lot of skill to conveying tacit knowledge well, and making it as legible as possible without losing key nuance. And different techniques work better for the two kinds. A lot of my issues with the Cambridge maths course is an extreme focus on legible knowledge over tacit - the underlying intuitions and motivations.

How to teach

There are two key problems when teaching, that any good teaching advice must account for:

-   Limited ways to convey information

    -   The ideas I’m teaching are stored as implicit concepts in my mind, but in order to convey them, I must translate them into language. This language maps to ideas in _my_ head according to all of my implicit knowledge and worldview, but translates into the student’s head according to _their_ implicit knowledge and worldview. This often creates errors

    -   And converting concepts to language is _inherently lossy_, ideas have a lot of tacit nuance that is hard to capture

    -   Essentially, words can only convey legible knowledge, and I need to figure out how to hack this to convey tacit knowledge. Or how to find alternate information channels

    -   Alternately, I need to have error checking mechanisms to _notice_ when I’ve failed to convey something well.

-   [Typical Mind Fallacy](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense)

    -   A _key_ part of learning is combining knowledge you hear with the ideas already in your head.

    -   But I only have access to what’s in _my_ head, not what’s in the student’s. And by definition this is different - I already understand what I’m teaching!

    -   This is a crippling blow to my ability to teach, and I need to be constantly aware of this and trying to build models of what’s in the student’s heads, and how they receive what I’m saying.

Here are some of the most important tools I have for addressing these problems:

-   High-level picture

    -   The student’s knowledge graph is _big_. So the first, and most important part, is identifying which part of the graph they should add these ideas to

    -   Thus you should _always_ highlight where this fits in to the bigger picture. Which questions are we currently trying to answer? Why is this idea interesting at all? Where can the student use this? What are its limitations?

    -   This should _always_ be the first thing when introducing a new idea

-   Prioritising information

    -   Learning is information compression. This fundamentally means that the student needs to be paying selective attention. When learning something new, the [Pareto Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle) always applies - 80% of the importance lies in 20% of the ideas.

    -   Identifying this 20% is significant cognitive labour, because it’s not immediately obvious. They need to pay attention to _everything_ and later filter.

    -   But, the question of “what matters here” is tacit knowledge that I already have! This talk is high labour for the student, but easy for me. Thus, the most important thing a teacher can possibly do is to highlight what matters and what doesn’t, to tell the student where to focus their attention.

    -   In practice, you should _always_ be saying “this is really, really important” or “these are just fiddly details” or “this is a bit of a niche edge case” etc. It is _extremely hard_ to do this too often.

        -   And give more _time_ to the important things. If you say an important point, write it down and put a box around it. Explain _why_ it’s important and how it fits into the bigger picture. Give an alternate explanation, or an example.

        -   This is really easy to miss if you think of teaching as information _transfer_ - where your goal is to tell the student everything and hope they figure out what to pay attention to themselves.

    -   Another tool: Have frequent summaries, highlighting the key points in the previous section.

        -   This is further useful to highlight _connections_. Some ideas will fit into their knowledge graph more easily than others, and pointing out connections can leverage the easy connections to make hard ones easier

    -   I’m a big of the advice “say what you’re going to tell them, tell them, and say what you’ve just told them”, I think it’s a good way to implement this principle

    -   The fundamental principle behind this is that students retain a tiny fraction of what they hear. If you give a one hour talk, they’ll retain maybe a few minutes worth of content. This is a fundamental fact of the learning process, and the only thing you can do about it is to _control_ what they retain. Focus their attention on the important parts

        -   This mindset helps me _identify_ what’s important. Set a 5 minute timer, and write down everything important that you want people to retain. These are the key points around which your talk should be structured!

        -   Everything else you say should be intended to help these key points stick better - to highlight connections between them and to existing knowledge, to ensure good information transfer, and to convey the tacit knowledge underlying the key points

    -   Another key skill of prioritisation is _cutting things_. If one part is irrelevant, or it’s boring and fiddly, cut it! It’s painful to not talk about everything cool, but you have limited time - if you don’t actively prioritise, you aren’t avoiding trade-offs, you’re just ceding control to “whatever you leave last”

        -   Anyone who’s gone to a talk by me knows that I have yet to internalise this lesson

    -   If there’s one point you retain from this post, let it be this one - this is _incredibly_ important, and the main mark of a good teacher vs a mediocre one

-   Understand pre-requisites

    -   A consequence of the Typical Mind Fallacy, is that it’s super easy to forget that your students don’t have all the context you have! This manifests as people teaching ideas without the pre-requisites.

        -   The underlying idea: the ideas you teach are in _your_ knowledge graph, and are built upon existing ideas - these are the pre-requisites. You need to figure out whether the students

    -   A common secondary mistake is to understand pre-requisites, and then try to explain all of them!

        -   A good framing here is [inferential distance](https://wiki.lesswrong.com/wiki/Inferential_distance#:~:text=Inferential%20distance%20is%20a%20gap,person%20trying%20to%20understand%20it.). The inferential distance of a new idea is the number of steps of new concepts someone must understand before they can understand the new idea. Eg, to teach a young kid about the quadratic formula, they first need to understand the idea of polynomials, for which they need to understand algebra - this is three inferential steps.

        -   A general rule of thumb: _never_ teach things with more than 2 inferential steps. An idea just learned is shaky, and doesn’t yet have good connections built. It’s very, very hard to anchor new ideas onto new ideas.

    -   This is _really hard to get right_. Pre-requisites require you to have a good model of somebody else’s mind. A useful technique is often to do a practice run on someone from your target demographic, and ask them to flag everything that confuses them.

    -   Further, for groups, this can be an intractable problem, everyone has different prior knowledge. You need to have a clear picture in your head of who the talk is aimed at.

-   Students should learn actively, not passively

    -   It’s _really_ easy for a student to just passively sit in a stream of information, taking none of it in. This achieves neither of your goals, because to form connections, compress information and connect it to their knowledge graph, they need to be putting in _some_ cognitive labour.

    -   Good ways to encourage this: explicitly telling them that this is important, giving exercises and time to think through them, asking the audience regular questions and giving them some time to think.

        -   Question asking has the failure mode where most people won’t volunteer, or just zone out a bit - I lack great solutions to this problem

    -   This is _much_ easier to handle in smaller settings, I’m bad at handling it in talks. The main solution I have is just to be engaging and keep the pace going well.

        -   Often people will zone out, so having regular breaks, and check-points of “if you weren’t following, we’re changing topic so it doesn’t matter” can help to rectify this

            -   Even short, 30s-120s breaks can be helpful! Encourage people to get up and stretch.

            -   Breaks never _feel_ important, but they really, really help

-   Give examples

    -   Examples are an _insanely_ powerful tool for teaching things well, and people rarely use them enough. I have never given a class with too many examples (and believe me, I’ve tried)

        -   “You can teach a class with no content, only examples; you can’t teach a class with only content, no examples”

    -   Why examples are awesome:

        -   Examples are an excellent way to resolve lossy information transfer - they’re a completely different channel of communication than normal. If nothing else, they serve as an error check

        -   Examples are a great way to transfer tacit knowledge, without necessarily making it legible - this is what it means to build intuition

        -   Examples can help fit things in to the bigger picture, they can motivate the ideas, and locate where they fit in to the student’s knowledge graph

        -   By giving the student a pool of motivating examples, they can often _generate_ the ideas themselves by generalising from the examples

        -   Examples can bridge the gap from “understanding the knowledge in the abstract” to “understanding where to _use_ these ideas, and where they should come up”

    -   How to use them?

        -   Often when giving a point, I’ll give a micro-example to give context to it - eg “sometimes straight lines aren’t enough to model data, eg with a quadratic”. This should be quick and effortless, the example should make immediate sense to the students, with 0 inferential steps

            -   (I can’t believe it’s taken me 18 posts to get to my first nested example :( )

        -   Examples can be good at the start, to motivate things and show the questions we’re trying to answer. It can be good to give an example, and then constantly refer back to it as we generalise the example into a concept

        -   After introducing a complex idea, go through a long example and illustrate which parts of the example embody the complex idea

        -   Use examples to illustrate the importance and relateability of an idea - eg if explaining how to think about good planning to students, give an example of a student with a deadline crisis that they missed - everyone relates to this

    -   Examples contain a _lot_ of information, so the idea of information prioritisation applies strongly here - tell the students what to pay attention to in the example, and why it’s interesting

-   Visual information and diagrams

    -   Often tacit knowledge manifests as a literal picture in my head - draw this!

    -   This is another good alternate communication channel

    -   Our visual memory and processing is often much better than our abilities with language - this can work well for clarified confusing and complex parts

-   Pacing

    -   An easy mistake that I often fall into is to give a section the amount of time it takes me to say it. I convert the ideas into words, and just read through what I’ve come up with.

        -   This is the fallacy of viewing teaching as giving an information stream! Time should be allocated for people to process and compress information, and they need more time for hard parts and less time for easy parts

    -   This is _hard_ to get right intuitively - when you understand an idea, it feels easy!

    -   A good trick: make a high-level summary, and rate each section out of 5 for difficulty. Then go and explicitly give more time to those sections - eg add more intuitions, say the key ideas more, give more examples

        -   Note - pacing doesn’t mean the speed at which you speak, it’s about the time you give to different ideas!

        -   Note that difficulty =/= importance. If one part is hard, but unimportant, cut it. Or give a brief overview, explain the important idea, and say “don’t sweat the details”

    -   Another trick - explicitly tell students which ideas are important and worth paying attention to, and which aren’t

    -   If doing a practice run (which you totally should), regularly check in with the test audience about pacing - **the default state of the world is that you get pacing wrong**

    -   It's _key_ to get pacing right - people zone out in slow, easy sections, and get lost in fast, hard sections. Your job as the teacher is to keep as many people as possible absorbing information at the optimum rate.

    -   It’s very hard to give accurate time estimates for things - my trick is to have a bonus section at the end which I’ll cut if need be, and to pace in the moment according to my existing notes and my intuitions

-   Understand the _mindset_ behind a question

    -   When somebody asks a question, the default response is to answer it. This is a failure to be deliberate! The student asks a question because they’re confused about something, and your goal is to resolve that confusion - answering the question directly is just a means to an end.

        -   This is an important distinction, because often questions are _weird_. They’re confused, or don’t quite make sense, or are asking about unimportant things. This manifests, for me, as the student’s mind [not making sense](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-14-minds-should-make-sense). And it’s easy to get frustrated, or just to answer the dumb question directly. But this is ineffective.

            -   A related effect - somebody asks a question that isn’t the _real_ question they want to ask. Eg, a student at a university open day who asks “how many A-Levels did you do?”, when what they really care about is “how many A-Levels do _I_ need to do to get in”

        -   Your goal should be to **understand the state of mind from which that question made sense** - once you’ve done this, you can often resolve the confusion directly, or answer the question they really care about.

            -   Do this by asking clarifying questions, trying to answer and saying “did that answer your question?”, giving them several interpretations of what they’re _really_ asking and asking whether any resonate, paraphrasing the question back to them, etc.

            -   The main trigger to look for here is a note of confusion - the question feeling a bit off, or out of nowhere, something isn’t quite making sense.

            -   It’s a delicate balance between doing this and moving on with the talk - try to gauge whether many people share the same confusion, if not, just move on

    -   Often doing this can uncover **Pedagogical Content-Knowledge**, common ways that people misunderstand the ideas you’re teaching. It’s super valuable to collect these, because then you can recognise them in future and dissolve them directly.

-   Illusion of transparency

    -   A consequence of the typical mind fallacy - it’s easy to _think_ you’ve clearly communicated knowledge when you really, really haven’t. As a consequence, you need to put a lot of effort into being grounded and calibrated - because often a confused audience won’t _feel_ like a confused audience to you.

    -   Ask questions! Especially ones that highlight the key ideas, eg “how to do easy thing X” or “what was the key idea in here?”

    -   Do hand-polls - ask people to indicate their understanding by putting their hand up high if it’s clear, and low if it’s less clear. This is a good technique, because most people will actually do it, unlike “does anyone have any questions?” or “is this making sense to people?”

-   Seek feedback and iterate

    -   Teaching is _hard_. You’re fundamentally trying to convey tacit knowledge, via lossy and low bandwidth communication channels, into an alien mind that you have very limited access to. **The default state of the world is that you suck at this**

    -   The solution is to regularly ask for specific, actionable feedback and calibration, and to actually put meaningful effort into updating on this! Feedback is one of the main ways you can better understand the mind of a student.

Teaching 1-on-1

Practicing tutoring and explaining things one on one can often be more valuable! I think a great use of time for most students is to do tutoring - it’s pretty fun, you get paid decently, and you get _way_ better at explaining ideas. And the ability to explain an idea clearly in a conversation is an _amazingly_ applicable skill - I use this all the time in daily life.

The main difference is that it’s a lot easier to get them to be active, and it’s much easier to adapt the pace and difficulty well. Essentially, invert all of the ideas in my post on how to [learn from conversation](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/mini-blog-post-5-how-to-learn-from-conversations)

-   The _key_ technique is asking the student to paraphrase what you’ve said back to you

    -   This forces them to be active, and to process information

    -   It identifies errors, and helps you to correct them

        -   Often you can then recognise th

    -   It helps build a model of what’s going on in their mind

    -   It helps you calibrate the difficulty and pacing

    -   If you’re a tutor who doesn’t do get the student to this, I think you’re missing out on a _major_ free win

        -   This is also super effective when explaining ideas to friends, though can seem a bit rude

-   Get the student to tell you the key points/ideas in what you’ve said

-   Get the student to generate examples, especially typical examples

-   Here, understanding the mindset behind the question is _even more important_. You should _always_ do this when they ask a question, especially if they seem dissatisfied with your answer.

Teaching Maths

-   It’s easy to neglect the tacit information - the intuitions, underlying concepts, motivations. This is _terrible_. One of the most important parts of teaching maths well is to convey this high-level overview

-   Every proof can be heavily compressed. Most proofs have some key ideas, followed by repeatedly doing the obvious thing. “Repeatedly doing the obvious thing” will inevitably be compressed in the student’s mind, so you should skip saying it at all, and _just_ give the key ideas

-   Examples, especially motivating examples, are _incredibly_ important. It’s really, really hard to learn a new concept without having a clear motivating example in mind.

    -   Examples teach tacit knowledge well - they illustrate what you can and can’t do, and why you care about ideas

-   After learning rigour for a while, you’ll end up with [post-formal](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/theres-more-to-mathematics-than-rigour-and-proofs/) intuitions, where you mostly ignore rigour and think intuitively, but can drop into rigour if need be. Most of the cognitive labour in maths is reaching this point, and a good teacher will try to give as much of the post-formal intuition as possible

-   Maths, especially pure maths, is often formalising an intuition. Probability is the formal study of uncertainty. Groups are the formal study of symmetries. Topology is the formal study of continuous deformations (things which don’t rip or glue). Pointing this out is vital

    -   A good way to find these is to notice which questions the topic newly lets you answer. This is a great way to motivate things!

-   Diagrams are _awesome_

-   Often you begin being able to answer a type of question with a lot of tacit knowledge, and are expected to pick all this up with examples. Often 80% of this can be captured in an explicit algorithm - this is a great way to make tacit knowledge legible.

Teaching Applied Rationality/Life Advoce

-   These are _much_ more about tacit knowledge than explicit, so these should be done in a workshop format with a big focus on exercises

    -   Emphasise that everything is highly personal and subjective - all ideas should be adapted to your mind and your circumstances

    -   Pairing people up works well for getting them to actually do the exercises!

-   Ideally, boil down the tacit knowledge to a rough algorithm, and alternate explaining steps and getting the students to practice them

-   The impact of the class is mostly students retaining key insights and mental habits - what the “time when I should apply this idea” feels like from the inside. _This_ is the small fraction of information they’ll retain. Thus it’s your job to boil down the idea to these habits, say this explicitly, and structure the class to reinforce them

-   Much of the impact comes from the students taking action after the class - this is hard! You want to emphasise this, and minimise barriers

    -   Give time for the students to generates lists of ways to apply the ideas, and how they’re relevant in _their_ day to day life

    -   Give time for them to set reminders for actions taken after the class

-   Make it _feel_ actionable - it’s easy to think an idea is important, but for it to feel abstract. Eg, to think that [prioritisation](https://www.neelnanda.io/blog/prioritisation) is a good idea, but to never get round to it.

    -   Emphasise how the idea fits into everyday life and everyday problems

    -   Give a _lot_ of examples of how to use it - this can form connections like “oh! I never thought of using it for that”

        -   This conveys the tacit knowledge of _when_ to use the idea!

-   Emphasise relatability and importance - give examples of a bad situation where the technique wasn’t applied, and make it feel visceral and relatable

Conclusion

If you’re planning on teaching something in the future, I hope these thoughts were useful! But even if not, I think these skills transfer excellently to explaining things in everyday life. And that thinking about teaching can make you a much more effective learner.

I find that often, as a student, I can help the teacher be more effective by asking the right questions - asking them which information is the most important, checking that my understanding is correct by paraphrasing back, asking them for the motivations and higher-level picture. The feeling of “something not fitting into my knowledge graph well” can be made into a pretty visceral one. And realising the habits of students that hinder them from learning, like being passive instead of active, and not trying to do information compression themselves, can help me recognise when _I_ fail to do those things!
