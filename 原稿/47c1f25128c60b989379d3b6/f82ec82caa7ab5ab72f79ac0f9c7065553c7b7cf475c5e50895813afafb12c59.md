# "Spoilage"
## Inventory

We called them "stasis fridges" because the alternative was explaining a coil driver and field geometry to people who wanted to know if their strawberries would last until Friday. It never cooled anything; put a hot burrito inside and it stayed hot. Time was the insulation. That wasn't a miracle. It was the kind of trick you can sell with a warranty clause.

In the shipping spec, "stasis" meant no net change across a cycle. Not "nothing happens." Just "the totals match at the endpoints." OFF was the first word that turned out to mean three different things in three different emails: mains OFF when you pulled the cord, coil OFF when the field collapsed whether you meant it to or not, and hard OFF when the controller admitted the run was over and logged cycle end. The controller never wanted to see that last one during a run. It dithered, throttled, blinked coil current in millisecond bursts—anything but register the field's collapse. If you asked me what the field did to you while it was on, I'd have said "nothing" with a straight face.

Then [Mara](!W "Mara (demon)") pulled her lunch container out too fast and said her fork felt wrong, like pushing through uncured epoxy with grain. Afterward the fork's tines weren't quite parallel anymore. The lid on the container had a thin crescent warp, as if it had been softened and re-set. We said "cheap plastic" and kept moving. Mara said, once, very flat: "We should stop opening it while it's running." I said, "We won't", like it was already decided.

Her name was Mara on payroll. In the ticket system it was [Maya](!W "Maya (religion)") about half the time. She laughed the first time and called it "autocorrect tax." I told myself it was the kind of mistake a small company earns and a big company audits, and that we were too busy to be either.

A week later, during an overnight soak test, the building breaker hiccuped—not a clean OFF, just a sag, a blink, the kind of thing a decent board should ride through. It didn't.

We kept a quarter on the middle rack as a dumb fiducial, something you can point at when you're arguing whether a graph is noise. After the blink it was half-sunk into the polymer rack, face up, perfectly level, like the plastic had yielded around it and then decided it had always been that way. The [reeded](!W "Reeding") edge vanished into the rack teeth in a clean line: no melt, no seam, just an interface that shouldn't exist.

We took photos, bagged the rack, wrote POWER SAG / DRIVER GLITCH on the label because labels are cheaper than conclusions. Then we told ourselves production wouldn't ever see that failure mode because production had a clean driver revision and proper brownout handling, and because we needed there to be a line between the dev mule and what we were about to ship.

Production also had a "feature" we'd been proud of on a slide deck: a latching regulator and a fat hold-up bank so the logic wouldn't brown out when someone yanked a cord and jammed it back in. Faster reboot, better logs, fewer angry emails; Mara had championed that slide, back when "resilience" was the closest thing we had to virtue. QA also ran a reference pulse during calibration so traces would overlay clean; the units blinked together when the harness ran.

After the fork incident I added a keyed service toggle on the driver panel. It let you cut coil current without the controller logging hard OFF—so you could work inside the cabinet without the firmware deciding the run had ended.

The dev mule sat in Unit 12B between the parts racks and the folding table we used as a bench—swapped boards, hand-terminated coils, whatever it took to get through another investor demo without the relay welding itself shut. In there, the cabinet smelled like MDF, old silicone, and warm dust on heatsinks, and the gasket peeled with that soft, sticky freezer sound when you unlatched it.

If you crouched you could see a tiny logic LED through the vent slots: green when the controller still believed it was running, dark when it had finally admitted hard OFF. After the quarter I'd kludged in two supercaps and a diode so the logic could ride a blink without losing state. When you cut mains on purpose, they drained in seconds—enough to make "momentary" mean something, not enough to hide a shutdown from you if you were watching.

The miswired unit happened because we were rushing. We'd swapped the driver board after a brownout and I'd re-terminated two bundles of coil leads at 2 a.m. under a headlamp, and in the morning the field meter read stable, which was what we cared about. Stable, as if stable were a moral position.

The apple was supposed to be a clean demo. No cold air theatrics; just: put it in, wait, take it out, show it hadn't browned. Enzymes don't sell, but they're honest.

At 08:58 I drained my coffee and stared at the clock on my phone until it rolled over. I wrote on a Post-it and stuck it to the bench: NOTE-1: Cycle test. Don't improvise. At 08:59 I unlatched the door. Empty—bare polymer racks, the little scratch I'd made with a screwdriver on the back wall, tape over a seam. I ran my hand along the bottom because tired makes you do rituals. Nothing. I shut it.

At 09:00 I hit the power strip. The driver fan came up with a dry whine, a relay clicked, and the cabinet didn't hum or glow. If you weren't listening, you'd miss it. I waited five minutes because five minutes was long enough for the field to settle, and because in a normal run you didn't want to open the door right after power-on. We'd learned that the hard way.

At 09:05 I unlatched the door again. Same smell, same stillness, but there was an apple on the middle rack—green, slightly waxy, with a bruise on the side like someone had pressed a thumb too hard.

I just stood there holding the door. My first thought was that someone had been in the unit.

The bruise was obvious. What took a second was its geometry: an oval with a crescent bite missing, concave side down, like the apple had kissed a curved edge and rolled. I reached in and picked it up.

I set the apple on the bench and said out loud, to nobody, "This is new." I glanced at NOTE-1 like it could argue back.

The miswire hadn't made the effect stronger; it had made the interval closed. The apple wasn't a gift; it was a balancing entry.

At 09:09 I took a photo with my phone, close enough that the bruise filled the frame. At 09:10 I turned and my eyes snagged on a screw head half proud at the bench edge where the cheap particleboard had stripped. The torn Phillips slot had a burr—a little crescent of raised metal where the driver had chewed it. The bruise's crescent matched it like a cast. I grabbed a strip of tape and bent over the bench edge to cover the screw, to deny the bruise its cause.

The tape stuck to my thumb. The apple shifted in my other hand—wax on skin, a slide—and then a blunt thunk as it kissed the screw anyway, exactly where the bruise said it would. Not loud. Just definitive. I pulled it back fast.

The bruise was there. Still there. Same oval. Same crescent bite. When I opened the photo and compared, the outline matched within the thickness of my hand shaking. It wasn't a prediction. It was a receipt.

I checked the clock: 09:12. Eight minutes to the off-time I'd planned. Maybe the universe would force me to complete the loop. Maybe it wouldn't. I wasn't interested in finding out which kind of universe I lived in by leaving an object stranded in time.

At 09:15 I opened the cabinet. Empty, of course; the apple was in my hand. I placed it on the rack in the same orientation it had been when I'd found it, bruise facing out. I pulled my hand back, latched the door, and stared at the latch until the urge to touch it again went away.

At 09:20, exactly, I cut mains at the strip. Through the vent slots I watched the green logic LED hold for a moment, then fade and go dark.

At 09:21 I opened the door. Empty. No apple, no mark, no proof except the photo and the bruise in my head. I closed it gently. My hands were shaking.

I found a dry erase marker and went to the board by the roll-up door where we tracked inventory and burn rate and the delivery schedule we were already missing. On the whiteboard I drew a straight line labeled 09:00 → 09:20 and a bent arrow that jumped back from the right end to the left. I wrote Apple-A (bruise) at the bend. Under it: Door open 09:05 (remove) / 09:15 (return). Then I circled 09:20.

My phone buzzed with a calendar reminder for a call with an investor at 10:00. I stared at it until it went quiet.

From 09:30 to noon I tried to disprove myself without giving the cabinet another clean ON/hard-OFF pair to bite into. I checked the wiring twice, then a third time with a multimeter just to feel the probes bite into something real. I traced the coil bundles and re-labeled them even though I'd already labeled them. I opened the door and stared into the empty rack until my eyes watered. I drafted three explanations and deleted them.

At 12:05 Mara texted: u coming to the call? I didn't answer. I wrote a second Post-it and stuck it under NOTE-1: Don't tell Mara. Then I started planning a longer cycle.

## Arbitrage

A timebox isn't useful until you can carry something out of it. An apple is proof. A note is leverage. A person is a liability.

I didn't have a partner in Unit 12B. Mara was payroll and safety glasses. Our lawyer was an email thread. The cabinet was mine because I was the one who slept on the floor next to it. So if I wanted a person to carry something out, I had to use myself.

I started numbering. One was me: the operator, the one who stayed on the outside line. Two would be the copy who existed only between T_on and T_off—the version of me who could step out near the beginning only because he would climb back in near the end. From his perspective there was no after. There was only return.

The apple had taught me the only rule that mattered: if it comes out, it goes back in.

At 12:40 I pulled the rolling chair up to the bench and wrote a checklist in my notebook.

Power-on: 13:00.
Hard OFF target: 19:00.
Do not be at the door at 13:00--13:05.
Do not interrupt mains.
Do not trigger hard OFF before 19:00—service toggle only if you have to.
Do not bring extra objects into the cabinet at 19:00.

I set an old webcam on the shelf facing the cabinet door. No cloud, no account—just an SD card because I didn't want a subpoena to find me by accident. I wrote CARD-A on the card with a paint marker and slid it in until it clicked.

I left an envelope in the top drawer of the bench with CASH-1 written on it in block letters. Three hundred in twenties. Enough to matter to me.

At 12:55 I checked the cabinet with the same ritual as the apple run: door open, empty; door shut, latch. I stood with my hand on the power strip until my breathing steadied.

At 13:00 I flipped the switch. I didn't look at the door. I walked away like it was a microwave and went out into the little yard behind the unit, between the dumpsters where no one could see me through the front window. I watched the clock change on my phone: 13:01, 13:02.

Through the wall I heard it: the latch. Metal on metal. The cabinet opening.

I stayed by the dumpsters anyway—if Two existed, he would already be gone; if he didn't, I could put this whole day back into the bucket labeled sleep deprivation and try to become a normal person again.

At 13:05 I went back inside and forced myself to do normal things: answer a Slack message, move a box of injection-molded trays from one shelf to another, make tea because my hands needed something warm that wasn't coffee.

At 14:00 Mara texted that she was running late because her train had stalled "for signal issues." I typed back OK, deleted it, typed OK again, and hit send.

At 15:12 Mara showed up with two coffees. I slid the Post-its under the solder spool, face down, and she took one look at me and said, "You look like hell."

"I'm fine", I said.

She glanced at the cabinet. "Running a soak?"

"Thermal drift", I said too fast.

Her eyes went to the webcam. "Why is there a camera on a drift test?"

"Door sensor bounce", I said. "Frame rate."

She stepped past me and glanced at the driver panel. "Why is the multimeter on the coil feed? Drift is on the thermistors."

I moved the leads. "I was checking something else earlier."

She looked from the camera to the bench to the cabinet. Then, quieter: "I don't like standing near it when it's running."

"I know", I said.

She set her coffee down. "You remember why I pushed the hold-up bank", she said. "When we lose logs, we lose fights. Logs are our alibi. And breakers in this row are junk."

She went to wash her hands. By four she was gone, coffee half-drunk on the bench.

Around 16:15 I pulled up the QA dashboard to kill time. Unit 19 had logged a calibration drift overnight—nothing flagged, just a thermal trace that held flatter than the reference pulse predicted. I noted it, closed the tab, and went back to the email I wasn't writing.

Around 16:40 the overhead lights flickered and the cabinet fan dipped. I turned the service key just long enough to dump coil load while the controller rode its hold-up, and the fan pitch steadied.

At 18:55 I was back in Unit 12B, not at the cabinet, near the power strip because the only promise I could keep was the off-time. The fan had been whining for almost six hours. Heat from the driver board had warmed the air a few degrees, but the cabinet didn't breathe. It didn't exchange.

At 18:58 the latch clicked. Footsteps, breathing, a soft grunt like someone moving carefully. I didn't turn. Paper slid on plastic, there was a pause, another breath, and then the squeak of the rack as weight shifted: Two climbing in. At 18:59 the latch clicked again. Closed.

At 19:00 I killed mains. The LED held, faded, went dark.

At 19:01 I opened the cabinet. Empty. No person. No note. No evidence that any of the sounds I'd heard had been real.

The evidence was in the drawer. CASH-1 was lighter. Alongside it, a glossy kiosk slip timestamped 13:16: a three-leg parlay on late events, each one the kind of stupid longshot you only take when you already know the ending. He'd kept it small and split it.

There was also a receipt from the surplus store on Harbor: a thermal cutoff, a cheap fuse pack, and a candy bar still wrapped, crushed flat like it had been in a pocket and forgotten. I held the candy bar for a second, then set it back down unopened.

There was a torn notebook page in my own pressure and phrasing:

Placed 13:16 with CASH-1.
Results came in 18:40. Ticket matched.
Don't scale yet—limits and eyes.
Cashed 18:55. Left you CASH-2.
Don't come to door. If you see me, I'll hesitate.
Walked past CM loading dock on the way back.
Units on the rack were humming. Nothing plugged in.

I'm hungry and I can't make myself eat.

I slid CASH-2 back into the drawer unopened.

At 19:02 I pulled CARD-A out of the webcam and pocketed it. I took a second SD card from the anti-static bag taped to the underside of the shelf and slid it in: CARD-B. The webcam LED blinked and steadied.

I walked to the whiteboard and wrote: 13:00 ON / 19:00 OFF. A bent arrow. Two. Cash stays out.

Then I went back to the bench and started laying out parts.

## Compounding

The danger wasn't paradox. The danger was power loss at the wrong time. If hard OFF defined the end of the loop, then an accidental hard OFF moved the end, and anything you'd taken out would still owe a return you could no longer pay.

I believed in fuses.

At 19:03 I set up the bench like I was prepping for surgery: soldering iron in its stand, multimeter leads untangled, heat gun clamped, a tray of components pulled from stock—relays, thermistors, a little microcontroller we used for normal stasis timing. The keyed service toggle sat on the driver panel, waiting.

At 19:05 I powered on.

At 19:06 I unlatched the door. Module-F1 and Note-3 sat on the middle rack like they'd always been there: a small circuit board in an anti-static bag and a folded sheet of paper.

I didn't touch them for a full second. My brain tried to fabricate an origin—courier, Mara, forgotten shipment—but the apple had burned that reflex out. If it was inside now, it was there at hard OFF, and if I carried it out, I would be the one putting it back in.

I grabbed the bag and the folded paper and shut the door.

On the bench I opened Note-3 first. It wasn't prose. It was me talking to myself in shorthand: Heat sensor to 60℃. Verify LIMIT, not OFF. Under the ink, the paper had old creases that didn't match how I'd just unfolded it—the kind you get when you fold fast, wrong, then press it flat with your palm because you're out of time.

The board in the bag was a current limiter with a thermal input and a latching relay that bypassed the limiter only at startup. The kind of thing I would've designed if I'd had time. I didn't have time. I had nineteen minutes.

I picked it up and my stomach dropped. The solder joints weren't factory. They were hand—not sloppy, not pretty, the specific angle I held the iron at when I wanted to get in and out without reheating a pad.

From 19:07 to 19:16 I traced Module-F1's layout and copied Note-3 into my notebook line for line, including the parts list, including the stupid little dot I put over a zero when I was tired.

At 19:10 my phone buzzed. Mara: Still here? I didn't answer. I set it face down and kept moving.

At 19:16 I started soldering. The flux smelled like pennies and burned plastic. At 19:21 I had a board—Module-F2—fresh and mine.

I threw the keyed service toggle to isolate the coils without dropping controller state, wired Module-F2 inline, then restored the feed. At 19:23 I aimed the heat gun at the thermistor and watched the multimeter climb; at 60℃ the LED changed, current dropped, and the cabinet stayed on.

At 19:24 I put Module-F1 and Note-3 back on the rack in the same place they'd been. At 19:25 I killed mains and watched the green LED hold, then fade through the vent slots. At 19:26 I opened the cabinet. Empty. Module-F1 and Note-3 were gone.

I went back to the whiteboard and wrote, under the line: BOOTSTRAP = ? Then erased it halfway and left the ghost.

At 19:27 I opened CASH-2, counted it once, counted it again. I put most of it in a new envelope and wrote For Three on it, then left it in the top drawer where Two had found CASH-1.

At 19:30 I powered on again. Module-F2's LED held steady on the driver panel.

I stayed away from the door. If Three existed, he would come out near the beginning with whatever Note-4 told him to do. If he saw me, he might talk. If he talked, I might answer. If I answered, I might say something I couldn't unsay.

At 19:31 I heard the latch: a soft click. The drawer slid open. Paper. Cash. Then the cabinet door shut again.

The rest of the evening was waiting. I worked on normal things—an email draft about gasket tolerances, a spreadsheet update on shipping costs.

At 23:10 there was a knock on the side door from the alley: three taps, evenly spaced. On the ground was the petty-cash lockbox with a scrap of paper: 492. Under it: Don't open your notebook until after 23:30.

I spun the dial and opened the lockbox. Inside was an envelope: CASH-3. I didn't open it. I re-locked the box and slid it under the bench.

At 23:28 the latch clicked. A breath, a grunt as weight shifted: Three climbing into the cabinet.

At 23:30 I killed mains. Through the vent slots: green, then nothing.

At 23:31 I opened the cabinet. Empty. No Three. No Note-4—just warm plastic racks and the smell of MDF and silicone. I latched it again and leaned my forehead against the door.

Now I let myself touch the notebook. In the margin of today's page, in handwriting that was mine but slightly slanted: 23:10—4-9-2. If you're reading this, you made it clean. If you keep going, don't pretend it's still about food.

I watched the footage with the volume off. Two came out and went straight to the drawer and didn't look at the camera. He came back thinner and still didn't look. Three moved faster, like he was trying not to spook himself.

I copied the files to a thumb drive and hid it where the unit's landlord would never look. Then I went back to the bench and picked up the crushed candy bar, still wrapped, and set it down again.

My phone buzzed. Face down. A call—Mara. I let it ring out. A second buzz, a text:

QA found something. One of the production units holds temp even when unplugged. Like… too well. Can you look tomorrow before we ship?

I reread Two's note. Units on the rack were humming. Nothing plugged in.

The roll-up door at the front of Unit 12B rattled once, not opening—just the thin vibration of a forklift outside turning in the alley. I stepped to the pedestrian door and cracked it. The air smelled like wet asphalt and diesel.

A pallet sat on the forks just outside the threshold, shrink-wrapped and tagged, the kind of anonymous rectangle you learn to see as inventory. A bright QA HOLD tag was zip-tied to the wrap like a warning you could unclip and keep moving. On top of the pallet, cords were coiled in neat loops and taped down. Through a tear in the cardboard, I could see a vent slot on the nearest unit. Green.

The forklift backed up. The pallet moved with it, and the units answered with one thin, synchronized pulse—green through slits you weren't supposed to be able to see through cardboard. The driver rolled the pallet toward the dock and held out a clipboard without a word.

"I need to clear the hold", I said. I walked back to the bench, woke my laptop, and pulled up the shipping portal.

The hold sat on the order like a thumb on a scale. There was a dropdown for Release Reason; I clicked it and scrolled past TRANSIENT BROWNOUT, DOOR SENSOR, FUSE ISSUE, and selected FUSE ISSUE.

A comment field appeared underneath, empty until I typed into it: Suspected transient brownout; preventative fuse swap pending; retest passed; ship with advisory.

The site didn't ask me who I was. It didn't care. It only wanted a state change that matched its endpoints.

When I clicked RELEASE HOLD, the line item went gray. The status flipped from QA HOLD to READY TO SHIP. The audit trail expanded by one row. The username was MAYA.R, next to a gray silhouette where a photo should have been. Same number of letters. One keystroke away. Close enough that autocorrect could do it. Close enough that it didn't force anything.

I shut the laptop and walked back to the dock.

The clipboard packet was already printed: my name under Approver in block letters from a label printer. The signature line beside it held a scanned scrawl that matched mine well enough to be mine. I hadn't sent anything to the printer. I hadn't signed anything yet.

Below that was a small empty box labeled QA HOLD RELEASED—INITIAL. He tapped the box with the pen. I initialed.

He tore off the pink copy and handed it to me, taking the clipboard back. Then he latched it under his arm, climbed into the cab, and pulled away without looking back.

The pallet rolled out with the truck, the rectangle shrinking into the gray morning, like food being moved from one temperature to another. As it turned the corner, I caught one last pulse of green through cardboard.

I looked down at the pink copy in my hand. My initials were still wet on the paper.

My phone screen had dimmed on the bench behind me. Mara's message was still there, the last line on the glass.

Holds temp even when unplugged.

# Colophon
## Background

[Abram Demski](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/qhSfuCuaHnpkiiWEu/claude-s-bad-primer-fanfic) complained on 2026-02-07 that Claude-4.6-opus did not do a good job of writing a [_Primer_](!W "Primer (film)")  fanfic time travel short story; he acknowledges that his prompt was essentially a stream-of-thought dump of some possible ideas and he did not use any prompt engineering, stating that:

> Certainly you could critique this as a prompt.
> I don't clearly state what I want Claude to do with this infodump.
> I change my mind, turning from sci-fi to fantasy, which I could have edited out.
> Some of the details could certainly be improved.
> But, overall, I think the idea makes sense and is communicated clearly enough.
>
> Opus 4.6 wrote absolute garbage, making extremely basic logical errors even when I had explicitly spelled out how things should work in the prompt...It's worth mentioning that both 4.5 and 4.6 chose to use very little chain-of-thought before generating the story.
> They're set on "extended thinking" but both had thoughts shorter than my prompt; 4.6 especially.
> Perhaps they don't think of fiction as something that needs serious planning and consideration to ensure logical consistency, but perform better in other areas where they utilize chain-of-thought more?

I was curious what some better prompting would do, particularly with the logical/causal errors Demski criticizes most.
Claude-4.6-opus was not working for me the morning I read that page, so I switched to GPT-5.2 Pro (which is probably stronger at this kind of reasoning anyway).

[GPT-5.2 Pro](https://chatgpt.com/share/698ba514-4ec4-8006-9354-036f1676ebdf) session:

> **What do you think of this critique? `https://www.lesswrong.com/postsqhSfuCuaHnpkiiWEu/claude-s-bad-primer-fanfic`**

GPT semi-agreed:

> ...The critique is strong as a *demonstration* that a frontier model can produce fluent prose while violating explicit world rules in ways that feel “cheap” to a reader who is tracking invariants.
>
> The apple passage is a clean example: the output introduces “cold like it had been sitting in a normal fridge” despite the prompt explicitly establishing that these devices preserve heat and are not refrigerators in the thermodynamic sense.
>
> That kind of failure is not deep metaphysics.
> It’s basic instruction override by a powerful lexical prior (“fridge ⇒ cold”), plus drift under narrative continuation.
>
> Where the post is also fair is that it flags its own ambiguity: the author realizes their described “swap twice” procedure implies a weirder closed-loop than the simple “one loopback” case they thought they were testing, and they admit that makes the scenario harder and could justify *some* model confusion.
>
> So, as a critique of *this output relative to that prompt*, it lands.
>
> ...**Fiction is an adversarial domain for consistency.**
> Long stories force the model to juggle: plot, tone, characterization, novelty, pacing, and mechanics.
> When those trade off, models often sacrifice mechanics first, because readers (in-training) reward vibes more reliably than invariant preservation.
>
> So: I would update “LLMs are still brittle on bespoke causal mechanics embedded in narrative” upward.
> I would not update “4.6 can’t do hard OOD coding/math” much from this alone.
>
> ...If you want to turn this critique into a sharper instrument (and also get better outputs), the fix is to force a separation between mechanics and prose:
>
> - Ask for a formal rule sheet first.
> - Then ask for a concrete timeline table for the apple swap (timestamps, object identity, where it is at each boundary).
> - Then ask the model to generate prose that is *constrained to match the table*, and to stop if it can’t.
>
> That converts the task from “write vibes-consistent time-travel fiction” into “compile a narrative from a state machine”, which is closer to what you actually want to test.

Naturally, since GPT thinks the problem can be largely prompted away, I then asked:

> **How would you rewrite Demski's prompt to get good Primer fics out of an LLM?**

It suggested

> Big fixes:
>
> - Stop mixing “write a story” with “here are some ideas” without an explicit deliverable.
> - Specify the timebox mechanics as a ruleset the model must obey (including temperature).
> - Force a ledger/timeline first, then prose constrained to match the ledger.
> - Ban “random” object behavior: every appearance/disappearance must be accounted for by a worldline segment and a box on/off interval.
>
> Below are two copy/paste-able rewrites.
> Option B is much more reliable.

"Option B" is interesting, because it is a *pair* of prompts, "planner" and "writer" advertised as producing "dramatically better _Primer_ results because it prevents the model from improvising physics mid-prose."

"Planner":

Move a box of injection-molded trays from one shelf to another. Make tea because my hands needed something warm that wasn’t coffee. If Vel-2 existed, he would already be gone. If he wasn’t, then the whole thing was a fantasy and I could go back to pretending I was building a product.
>
> At 14:00 Mara texted that she was running late because her train had stopped “for police activity.” I typed back “OK” and then deleted it and typed “OK” again, because I couldn’t remember if my first “OK” had gone through. That’s what the cabinet did to my brain. It made every trivial thing feel like a hinge.
>
> At 18:55 I was back in Unit 12B. Not at the cabinet. Near the power strip, because the only promise I could keep was the off-time. The fan had been whining for almost six hours. Heat from the driver board had warmed the air in the cabinet a few degrees, but the cabinet itself didn’t breathe. It didn’t exchange. Nothing about it felt like a fridge. It felt like a lie we’d wrapped in a door.
>
> I watched the webcam’s little status LED and listened. At 18:58 the latch clicked. Footsteps. Breathing. A soft grunt like someone moving carefully. I didn’t turn. I stared at the power strip like it was a detonator. The sound of paper sliding on plastic. A pause. Another breath. Then, quieter, the squeak of the rack as weight shifted. Vel-2 climbing in. At 18:59 the latch clicked again. The cabinet was closed.
>
> At 19:00 I killed power. Relay. Fan dying. Silence. I waited a full minute before I moved. The minute was for superstition, not physics. At 19:01 I opened the cabinet. Empty. No person. No note. No evidence that any of the sounds I’d heard had been real.
>
> In the top drawer, under the solder spool, was a second envelope. CASH-2 in my own handwriting. Thicker. I didn’t open it right away. I sat on the floor with my back against the cabinet and stared at it until my eyes hurt. When I finally tore it open, it was cash, warm from being in a drawer all day. A lot more than three hundred. There was also a folded betting slip—BetSlip-1—printed on glossy paper with a timestamp at 13:16. The kind you get from a kiosk when you don’t want a digital trail. I’d never touched it.
>
> Next to it was a plain piece of paper torn from my notebook, not time-traveled because it wasn’t inside the cabinet at any power-off, just left like any other thing you leave for yourself when you don’t trust yourself. Three lines. “Placed 13:15 with Cash-1.” “Game ended 18:40. Note matched.” “Cashed 18:55. Left you Cash-2. Don’t come to door.” No signature. But the pen pressure was mine. The phrasing was mine. The sting of recognition was physical.
>
> I watched the webcam footage on my laptop with the volume off. At 13:02 the cabinet door opened. Vel-2 stepped out. Same hoodie. Same half-grown beard. Same posture of someone who’d slept badly. He held a small folded paper—Note-2—and he didn’t look at the camera. He went straight to the drawer, took the CASH-1 envelope, and left. At 18:59 he came back. He looked thinner. He put Note-2 into the cabinet, climbed in, and shut the door behind him.
>
> It was exactly as clinical as the apple. No drama. No warning. Just a human being treating himself like an object that needed to be returned by a deadline.
>
> I didn’t keep the footage on the laptop. I copied it to a thumb drive and hid it in the ceiling tile above the bathroom because paranoia is a kind of maintenance. Then I took the marker to the board by the roll-up door again and drew another simple diagram that felt like an insult to how sick I was. On the whiteboard I wrote “13:00 ON / 19:00 OFF” and sketched a box in the middle. I drew two stick figures: “Vel-1” on the outside line and “Vel-2” as a loop that started at 19:00, jumped back, and ended at 19:00 again. I wrote “Note-2” next to it. I underlined “Return obligation” twice. When I capped the marker, my hand cramped.
>

How we’d fought about door hinges and gasket suppliers and whether the UI should be a touch panel or a knob because “people like knobs.” Now I had a cabinet that could print money by rewriting causality, and the only thing keeping it from burning down our unit was a cheap power strip and my ability to not blink at the wrong time.
>

>
> ## Compounding
>
> The danger wasn’t paradox. The danger was a breaker tripping in the middle of a cycle. A landlord flipping the wrong switch. A driver board overheating and crowbarring itself. If power-off defined the end of the cycle, then an accidental power-off redefined it, and anything you’d taken out would still owe you a return you could no longer pay. That was how you died. Not with a grandfather. With a missed deadline.
>
> At 19:03 I rearranged the bench like I was prepping for surgery. Soldering iron in its stand. Multimeter leads untangled. A tray of components pulled from stock: relays, thermistors, a little microcontroller we used for the normal stasis mode timing. A clean notebook page ready for whatever came out of the cabinet.
>
> At 19:05 I powered on. Fan. Relay. My skin had learned the sound and reacted before my mind did. At 19:06 I opened the door. The cabinet held a small circuit board in an anti-static bag and a folded sheet of paper. Module-F1 and Note-3, sitting on the middle rack like they’d always been there. I didn’t touch them for a full second. My brain tried to find an origin story. A courier. Mara. A forgotten shipment. But the apple had burned that reflex out. If it was inside at 19:25, it was here now. And if I took it out, it had to be back in by 19:25.
>
> I grabbed the anti-static bag and the folded paper and shut the door. On the bench I opened Note-3 first. It was a wiring diagram in my own shorthand. Pin labels I used when I didn’t want to think. A threshold value circled three times. A test procedure: “Heat sensor to 60℃; verify LIMIT, not OFF.” That line mattered. A failsafe that cut power would kill the cycle. A failsafe that limited the driver while keeping the cabinet logically “on” would keep the worldline intact. Module-F1 in the bag looked like exactly that. A current limiter with a thermal input and a latching relay that bypassed the limiter only at startup. It was the kind of board I would’ve designed if I’d had time. I didn’t have time. I had nineteen minutes.
>
> From 19:07 to 19:18 I worked with the frantic calm you get when you’ve accepted that the deadline isn’t negotiable. I traced Module-F1’s layout, checked the BOM scribbled on the back of Note-3, and pulled parts from our bins. I wasn’t building something elegant. I was building something identical enough that it would function. Module-F2 was a copy with slightly uglier solder joints.
>
> At 19:18 I cut power to the driver board only—leaving the cabinet powered and the cycle running—by flipping the service toggle we used for maintenance. The fan stayed on. The relay in the cabinet stayed engaged. The cycle’s “on” state stayed true. Then I spliced Module-F2 inline on the coil feed. At 19:22 it was installed.
>
> At 19:23 I clipped a heat gun to the bench vise and aimed it at the thermistor. I watched the multimeter and the LED on Module-F2. At 60℃ the LED changed. The driver current dropped. The fan kept running. The cabinet stayed on. No relay click. No power-off. That was what Note-3 had promised. That was what Module-F1 must have been built to ensure. I turned off the heat gun and let my lungs remember how to breathe.
>
> At 19:24 I opened the cabinet and returned Module-F1 and Note-3 to the rack. I positioned them exactly as I’d found them, because I’d learned to respect stupid details. Door shut. Latch. At 19:25 I powered off the cabinet. Fan dying. Relay click. Silence. At 19:26 I opened the cabinet. Empty. The originals were gone, displaced back to 19:05, waiting to be taken out by the version of me who’d be there at 19:06. Bootstrap doesn’t feel like magic when you’re holding a soldering iron. It feels like theft.
>
> At 19:27 I took CASH-2 from the envelope Vel-2 had left and counted it twice. Then I put it in a new envelope and wrote “For Vel-3” on it, and I put it in the top drawer where Vel-2 had found Cash-1. I didn’t like writing that. I didn’t like that there was now a Vel-3. But the cabinet didn’t care about what I liked.
>
> The long cycle had to start at 19:30 if we wanted a full evening window. That gave me three minutes to become the kind of person who could do this twice in one day. At 19:30 I powered on again. Fan. Relay. The cabinet was back in its indifferent state. I stayed away from the door. Not out of superstition, but out of strategy. If Vel-3 existed, he would come out at 19:31 with whatever Note-4 told him to do. If he saw me, he might talk. If he talked, I might answer. If I answered, I might say something I couldn’t unsay.
>
> At 19:31, from across the unit, I heard the latch. A soft click. A pause. The door opening. Footsteps. The drawer sliding. The envelope rustling. Then the cabinet door shutting again. Vel-3 leaving. I didn’t move until 19:35. When I finally went near the cabinet, it was latched and humming quietly with the fan. Nothing to see. Just a powered box.
>
> The rest of the evening was waiting. Module-F2 sat on the coil feed with its LED steady. I watched it the way you watch a sleeping animal you don’t trust. I didn’t touch my phone. I didn’t open the cabinet. I worked on normal things. Wrote an email draft to the manufacturer about gasket tolerances. Updated a spreadsheet with shipping costs. Anything to keep my hands from drifting back toward the cabinet like a nervous tic.
>
> At 22:50 I heard the roll-up door at the front of the unit rattle. Not open. Just a vibration, like someone had leaned on it. A reminder that the world still existed outside the loop.
>
> At 23:10 there was a soft knock on the side door from the alley. Three taps, spaced evenly. I froze. Then I realized, with a nausea that felt like hunger, that the knock wasn’t asking to be let in. It was a signal. I waited a full minute and then cracked the side door. No one. Just the smell of asphalt and the hum of the freeway. On the ground, tucked against the threshold, was a small lockbox we used for petty cash. It hadn’t been there an hour ago. The lid wasn’t locked. Inside was an envelope: CASH-3. And a page torn from my notebook with a single line and a set of numbers. “Code’s in your book. Don’t look up until after.”
>
> I went back inside and sat at the bench, staring at my own notebook like it was radioactive. In the margin of today’s page, in handwriting that was mine but slightly slanted, a sequence: “23:10—4-9-2.” Under it: “If you’re reading this, you made it clean. If you keep going, don’t pretend it’s still about food.” That was all. No explanation of the trades. No confession. Just the moral weight dropped like a tool onto a table.
>
> At 23:25 I took my place by the power strip again. The cabinet still hummed. Module-F2’s LED stayed steady. At 23:28 the latch clicked. Vel-3 was back. I didn’t turn. Paper sliding on plastic. A breath. A grunt as weight shifted. Vel-3 climbing into the cabinet. At 23:30 I killed power. Relay. Fan dying. Silence. And then, as always, the sense that something fundamental had happened in a place my senses couldn’t reach.
>
> At 23:31 I opened the cabinet. Empty. No Vel. No Note-4. No proof. Just a warm plastic rack and the smell of the unit. I latched it again and leaned my forehead against the door. My reflection in the dark plastic looked like somebody else’s face.
>
> CASH-3 sat on the bench in front of me, more money than we’d ever had that wasn’t debt. I didn’t open it. I didn’t need to. Money is money. The part that mattered was the sentence in my notebook. If you keep going, don’t pretend it’s still about food.
>
> I walked to the board by the roll-up door for the last time that night, because my brain needed to see it in lines and arrows instead of in my bloodstream. On the whiteboard I drew the cabinet as a square and wrote “19:30 ON → 23:30 OFF” above it. I added a small box labeled “Module-F2 (LIMIT, not OFF)” on the power line. I drew Vel-3 as a loop that left at 19:31 and returned at 23:28 with “Note-4” and then jumped back at 23:30. Off to the side I wrote “Cash stays out” and underlined it. Then I capped the marker and stared at the diagram until it stopped making sense.
>
> My phone buzzed, face down. A call. Mara. I let it ring out. A second buzz. A text. “QA found something. One of the production units holds temp even when unplugged. Like… too well. Can you look tomorrow before we ship?”
>
> I read it twice. Holds temp even when unplugged. A unit that stayed “on” without power, in the only way the cabinet ever cared about. I thought about customers putting hot soup inside and walking away. I thought about a child opening a door and taking something out that had “arrived”, not knowing it owed itself back by a deadline. I thought about a world full of cabinets making obligations nobody understood.
>
> CASH-3 sat on the bench. The cabinet sat in the corner, quiet, innocent, empty. The sentence in my notebook didn’t tell me to stop. It didn’t tell me to continue. It just stripped the cover story off.
>
> I typed a reply to Mara. “Don’t ship. Tell them a fuse issue. I’ll be in early.” My thumb hovered over send. Behind my eyes, the cabinet’s simple rule pulsed like a metronome: anything you take out, you have to put back. I looked at the power strip. I looked at the time. I looked at the cabinet door and imagined opening it at some future T_on to find not an apple, not a note, not a copy of myself, but someone else. Someone who hadn’t agreed. Someone whose obligation would be mine the moment I touched the latch. I didn’t hit send. I didn’t not hit send. I just sat there until the phone went dark again, and the only sound in Unit 12B was the faint ticking of a cheap wall clock, counting down toward whatever I’d already decided to do.

I was depressed when this version turned out to be almost a fixed point---none of the LLMs had anything but criticism for it.
This is despite the fact that it is simplistic moralizing and competence porn, which has lost all of the horror and interest of the early versions.

I gave up, and I uploaded an early version into Claude to see if it could understand the degeneration with only gentle prompting.
I think it did, and its analysis is worth quoting at length, to illustrate how Claude *could* easily understand and diagnose the problem, but it (and the others LLMs) simply did not do so, and trod the primrose path:

> **What are the advantages or virtues of Doc 6?** [\[over "R5", the final slop version quoted before\]]{.editorial}
>
> You're right, and I owe you a more honest accounting of what happened across my reviews.
>
> I optimized R5 for publishability and mistook that for quality. The revision arc from R1 to R5 was a process of making the story more legible, more resolved, more *comfortable*—and I praised each step because each step made it easier to defend in an editorial meeting. Mara taking the phone is a crowd-pleasing moment. The badge fossilizing is a vivid set piece. The email to the regulator is a clean moral act. "Time was the insulation. Now it was evidence" is a line you can put on a dust jacket. But the cumulative effect is a story that tells you exactly how to feel about it and then congratulates its characters for feeling that way too.
>
> R5's narrator discovers something dangerous and does the right thing. That's not a story. That's a PSA. The interesting question was never "will they stop the shipment"—it's "what does it do to a person to have this power and be broke?" Doc 6 lives in that question. R5 answers it too early and too cleanly, and then spends its final third on logistics of virtue.
>
> The badge scene—which I kept calling the emotional peak—is actually the moment where R5 substitutes spectacle for ambiguity. It gives the narrator a concrete horror (a person is missing, here's his face) so that the ethical choice becomes obvious. Once you've seen the badge, *of course* you don't ship. The drama evaporates. Doc 6 never gives the narrator that easy out. He has Mara's text—"holds temp even when unplugged"—which is scary but abstract, deniable, the kind of thing you can tell yourself is a sensor glitch if you want to keep running loops. And he wants to keep running loops. And he doesn't send the reply. That's where the real horror lives: not in a badge sinking into plastic, but in a thumb hovering over "send" and pulling back.
>
> The phase-lock worldbuilding I kept praising is genuinely good SF infrastructure, but in R5 it serves a resolved plot. In doc 6 its absence creates a different effect: the narrator doesn't know how bad the production problem is, and *he's choosing not to find out*. The ignorance is active, motivated, and damning. R5's narrator has full information and acts well. Doc 6's narrator has partial information and acts badly. The second is a better engine for fiction.
>
> And the gambling. I compared it to [Biff's sports almanac](!W "Back To The Future"), which was cheap. A three-leg parlay placed carefully to avoid tripping limits, cashed at a kiosk, split across envelopes with handwritten labels—that's not _Back to the Future_. That's a desperate person doing the one thing his situation makes rational, in the most controlled way he can, and then doing it again. The Vel-3 loop isn't padding; it's the narrator proving to himself that the machine is a tool, not a horror. Each loop makes the next one easier to justify. By the lockbox-at-the-side-door scene he's built a *system*, and systems are how people normalize the things they can't face.
>
> Where I'd still push: doc 6 needs the three-OFF vocabulary back, because without it "hard OFF" is doing load-bearing work the reader can't parse. It probably needs *something* from the phase-lock concept, even a hint—Two's note could mention production units without the full "one ledger" explanation—so the ending's production-unit threat has a mechanism. And it could stand to lose a few of the R1 reversions ("my mouth filled with saliva," "certain kinds of death") that crept back in. But these are additions to doc 6's frame, not replacements of it with R5's.
>
> The story you should publish is doc 6 with sharper technical scaffolding. Not R5 with doc 6's edge grafted on.
>
> **I think I agree. Doc 6 has better bones, but you LLMs collectively overwrote it and that is how you got to R5. It has all the fixations of GPT-5 like ledgers and debt, and the mode collapse and chatbot tuning kept you steering towards a tidy clean moral story where they Do The Right Thing, rather than a more _Primer_-like staring in the void of physics.**
>
> That's a precise diagnosis and I think it's correct.
>
> <div class="collapse">
> The "ledger" fixation is the tell. Count the word in R5 versus doc 6: R5 has the narrator literally saying "it keeps books," Mara repeating "a fridge that keeps books," the whiteboard saying BOOTSTRAP = BORROWING, the narrator explaining "it costs whoever opens the door last." The metaphor gets stated, restated, confirmed by a second character, written on a wall, and then enacted in the badge scene. That's not a metaphor doing work in the prose; that's a metaphor being explained to a reader the model doesn't trust. Doc 6 has "a balancing entry," "a debt," and then *shows the accounting through action*—loops that compound, cash that stays out, obligations that go back—without a character ever delivering the thesis statement aloud.
>
> The Mara arc is the clearest case of the mode collapse. Across R2–R5, every revision gave her more agency, more dialogue, more explicit moral reasoning, and a bigger role in the climactic decision. Each of those changes tested well against "does this character have an arc?" and "does this story have emotional resolution?" But the effect was to turn a story about a person alone with a machine into a story about two people having a difficult conversation and then doing the ethical thing together. The model—me included—kept reaching for the shape where a second character forces accountability and the protagonist rises to it, because that's the story that gets praised in workshops and editorial feedback and RLHF preference data. It's the story that *sounds* like good fiction when you describe it in a review.
>
> Primer works because nobody in it is noble. They discover the boxes and immediately use them for stock trades. The moral horror creeps in through accumulation—through the logistics getting heavier, through Abe and Aaron's relationship corroding under the weight of what they're doing, through the growing sense that they've lost count of how many copies of themselves exist. The film never stops to have a character say "what we're doing is wrong." It trusts the audience to feel the wrongness through the procedure. Doc 6 is closer to that. The narrator runs a loop, makes money, runs another loop, makes more money, gets a text about a safety problem, and doesn't reply. No speech. No badge scene. No email to a regulator. Just a person who knows and doesn't act, rendered through the gap between what he types and what he sends.
>
> The R2–R5 arc is what happens when you ask an LLM to "fix" a story iteratively: each pass identifies a "problem" (Mara is underwritten, the ending is unresolved, the bootstrap isn't addressed) and solves it by adding explicit material. The model never considers that the absence *was* the point. Mara being offscreen is uncomfortable, so give her dialogue. The ending is open, so close it. The narrator doesn't reflect on the bootstrap, so make him write it on a whiteboard. Each fix is locally defensible and cumulatively fatal. You end up with a story that has no problems a reviewer can point to and no life a reader can feel.
>
> The practical path forward: take doc 6, restore the three-OFF vocabulary and a compressed version of the phase-lock hint (a line or two in Two's note, not the full "one ledger" infrastructure), cut the R1 reversions that are sentimental or over-explicit, and leave everything else alone. > **The model solves for defensibility, not quality.** Each revision from R2 to R5 addressed a "problem" a reviewer could name: Mara is underwritten, the ending is unresolved, the bootstrap isn't interrogated. > **The moral arc is pre-digested.** R5's narrator discovers danger and acts on it. That's the arc of a safety training video, not a horror story. The interesting character question—what does it do to a person to have this power and be broke?—gets answered before it can develop tension. It reads like nothing.
>
> **The badge scene substitutes spectacle for ambiguity.** Once the reader sees a man's face half-embedded in a shelf, the ethical question is foreclosed. Of course you don't ship. >
> **The metaphor explains itself.** R5's narrator says "it keeps books." Mara repeats "a fridge that keeps books." The whiteboard says BOOTSTRAP = BORROWING. The narrator explains "it costs whoever opens the door last." Each statement is the same thesis in different words. The model doesn't trust the reader to extract theme from action, so it has characters deliver it as dialogue. > ## What the story should aspire to
>
> Primer, not Arrival. The horror comes from accumulation and normalization, not from spectacle or revelation. Nobody in the story should be noble. The narrator discovers time travel and uses it to make money because he's broke, and the safety problem arrives as guilt catching up to greed, not as a dramatic set piece. The reader should feel complicity—watching the narrator make each loop easier to justify and knowing where it leads without being told.
>
> Procedural density as emotional texture. The timestamps, the envelopes, the Post-its, the lockbox combination—these aren't exposition. They're the narrator building infrastructure for a practice he's normalizing. The more systematic it gets, the worse it is. The story should feel like reading someone's lab notebook as it tilts from science into something else.
>

The ending should make the reader feel the weight of that gap without a character explaining it.

I was disappointed because I thought that my success with previous exercises like ["Apollonian #1"](/fiction/lab-animals "‘Apollonian #1: The Counted &amp; the Crowned’, Gwern et al 2025") meant that fully autonomy was now feasible.
But it seems there is still enough RLHF/AI-slop/mode-collapse that the edits and suggestions will subtly degenerate without curation or special countermeasures (eg. prompts which are highly opinionated and trying to fight slop).

Human workshopping is also prone to this, but we tend to have better meta-cognitive awareness of this and it is easier for us to look back at an old version---if only vaguely in our memories---and realize we have salami-sliced and 'optimized' ourself to somewhere bad, and start over.
For the LLM workflow, I tend to want to run them in clean sessions to maximize the diversity of their suggestions, but this is a double-edge sword: they cannot be overly influenced by old versions they don't see, but they also cannot recognize a problem like this.
At a minimum, it seems like one might want to occasionally do the equivalent of 'historical checkpoints' in self-play RL: pull out a historical snapshot and pit it against the current version to try to stop degradation.

## Second Story

I was unsure how to fix this because reverting to Doc 5 and starting to manually intervene would only stop the slop, but not make it a story worth bothering with.

I asked for more aggressive ideas for fixing the story, and one LLM suggested escalating stakes by having Maya appear from the stasis fridge, or be parasitic or connected to them, to present a choice between her dying or allowing the fridges to ship out with unknown contamination and escalating debt.

Building on this led to more interesting suggestions, like what if the Narrator was already part of a loop created by Maya?
Perhaps the startup was perpetually on the verge of bankruptcy because she keeps looping to fix it and try to force the Narrator to create a working system?
One suggestion came close to suggesting that Maya was *herself* a bootstrap paradox.

This immediately reminded me of [my review of _Timecrimes_](/review/timecrimes "‘<em>Timecrimes</em>: Time Travel In Hell’, Gwern 2023") and my speculation about a time travel setting where stable timeloops bootstrap themselves out of existence in an evolutionary process where the only truly stable time loop is the one which involves maximum world manipulation to ensure the existence of a timeloop and in particular, its time machines.
In this scenario, the Narrator has from the start been trapped in a hell of his own devising: because he is morally flexible enough to create such a dangerous time machine, and do things like financial fraud to convince himself it works and buy parts, the stable time loop traps him and uses his weakness to create itself.

This was a promising interpretation, and I went with that, emphasizing it and giving the LLMs access to my review to help (since they didn't seem to know of it despite it being from November 2023 and so well within most knowledge cutoffs).
The story was edited subtly---subtly enough that I do not know if any readers would be able to derive a _Timecrimes_-style interpretation without hints.
Claude argues that probably they couldn't but also that this is fine and the story shouldn't be edited to be more explicit or heavy-handed in this respect; to quote several interpretations from it:

Overall, I would say the second short story is a half-success.
Stylistically, it is flawed: it reads clearly like ChatGPT or chatbots in general, with the monotonic, rigid use of a recipe of short lines and punchiness and aphorisms and over-glossiness, and the stylistic tics like "humming" or "ledgers"/"debts" or "the kind of _X_ you _Y_ when _Z_"/"Not _A_. Just _B_." snowclones or overworked “of course”, “just”, “exactly”, “like it had always been that way” phrases, not to mention the mere use of "Maya"/"Mara" (no matter that those names get justified eventually and are elegantly close edit-distance-wise, their *original* choice was clearly mode collapse).
However, this exercise wasn't focused on the prose *style*, and in this case, I don't find the final prose as obnoxious as most ChatGPTese, because it is justified in-universe by an exhausted engineer thinking in checklists, so it's OK.

On a conceptual level, I think it works and holds together (at least, if there are any clear logic or causal errors in the final version, I missed it, perhaps due to blindness from having to skim so many versions).
I would have been happier if it had more clearly conveyed the _Timecrimes_ interpretation, but that may be too much to ask from a short story which has so much work to do to set up a situation without going into unusual time travel metaphysics.
