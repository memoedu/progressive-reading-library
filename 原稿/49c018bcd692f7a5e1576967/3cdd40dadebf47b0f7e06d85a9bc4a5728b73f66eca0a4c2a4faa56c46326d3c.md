Google’s Wikipedia-killer, [Google Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol "Knol"), has passed some anniversaries; in January 200917ya, it reached its 6-month anniversary. 6 months is a fairly good time-period to look back—long enough that the hype has died down, and for big things to have been done (8500 hours is a long time online).

How had it done by then? Faster than [Citizendium](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2011/10/five-year-old-wikipedia-fork-is-dead-in-the-water/ "Citizendium turns five, but the Wikipedia fork is dead in the water"), even, it was dead. As a [long-time Wikipedia editor](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-resume "'Wikipedia Résumé', Gwern 2010"), one might question my objectivity. So we’ll see what a relatively trusted third-party, _[Ars Technica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Technica "Ars Technica")_, [says](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2009/01/google-knol-six-months-later-wikipedia-need-not-worry/ "Google Knol six months later: Wikipedia need not worry"):

> What happened to Knol? Announced by Google in late 200719ya and launched in July 200818ya, the site was meant to bring more credible (read: not written by anonymous Wikipedians) “knowledge units” to the web, and it would allow the authors to cash in on their work. But it’s 200917ya, and Knol appears to be notable largely for its non-notability…Now the bad news: no one’s reading the site, and it’s awash in poor content.
> 
> As for the quality of the content, Google’s attempt at monetizing (both for itself and for its authors) the Knol entries has had a perverse effect. While it has attracted plenty of detailed commentary from learned professionals, it’s drowning in plenty more that is basically spam, plagiarism, or a stub, thrown up in the apparent hope of making some quick cash. (Though because of point number one, that’s not happening, either.)
> 
> Take “Barack Obama”, for instance. A search for his name brings up 809 entries; since most Knol users appear to write their own entries rather than add to others (for which no compensation is forthcoming), the proliferation of entries is inevitable. And it’s not at all clear that the best ones are rising to the top.

## [It’s Larger Than Wikipedia Was?](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#its-larger-than-wikipedia-was "Link to section: § 'It’s larger than Wikipedia was?'")

At six months, Wikipedia only had a few hundred contributors and ~6,000 articles; but Knol had ~100,000. Surely this demonstrates that Knol has met with some sort of success?

Well, Knol ‘knols’ do not correspond to Wikipedia articles. Knols repeat themselves. Worse, they’re often spam, copyvios, repetitive. The true count of knols that are original to Knol and unique is probably very low—quite possibly down to ~6,000.

### [Growth Is Important](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#growth-is-important "Link to section: § 'Growth is important'")

But getting bogged down in article count omits a major metric, and perhaps the most important metric: rate of growth. Wikipedia at 6 months could claim 6,000 articles, but what’s important is that just 5 months (a little less than twice as) later, the article count had _more_ than doubled to >13,000. This was the knee of the curve that would lead to Wikipedia’s >3 million articles (for up-to-date statistics, see [Special:Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Statistics "Special:Statistics")).

### [An Equal Comparison Is Not Equal](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#an-equal-comparison-is-not-equal "Link to section: § 'An equal comparison is not equal'")

Another profound point is that if Knol is _only_ equal to Wikipedia, then it is a worse model than Wikipedia.

We should expect more of Knol than of Wikipedia at similar stages! Knol has, by virtue of its position in time, _numerous_ advantages over [Nupedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nupedia "Nupedia")/Wikipedia. We should expect a lot more.

It has:

1.  A clear license regime. Thanks to 8 years of [Creative Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Commons "Creative Commons"), the choice is not limited to just the problematic [GFDL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Free_Documentation_License "GNU Free Documentation License").
    
2.  8 years of hardware advances; ~5 iterations of Moore’s law.
    
3.  8 years of wiki development, demonstrating dead ends, the good ideas, & what remains to be improved. Imagine if Knol had to start with the state-of-the-art in 200125ya. It would be truly gruesome. (Anyone looked at the very old Wikipedias in Nostalgia, or old [Usemod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UseModWiki "UseModWiki") wikis like [Ward’s Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WikiWikiWeb "WikiWikiWeb [C2, Ward’s Wiki]")? They’re hideous and unusable! They make me quite grateful for 200917ya MediaWiki with all its modern conveniences.)
    
4.  The backing of a commercial juggernaut, with its many benefits:
    
    1.  world-class hosting & technical support
        
    2.  which they did not have to develop
        
    3.  long-term backing (Bomis turned out to support Wikipedia for a good 5 years, but there was no guarantee)
        
    4.  world-class software development resources[1](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#fn1)
        
    5.  Massive publicity. At launch Knol had infinitely more publicity than did Wikipedia; much of this buzz was due simply to who the backer was.
        
5.  A public educated to read wikis, and to use them.
    
    This is a greater advantage than it seems. How many people could Wikipedia hope to draw on at day 1—that cared even a little about [Free content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_content "Free content"), that knew what a wiki was, that wouldn’t dismiss it as hopeless, and had an editing familiarity with wikis? Darned few. We had to constantly evangelize and educate people about wikis, and by dint of unremitting effort create the English Wikipedia and make it interesting and valuable enough that people would contribute who didn’t fulfill any of those criteria. En was the existence proof that large-scale wikis were possible and valuable. Knol, on the other hand, can draw immediately on that pool of people Wikipedia created.
    
6.  A model targeted directly at people unhappy with Wikipedia. Are you an expert tired of ‘anti-expertism’ on Wikipedia? Why try to get along with those bumpkins when you could have your own article completely to yourself on Google Knol (and get paid for’t)? Wikipedia appealed to those unhappy with Nupedia. Nupedia when Wikipedia launched was a lot smaller than Wikipedia was when Knol launched. I think this pool of possible contributors was thus also much larger for Knol than it was for Wikipedia.
    

# [Why Knol Was Doomed to Fail](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#why-knol-was-doomed-to-fail "Link to section: § 'Why Knol was doomed to fail'")

With Wikipedia by this point, the basic concept of collaboration had been proved. With Knol, we see only the divisiveness of the payments system, and a few isolated authors striving on their own.

The important thing here is that people work on different things. No article writer on Wikipedia is discouraged by the fact that, say, Tim Starling is being paid for his system administration and PHP hacking—they don’t want to do it and are happy to exchange donations for the practical results of Tim’s labor viz. high [uptime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptime "Uptime") and faster servers. But if, say, every [FA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_articles "Wikipedia:Featured articles") author got $1,558.43$1,0002009, then perhaps writers of ordinary articles will get discouraged, or the bitter battles over what will be an FA or not will burn out the best editors.

Intuition suggests, and some results support, the hypothesis that paying only some contributors can “[crowd out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivation_crowding_theory "Motivation crowding theory")” other unpaid contributions (through shifting the reason for contribution [from intrinsic to extrinsic motivation](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1997-frey.pdf)). See for example:

-   [“Pay Enough Or Don’t Pay At All”](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2000-gneezy.pdf "'Pay Enough Or Don’t Pay At All', Gneezy & Rustichini 2000"), Gneezy & Rustichini 200026ya found that “offering money did not always produce an improvement: subjects who were offered monetary incentives performed more poorly than those who were offered no compensation”; the theoretical model & [experimental lab results](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2001-fehr.pdf "'Do Incentive Contracts Crowd Out Voluntary Cooperation?', Fehr & Gächter 2001") have been extended to some interesting real world results:
    
    1.  paying for blood donations [reduces blood donations](https://www.amazon.com/Gift-Relationship-Human-Social-Policy/dp/1565844033)
        
    2.  financially penalizing tardiness at all [increases tardiness](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/468061)
        
-   [“Crowding Effects: How Money Influences Open Source Projects and its Contributors”](https://stuermer.ch/blog/documents/ethz_LinuxTag2007_CrowdingEffects.pdf), covering Linux results
    
-   NYU professor Panos Ipeirotis on the backlash when he began [paying at all](https://www.behind-the-enemy-lines.com/2011/05/pay-enough-or-dont-pay-at-all.html "Pay Enough or Don't Pay at All") for [Amazon Mechanical Turk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Mechanical_Turk "Amazon Mechanical Turk") tasks
    
-   UP researcher Alexey Komissarouk on how [paying more on Mechanical Turk](https://alexeymk.com/flipping-coins-through-mechanical-turk-part-2/ "Flipping Coins through Mechanical Turk: Part 2") led to more lying on the assigned tasks, even by the most highly rated Mechanical Turkers
    

There may be no happy medium between paying only for things which won’t get done otherwise (tedious system administration), and paying for most everything; I think Knol is in that unhappy middle.

# [Knol Did Fail](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#knol-did-fail "Link to section: § 'Knol did fail'")

How did Knol fare since I diagnosed its high levels of failure and predicted general doom in January 200917ya? Google continued to update the site, but then stopped any updates in [December 2009](https://gwern.net/doc/www/web.archive.org/41c09c7d7ca5307986a0476f37d02fe1a1cefaae.html), and the site stagnated over the 94 weeks since. Intermittent technical problems have lead to the accusation that Knol has [been abandoned](https://googlesystem.blogspot.com/2011/01/abandoned-knol.html "Abandoned Knol")—presumably for hotter initiatives like [Google Wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Wave "Google Wave"), which was publicly announced, released, and abandoned during the interval that Knol has stagnated; or [Google+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google%2B "Google+"), which looks like it will have more staying power than its predecessor Google Buzz.

User activity is similarly pretty low. On 2011-10-13, I took a look at Knol’s “What’s New” section on its [front page](https://gwern.net/doc/www/knol.google.com/901d1a9acc7ce3db980ff718c789d076a3083490.html).

Over half a day for the entire site, 10 knols were changed in some fashion; edits probably are not huge updates given the relatively large number of revisions like 135 or 90. A quick-and-dirty estimate of the userbase: 1 edit per hour, each by a unique user, who edits on average once a week implies a userbase of 1 × 24 × 7 = 168.

Traffic-wise, one of them has a respectable 80k views (an [accounting knol](https://gwern.net/doc/www/web.archive.org/fe3142851c4d39aa3e5a777f88ffce110a2cac5c.html)), but this seems to be lifetime views. Wikipedia, of course, has more like 10 edits a second, and even a dry article like [Accountancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accounting "Accounting") will pick up ~[62k](https://stats.grok.se/en/201109/Accountancy) views a month. (I couldn’t find a direct equivalent for that knol—[Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Category:Book:Accountancy "Category:Book:Accountancy") has so many accounting-related texts, and Stats.grok.se doesn’t cover Wikibooks.) Another amusing comparison is seeing how often Knol is [submitted to Reddit](https://gwern.net/doc/www/old.reddit.com/41b75ee2dd9a5af1d35e832b983599a1594756d4.html) versus [Wikipedia submission](https://old.reddit.com/domain/en.wikipedia.org "English Wikipedia on Reddit"). In August 200917ya, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2009/08/11/poor-google-knol-has-gone-from-a-wikipedia-killer-to-a-craigslist-wannabe/ "Poor Google Knol Has Gone From A Wikipedia Killer To A Craigslist Wannabe") reported on Knol’s declining traffic as measured by [Quantcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantcast "Quantcast"); in October 201115ya, [Quantcast’s estimates](https://web.archive.org/web/20120607130330/http://www.quantcast.com/knol.google.com) shows Knol cycling from a high of <300k monthly visitors to a low of ~170k, with ~260k at the endpoint in July, although it appears to have never again reached its January 200917ya peak of 300k monthly visitors. (For comparison purposes, one site [claims](https://www.internetworldstats.com/emarketing.htm) that the number of global Internet users increased from 1.596 billion in March 200917ya to 2.110 billion in June 201115ya; Wikipedia continues to remain popular, [to Alexa](https://web.archive.org/web/20140103093117/https://www.alexa.com/siteinfo/wikipedia.org).)

## [Knol Death-Watch](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#knol-death-watch "Link to section: § 'Knol death-watch'")

So even in absolute numbers, Knol has stagnated in most every respect. Some of the links above are from months or years ago—no one cares enough about Knol even to discuss it. (Somewhat like [Citizendium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizendium "Citizendium").) Knol has all the characteristics of a doomed Google service, which leads to an interesting question: _when_ will Google kill off Knol?

To predict, we should consider [past examples](https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.greaterwrong.com/3f51eed991384d278113d4524cfc69dc88b47aef.html "'Inside vs Outside View', LessWrong 2026"): services can linger for a very long time, entirely neglected, or die very quickly. Which group does Knol fall into?

-   Yahoo: [Geocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeoCities "GeoCities") is very like Knol in being a collection of public content created by individuals and hosted on a corporate site, and was ignored by Yahoo for almost a decade before its shockingly abrupt shutdown, [Delicious.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delicious_\(website\) "Delicious (website)") did little better while [Flickr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flickr "Flickr") is still with Yahoo (and still neglected).
    
-   Google: [A number of services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Discontinued_Google_services "Category:Discontinued Google services") with steady users, like [Google Notebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Notebook "Google Notebook") or Google Wave, have been killed off. We can look at them one by one:
    
    1.  [Google Answers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Answers "Google Answers"): public content; closure announced November 200620ya, shut down December 200620ya. All the archived question-answers and pages are still hosted by Google.
        
    2.  [Google Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Base "Google Base"): public content appearing in Google searches; visibility eliminated October 200917ya, APIs deprecated December 201016ya. Unclear where the content went—seems to’ve been incorporated into other Google services.
        
    3.  [GOOG-411](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOOG-411 "GOOG-411"): private service; closure not previously announced, November 201016ya. The content is private and folded into Google’s audio analysis algorithms.
        
    4.  [Google Lively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Lively "Google Lively"): introduced July 200818ya, closed December 200818ya. Apparently little or no user-generated content, and the content/codebase is unavailable.
        
    5.  [Google Mashup Editor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Mashup_Editor "Google Mashup Editor"): closure announced January 200917ya, closed August 200917ya. User-generated, their content apparently made available indefinitely on Code.google.com (unclear how useful that would be to the users)
        
    6.  [Google Page Creator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Page_Creator "Google Page Creator"): closure announced 200719ya, user-signups disabled September 200818ya, closed 200917ya. User-generated, all content shifted to hosting on [Google Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Sites "Google Sites") and presumably still available.
        
    7.  [Google SearchWiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_SearchWiki "Google SearchWiki"): opened November 200818ya, closed March 201016ya. User-generated but mostly private, the content (such as it is) is available to users in private.
        
    8.  [Google Video Marketplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Google_products#Discontinued_in_2007 "List of Google products § Discontinued in 2007"): announced 200620ya, closed August 200818ya. All content unavailable, but the content appears to have been completely commercial, and none of the content seems to be unique, so the disappearance isn’t a big concern.
        
    9.  [Google X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_Development "X Development"): something of a joke or mistake; irrelevant.
        
    10.  [Google Code Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Code_Search "Google Code Search"): [closure announced](https://googleblog.blogspot.com/2011/10/fall-sweep.html "A fall sweep") October 201115ya, closed January 201214ya. Presumably entirely unavailable, but as a search utility, it was private use only.
         
    11.  [iGoogle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IGoogle "IGoogle") social features & [Google Buzz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Buzz "Google Buzz"): closure announced October 201115ya, closed November/December 201115ya. User-generated content, but the posts will remain publicly visible and users can download their material.
         
    12.  [Jaiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaiku "Jaiku"): closure announced October 201115ya, closed January 201214ya. User-generated content; apparently Google will _not_ continue to host the content like Buzz or Page Creator, but the codebase was open-sourced in 200917ya and Google will let users download their own material.
         
    13.  Academic API access: closure announced October 201115ya, closed January 201214ya. Private, for academics only; no user-generated or public aspects. (Presumably most academics using it have published their results already.)
         

Looking through the most relevant examples, Google tends to shut down services fairly fast and is surprisingly good about preserving data, in line with its [data liberation views](https://web.archive.org/web/20140207144847/https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3024190). Other examples are relevant: Google eliminated file storage for its [mailing lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Groups "Google Groups"), it gave the lists something like half a year of lead-time, and its Usenet archives have been poorly maintained since 200125ya without being eliminated.

That it might do so is not a great challenge: disk space is cheap—fractions of pennies per gigabyte—and Google could shift Knol to all-[static content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_web_page "Static web page"), eliminating any software maintenance overhead. Unlike Google Notebook, knols are inherently public works, meant for public distribution and consumption, so it may be better to compare Knol against Google Groups rather than Google Notebook. Knol has not been shut down _yet_ 2 years after its lack of noticeable success became clear, suggesting that it won’t be shut down any time soon. Weighing Google’s usual announce lead-time, the public nature of the content, its survival until now, and so on subjectively, these probabilities feel about right:

-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 201115ya: [10%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3836 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2011")
    
-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 201214ya: [25%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3837 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2012")
    
-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 201313ya: [25%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3838 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2013")
    
-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 201412ya: [20%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3839 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2014")
    
-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 201511ya: [15%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3840 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2015")
    
-   Google Knol will be shutdown in 2016: [5%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3841 "Google Knol will be shutdown in 2016")[2](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#fn2)
    
-   Google Knol’s shutdown will leave the knols’ content publicly accessible at some URL: [90%](https://predictionbook.com/predictions/3835 "Google Knol's shutdown will leave the knols' content publicly accessible at some URL")
    

(In March-May 201313ya I conducted [an extensive study of Google shutdowns](https://gwern.net/google-shutdown "'Predicting Google closures', Gwern 2013") to come up with some predictive model of shutdowns; while obviously Knol had been shutdown already, I still used the model to calculate a 5-year survival estimate for Knol from the day the shutdown happened, which turned out to be a suitably-low 43%.)

### [Chronicle of a Death Foretold](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#chronicle-of-a-death-foretold "Link to section: § 'Chronicle of a Death Foretold'")

On 2011-11-22, Google put the speculation to rest when it [announced](https://googleblog.blogspot.com/2011/11/more-spring-cleaning-out-of-season.html "Official Google Blog: More spring cleaning out of season"):

> We launched Knol in 200719ya to help improve web content by enabling experts to collaborate on in-depth articles. In order to continue this work, we’ve been working with Solvitor and Crowd Favorite to create [Annotum](https://annotum.wordpress.com/ "Annotum"), an open-source scholarly authoring and publishing platform based on WordPress. Knol will work as usual until April 30, 201214ya, and you can download your knols to a file and/or [migrate them](https://wordpress.com/blog/2011/11/22/google-knol-wordpress-annotum/ "Google Knols Move to WordPress: The Annotum Platform") to WordPress.com. From May 1 through October 1, 201214ya, knols will no longer be viewable, but can be downloaded and exported. After that time, Knol content will no longer be accessible.

This immediately resolved the above 7 predictions. I was correct to most expect Knol’s shutdown to come in 201214ya or 201313ya (and not earlier or later), however, my final prediction signally failed—the announcement is clear that the knol content will _not_ be hosted indefinitely but will be deleted[3](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol#fn3) (‘accessible’ being an euphemism). Why did I blow it? My reasoning was based on one of my standard tools while [predicting](https://gwern.net/prediction-market "'Prediction Markets', Gwern 2009"): the Outside View. Google had kept public content hosted in the past when it shut down projects (see the previous list), so it would probably continue to do so. The Outside View technique fails, however, when the same fundamentals that give it predictive power do finally change and a new regime is entered. In retrospect, there may have been such a regime change within Google when [Larry Page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Page "Larry Page") became CEO in April 201115ya and began pushing for a Google-wide focus on social networking, with a ruthless approach to projects which were not succeeding or connected to social network. This was all laid out in _Wired_’s [“Inside Google+—How the Search Giant Plans to Go Social”](https://www.wired.com/2011/06/inside-google-plus-social/), which I read at the time but examined more for how Google+ might differ from Facebook than how it might affect allocation of Google resources.
