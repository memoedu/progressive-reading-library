<div class="abstract">
> This essay tests a simple claim by an LLM: that a particular human poem had a real subject but weak realization.
>
> On 2026-03-15, I asked GPT-5.4 Pro rewrite it under explicit formal constraints; it chose to invent a sonnet, a split-screen human/machine poem, and an alliterative version.
> I then iterated them using scansion and cross-model critique.
>
> The rewrites *are* better.
> Constraint improves cadence, imagery, and closure, and the split-screen version in particular turns theme into form rather than merely paraphrasing it.
>
> But the gains are mostly local: revision critiques execution, and does not expand the space of possibilities.
> Constraint raises the floor for LLM poetry.
> It does not raise the ceiling.
> What is necessary is to expand the room, and break the walls, by adding more, such as necessity.
> None of the rewrites become major poems, because formal optimization cannot supply what is missing: conceptual surplus, allusive depth, and pressure from material outside the poem’s initial premise.
>
> I take this as further evidence that LLM poetry benefits from many runs, explicit concept-loading, and large models.
</div>

> <div class="poem">
> I read on my iPhone, stopped at a red light, & next to my car \

> yelling over traffic, I’m PLAYING, sun on the child’s face \

>

>

> my eyes are animal eyes with animal need to gaze out
>
> at red lights & be given the useless-lovely data of a sparrow skimming \
> to a telephone wire, a child at a rainbow-painted stringed instrument, \

>
> low, high? Low, high? Perception a note not played again, \
> & when the light turns green my car drives, \
> I am finishing my song. The light is yellow now.
> </div>

I didn't care for it, and said as much, and GPT agreed:

> ...Yes—that’s exactly where it thins out.
>
> The poem’s *idea* is doing nearly all the work; the language is mostly just transporting it. You can feel that in a few places:
>
> - The lineation is arbitrary. Breaks don’t create pressure (no [enjambment](!W) tension, no rhythmic pivot), so as you noted, it survives flattening into prose with minimal loss.
> - The sonic field is underdeveloped. There’s little patterned recurrence—no real alliterative spine, vowel harmonics, or metrical expectation being set up and then varied. The diction is competent but not *charged*.
> - Syntax is default expository. Clauses proceed in a “thought, elaboration, contrast” pipeline that reads like an essay paragraph broken into lines.
>
> The key problem isn’t that it’s [free verse](!W); it’s that it’s *uncommitted* verse. Compare to something like [Jorie Graham](!W) or [Carl Phillips](!W), where even without strict meter, you still get:
>
> - controlled breath units (line = perceptual event),
> - recurrence structures (lexical, syntactic, sonic),
> - pressure from delay/suspension.
>
> Here, the closing claim—“my eyes are animal eyes”—*should* land like a [volta](!W "Volta (literature)"), but it isn’t prepared musically. It arrives as a conceptual assertion rather than a sonic inevitability.
>
> **How to strengthen it (without turning it into a [sonnet](!W)):**
>
> 1. **Impose a constraint you can feel.**
>
>    Even a loose accentual scheme (say, 3–4 stresses per line, with periodic deviations) would give the reader something to ride.
>
> 2. **Exploit the image system sonically.**
>
>    The sparrow/piano/fingers cluster is begging for patterned consonants (sk-, tr-, p-) and percussive echo to mirror the tapping.
>
> 3. **Make the line breaks do work.**
>
>    Break *against* syntax so that key words are delayed or doubled in meaning. Right now, most breaks are after-comma pauses.
>
> 4. **Engineer the volta.**
>
>    Seed “animal” earlier—phonically or imagistically—so the final line resolves a pattern rather than introducing a thesis.
>
> 5. **Cut abstraction by ~20%.**
>
>    Every abstract noun that remains should earn its place via placement or echo, not just semantic necessity.
>
> Put bluntly: the poem knows what it wants to say, but it hasn’t decided how language must behave to make saying it unavoidable.

This was a bold set of claims, and I became curious if it could indeed fix the poem autonomously *without* degrading the way that ["Spoilage"](/fiction/spoilage) had (although I didn't want to push the revisions as far as "Spoilage").

So I told [GPT-5.4 Pro](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4/#pro "‘Introducing GPT-5.4 Pro’, OpenAI 2026") to fix it with my usual brainstorm-style prompt (followed by requiring use of my usual poem [commentary & scansion markup](/style-guide#scansion) to help scaffold revision):

> **Try rewriting it. Brainstorm 10 detailed revisions aiming at different effects. Revise each one repeatedly. Rate them 1–5 stars and print out the final top 3...for the 3 finalists, apply the Gwern.net Scansion MoS format, and revise and iterate. Critique and analyze the resulting versions.**

Did the formal structure and revision help?
Yes.

I think that the constraints and iteration/critique did improve the 3 poems; but that GPT-5.4 Pro is wrong in saying the sonnet is ever as good as the split-screen.
I think the split-screen is clearly superior to both, although they have some good points (eg. "grackle" is more interesting than Hays's "sparrow").

# What Is Missing?

What is missing compared to my better AI poems like ["Silver Bird"](/fiction/silver-bird "‘Silver Bird Above San Francisco’, Gwern et al 2025") or ["Apollonian #1"](/fiction/lab-animals "‘Apollonian #1: The Counted &amp; the Crowned’, Gwern et al 2025")?
I think the results are consistent with my emphasis on richness of concept and allusions.

The current poems are all relatively "empty", as they do not benefit from my usual process of building up databanks and sets of words or phrases or ideas to draw on and [prime](!W "Priming (psychology)") the LLMs.
They are small variations on ideas already in the Hays original (like the species of the bird, which is better but still performing the same literary function).

"Split-screen" isn't held back as badly because it has a cute formal conceit which drives its interest; whereas in the sonnet, I feel acutely how routine and boring it is.
In particular, it has an overly tidy and unambiguous ending; Kimi K2.5 Thinking correctly suggests that there are much more interesting endings, like ["Recommendation: proceed now. / I—"]{.poem} or ["Recommendation: proceed now / Recommendation: proceed now"]{.poem}:

<div class="collapse">
> The pat conclusion is a *default*, not a *necessity*—the formal conceit doesn't mandate it, but nothing in the prompt architecture pushes against it either. The machine/human alternation is structurally *capable* of openness, but GPT defaults to closure because:
>
> 1. **The Hays original supplies a narrative arc** (red light → green light → departure), and the revision inherits this teleology without questioning it
> 2. **The "cute" formal conceit is self-sufficient**—it doesn't *need* to complicate itself to demonstrate its intelligence
> 3. **No external pressure** (your 'databanks', a contradictory thematic mandate, a forbidden word or image) forces the ending to *earn* its resolution or abandon it
>
> What would an **unclosed** Split-Screen look like? Perhaps:
>
>> <div class="poem">
>> Recommendation: proceed now. \
>> I—
>> </div>
>
> Or the human line refusing to answer. Or a 7^th^ couplet where the machine line *repeats* (["Recommendation: proceed now / Recommendation: proceed now"]{.poem}), the human line having already left. The form allows this; the prompt didn't require it.
>
> Your point about "visible alternatives" is crucial. The revision process critiques *execution* but not *possibility space*. It asks "is this line good?" not "should this line exist?" The 'databank' method, by contrast, floods the possibility space with *incompatible* alternatives—medieval bestiary birds against thermal imaging grids—and forces selection under pressure.
>
> The Split-Screen is good *within its assumptions*. Your method questions the assumptions before the first line.
</div>

[External pressure.]{.margin-note} There's nothing 'outside' driving the LLMs out of their comfort zone---nothing like me imposing an out-of-the-blue theme like medieval bestiaries, thermal imaging patents, or Saint Augustine on attention, which would force the chatbot out of its comfort zone and towards radical new possibilities.
Something outside the poem’s initial premise has to arrive and start making demands; and the original Hays is too impoverished to do this for the LLM revisions.

The LLMs optimize for all the visible esthetic properties one could desire, assisted by scaffolding, but they are still lacking some engine with fire---surplus, pressure from outside the poem, voice under stress, moral ambiguity, weird necessity or inexplicably good lines that feel not merely improved but inevitable.
Where Hays works, it shows that in lines like ["I AM FINISHING MY SONG—"]{.poem} or ["my eyes are animal eyes with animal need to gaze out"]{.poem}.

To quote GPT-5.4 Pro:

> Constraint helped because it forced decisions.
> It failed because decision-forcing is not the same thing as imaginative enlargement.
> The next leap will not come from tighter meter alone.
>
> It will come from importing a foreign archive, preserving awkward agency, and letting the machine/human boundary get messier instead of cleaner.
