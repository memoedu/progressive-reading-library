Sometimes in research, despite [my best search techniques](/search "'Internet Search Tips', Gwern 2018"), I am unable to get a digital copy of a paper or book to upload to Libgen.
This page lists my failures to date, and offers bounties for anyone who can get me a digital copy of them (often possible via a university ILL department, which are no longer accessible to me).
Internet Archive scans are not accepted due to low quality, unless there are extenuating circumstances.

Unless otherwise specified, the current bounty is \$10 for papers/theses/chapters & \$20 for books/videos/miscellaneous; I can do PayPal or Amazon gift cards most easily, other payment methods will be more work.
(I may pay bounty+costs if not unreasonable or you contact me in advance with the total.)

To claim a bounty, [contact me](/me#contact) with the copy.
(Payments are first-come-first served unless you contact me in advance so I can remove it from the list & avoid unnecessary duplication.)

- Bledsoe, W. W. 1968. "Semiautomatic Facial Recognition", Technical Report SRI Project 6693, Stanford Research Institute, Menlo Park, California. (as cited in Boyer's ["In Memoriam: Woodrow Wilson Bledsoe"](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~boyer/bledsoe-memorial-resolution.pdf) which describes it as reporting a breakthrough in facial recognition and even outperforming humans to some degree, but does not seem to be publicly available) [paper]

    It is unclear where a copy might be found; [SRI International](!W) should have a copy, and, another likely place seems to be the ['Woodrow "Woody" W. Bledsoe papers, 1949--1996'](https://txarchives.org/utcah/finding_aids/00064.xml) held by the [University of Texas at Austin](!W) archives, which lists a number of highly relevant-sounding entries; it clearly contains the face photographs Bledsoe used ("Faces, 1968--1969", which is consistent with the [description in _Wired_](https://www.wired.com/story/secret-history-facial-recognition/ "‘The Secret History of Facial Recognition: Sixty years ago, a sharecropper’s son invented a technology to identify faces. Then the record of his role all but vanished. Who was Woody Bledsoe, and who was he working for?’, Raviv 2020")), and I suspect that either "Papers on facial recognition, 1964--1987" or "Facial recognition, 1969" contain or refer to the technical report (or a superseding document).

    (Unfortunately, the UT archive website does not include any duplication/scanning policy, requiring one to contact them. Likewise, SRI's policy on accessing archive documents is unclear.)

_Great Mambo Chicken_:

There was the hyper-g work done on chickens, for example, by Arthur Hamilton ("Milt") Smith in the 1970s. Milt Smith was a gravity specialist at the University of California at Davis who wanted to find out what would happen to humans if they lived in greater-than-normal g-forces. Naturally, he experimented on animals, and he decided that the animal that most closely resembled man for this specific purpose was the chicken. Chickens, after all, had a posture similar to man's: they walked upright on 2 legs, they had 2 non-load-bearing limbs (the wings), and so on. Anyway, Milt Smith and his assistants took a flock of chickens—hundreds of them, in fact—and put them into the 2 18-foot-long centrifuges in the university's Chronic Acceleration Research Laboratory, as the place was called. They spun those chickens up to 2-and-a-half gs and let them stay there for a good while. In fact, they left them spinning like that day and night, for 3 to 6 months or more at a time. The hens went around and around, they clucked and they cackled and they laid their eggs, and as far as those chickens were concerned that was what ordinary life was like: a steady pull of 2-and-a-half gs. Some of those chickens spent the larger portion of their lifetimes in that goddamn accelerator.

Well, it was easy to predict what would happen. Their bones would get stronger and their muscles would get bigger—because they had all that extra gravity to work against. A total of 23 generations of hens was spun around like this and the same thing happened every time. When the accelerator was turned off, out walked ... great Mambo chicken!

These chronically accelerated fowl were paragons of brute strength and endurance. They'd lost excess body fat, their hearts were pumping out greater-than-normal volumes of blood, and their extensor muscles were bigger than ever. In consequence of all this, the high-g chickens had developed a 3× increase in their ability to do work, as measured by wing-beating exercises and treadmill tests.
