We know that ["AI is whatever doesn't work yet"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_effect). We also know that people often contrast AI (or DL, or LLMs specifically) derogatorily with classic forms of software, such as regexps: why use a [LLM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model) to waste gigaflops of compute to do what a few good regexps could...?

So I am amused to discover recently, by accident while wondering 'what does the "regular" in ["regular expression"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_expression) mean, anyway?', that it turns out that **regexps are AI**.

In fact, they are not even [GOFAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOFAI) symbolic AI, as you immediately assumed on hearing that, but they were originally *connectionist AI* research!

Huh? "Gwern, how could a regular expression or regular language, clearly intended for processing text, like in my text editor, *possibly* be a neural network in any way, shape, or form?"

Well, see for yourself---it turns out, [per Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_expression#History), that 'regular events' were introduced by [Kleene himself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Cole_Kleene) with the justification of modeling [McCulloch-Pitts neural nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCulloch-Pitts_neural_nets)! (Which are then modeled by 'regular languages' and conveniently written down as 'regular expressions', abbreviated to 'regexps' or 'regexes', and then extended/bastardized in countless ways since.) To quote from his [1951 RAND paper](/doc/ai/nn/1951-kleene.pdf#page=49 "‘Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata § pg49’, Kleene 1951 (page 49)") introducing the term for the first time (and conveniently available online):

> It thus appears that the appropriate mathematical abstraction for us now is to treat the problem of explaining behavior as though organisms and machines were immortal, having an infinite future though a finite past. We want to provide for behavior that could be used *ad infinitum*, if merely the nerve net and effector mechanisms were immortal.
>
> By trying to provide for behavior over an infinity of time by a finite mechanism, we have a model for the real problem of providing for complex behavior over a long finite lifetime by a relatively small mechanism.
>
> ...**7. Regular Events**:
>
> **7.1 "Regular events" defined**: We shall presently describe a class of events which we will call "regular events". (We would welcome any suggestions as to a more descriptive term. McCulloch and Pitts use a term ["prehensible"](/doc/ai/nn/1943-mcculloch.pdf#page=12 "‘A logical calculus of the ideas immanent in nervous activity § pg12’, McCulloch & Pitts 1943 (page 12)"), introduced rather differently; but since we did not understand their definition, we are hesitant to adopt the term.)...Our class of *regular events* will be defined inductively, starting with the definite events, and using 3 operations E ∨ F, E F, E ✱ F; ie. it shall be the last class containing the definite events, and closed under these operations.

The 'regular' here is not well-defined, as Kleene concedes, and is a gesture towards modeling 'regularly occurring events' (that the neural net automaton must process and respond to). He admits "regular" is a terrible term, and asks for a better term; but apparently, no one came up with anything better, and so we're stuck with it.

(This historical background does make it less surprising that you can do neural net-like things with regular expressions, when we look at them as sparsely-connected discrete perceptrons operating on symbols rather than floating-point numbers, and so it makes sense that you can have [differentiable](https://google-research.github.io/self-organising-systems/2022/diff-fsm/) [finite state machines](https://github.com/gtn-org/gtn) or you can [evolve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00600 "‘Minimum Description Length Recurrent Neural Networks’, Lan et al 2021") accurate neural nets for grammar tasks or [train cellular automata](https://google-research.github.io/self-organising-systems/difflogic-ca/), or that you can [encode minimax-search chess engines](https://nicholas.carlini.com/writing/2025/regex-chess.html "‘Regex Chess: A 2-ply minimax chess engine in 84,688 regular expressions’, Carlini 2025") into regexes. It also suggests that perhaps the dominance of regular expressions as we know them has not been a good thing, and has obscured the existence of many alternative ways to do these tasks, like in forgotten early programming languages such as [SNOBOL](!W) or alternative paradigms like [parser combinators](!W) or [pushdown automata](!W).)
