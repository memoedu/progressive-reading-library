The main stanzas should be written in (possibly loose) classic dactylic pentameter alternative rhyme ABAB, with the above-described chorus.
(This is an unusual meter, rarely used, but dactylic pentameter is showing off *dexterity*, akin to Lord Byron's famous exercise "The Destruction of Sennacherib" https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/43827/the-destruction-of-sennacherib (which is in a different meter than dactylic pentameter, anapestic tetrameter, but still showing off): 'Look how easy I make this look, bro. look how handy I am, in describing a hand-some man'. It's a sprezzatura. 'This seems so easy... but if you're a poet, or you think about it a little, you realize it'. In a sense, the reader is being carried by the long rocking lines, in the hands of a skillful poet-father, the way the woman feels held and protected. The dactyllics are the fingers holding the pen.)
Strict dactylic pentameter is very difficult, and you may need to loosen it slightly, but always try to be as strict as possible, to have something to sacrifice for a good reason.

## CRITICAL ASSESSMENT: What a Poetry Editor Would Say

<strong>Strengths:</strong>

1. <strong>The orange earns its symbolic weight without ever being called a symbol.</strong> It appears at the meeting, is invoked at the courtship ("the arc I could hold"), and returns at the end. The reader feels its recurrence rather than being told it matters.

2. <strong>The Freddy Krueger stanza solves a problem.</strong> The brief emphasized the duality (hands that harm versus protect), and this was missing. Now the poem acknowledges the shadow—she *knows* hands can destroy—which makes the trust more meaningful.

3. <strong>"There it goes. There's the line"</strong> is the poem's most colloquial moment, and it's placed at the climax. The shift in register signals arrival: we're no longer in memory, we're in the immediate present, watching.

4. <strong>The fragment-sentences work.</strong> "Learning it. Gripping." / "The size of a hand. And the father. And now there was you." / "Hers to yours. Yours to my own." These mimed perceptions—the syntax enacts cognition.

5. <strong>The "name" callback is structurally satisfying.</strong> Twenty-odd years without name → the grammar she knew → And the name. The poem earns its closure.

<strong>Weaknesses / What Heaney Would Push On:</strong>

1. <strong>"A grammar I'd learned before language"</strong>—Clever, perhaps *too* clever. Frost: "The ear does the work, not the puzzle." It's defensible but risks feeling like a workshop line.

2. <strong>"Stitching the edges of danger to safety and light"</strong>—"safety and light" is two abstractions where one concrete would be stronger. What does she *see*? The calluses? The hair on the forearm? The poem is more concrete elsewhere.

3. <strong>"Yours are the answer"</strong>—Flat. It announces rather than shows. What if it were: "But yours—the rope of the vein is a thread / Stitching..." The em-dash does the pivot without the declaration.

4. <strong>The chorus couplets after IV and V</strong>—"hold me up / hold me down" is strong, but "trace my spine / yours and mine" feels slightly generic. Is there a more specific pair?

## THE SENNACHERIB EFFECT: Does It Work?

The test: Does the poem seem simple on first read, then reveal its craft on second?

<strong>First read:</strong> A woman remembers her father, meets a man at a party, falls in love, has a daughter, walks holding hands. The orange is sweet. The ending is warm.

<strong>Second read:</strong> The dactylic tetrameter is nearly flawless—and dactyls are *hard*. The orange isn't just sweet; it's the fulcrum (the word appears) around which everything pivots. "The catch and the fall" is both the fruit and the falling in love. "Pressed light" is both an adverb and a noun. "And the name" answers "without name" from forty lines earlier. The Freddy Krueger stanza isn't a detour; it's necessary—without acknowledging that hands can harm, the trust is sentimental.

<strong>Third read:</strong> The choruses aren't ornament; they're compression. Each couplet is a week of ordinary devotion collapsed into fourteen syllables. The poem is a lifetime in seven stanzas and a catalog of weekdays.

I think it approaches the effect. The craft is real. Whether it achieves the *inevitability* of Heaney's best work—that sense that no word could be otherwise—is a question for readers, not the maker.

### On starting with the orange

Moving the orange stanza to I and pushing the father to II does three things:

* <strong>Hook</strong>: we start with a visual, kinetic scene and implicit attraction instead of exposition about the father.
* <strong>Psychology</strong>: “my body just knew it / I had known it before” now functions as a hinge into II, where we see what, exactly, the body knew (rifle/massage).
* <strong>Structure</strong>: the father is still clearly the template (II, III, IX), but he’s now *backgrounded*—which matches your thesis: he’s the prior grammar, not the main episode.

If you decide you want stricter chronology again, you can always flip I/II back. But in terms of impact per line, orange-first is almost certainly stronger.

# Your Hands And Mine

<strong>I</strong>

Checking the rifle for ghosts in the dead of the night,
Father would stand in the hall with the darkness behind.
Morning, his thumbs in the meat of my shoulders pressed light,
Waking the dreamer—the hunter, the healer combined.

*Tuesday, nightmare: check the lock.*
*Wednesday, waking: watch the clock.*

---

<STRONG>II</STRONG>

You with an orange describing its slow little orbit,
Palm over palm in the light while I stood by the door.
Couldn't say why, but my body already absorbed it:
Size of your hand. And the arc. I had known it before.

*Thursday, party: toss the fruit.*
*Friday, dancing: scuff my boot.*

---

<STRONG>III</STRONG>

Standing together out there on the porch in the cold,
Palm on the small of my back, and already I knew.
Grammar I'd learned before language, an arc I could hold:
Father, the orange, the catching—and now there was you.

*Monday, massage: find the knot.*
*Tuesday, bedroom: hit the spot.*

---

<STRONG>IV</STRONG>

Freddy's red glove on the poster was something to dread—
Razors for fingers that sliced through my dreams in the night.
But yours: just the rope of a vein. And the vein is a thread
Stitching the edge of the nightmare to morning and light.

*Saturday, tripping: hold me up.*
*Sunday, sleeping: lay me down.*

---

<strong>V</strong>

We made a shorthand for speech in the hush of the kitchen,
*Yes* was your fist doing maneki, beckoning near.
*No* was your fingers together, a mouth you would stitch in,
Pinched little gate over teacup and saucer and fear.

*Wednesday, breakfast: pour the tea.*
*Thursday, nightstand: sign to me.*

---

<STRONG>VI</STRONG>

Spider-fine tracings you walked up my hip in the darkness,
Walking their dactyls from pelvis to hollow of thigh.
Crack of release and the grief I had stored in the hardness,
Something I'd carried for years. And you stayed. And the cry.

*Friday, moonlight: trace my spine.*
*Saturday, tangled: yours in mine.*

---

<STRONG>VII</STRONG>

Monday, the kitchen, you knelt to the grief in the plumbing,
Forearms like rope that could tether a ship by its prow.
Edge between what we can fix and what's already coming—
That was the line that your knuckles were tracing out now.

*Friday, driveway: check the oil.*
*Saturday, garden: turn the soil.*

---

<STRONG>VIII</STRONG>

When I grew heavy you'd palm at my belly and wait for
Movement: a kick, then another. The future in bone.
Checking the bolt and the furnace, you guarded the gate for
Something the size of a fist. And I wasn't alone.

*Tuesday, bedside: hold the cup.*
*Wednesday, hallway: help me up.*

---

<STRONG>IX</STRONG>

Shaking, you held her—your palm was a bowl for her skull.
I watched you become what my father was, steady and same.
Her fingers, a starfish, pulled down on your thumb, and the pull
Was Freddy's red glove become mitten. The worth. And the name.

*Thursday, cradle: rock the girl.*
*Friday, walking: watch her whirl.*

---

<strong>X</strong>

Three on the sidewalk: her hand into yours, into mine,
Swinging between us, a fulcrum of screaming and bone.
Orange arcs up in my mind. There it goes. There's the line
Running from that arc to this. Hers to yours. Yours to my own.

*Monday, doorway: pull me near.*
*Tuesday, sidewalk: keep me here.*
*Wednesday, bedroom: hold her tight.*
*Thursday, window: close the blind.*
*Friday, hallway: hand in mine.*

Her hand into yours. And yours into mine.
