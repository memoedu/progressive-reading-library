# [Colophon](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#colophon "Link to section: § 'Colophon'")

“Craneyard” was written in April 2026 to demonstrate my current AI poetry scaffolding workflow; this documents that workflow, to help explain to my fellow LLM users how decent creative LLM writing works.

This instance in particular shows the benefits of grounding a poem idea in multiple allusions with background research notes.

(Many LLM outputs throughout this process can be seen in the [“Elegy in a Craneyard” graveyard notes](https://gwern.net/doc/ai/nn/transformer/gpt/claude/4/2026-04-05-gwern-elegyinacraneyard-llmgraveyard.md.txt "'‘Elegy in a Craneyard’ graveyard notes', Gwern et al 2026").)

It emphasizes high concepts to create “friction”/non-default outputs, [priming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priming_\(psychology\) "Priming (psychology)") to enrich the available options, and then search/iteration to overcome the “view from nowhere” and “neural nets are lazy” problems that make almost all chatbot-LLM-written poetry unsatisfying (and so strikingly impoverished compared to even small ancient “base models” [like GPT-3 was](https://gwern.net/gpt-3 "‘GPT-3 Creative Fiction’, Gwern 2020")); see also [“At a Red Light” writing experiment](https://gwern.net/fiction/hays "‘Human Perception at a Red Light’, Gwern & Pro 2026"), [“Bits Beat AI Slop”](https://gwern.net/blog/2025/good-ai-samples "‘Adding Bits Beats AI Slop’, Gwern 2025"), [“Apollonian #1”](https://gwern.net/fiction/lab-animals "‘Apollonian #1: The Counted & the Crowned’, Gwern et al 2025") vs [“Spoilage”](https://gwern.net/fiction/spoilage "'Spoilage', Pro et al 2026"), etc.

The high concept is ‘the Seaport of Oakland’s iconic cranes meets the Old English poem “The Ruin” & Thomas Gray’s “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard”, as backed up by LLM-powered “deep research”’ (to prime a large pool of concepts).

## [Genesis](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#genesis "Link to section: § 'Genesis'")

This concept came to me while driving back one night in March 2026 from a dinner party at S— K—’s and watching the cranes yet again. I had often thought to myself that they looked like dinosaurs in the fog (although not _Star Wars_ [AT-AT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT-AT "<em>AT-AT</em>") vehicles—a popular comparison but which turns out to be spurious), and wanted to write a poem on them, or perhaps generate an illustration, but there was not yet enough _there_ there to write about. (Yes, they do look like Brontosaurus or something… but then what? Just the bare comparison would barely support a haiku.) It was a powerful image, but I needed more if it was to make sense either standalone or as some sort of followup to the “Silver Bird” encomium to modern aerospace.

Then [it suddenly hit me](https://gwern.net/ai-daydreaming "‘LLM Daydreaming’, Gwern 2025"): “Elegy in a Churchyard” → “Elegy in a Craneyard” + “The Ruin” = good AI poem topic! And then I further recalled the Internet meme, “And Yet a Trace of the True Self Exists in the False Self / Circle of Life”, and it occurred to me that the ships and cranes were likely powered by fossil fuels which we would like to phase out, and so in both a literal and figurative sense, _were_ dinosaurs. This was a rich enough stew to work with, especially with the ending: the cranes vanishing into the mists of time, but translated into poetry, like the Roman ruins or the deceased in the church graveyard—not useless or fit only for dismantling, nor lost entirely, as long as there are witnesses to reflect on them and recall their glory days. (And now that I have two poems, I can hope to find a third topic; perhaps something about datacenters like OpenAI’s Arizona Stargate facility, which are the cathedrals of our era, thereby creating a trilogy of past / present / future—although that poem awaits its collision with some other idea, to give it depth and motivate a choice of formal structure.)

## [Hybridization](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#hybridization "Link to section: § 'Hybridization'")

I ran through several iterations before “hybridizing” (usually expanding the prompt and starting a fresh session, as a kind of poor-man’s [“context distillation”](https://gwern.net/doc/www/arxiv.org/1971a139675a51e52fab71b37ffee880b2566f2d.pdf "‘Context Distillation: Learning by Distilling Context’, Snell et al 2022")), and then bringing in my more advanced ‘scaffolding’ requirements:

> …I have used LLMs to brainstorm a large set of draft poems to recombine and modify into a final poem. We need to take the candidate hybrids and start carefully critiquing and revising them.
> 
> **Procedure**
> 
> As part of the revision, you will need to write out poems in an augmented form, with each line written normally and then in a [scansion-notated form in HTML comments](https://gwern.net/style-guide#scansion). The exact form of scansion is copied below.
> 
> To write this hybrid poem, brainstorm poem candidates based on this prompt and the samples. Write 5 hybrid poem candidates. To do so, for each candidate:
> 
> 1.  define the formal structure of the poem in lines, meter, rhyme scheme, length, etc.
>     
> 2.  write out a summary of the poem, first as an abstract, and then stanza by stanza
>     
> 3.  write out some sample lines from the hypothetical candidate. (Remember to include scansion.)
>     
> 4.  revise and critique the approach. After that,
>     
> 5.  write out the entire candidate poem. (Each line must have the scansion!)
>     
> 6.  revise and critique the candidate poem at least 3 times, starting with an analysis of how well it works globally, and down to the line by line level.
>     
> 7.  write out the final candidate poem.
>     
> 8.  analyze and evaluate; rate it 1–5 stars.
>     
> 9.  excerpt the best lines, images, phrases, and tricks from it.
>     
> 
> Finally, write out a single hybrid poem with all of the best parts of all poems to create a single unified, complete, poem…

The scaffolding requires the LLM to write out a formal analysis of each line. This helps avoid regression while editing, and allows a laser-focus on the language. (I speculate that it also helps with tonal or register or formal shifts, because the LLM can write those down explicitly, and this gives the chatbot personality “permission” to write freely.)

I developed the inline metadata trick for writing [“Your Hands and Mine”](https://gwern.net/fiction/your-hands-and-mine "'Your Hands And Mine', Gwern et al 2025"), where I noticed that GPT would keep slipping up and introduce a rhyme/syllable error after fixing another, as if it had blind spots or its working memory got overloaded. This made sense to me, because LLMs do not do well at highly reliably tracking a lot of implicit mutable state, like tracking syllable count _and_ stress _and_ rhyme per line across a whole poem, while also trying to actually think about the semantics and esthetics, so similar to arithmetic, we help the LLM by forcing it to write out notes to a scratchpad—and since it is Markdown, we can just stuff them into an easy HTML comment. (The metadata headers are also fascinating to read in showing how close a reader LLMs can be, and skilled at literary criticism and verbalizing things I would struggle to, while still struggling with phonetics; the impression one has is of a born-deaf professor of rhetoric analyzing a poem at the blackboard.)

For example, the first line in the Markdown source looks more like this:

```
<!-- SECTION A (L01–L10, OE + lacunae): job = invoke ruin and
manuscript-fragment together; domain = Exeter Book / industrial archaeology;
tone = awed, broken; structural-enjamb = drop to single-word L08 and
then damaged fragments; key-allusions = ‘The Ruin’, giant-work,
manuscript loss. -->

...

These crane-corpses, wondrous— / calamity crumpled them,
 <!-- L01 OE: 3+2: CRANE-CORPS-es WON-drous / ca-LAM-i-ty CRUM-pled. --> \
```

## [Assessment & Open Questions](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#assessment-open-questions "Link to section: § 'Assessment & Open Questions'")

Overall, this took about a day of work, most of it spent waiting on long LLM runs and copy-pasting back and forth with little editorial or creative input on my end after the initial setup.

Authorship-wise, I list myself as the primary author, despite doing even less wordsmithing than usual and not making any ‘crucial’ decisions in terms of curation or selection (similar to the major decisions I made in creating [“_Tilakkhana_”](https://gwern.net/fiction/tilakkhana "‘<em>Tilakkhana</em>: The 3 Scars of Existence’, Gwern et al 2025") out of several unrelated samples). If you read through the early samples in [the graveyard file](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#gwern-2026-craneyard-graveyard), there’s less variety than in past experiments like the [“This Last Pain” graveyard](https://gwern.net/fiction/this-last-pain-graveyard "‘‘The Fourth Truth Of Pain’ Graveyard’, Gwern et al 2022"); and when they are drastically different, they tend to be weak and flabby, especially the more transhumanist sci-fi-heavy takes. Nothing here wound up truly _surprising me_—there was no idea which, upon search, escaped its creator and surprised them with unexpected depth. So in this case, I had an unusually specific, detailed high concept as a starting point, which constrained the LLMs too much to rise to the level where I would list Claude as the first author. And this may explain why [Claude could shine here](https://gwern.net/fiction/craneyard#claude-overperformance).

Had I intervened more, I think I would have tried to force more experimentation in meter—perhaps lean much more into the damaged fragments idea, or try to invent an entirely new meter to reflect a strange posthuman future somehow (similar to the invented alliterative meter in “Apollonian #1” which tries to reinvent Pindar); and considered the politics of Oakland more, as the deep research reports turned up interesting American politics I was unfamiliar with, and as it is, there is not much commentary there beyond a vague technological inevitability.

But I didn’t feel I had the time or energy to do so, as [Inkhaven 2](https://vishalblog.substack.com/p/for-your-consideration-inkhaven-2 "For Your Consideration: <em>Inkhaven 2</em>") was running, and I am backlogged on several poetry projects as it is (the “coastal spondaic” invented meter, or properly writing up the “pressure-cooker” meter of “Apollonian #1”). Perhaps I also lacked faith in the LLMs being able to find an ‘interesting’ meter which could reflect all of the influences simultaneously, and settled for a more straightforward historical progression of meters?

And a few parts wound up making me uneasy with how much they sound like chatbots: “The children played at cranes. The cranes played back / at dinosaurs. The joke outlived the crowd.”, or “like theorems in a language no one speaks.”, or “Between the ruin and the monument: / the loneliest address a structure knows.” They are too pat and aphoristic, too tidy, too regular and metronome-like; I would have improved them, or at least considered them at greater length, if I had more time and wanted to intervene more in this demonstration.

I was also not particularly sold on the triptych of “Vessel”, which doesn’t function as elegantly as “so much depends” does in “Silver Bird”; the repetition is OK but it lacks insight.

But overall, I am satisfied with the result and with how little manual effort I had to put into it with my refined workflow, and I hope the simplicity of my prompts will inspire people to be more ambitious with their own LLM usage (and not settle for the kind of AI slop they usually do).

_Demand more of your LLMs_, and give them the data and time that they need to really think!
