<div class="abstract reader-mode-not">
> A posthuman traveler finds the container cranes of the Port of Oakland still standing perhaps centuries hence, reflecting on their work, history, and abandonment.
> The cranes' silhouettes in fog resemble Brontosaurus silhouettes; the poem traces the recursive material chain (bone → oil → steel → the shape of bone) to literalize the "a trace of the true self exists in the false self" meme.
>
> Specifically: The poem is a 6-part mixed-form elegy in which a posthuman speaker encounters the Oakland cranes as ruins, remembers their labor, absorbs their history, and finally blesses their transformation into what the fog had always suggested: dinosaurs returned in steel.
> [§A](#section-a) treats them as Old English giant-work and damaged manuscript.
> [§B](#section-b) gives the Gray-like meditative arrival.
> [§C](#section-c) turns the three-minute container cycle into industrial liturgy.
> [§D](#section-d) preserves bureaucratic terminal language as a fossil layer and folds in the playground irony.
> [§E](#section-e) puts the cranes in the limbo “between the ruin and the monument”.
> [§F](#section-f) makes the dinosaur/oil/steel recursion explicit and ends in benediction: stay, wade, become your myth.
>
> Poem (elegy/encomium, mixed meter: Old English alliterative verse → blank verse → free verse, ~70 lines).
> Formal models: Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" and the anonymous Old English "The Ruin".
> Written April 2026 [with extensive LLM collaboration](#colophon) (primarily [Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-6), [GPT](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4/#pro "‘Introducing GPT-5.4 <span class=editorial>[GPT-5.4 Pro]</span>’, OpenAI 2026"), & [Kimi](https://www.kimi.com/blog/kimi-k2-5)).
>
> Sequel to ["Silver Bird Above San Francisco"](/fiction/silver-bird).

<!-- COMMENTARY

STRUCTURE & FORM:
- The poem is a hybrid elegy whose governing English ancestors are Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" and the Old English "The Ruin".
- Section A reenacts "The Ruin": split half-lines, caesura, giant-work diction, and lacunae. The cranes appear first not as modern machines but as a damaged manuscript artifact.
- Once the posthuman speaker arrives in B, the poem switches into blank verse. This is deliberate: Gray supplies the meditative English measure once the poem moves from archaeological fragment to reflective address.
- Documentary interruptions remain unmetered. They are the surviving bureaucratic fossil layer: terminal language outliving workers, shippers, and readers.

KEY FORMAL TECHNIQUES:
- "Articulate" in B is kept as a predicate adjective, not a verb: the cranes are jointed/articulated machines, eloquent ruins, and theorem-like forms.
- "Vessel." is repeated three times with semantic drift. In D it is a failed systems query. In E it has become prayer. In F it is no longer asking for an external ship; it names the cranes themselves as bearer, reliquary, and final form.
- The coda seeks apotheosis rather than mere simile. The fog first translates machine into dinosaur-image; sea-rise lets image become fate. "Made real" imports the pathos of a beloved artifact becoming fully what imagination and love always half-knew it to be, without dragging in nursery diction.
- The final return to full pentameter in L68–L70 is intentional. The close should not dissolve; it should tighten.

THEMES / ALLUSIONS:
- Gray: L11 echoes "The curfew tolls the knell of parting day", relocating elegy from churchyard to port.
- "The Ruin": A compresses ["Wrætlic is þes wealstan" / "enta geweorc"]{.poem} ruin-awe into industrial giant-work. The one-word line "held." and the lacunae imitate manuscript breakage as much as physical corrosion.
- Documentary / found-poem poetics: D lets tariff-log / terminal-language survive after the life it once administered. The contract outlives the contractors.
- Dinosaur / oil / steel recursion: the poem adopts mythic rather than geologically scrupulous compression. "Dead bone to oil" is symbolic genealogy, not petroleum chemistry. The point is recurrence of form through matter.
- "Portainer" is both historical brand and formal hinge. The stripping-layers catalogue moves from surface coatings and plans down to the crane's proper machine-name, then beneath that to the saurian archetype.
- "Cypress fell in tiers" localizes the 1989 Loma Prieta aftermath to Oakland and quietly echoes stacked decks, stacked boxes, and layered collapse.
- "Translated" in the last line is triple-purpose: carried across, rendered into another language, transformed into another form.

LOCAL / HISTORICAL ANCHORS:
- Middle Harbor is named elliptically, not as brochure copy. The poem wants place-pressure, not exposition.
- Outer Harbor appears in the documentary layer as administrative designation.
- "Portainer" points to Bay Area container-crane history and keeps Oakland specific without forcing a date-stamp into the body.
- The playground passage accepts poetic compression. Its task is not municipal exactitude; it is to set public play under industrial sublime, and then turn that play into dinosaur-chiasm.

RHYTHM / METER NOTES:
- OE lines in A are audited by stress-count and alliteration, not syllable-count.
- Most lines in B–E and key lines in F are 10-syllable blank verse; a few are intentionally short or long in F to mark revelation, compression, or ritual imperative.
- The loosest regular pentameter line is L15. Its count is clean, but its stress texture is denser than the surrounding verse. That is acceptable because "articulate" is carrying semantic freight worth the pressure.

REVISION LOCKS:
- Keep L11, L33, L43, L54, L63, and L70 unless a later revision improves form, local truth, and tonal pressure all at once.
- Do not flatten "The machines got work. The men got scrapped." into historical prose; its violence is part of the poem's historical violence.
- Do not revert the coda to "for real". The poem wants transfiguration, not colloquial actuality.
-->

# Colophon {.collapse}

<div class="abstract-collapse">
"Craneyard" was written in April 2026 to demonstrate my current AI poetry scaffolding workflow; this documents that workflow, to help explain to my fellow LLM users how decent creative LLM writing works.

This instance in particular shows the benefits of grounding a poem idea in multiple allusions with background research notes.

(Many LLM outputs throughout this process can be seen in the ["Elegy in a Craneyard" graveyard notes](/doc/ai/nn/transformer/gpt/claude/4/2026-04-05-gwern-elegyinacraneyard-llmgraveyard.md.txt){#gwern-2026-craneyard-graveyard}.)
</div>

It emphasizes high concepts to create "friction"/non-default outputs, [priming](!W "Priming (psychology)") to enrich the available options, and then search/iteration to overcome the "view from nowhere" and "neural nets are lazy" problems that make almost all chatbot-LLM-written poetry unsatisfying (and so strikingly impoverished compared to even small ancient "base models" [like GPT-3 was](/gpt-3 "‘GPT-3 Creative Fiction’, Gwern 2020")); see also [“At a Red Light” writing experiment](/fiction/hays "‘Human Perception at a Red Light’, Gwern & Pro 2026"), ["Bits Beat AI Slop"](/blog/2025/good-ai-samples "‘Adding Bits Beats AI Slop’, Gwern 2025"), ["Apollonian #1"](/fiction/lab-animals "‘Apollonian #1: The Counted &amp; the Crowned’, Gwern et al 2025") vs ["Spoilage"](/fiction/spoilage), etc.

The high concept is 'the Seaport of Oakland's iconic cranes meets the Old English poem "The Ruin" & Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard", as backed up by LLM-powered "deep research"' (to prime a large pool of concepts).

## Genesis

This concept came to me while driving back one night in March 2026 from a dinner party at S--- K---'s and watching the cranes yet again.
I had often thought to myself that they looked like dinosaurs in the fog (although not _Star Wars_ [AT-AT](!W) vehicles---a popular comparison but which turns out to be spurious), and wanted to write a poem on them, or perhaps generate an illustration, but there was not yet enough *there* there to write about.
(Yes, they do look like Brontosaurus or something... but then what? Just the bare comparison would barely support a haiku.)
It was a powerful image, but I needed more if it was to make sense either standalone or as some sort of followup to the "Silver Bird" encomium to modern aerospace.

Then [it suddenly hit me](/ai-daydreaming "‘LLM Daydreaming’, Gwern 2025"): "Elegy in a Churchyard" → "Elegy in a Craneyard" + "The Ruin" = good AI poem topic!
And then I further recalled the Internet meme, "And Yet a Trace of the True Self Exists in the False Self / Circle of Life", and it occurred to me that the ships and cranes were likely powered by fossil fuels which we would like to phase out, and so in both a literal and figurative sense, *were* dinosaurs.
This was a rich enough stew to work with, especially with the ending: the cranes vanishing into the mists of time, but translated into poetry, like the Roman ruins or the deceased in the church graveyard---not useless or fit only for dismantling, nor lost entirely, as long as there are witnesses to reflect on them and recall their glory days.
(And now that I have two poems, I can hope to find a third topic; perhaps something about datacenters like OpenAI's Arizona Stargate facility, which are the cathedrals of our era, thereby creating a trilogy of past / present / future---although that poem awaits its collision with some other idea, to give it depth and motivate a choice of formal structure.)

## The Scaffolding Workflow

I then wrote out a description of the idea, and the 2 poems and meme I wanted to incorporate, and defined my usual search process:

> ...To write this poem, brainstorm poem candidates.
> Write 20 poem candidates.
> To do so, for each candidate:
>
> 1. think up an interesting poem meter or formal structure.
> 2. list relevant poets, poems, schools of esthetics, or other inspirations.
> 3. define the formal structure of the poem in lines, meter, rhyme scheme, length, etc.
> 4. write out a summary of the poem, first as an abstract, and then stanza by stanza
> 5. write out some sample lines from the hypothetical candidate.
> 6. revise and critique the approach. After that,
> 7. write out the entire candidate poem.
> 8. revise and critique the candidate poem at least 3 times, starting with an analysis of how well it works globally, and down to the line by line level.
> 9. write out the final candidate poem.
> 10. analyze and evaluate; rate it 1–5 stars.
> 11. excerpt the best lines, images, phrases, and tricks from it.
>
>
> Below is additional material on the topic to help inspire you. After reading all reference materials below, begin the brainstorming process...

I included the databank, the text of the two poems, meme image and a few small thumbnails of the Oakland cranes for reference.

## Hybridization

I ran through several iterations before "hybridizing" (usually expanding the prompt and starting a fresh session, as a kind of poor-man's ["context distillation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15189 "‘Context Distillation: Learning by Distilling Context’, Snell et al 2022")), and then bringing in my more advanced 'scaffolding' requirements:

> ...I have used LLMs to brainstorm a large set of draft poems to recombine and modify into a final poem.
> We need to take the candidate hybrids and start carefully critiquing and revising them.
>
> **Procedure**
>
> As part of the revision, you will need to write out poems in an augmented form, with each line written normally and then in a [scansion-notated form in HTML comments](/style-guide#scansion).
> The exact form of scansion is copied below.
>
> To write this hybrid poem, brainstorm poem candidates based on this prompt and the samples.
> Write 5 hybrid poem candidates.
> To do so, for each candidate:
>
> 1. define the formal structure of the poem in lines, meter, rhyme scheme, length, etc.
> 2. write out a summary of the poem, first as an abstract, and then stanza by stanza
> 3. write out some sample lines from the hypothetical candidate. (Remember to include scansion.)
> 4. revise and critique the approach. After that,
> 5. write out the entire candidate poem. (Each line must have the scansion!)
> 6. revise and critique the candidate poem at least 3 times, starting with an analysis of how well it works globally, and down to the line by line level.
> 7. write out the final candidate poem.
> 8. analyze and evaluate; rate it 1–5 stars.
> 9. excerpt the best lines, images, phrases, and tricks from it.
>
> Finally, write out a single hybrid poem with all of the best parts of all poems to create a single unified, complete, poem...

The scaffolding requires the LLM to write out a formal analysis of each line.
This helps avoid regression while editing, and allows a laser-focus on the language.
(I speculate that it also helps with tonal or register or formal shifts, because the LLM can write those down explicitly, and this gives the chatbot personality "permission" to write freely.)

I developed the inline metadata trick for writing ["Your Hands and Mine"](/fiction/your-hands-and-mine), where I noticed that GPT would keep slipping up and introduce a rhyme/syllable error after fixing another, as if it had blind spots or its working memory got overloaded.
This made sense to me, because LLMs do not do well at highly reliably tracking a lot of implicit mutable state, like tracking syllable count *and* stress *and* rhyme per line across a whole poem, while also trying to actually think about the semantics and esthetics, so similar to arithmetic, we help the LLM by forcing it to write out notes to a scratchpad---and since it is Markdown, we can just stuff them into an easy HTML comment.
(The metadata headers are also fascinating to read in showing how close a reader LLMs can be, and skilled at literary criticism and verbalizing things I would struggle to, while still struggling with phonetics; the impression one has is of a born-deaf professor of rhetoric analyzing a poem at the blackboard.)

For example, the first line in the Markdown source looks more like this:

~~~{.Markdown}
<!-- SECTION A (L01–L10, OE + lacunae): job = invoke ruin and

These crane-corpses, wondrous— / calamity crumpled them,

## Curation & Authorship

Once I had a final hybrid poem from each LLM, I had Claude choose the best one to work with, because it seemed like all the LLMs consistently ranked its poems highest on average.

Only at this point did I begin reading the poem in earnest and making manual edits.
Primarily, adding reference hyperlinks and adding metadata commentary, although I also had to decide a few factual questions: the LLMs were troubled by the potential overclaiming of the Oakland playground, and I decided the esthetic effect was too important and if anyone was misled, that was permitted by poetic license.

After a first manual pass to acquaint myself with it, I then ran [the _Poetry_ magazine prompt](/fiction/this-last-pain-graveyard#poetry-magazine-prompt) to refine it.

This yielded only a handful of edits before I declared it done.
(The biggest edit was killing a line explicitly stating the dinosaur/oil meme.)
I then checked it all against the [Manual of Style](/style-guide), as usual finding many errors.
Then I wrote this Colophon.

## Assessment & Open Questions

Overall, this took about a day of work, most of it spent waiting on long LLM runs and copy-pasting back and forth with little editorial or creative input on my end after the initial setup.

Authorship-wise, I list myself as the primary author, despite doing even less wordsmithing than usual and not making any 'crucial' decisions in terms of curation or selection (similar to the major decisions I made in creating ["_Tilakkhana_"](/fiction/tilakkhana "‘<em>Tilakkhana</em>: The 3 Scars of Existence’, Gwern et al 2025") out of several unrelated samples).
If you read through the early samples in [the graveyard file](#gwern-2026-craneyard-graveyard), there's less variety than in past experiments like the ["This Last Pain" graveyard](/fiction/this-last-pain-graveyard "‘‘The Fourth Truth Of Pain’ Graveyard’, Gwern et al 2022"); and when they are drastically different, they tend to be weak and flabby, especially the more transhumanist sci-fi-heavy takes.
Nothing here wound up truly *surprising me*---there was no idea which, upon search, escaped its creator and surprised them with unexpected depth.
So in this case, I had an unusually specific, detailed high concept as a starting point, which constrained the LLMs too much to rise to the level where I would list Claude as the first author.
And this may explain why [Claude could shine here](#claude-overperformance).

Had I intervened more, I think I would have tried to force more experimentation in meter---perhaps lean much more into the damaged fragments idea, or try to invent an entirely new meter to reflect a strange posthuman future somehow (similar to the invented alliterative meter in "Apollonian #1" which tries to reinvent Pindar); and considered the politics of Oakland more, as the deep research reports turned up interesting American politics I was unfamiliar with, and as it is, there is not much commentary there beyond a vague technological inevitability.

But I didn't feel I had the time or energy to do so, as [Inkhaven 2](https://vishalblog.substack.com/p/for-your-consideration-inkhaven-2) was running, and I am backlogged on several poetry projects as it is (the "coastal spondaic" invented meter, or properly writing up the "pressure-cooker" meter of "Apollonian #1").
Perhaps I also lacked faith in the LLMs being able to find an 'interesting' meter which could reflect all of the influences simultaneously, and settled for a more straightforward historical progression of meters?

And a few parts wound up making me uneasy with how much they sound like chatbots:
["The children played at cranes. The cranes played back / at dinosaurs. The joke outlived the crowd."]{.poem}, or ["like theorems in a language no one speaks."]{.poem}, or ["Between the ruin and the monument: / the loneliest address a structure knows."]{.poem}
They are too pat and aphoristic, too tidy, too regular and metronome-like; I would have improved them, or at least considered them at greater length, if I had more time and wanted to intervene more in this demonstration.

I was also not particularly sold on the triptych of ["Vessel"]{.poem}, which doesn't function as elegantly as ["so much depends"]{.poem} does in "Silver Bird"; the repetition is OK but it lacks insight.

But overall, I am satisfied with the result and with how little manual effort I had to put into it with my refined workflow, and I hope the simplicity of my prompts will inspire people to be more ambitious with their own LLM usage (and not settle for the kind of AI slop they usually do).

*Demand more of your LLMs*, and give them the data and time that they need to really think!
