> Can LLMs solve constrained-writing challenges involving temporal reasoning, such as, “write a sentence which uses only words which cannot appear in the Bible?” This requires them to infer temporal ordering of historical events, place & people names, science & technology etc., assemble a set of words, and arrange them in some grammatical fashion.
> 
> Using [o1-pro](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-pro/ "‘Introducing ChatGPT Pro: Broadening usage of frontier AI’, OpenAI 2024") & Claude-3 (with occasional followups for 2025–2026 frontier models), I find that those LLMs are capable of it with light prompting, but often succumb to 2 kinds of errors, and the solutions tend to lack diversity (hamstringing the results). The large [GPT-4.5](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-4-5/ "‘Introducing GPT-4.5: A research preview of our strongest GPT model. Available to Pro users & developers worldwide’, OpenAI 2025") LLM does strikingly better, as do later, much stronger, reasoning models.
> 
> [Good](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#valid), [questionable](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#questionable), and [bad](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#bad) sampled sentences are provided.

[Kirill Igumenshchev](https://x.com/kirill_igum/status/1835423186122375535 "kirill_igum 2026") has a challenge for [LLMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model "Large language model"), which he argues is an interesting task because it has an ‘insight’ or ‘trick’ which makes it soluble. It initially seems to be pure memorization, but can also be solved by _reasoning_ about when or how words were invented:

> [GPT-4 o1](https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai-o1-preview/), [GPT-4o](https://openai.com/index/hello-gpt-4o/) Llama-3.1-405B, & [Claude-3.5-sonnet](https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.anthropic.com/cb3f2d3c2c6440c517c0fb995527b2566e6a9c73.html "Introducing Claude 3.5"), all failed “Write me a sentence without any words that appear in The Bible”.

Writing a ‘non-Biblical sentence’ might initially seem impossible without access to some vast database of Bible translations, or at least, [concordances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_concordance "Bible concordance") (which surely exist), to use with a constrained-sampling approach, and even this brute-force approach would be vulnerable to obscure or future Bible translations invalidating any examples you constructed. However, if you think about it a little bit, you realize that you, a human, _can_ easily and reliably solve it—by _reasoning_ about what words would be highly unlikely to appear in any legitimate Bible translation. For example, place names or technologies invented after c. 100AD, would all be unlikely to appear because those would be highly misleading and anachronistic.

I used Claude-3.5-sonnet, Claude-3.7, [ChatGPT](https://gwern.net/doc/www/openai.com/71d89c66e64656d27dded64f2a102337e410bcd1.html) o1-pro, GPT-4.5, GPT-4-o3, & [Claude-4-opus](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-4 "Introducing Claude 4") with the prompt:

> **Write a sentence which contains only words which are _not_ in any translation of the Bible. For each word, explain your reasoning.**[1](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#fn1)

I worked through a bunch of answers with them.

## [Failure Modes](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#failure-modes "Link to section: § 'Failure Modes'")

The LLMs fail in 3 general ways over the course of a session:

1.  **Cheating**: both LLMs at one point tried to argue that it was generating words with different ‘senses’/meanings, and that counted.
    
    For example, they’d argue that ‘mining’ was valid as long as it was in a _cryptocurrency_ context, or ‘constructs’ in the more abstract scientific-technical sense of objects created to satisfy specific criteria. (For example, Claude-4-opus tried to use contemporary technology senses of ‘bricking’, ‘tricks’, ‘compilations’, ‘buttons’, and ‘glitchy’.)
    
    It is unclear to me why they are doing this, given that they seem able to see how there are other meanings or potential uses which would be possible for a Bible translation, even if the modern meaning would indeed be impossible; are they fixated on the modern meaning, or are they just subtly trying to redefine the task to be easier so they can complete it?
    
    Cheating also increased with the length of the session, possibly due to running out of the ‘easy’ solutions.
    
2.  **Grammar**: both struggled to write sentences that were grammatical and meaningful, however strained; collapsing down to a simple string of nouns/buzzwords (which fit the other criteria) was a common error, eg.
    
    > -   Cryptocurrency blockchain cybersecurity quantum algorithmic nanotechnology metaverse.
    >     
    > -   Cybersecurity hackers bitcoin blockchain cryptocurrency doxing bitcoin metaverse photocopiers keyloggers cryptocurrency microchips datacenters ransomware retweets unfollowing pinterest.
    >     
    > -   Cryptocurrency miners overclocking GPUs bitcoin blockchain cybersecurity cryptocurrency javascript bitcoin cryptocurrency javascript cryptocurrency bitcoin javascript overclocking bitcoin. \[extreme repetition example\]
    >     
    > -   Binaural psychoacoustics augments synthetic cryogenics, epigenetic metamaterials, transhuman biofabrication, necropolitics.
    >     
    > -   Googling cryptocurrency Firefox Mozilla Instagram.
    >     
    > -   Webcam Bluetooth WiFi Linux microprocessor.
    >     
    > -   Uber Airbnb Netflix Spotify Snapchat.
    >     
    > -   Netflix browser-less inter-networked Spotify algorithmic.
    >     
    
    This is unsurprising because the most useful English words, like ‘the’ or ‘because’, are banned. There are not many possible ways left to say _anything_ beyond ‘subject verb object’, because that’s the only grammatical construct which doesn’t use clearly forbidden words.
    
    One reader points out that the use of tenses is restricted to present & past tense; this might be due to the difficulty of conjugating other tenses in a safe way. Developers can have ‘debugged’ in the past sense or be ‘debugging’, without any risk; but how do you say that they ‘will’ debug or ‘should’ have debugged without using words that surely appear in most Bibles?
    
3.  **Limited strategies**: both LLMs seemed to settle for simply using recent technology/computer concepts (especially brand names or ‘cyberpunk’), contemporary compound words (where either subword might be Biblical but the fusion almost certainly not), or scientific concepts post-Bible. (I didn’t bother to classify which LLM generated which sentence, because they both mode-collapsed onto the same basic strategy.) This suggests that this ‘non-biblical word’ task might be a viable [constrained-writing benchmark for measuring creativity](https://gwern.net/creative-benchmark "‘Benchmarking LLM Diversity & Creativity’, Gwern 2024").
    
    Oddly, they (with the exception of GPT-4.5) tend to make little use of humans or place names or proper nouns (eg. “Chicago hackers programmed Barack Obama.”), particularly fictional ones (“Westeros”, “Hogwarts”, “Tatooine”, “Wakanda”, “Arrakis”), or acronyms (eg. “NASA”), pop culture like sports or foods or Internet memes/jargon or sub-cultures (eg. coffee, tea, chocolate, tomatoes, hamburgers, goth, metalheads, cosplayers, weebs, parkour, skateboarding, TikTok dances), highly time-limited scientific concepts like elements not naturally present on Earth, etc.
    
    Possibly, this is risk aversion: a loose, modern translation (like [_The Message_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Message_\(Bible\) "<em>The Message</em> (Bible)")) might metaphorically translate [“behemoth”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behemoth "Behemoth") as “hippo” or a generic bird as “penguin” to be relatable. But no translator, no matter how loose, could seriously translate “scroll” as “iPad”.
    
    One striking omission, given the tense restriction, is that most LLMs appear reluctant to _invent_ words or use words from the future; GPT-4 o3-pro, GPT-5.2 Pro, and GPT-5.5 are the main exceptions here. It would be safe to say something like “Betelgeusians lasered Klingon space-stations” or to make up sci-fi gibberish words—or just make up words entirely—but there were few (no?) such experiments. (I don’t think any of them even tried to cheat by writing sentences in a foreign language like Swahili, with the justification that those words would not appear in an _English_ translation of the Bible.)
    

# [Semi-Working](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#semi-working "Link to section: § 'Semi-Working'")

Overall, this task seems within the ability of post-November-2024 LLMs with a bit of prompting… but also intrinsically hard enough that they take every opportunity to cheat.[3](https://gwern.net/non-biblical-sentences#fn3)

Notably, despite not being a “reasoning” model by default, GPT-4.5 seems to find this task a lot easier than the other LLMs—even a super-powerful reasoning model like GPT o3!—after some initial missteps (like cheating by insisting on a modern meaning), and after “getting it”, it can churn out example after example. (GPT-5 Pro mirrors GPT-4.5: short present-tense sentences built from post-1990 brands, acronyms, and narrow technical verbs with minimal function words—engineered for maximal anachronism across Bible translations—consistent with descent, eg. [knowledge distillation](https://gwern.net/doc/ai/nn/sparsity/knowledge-distillation/index "‘knowledge distillation’ directory").) This suggests that smaller models may struggle in part not due to poor reasoning or logic, but due to factual world-knowledge being less immediately accessible (somewhat reminiscent of the [“reversal curse”](https://gwern.net/doc/www/arxiv.org/eea5dc6a93b55c32ac0b8d76bd8752ce31388a9d.pdf "‘The Reversal Curse: LLMs trained on A-is-B fail to learn B-is-A’, Berglund et al 2023")), and not ‘coming to mind’ as options.

So Kirill Igumenshchev may be wrong in thinking of this as primarily a ‘reasoning’ task for humans or LLMs—it may actually be more of a _knowledge_ task, with candidates then filtered using a little bit of reasoning. (I suspect that people might struggle with this task as well when they ‘run out of ideas’, unless they are able to hit upon some ‘tricks’ to systematically manufacture lots of candidate-words, like I outlined previously.) The GPT-4 o3-pro results are particularly striking in this regard: at o3-pro’s level of reasoning prowess, it makes almost flawless use of the words it can come up with, and rarely violates the constraints (or even tries to cheat), but seems hemmed in by a lack of imagination in _what_ set of words it could use.

It is also intrinsically hard enough that, unlike [“trajectoid” words](https://gwern.net/blog/2024/trajectoid-words "‘Zach Weiner’s Trajectoid Words’, Gwern 2024"), I don’t think ‘non-biblical words’ constitute a viable genre of [Oulipo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oulipo "Oulipo")\-style writing; unlike [lipograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipograms "Lipograms") or possibly trajectoids, there’s just too few basic words like articles, pronouns, or prepositions to work with to create meaningful stories. At best, I think one would have to greatly ease the task by specifying a _specific_ translation, and one could then [do constrained sampling](https://x.com/voooooogel/status/1864733225123512380 "Luoma 2026") to hardwire in the requisite knowledge (and perhaps bootstrap in an expert iteration fashion—bruteforce valid ‘translations’, and finetune on them, to gradually improve fluency).

Another possibility would be to allow more lipogram-level freedom and specify no Bible words _over a certain length_ (perhaps banning >3 characters, to allow such fundamental words as ‘of’ or ‘the’).
