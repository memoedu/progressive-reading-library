## Part 1 - Howard Nielsen Interview

**Michael Nielsen:** So it's Saturday December 27th in the United States and I think December 28th in Australia. And I'm going to interview my father, Howard Nielsen, about his ne'er-do-well life and crimes. I don't know, Dad, let's see. I have a long list of questions here, which I'll try not to refer to. We might as well start with—actually, I realized I'm not sure I know where you were born. I know you grew up in Monash, but were you actually born in Monash or Barmera or one of those places?

**Howard Nielsen:** Yeah, born in Barmera, which is a few kilometers away from Monash, and Barmera is on the shores of Lake Bonney. So that's what Barmera is most known for, quite a big lake off the Murray River.

**Michael:** So is Barmera bigger than Monash then? I mean it's large enough to have a hospital or—I mean were you born in a hospital actually?

**Howard:** Yeah, yeah, it's got a hospital and a school. Football teams, the sort of trappings of a country town. I don't know how many people there would be. Monash had about 300. I'd imagine Barmera would probably be 1,200 or 1,500 or something like that.

**Michael:** Okay, so Barmera is probably sort of comparable in size to Samford in fact and Monash is quite a bit smaller. It's almost Croydon size.

**Howard:** Yeah, pretty much. I think Samford would be a bit bigger in terms of population than Barmera, I haven't kept track of it over the years. So I don't know what it was then. But the biggest other town close was Berri and Glossop and the high school was actually located in Glossop because it was sort of halfway between Barmera and Berri, I think.

**Michael:** So did you grow up in the same house all the time as a kid?

**Howard:** Yeah. Well, that's sort of yes or no. When I was a bub, Mum and Dad lived in a house that was probably only 100 square metres or something like that. And then when I was young, not so young, eight to ten I probably was, I can remember Dad and a colleague friend of his actually making concrete blocks that they then used to expand the size of the house. So I lived in the same house, but it was an expanded house. Like Besser Blocks really they were that they made. I can still remember them making those out of a mould and concrete. So that would have doubled maybe even, yeah at least doubled the size of the house.

**Michael:** Okay, so from 100 metres to 200 square metres, that's still pretty small, but you didn't have nine siblings, you just had a few. Wow. I'm guessing there probably wasn't a whole lot of insulation or whatnot.

**Howard:** I wouldn't know. But I don't remember insulation being used, but it could easily have been. Obviously, it was the sort of weather that nowadays you definitely have insulation for—the very hot summers and the very cold early morning winters.

**Michael:** Did it get cold in Monash? It probably did.

**Howard:** Oh yeah, quite often early morning we used to catch the bus at five past seven to go to the high school. And it was not unusual to be zero degrees or down to—I think the coldest I remember is minus seven or minus eight. And hoses frozen with water in them. But yeah, it was definitely jumper weather in winter. And then during, by the time 10 o'clock came around in the morning, it was full sunlight, hardly any rain during winter. Well that's the only time we did have rain was winter, but it was hardly any. It was, I think, because Monash was on the desert rainfall line. So desert rainfall was 10 inches was the guiding light there. So you can see that there was quite a bit of sunlight really, but not much rain.

And so the only reason we're able to have grapes and apricots and whatever was because of the irrigation from the Murray River which irrigated about another 25 inches which brought up the total amount of water that was usable by the block owners or fruit block owners to about 25 or 30, I can't remember.

**Michael:** Yeah, that makes sense. I always kind of wondered a little bit about that because it does seem—I have a vague memory of going to Monash, but I do remember thinking it was incredibly dry. Not quite desert, but sort of getting on towards that dry.

**Howard:** Well, it was a desert rainfall line. Yeah, I mean, it was actually right on that line. Yeah, you wanted to say one side of it was desert and the other side wasn't. When you look at Monash, the valley was set up beautifully for irrigation from the channel going around the rim and all the water gravitating down through the fruit blocks. But that caused a whole lot of salt to be brought to the surface in the basement of the valley, if you like, which is where the football fields and tennis courts and bowls club was.

**Michael:** So really the whole place basically works because of the Murray River. If the Murray wasn't there, there'd be nothing there.

**Howard:** That's right. So what they did was there were a couple of brothers, the Chaffey brothers, who were actually from either Canada or the United States, I can't remember which, and they were irrigation experts, and they were brought in to decide whether or not it was a viable place for irrigated stuff and decided it was. And so Monash was one of those locations where having pumped water out of the Murray to a tower in Berri which was where the pump was, then that water got gravity-fed through the channel system out to the other places. And of course there were different parts of the Murray River that were fed up in different ways. We just happened to be on the one where the Berri pumping station was the closest to us and most relevant for irrigation.

**Michael:** What did your dad do? I remember you telling me stories about apricots and apricot farming, but I don't actually know whether that was the main thing or whether that was just sort of a side.

**Howard:** The main thing was dried sultanas. So grew sultanas and then they were picked and like the dried sultanas you get now in packages at supermarkets. But there was also some wine grapes. So there were two sorts of wine grapes, but they were probably only made up 15% of the grape harvest. And then what else was there? Apricots were the other ones. So it would have made up only 10% probably of what was irrigated. And I don't know, it might have been an acre or something like that. But overall, the fruit block—Dad had two fruit blocks, one close to where their house was, and then another one which I think they must have bought a few years later, about three k's away, so it was sort of the second block. And it was sultanas and some wine grapes as well, but no apricots.

**Michael:** How did your parents come to be in Monash?

**Howard:** I'll go back far enough. My grandfather, Rudolf Nielsen, he was from Denmark, so he was born in Denmark, and he was sent out from Denmark to join the merchant navy because he was a bit promiscuous with one of the household maids and got her pregnant definitely. So he's not alive now to say that's not true. So he joined the Navy, parents told him to shoo. And then he jumped ship in Adelaide when they got to Adelaide and he met my grandmother to be Rachel Clarissa Frick. So she was of German and English origin and she lived in Mannum which is just not that far out of Adelaide but still out of Adelaide at the time by quite a bit in terms of horse travel and all that sort of stuff. And they met and I don't know how they met, but they got married. She was only 16 and he would have been 22 or something like that. So that's, as I understand it, that's how the Nielsens began.

**Michael:** Can I just ask, so he jumped ship, meaning he would have been missed and in trouble? Is that kind of the implication there? Wow, okay. So he ran away from the Navy.

**Howard:** Yeah. Well, merchant Navy is different to the real Navy, I guess. So I don't know. Certainly he wasn't the only person who did that. A lot of people who were on those ships came out and just stayed. I don't know what category he was in.

**Michael:** This would have been about what? The 1890s or something? About that time?

**Howard:** Yeah I should know that actually. I've got some details on that. I've got a scrapbook sort of thing here somewhere so I could go back to that. So what happened was that when he went away to war as a soldier, when he came back the country gave returned soldiers something in return so he got the fruit block. So a whole lot of these settlements were actually soldier settlements. So there was like an RSL equivalent in central Monash, a memorial hall, quite a big hall. So he got that block and a little house. That was the house that we ended up living in. No, was it that one? Yeah, I think it was. But anyway, so when he came back from war, he was in Mannum prior to that. They shifted to Monash because that's where the blocks were. And so, yeah, I don't know exactly how long he worked the block for.

Yeah, there was quite a bit of—so they had 10 kids. Rachel and Rudolph had 10 kids. And I think you probably know two of them died. One of them died from pneumonia when she was about 30 or something like that. And the other one was killed. I don't know whether I've told you that story or not.

**Howard:** That's right, Auntie Mary. Well, and of course, I didn't get to know her. Obviously, she died when she was 13, and my dad would have been like 14 or 15 when she was killed. So she was raped and murdered basically within earshot of the house that Grandma lived in.

Yeah, we're still not totally sure what happened with grandfather, with Rudolph. Because he went to war—he went towards the end of the war, I think in 1917, to England and to France. And he was in, I think, artillery. But I don't think he saw an awful lot of action, but he certainly saw—he was part of the crew that sort of had to disassemble stuff from France back into hospitals and so horses and human beings and what have you. So he obviously suffered mentally from his experience. And so he was actually in a mental institution at the time that young Mary was killed. So you can imagine that Rachel was—that's a whole lot of burden on her for all sorts of reasons, but also probably on all the kids at some level. So Dad was, I think, third or fourth oldest. I think he had two brothers, Frank and Darcy, and Frank was older than Dad, and Dad was older than Darcy. And then Auntie Agnes was the oldest auntie and I only ever saw her a few times because she actually lived in Melbourne.

**Michael:** Actually, I met Uncle Darcy when we were in—I remember, in fact, we went and played tennis at the—I don't remember, it might have been Berri, I think, where we were playing tennis, and then we had lunch afterwards, or maybe it was before. He was a very—I don't know what the right term is, garrulous or something. He seemed very amiable and full of interesting stories and just great company. Basically, he was a great host.

**Howard:** Yeah, well he was—oh yeah, no, his whole hail fellow well met sort of thing, definitely Darcy. I got on really well with Darcy because he had a good sense of humour and always treated you like a real human being and all that sort of stuff. And liked his sport. I remember playing him once in tennis because he was probably in his late 30s at that point and I was only 13, I was playing A-grade tennis and I ended up playing—I was top of the Monash team at that age which was a bit unusual and it came to the point where I had to play Darcy one week.

**Michael:** Inter-family rivalry or intra-family rivalry.

**Howard:** That's right. Because Dad was basically, my Dad was essentially a better tennis player than Darcy, but Darcy was a very good player. And they won the upper Murray doubles titles a dozen times over the years. So Dad and Darcy sort of got along really well at one level. Then there was one sort of event that was not good.

Basically, during the Second World War, the general rule of thumb was that if you had property and producing goods and what have you, then you're expected to look after the farm. And so not everybody could go off to war. Somebody had to stay home and look after the farm, basically. And because Rachel was by herself at that point, particularly was just a female, that wasn't seen to be appropriate. So one of the boys had to stay home and should have been Darcy, because he was the youngest. That's the way it was supposed to work. But unknown to everybody else, Darcy decided to join up secretly. So he went and joined before Dad could join. They were about to do it at Moris at the same time. So of course, at the time, Dad was very angry with that because he felt that he should have gone and done his thing. Years afterwards, in talking to Dad, of course, he said war is a stupid thing and it's no good for anybody, probably with him, his personality was such that he was probably more likely to have been negatively affected by war than Darcy, who was a bit more flippant about those things. But Darcy actually joined the Air Force. And I don't know whether he was a pilot or whether he was part of the crew. And my uncle Frank, who was older than Dad, he joined the army.

**Michael:** And so your dad stayed in Monash through that time, right?

**Howard:** Yeah, and what happened was, part of what happened during wartime was that of course women were enlisted in the land army to actually help with food production and what have you, where men previously done it. So my mum joined the land army from Bundaberg and then she was sent down to the Riverland to pick grapes. So that's how they met.

**Howard:** And we wouldn't be chatting. So yeah, so that's sort of the back story. That they got married—no, but I've got it here somewhere. I think it's 1944 or something like that.

**Howard:** I might have been after the war, can't remember exactly, but I'm pretty sure if it was during wartime it was towards the end of the wartime, so 1944-45. **Michael:** Okay, so your grandfather, Rudolph, you said that he had some significant problems after the war. So what ended up happening to him and what happened to Rachel? How did things kind of pan out there?

**Howard:** Well, it's a bit—people are a bit unsure. There've been a couple of my cousins have taken it upon themselves to try to piece together what happened and why it happened and what have you. But if I was to put a story to it now, it would be that when he came back from the war, he wasn't the same self somehow or another. And he wasn't—he didn't act appropriately at home, whatever that means. I'm not sure what that means, whether he was angry or cranky or whatever else with kids and or Rachel or whatever, but to the point where obviously they felt that he was better off in basically an asylum is the way it was called then. So a couple of the cousins have tried to find out what he was in for and we've been told that a whole lot of records were burnt or no longer available or whatever else. But that wasn't something that was uncommon that people get told that because people don't want to tell them what's actually happened.

So he died while he was in that place in—would have been five years younger, before I was born. So 1946, I think. Well, either that or he was 46 years old. One of the two. But anyway, again, that's all in the same block of information that I can send.

**Michael:** Yeah, so, I mean that's a very sad story and it sounds like he was in the asylum for quite a long time, which must have been hard for him and hard for Rachel.

**Howard:** It sounds like it, yeah. So I remember I was one of the few cousins that was in Monash by the time I got to sort of teenagehood or whatever else, all the others, the brothers had all gone off to get their blocks, all the sisters had married and had moved out of Monash. It was only really my mum and dad still in Monash. Darcy was in Loxton, which was probably 25, 30 k's away. So we were the only ones in Monash and Dad was designated to be the, end up being the owner of the block. So he worked for more or less nothing really. Just his board and lodging and some dollars to keep the block going on the basis that when Grandma died he would get the block. Unfortunately what happened was that when she did die a couple of Dad's brothers and sisters put in a claim on some of the value of the block and so that caused a bit of consternation amongst the family. So I never really got to the full bottom of that, but I know it was something that Dad felt very sad about. I don't know which of the cousins were responsible for that. But I'm pretty sure it wasn't Darcy and it wasn't Frank. I think they were okay with it all. Of course, they were already earning their keep.

Mum was a hairdresser by trade. She had her own hairdressing salon in Berri and Monash and then at home. In fact my bedroom was next door to the—it was the next room in the house to the little hairdressing salon she ran.

**Michael:** Wow. So where did you go to primary school? Actually, did you go to kindergarten or anything?

**Howard:** I went to—there was no kindergartens then. I went to Monash Primary School. Monash Primary from year one. There were about 90 kids there.

**Howard:** Yeah, that's right, it's from the district really. So the nearest other primary school was Glossop Primary School, which is probably about seven or eight kilometres away. And then there was Barmera, which was a lot bigger, and Berri, which is a lot bigger again, and then Renmark, which was bigger and further away. **Howard:** Cobdogla, yeah. And so we played a bit of sport. Well, the good thing about, from my point of view, the good thing about Monash Primary School was that because there are fewer kids there than most of the big primary schools, you got to play everything. So you even got to play netball. So the boys used to play the girls at netball, which was ahead of its time really.

**Michael:** At probably about what age—11 or 12—the girls probably won pretty handily because they would have grown earlier.

**Howard:** Yeah, possibly. I think we were told we were a bit too rough. To ease it up a bit. I mean, you only played like a few games over a few years. It wasn't like there was a full complement or anything like that. And yeah, I still remember a lot of the kids. Actually, there's a photo that was taken in 1956 of year one, two and three class at Monash Primary School and during the centenary year or whatever it was they blew it up to be full size of a wall in the room. The wall was about five metres long and two or three metres high. And actually it was when we went down to Adelaide for Stuart's footy that I went to the school and met the woman at the front desk who was a woman called Lorraine Opest, which I remembered. And she didn't have a clue who I was, of course, we had just walked in the front door. But she said, after we got chatting, she said, you might be able to help out. We've got this photo on the wall and we don't know some of the kids.

And anyway, it's amazing what your brain must do because I went in there and all the kids that were in my grade, I knew. And they said, we didn't remember that was Robert Gillies or whatever it was. And the others, years one and two or one, two and three, I can't remember. Sharon was in that photo as well. So she had her friends there. So I pretty well, I think—

**Howard:** I was just year one, yeah. So because I was January, I would have been the youngest kid in the class.

**Howard:** No, it was just, whatever you were, if you were on the 1st of January, if you were 5, you were supposed to go to school. Of course, I knew a bit about it because Sharon was already at school for two years. But anyway, I had a bit of an induction into school life that was not very pleasant.
