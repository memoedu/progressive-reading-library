## Part 3 - Howard Nielsen Interview

## Moving to Brisbane and the Linton Cup

**Michael Nielsen:** Okay, so the next session. Last time we got up to you had basically finished high school and had moved up to Brisbane a year or so after your mum and dad had moved to Bundaberg. Do you want to just say a little bit about what your first impressions of Brisbane were? You moved up to go to Teachers College, right?

**Howard:** Yeah. So how it happened was at the same time as I was supposed to be going to Teachers College, the Australian tennis open was being held in Milton, Brisbane, and I was in the South Australian Linton Cup team playing at Milton more or less while college started. So I missed the first two weeks of college while I was playing Linton Cup, and also played in the Australian Open Juniors. So that was the first year that all the pros came back—Laver and Hoad and a few others. They took over all the spots that we previously had the previous year.

**Michael:** So this is 1969 then.

**Howard:** Yeah. Would have been the year 69. The interesting thing about going to tennis—I got jumped off the plane and had to go to our motels. We were staying at Indooroopilly, but the taxi driver cracked himself when I asked him to go to Indooroopilly. And then we had to also go to Coorparoo. So we got a very good education from the taxi driver about some of the more interesting suburb names.

**Michael:** Yeah.

**Howard:** So I'm not sure where that warrants a feature in the book. Anyway, so the idea was that we stayed as a team for the entire two weeks in a place at Indooroopilly. And I think we ended up third or something in our Linton Cup team. I actually played—Mike Lorenzo and I played on the centre court at Milton in the junior doubles, which we lost. So that was a nice little experience, even though of course there was an early morning match, so there were hardly any people there watching, but that was good.

**Michael:** So this would have—how did you actually get up to Brisbane? Did you drive up or did you fly?

**Howard:** No, flew. So the interesting thing—I was picked in the South Australian Linton Cup team, they knew that I was going to stay in Queensland afterwards. So if they wanted to, they could have picked somebody else for following years, sort of investing in another person rather than me again, because it was the second year that I played in the cup. But no, they paid for all my fares and all that sort of stuff, except for a return fare from Brisbane to Adelaide. So everything had been set up and that's how that worked.

**Michael:** How much did you actually send up?

**Howard:** I can't remember the details, but I probably sent up just a few packages of stuff and then it would have mainly had just clothes, tennis rackets, a few other things. Anything that had been at home in Monash that would have been useful would have already been sent up the previous year—Mum and Dad would have taken that up.

**Michael:** Okay. Alright, so you would have gone to retrieve it from Bundaberg.

**Howard:** Yeah, but the thing is I was going to wherever I was staying—I didn't need beds or wardrobes or anything like that. Wherever I was staying, either at Bundaberg Hotel or with a family in Brisbane, which is where I did stay for three years, obviously that was like board and lodging.

## Living with the O'Sullivans

**Michael:** Did you—actually, I'm really curious, where was that place?

**Howard:** That was at Wellington Road at Banyo. Banyo's out in the sticks a little bit at the time, just another suburb really. Fairly old suburb in some ways. And the O'Sullivan family were a really nice family. Terry and Lois and two older boys that were older than me and two young girls that were younger than me. They were 10 I think and 12 or something like that. And I had a little sleep-out bed on the veranda sort of thing. Spent three years there. When I think about it, I didn't really think about it at the time—it was just what happened. Had no car, so I went by train from Banyo Station or Virginia Station, one of the two, to Wooloowin Station, which is where college was.

**Michael:** It's not that far, I guess. I don't know, it would be what, a few stops, probably?

**Howard:** Yeah, probably half a dozen, I think. Northgate and Nundah. Actually probably maybe only four.

**Michael:** How much did it cost at the time, do you remember?

**Howard:** I don't. Well, as college students we had free travel, free rail, because we were under a bond from the state government. They paid us to go to college because they were short of teachers and they wanted to guarantee supply.

**Michael:** This was probably—Joh was probably premier by this point? Yeah.

**Howard:** He would have been, yeah. That was interesting in itself.

**Michael:** I guess you probably wouldn't have heard of Joh in South Australia, but you would have heard of him pretty quick once you came to Queensland.

**Howard:** Not really. Pretty much, yeah. I had a bit to do with Joh actually at one point when I was at college.

## Meeting Premier Joh Bjelke-Petersen

**Michael:** Oh yeah, what happened?

**Howard:** Well, it's a long story how it got to this, so I'll just give the short story to start with. At college, apart from doing a bit of studying, learning a few things, and going to prac and all that sort of stuff, tuning into lectures—it was like an extended secondary school really. A bit more sophisticated, but not much more sophisticated than year 11 or 12.

Somewhere or other, I got roped into being nominated as president of the student union. And so I duly got elected. Part of what we did at the end of the first year of that was we advocated to increase the allowance for teachers college students. That was knocked back a few times. We went and advocated. I probably met about a dozen ministers along the way, because it was a fairly important thing really, because the allowance that they had for most people was really hardly enough to subsist on. So there was a good argument for how that should change.

But at the end of the first year, my first year of college, '69, we realised that Joh's daughter was going to be a college student the following year, 1970. And so we hatched a little plan, which was to take our plight a little bit more in the public arena so that newspapers sort of ploughed the story about teachers college students not having enough money, and therefore affecting quality of teaching, etcetera. But what was actually happening, and we thought it might happen, was that—I think Beth was her name, really nice woman. We had a chat to her just generally speaking about stuff, and let her know that our plan was to probably go on strike maybe in the first few weeks of college. There was in no way a threat, it really was just "by the way, you might need to know, it's going to be announced shortly."

Anyway, before the strike could be announced, we got a phone call from Joh. Joh said, "We'd like to chat to you folks." So we went in, a little group of three or four of us there, had a chat with Joh about stuff, and we duly got the biggest increase of any union ever in the history of Queensland, which was from $10 to $13—so we had a 30% increase per week. And mostly, most people were paying about $7 or $8 a week for their board or rent or whatever.

**Michael:** Was she at Kedron Park Teachers College? \[Yes\] Okay, yeah, so kind of very awkward for her, no matter what, if you guys have decided that you're gonna go on strike and now all of a sudden—what does she do? She's actually in a pretty tough spot. So it's an interesting situation.

**Howard:** Well, I think Joh was smart enough to realise that he didn't want to put his daughter into that position. Small amount of money was involved anyway, and they should have done it per se. So that was a meeting with Joh—he actually wanted to know whether I could speak Danish, because he could speak some Danish.

**Michael:** Really?

**Howard:** He was born in Denmark. Anyway, it was quite an amicable meeting, all over in 15, 20 minutes. And the parting remarks were, "Okay, yeah, no, I'm sure we can do something about that. We'll let you know probably next week. I'll be talking to Cabinet tomorrow or next Monday or whatever it was." So there it was.

## Music and College Life

**Michael:** So just to go back—I was just sort of thinking about stuff that you brought up to Queensland with you. Obviously clothes and other items like that. It's occurring to me, I don't know whether or not you listened to music or had a record player or read books or did anything much like that. Did you bring records up or books or magazines or anything like that?

**Howard:** Probably would have bought a few things like that. I would have bought some cassette tapes more so than records because I had a few of those and then we could play those at various spots at college. There was always somebody who had some music going.

**Michael:** So what kind of music did you like?

**Howard:** Well, I quite liked some of the Beatles stuff and Bee Gees. But interestingly, when I got out to teach in my first year with my year five-six class, I had a music session at the beginning of every day. So we just came into class and I selected something that I thought might be of interest to people in the classical music line. So there's a whole lot of that. And then there are the occasional pop music stuff. And of course kids got to nominate some things that they might like to hear as well. So it would have been at least 10 minutes of that each day. I must have thought that music was a good way to start the day and to get people settled into something, even if it was just settled into their chairs.

So I've always actually liked music, but very varied stuff. Some Rolling Stones stuff I like, but other stuff I hated. In the classical music line it was things like the William Tell Overture and a few other Mozart, Tchaikovsky. But I couldn't say now who was what with all that. I wouldn't say I was big on music, but quite enjoyed it.

**Michael:** And the O'Sullivans—I don't think I ever met them. Did you stay in touch with them at all over the years?

**Howard:** No, not really. Terry was an interesting guy. He was always up for a talk about anything, particularly sport. He worked full time with Australia Post in the city. He was pretty vigorous with supporting sport, which I found out one day when he and his brother took me to Lang Park to watch my first ever match of rugby league. Wests played Norths. I didn't know much about it, but he was sitting next to me. I was sitting actually between him and his brother.

Anyway, things were going quite fine, just normal sort of crowd reactions. At one point I realised just how he had a bit of a volatile approach to barracking. And there was a guy in the crowd, two rows down, who said something that Terry objected to. So Terry told him his objection—I can't remember what it really was, but "what would you know mate," that sort of thing. And the guy turned around said, "what would you know, mate?"

Anyway, before I could do anything, Terry had jumped up and was about to leap across two rows of people and have a go at this guy. And his brother—he knew what was about to happen—so he held Terry back. I sort of thought, wow, that was a near miss really. But Terry wasn't really like that as a human being normally, but footy must have got him going there.

But they were just a really nice couple and I got along quite well with the young girls who just liked the idea of having another brother basically. And I did touch base with them for a little while, probably for a year afterwards, just the odd phone call or something like that. But I think they also had another person staying with them at that point. So they were fairly permanently having college students board with them. I probably got overtaken by whoever friend was next.

## Three Years at Teachers College

**Michael:** So it was a two-year teachers college, right?

**Howard:** It ended up being three. Well, it was two to start with, so I was on a two year scholarship. And then after the first year, they changed over to three years. And if you're already at college and you wanted to go into a third year, you could apply for that, which I did and which I got. Obviously that meant I just spent one more year studying, but also it meant that you got paid a higher rate when you started as well.

**Michael:** Okay, three years at Kedron Park Teachers College. We should probably talk more about that. I mean, that's where you met Mum. And you also had, I gather, quite a few adventures involving the Student Union and probably some involving tennis as well at that point. So let's start with Mum.

Actually, I'll tell you the version of the story which you told me many, many years ago or Mum told me many, many years ago, which is that you had both decided to run for the student council and Mum was going to run for secretary and you were running for president. I don't know where those respective ideas came from. Actually, I'd be pretty interested to know how you had the ideas. But then apparently Mum was like, "Okay, I'm going to go and meet the other candidates." So she's just sort of asking around, "How do I find out where these people are?" And when she asked about you, they said, "Go down to the tennis courts and there'll be a big tall guy bashing a ball backwards and forwards." And that's how the two of you met.

**Howard:** That's right. That's very accurate. And how we came to be nominated was interesting, because Tom and Carmel Hewlett — well, Tom was president of the student union the year before I was, and Carmel was secretary as well the year before. And they met.

**Michael:** I didn't know that.

**Howard:** But Tom was very keen to make sure that another person who was nominating didn't get elected because he thought he would be. And then he sort of knew of me a little bit, but not much really—mainly through my sporting stuff—and he basically enrolled me in the idea. And then Alan Morgan, who was in the same year as me, he was also from Bundaberg as it turns out. So he was nominated as Vice President and he convinced me that I should run as President.

And so it was me as President, Alan Morgan as Vice President, Wendy Ricardo she was then as Secretary, and then a woman, Cathy Pryor, was Assistant Secretary. And she took over as Secretary when Mum went over to Mount Gravatt College where the special needs courses were run. So she was only at Kedron for one year. That was about half of the term in office basically. So she went to Mount Gravatt and did her stuff there. She was still only on a two years scholarship. So she finished two years. Yeah, we just hooked up basically and the rest is history.

**Michael:** Do you remember your first conversation or what was the first actual impression? What actually happened?

**Howard:** Yeah, good question, because I still remember exactly where we were when we had our first conversation. Obviously she'd done her reconnaissance and decided that I looked okay at least. And apparently people seemed to think I was an okay person, so I passed that test without her chatting to me.

But then somebody pointed her out or—I've forgotten exactly how—and she was at the top of a set of stairs and she was leaning over the set of stairs and I was coming up the stairs and said, "Oh, we should probably talk." And I remember what she was wearing—a dress that was like a mixed colour of like a maroon and orange, which sounds interesting.

Yeah, she looked pretty good to me. So I thought not that looks are everything obviously, so yeah, and then so that was just to being elected and we ended up getting elected—I think I got 78% of the vote. So it was a fairly strong win basically.

**Michael:** What was your first conversation about then? Do you remember how long it was? Was it a short conversation? Was it a long conversation?

**Howard:** It was probably relatively short, like it might have been five minutes. And it was more just about this idea that we might get elected. I can't remember the exact detail or anything like that, but it was more just saying hello and knowing who was who, and then probably organised to meet with the other candidates, the others in our team basically. Four or five. And went on from there.

**Michael:** And when did you see her next after that?

**Howard:** It would have been probably daily really, to organise things and what have you. And certainly after the election—the election was probably only about two weeks after that. It might have been a bit longer.

**Michael:** Was this early in the year?

**Howard:** No, it was a changeover thing. They decided that previously they had elections at the start of the year and people hardly knew anybody. So they changed it during that year so that it was about two thirds of the way through that year when I got elected. We got elected around about August, September, something like that. And then we went through to like August, September the following year and it just kept going like that. So Tom and Carmel were only running the show for about seven months.

**Michael:** And then basically you had a year over which you were president and Mum was secretary and so on. Is that right?

**Howard:** Well, she was secretary for three of those months because she went over to Mount Gravatt.

**Michael:** Of course. Yeah. Because of the Mount Gravatt switch. Right. But okay. So how quickly did you realise this was going to be an important relationship?

**Howard:** Probably fairly quickly I would say.

**Michael:** What's fairly quickly? Are we talking about 30 seconds, 30 minutes, 30 days?

**Howard:** Well, the first 30 seconds were quite attractive. I didn't walk away saying, "I'm gonna marry that girl." But yeah, she was very good and alive and well and kicking and all that sort of stuff. A bit of a sense of humour was important. It's just one of those things when you meet somebody that you haven't met before and you go away with a positive vibe. So that's what happened.

And then we had our first unofficial date. Alan Morgan and Cathy Pryor were pretty good friends too. And we went to the pictures one night, the four of us. So that's when we sort of started holding hands and all that sort of stuff. It was good.

**Michael:** When was that? Like a few weeks after the first?

**Howard:** No, I think—well, there's a bit of intrigue, because I had been going out with another girl up till then. And then probably a few weeks after the election, after we teamed up, I sort of dropped her, I suppose, is the way of saying it. So she and I parted company and I had in mind that Wendy and I would hook up. So that's how it turned out. And then we got married two years after that, more or less.

## Getting Engaged and Married

**Michael:** When did you decide to get married and who asked who and how did it all happen?

**Howard:** Well, it sort of got to the point where we both felt like we probably didn't need to ask. But I remember the time when we thought—well, I think Wendy might have even said, "Well, we should probably get ourselves a bit organised." Because it was nearly the end of my year in college, and we knew that we were going to probably get married soon after college finished.

I think it'd be fair to say that we both were aware that we were going to get married. And then just formalised that.

**Michael:** So hang on, let me just see if I've got the timeline right. So you started college in 1969. You met Mum sort of the second half of 1969, September of 1969. And then it would have been early 1971 that you decided to get married. Is that like March or something of 1971 during your third year that you realised that you were going to "get organised" as Mum apparently said? Is that right?

**Howard:** Yeah, those words—I'm not sure whether it was "get organised," but "let's sort this out" or "let's get some dates." So that would have been it.

And that was pretty much it. Wendy had her second year of college obviously over at Mount Gravatt—it was pretty full on because it was special needs stuff which was new, like a totally new thing. And she had to travel from Redcliffe to Mount Gravatt. In the early days of that, but then she got a unit with another person over at Stones Corner, which is not that far from Mount Gravatt. And she had a car then in the second year, an old Hillman. So we got around a bit in that.

And then after she graduated at end of 1970 and was getting paid to teach, she bought a Mini Clubman, actually. And that became our car. Other than that, we used the old Hillman thing that she had and then used her dad's car a bit to get around in when we needed to.

**Michael:** So it must have been pretty hard on both of you if you were over in Banyo and she's over in Stones Corner and Mount Gravatt. I don't know how long it took you to make your way over but it would have been at least an hour, probably quite a bit more.

**Howard:** Yeah, about that. Mostly because it was Wendy—Wendy had the vehicle while I was still at Banyo. It was her mainly doing that travelling when we met up and etcetera. Because in my second year, I was president there from September through to the following September, more or less. And then I got elected into the Australian Student Teachers Union as the state secretary for that. So we had conferences and other events for that during second and third year. And also I was the president of the state Student Teachers Union, which again advocated for all student teachers. That would have been in the changeover years, '70, '71 sort of thing.

**Michael:** So what other stories do you have of that time?

**Howard:** Oh, zillions really.

I suppose a lot of it was around sport being played and all that sort of stuff. Because the college had a senior physical education lecturer who was extremely well organised and positive about students learning all the rules of as many games as possible so that when they went out as teachers they knew all those. So there was an intergroup competition that went on for the entire year in about 12 different sports. And so all groups played in a little mini comp at lunchtimes. So every lunchtime there was something on—volleyball, tennis, rugby union. Not all sports got a Guernsey but we did play rugby league as well. And basketball—I found that I really enjoyed playing basketball and volleyball. And we played hockey as well. They were games that I hadn't played competitively but ended up playing for the college as well in the Brisbane comps—basketball and volleyball and baseball.

So I enjoyed all those, but they took up time as well. So they dropped out soon after. When we got married, you have lots of things that you're doing outside of what you'd done previously with study. But I played a bit of baseball and a bit of basketball after I left college, I think for just one year.

But during the year, there was a big competition on that the sporty types all lapped up. Those that weren't sporty types knew that they were at least learning how to play the various codes of things. So it was good.

## The Hair Scandal

**Michael:** So my understanding is—I'm just going to change the topic a little. There was some scandal associated with Hair, salacious stories appearing in the Courier Mail about the youth of today.

**Howard:** Yeah, well, Wendy was more involved with that than I was actually. So Hair was presented in Sydney actually. And I think Wendy went to go—this is I think before I knew her actually. And she and others, I think they hired a bus and took a bus down to watch Hair. That was probably what the newspaper article was about—was teachers college students bothering to take transport down to Sydney to watch this terrible show. And then there was a follow-up show that one of the students actually produced in Brisbane as well that was not much different to Hair. So probably that happened a year or so later. That was about as scandalous as it got, I think.

**Michael:** Okay. I didn't realise that didn't actually involve you. It was just Mum.

**Howard:** Yeah, that's right. And she had a couple of friends—Susie O'Connor I think was an actress and into all sorts of things theatre-wise, and Wendy designed and produced a whole lot of props for musicals and what have you. So she had an interest in all that.

## Escorting Princess Anne

**Michael:** So there's another story I've heard bits and pieces of which is that somehow you ended up escorting Princess Anne around Brisbane. So can you tell me about how that came to be and then what actually happened?

**Howard:** I'd like to better say that they took a selection process of the most exciting young people to do that, but they didn't do that.

What actually happened was that the Premier's Department—well basically there was a royal tour going on and Princess Anne was part of that tour, and they wanted to have a big fashion show out at a place called the Oasis in South Brisbane that they could invite a whole lot of teenagers and young adults along to, and she would see all the fashion of the day and she'd be excited by all that, etcetera. And so there were a couple of people to escort her. If Wendy hadn't gone to Mount Gravatt, she would have been there. She would have done it with me as well, because what they did was they asked Kedron Park Teachers College principal to nominate two people, a male and female, to do the escorting. And because I was president of the Union and at the time Cathy was secretary of the Union, we got the nod.

And I remember being asked by Norm Anderson. He sort of asked me and said, "But if you don't want to do it, you can get somebody else to do it." And I thought, well, this could be interesting if nothing else. I wasn't a monarchist or anything. I wasn't a monarchist, so it's not like I was doing it because I believed in royalty. But then again, I didn't not do it because I didn't believe in royalty. I've changed my mind since then.

Anyway, so that's how we got selected—by being preselected through another process, I suppose. But the interesting thing that I learnt out of all that was that, well, A, we had to greet her at the front of the Oasis, take her inside, sit down, chat about what we chatted about, and then the fashion parade started. And then after the fashion parade, we took her outside to mingle with all the guests. So there were probably a few hundred young people there. So we took her out and introduced her to some of the people that I already knew.

She had quite a sense of humour, so it was good fun. She didn't object too much to my jokes. So I call them witticisms—some would call them half-witticisms, but anyway.

But the interesting thing that happened, which I haven't disclosed to more than a few people since then—during the whole show, a guy, Desmond Hughes his name was, was the very pronounced fashion director, if you can imagine what that might look and sound like. He was very full of himself, let's say, probably with good effect. About three quarters of the way, nearing the end of the show, he appeared out of nowhere with a great big flourish of a huge cape and he presented the Cloak of Australian Fashion to Princess Anne at her knees.

And I just sort of—yeah, because it was pretty easy to tell that she wasn't really into that stuff. She was doing this because, whatever.

So what happened then was the talking point that I haven't talked about publicly is because I thought it might throw some bad light on her. I'm sitting here, she's sitting next to me on my left, and she picked up the Cloak of Australian Fashion, turned around so I could hear it and gave it to her lady-in-waiting behind her and said, "Put that with the other 12."

**Michael:** Ha!

**Howard:** Which I can sort of imagine—a bit boring. So anyway, I laughed because it was funny. She appreciated that—I thought it was funny, not derogatory. So she must have picked that up by that time.

Anyway, so that's all done, said and done. But afterwards, of course, there was a little press conference. And at the press conference after we had the normal sort of obligatory comments about the day, the guy from the Melbourne Truth, which was a paper that was up for salacious gossip and what have you big time, he asked me, he said, "Oh, there was a moment there where you had a bit of a laugh about something when the princess turned around…"

And I said, "Yes, I must have heard something witty that she said." And I left it at that. And he said, "I saw exactly—what did she say?" I said, "I don't think—I really can't recall exactly what she said, so it's better off not recording anything."

Anyway, he knew that there was a story there that he would have put over the front page of the Truth probably. "Princess Denigrates Australian Fashion" or whatever. Anyway, I'm glad that I sort of intuitively knew it probably wasn't a good idea to answer the question.

**Michael:** Clickbait journalism pre-dates modern social media.

**Howard:** That's right. Exactly right. Anyway, that's my story and I'm sticking to it.

Actually, I thought nowadays would be a time to bring that up again when she was on a visit to Australia and just make contact with Buckingham Palace and say, "By the way, you might remember me, but back in 1970 Princess Anne and I got along really well and I'd like to catch up with her again this time when she's…"

**Michael:** Your 55 year past friend.

**Howard:** That's right.

## Being a Country Kid in the City

**Michael:** Other college stories?

**Howard:** Mostly sport really.

**Michael:** Do you have a sense of—I suppose you had already in fact been away from home quite a bit. So a lot of people have a very distinctive experience, but you'd maybe been a little bit more gradual because you'd been off in Adelaide already and it wasn't such a discrete experience. Was there some sense of—a lot of people just grow a lot when they leave home and they have some sense of becoming an adult because they've got to—did you have that sense of being stretched or…?

**Howard:** I probably did actually because I think one of the things I was going to raise with you as a topic was what it's like to be the country kid in a city. Because I definitely felt that—when you live in the country, you just do whatever you do. And in the city, you don't realise that city people see country people as… Well, they're surprised when they're good at something. Or not surprised, but I can remember a few times people saying to me—with the sporting stuff in particular—things like, "How come you're so good at that? You're from the country." And I said, "Well, I don't know. I'm accepting that I'm okay at things."

And then I got elected as a \[High School\] prefect, which in the day was a leadership position—as a senior prefect as well. And I took on responsibilities to lead teams of people. And so you did learn to be—if you've got some level of responsibility attached to you, then you sort of grew. You got older earlier in a sense.

**Michael:** So what was an example of such a responsibility?

**Howard:** Well, just organising first and second year students to get involved in things like going to sporting events or dances or something like that. I suppose the thing that I got feedback about was really positive—one of the things that prefects were invited to do, were directed to do by the principal of the school, was to help keep the school clean. And so it was our job to round up all the first and second year students and to clean up the grounds from time to time.

And most of the, well in fact all of the other prefects that I know of, did exactly that. They organised for the students to do that menial task. And I sort of felt that was a bit—I couldn't do that. So I actually picked up stuff with them. "Come on guys, let's go and do this."

And so I think I probably, without knowing I was winning brownie points, I think I got quite a few brownie points for that. Because then students would recognise that I didn't need to do that because nobody else did it. But actually picking up papers yourself as a prefect was seen to be below some of them perhaps. So that's just a little snippet. That's something that I remember having to think deliberately about what to do with that. Because it would have been easier just to organise the troops really.

**Michael:** Can you remember any sort of specific time when somebody looked down on you as a country kid or any specific thing where they were just a bit surprised or derogatory?

**Howard:** Yeah, it was nothing really derogatory. It was more the opposite. But I hadn't thought of myself as being somebody who wouldn't be as good as anybody else in an area that I felt I was good at. It's not like I went around comparing myself, but there was a bit of—it was probably one or two people that were a bit jealous of achievements because a couple of the other prefects certainly were but didn't just disclose it really.

There were a few other country kids that came to the school as well, so we got along really well, actually.
