## What Howard Was Most Proud Of From that Early Work

**Michael:** What are you most proud of out of all that work? Or what was striking—even just a good story? A disaster?

**Howard:** Well, when I'd been at schools as a teacher and as a principal, I'd always been really alert to and aware of the need for parents as the primary educators to be fully connected to their school and to their children.

So my mantra was: if you're a child in this schooling system and you saw that your parents—who were one of the biggest contributors to your life—and your teachers—who are the other biggest contributors to your life—if you saw them as not being at all connected or not working with each other, what would you think?

Or conversely, if you saw them really working well together and having a whole lot of positive contributions, would that affect your attitude to school?

And of course, I picked the inarguable, really. You can't argue that students would be less inclined to want to go to school if their parents and teachers were well connected together. Just one big—not so much happy family.

**Michael:** One big dysfunctional family.

**Howard:** Yeah, it could be just a dysfunctional one.

So I would say we had a big attitude change amongst principals of schools and teachers in schools. And the same—different attitude but a similar change—amongst parents who understood they had a role to play that they probably didn't really understand fully before.

There were lots of parents around who gave their kids a lot of support and were well connected with school. So that was just normal. But for a lot of kids that wasn't normal. And so there was just a gradual change.

Mind you, I look at it now—this is forty or fifty years later—it's sort of like, yeah, there's a whole lot more being done, but there's still the same issues, still the same problems within schools, between communities and schools and teachers and parents. Just at a different level.

**Michael:** What do you mean? What's an example?

**Howard:** Well, depending on what you were like as a teacher—for me, when I was a teacher, I invited all my parents to come along for parent-teacher interviews at the same time as the school year started. Which is quite different to parent-teacher interviews about how well you've done the last semester. I did that anyway.

But the whole concept was to transfer some of the responsibility for a child's education away from just the teacher totally, to teacher and parent. And one way of doing that was to invite parents along in the first two weeks of school and say, look, I've got Freddie in my classroom. I don't know him all that well. You know him a hell of a lot better than I do. What sort of things switch him on? What sort of things do I need to know?

And of course, most parents don't think they really know the answer to those questions, but once they start the conversation, they totally understand. And of course, most kids have got idiosyncrasies that can sometimes take you the whole year—or you never ever find out—because sometimes it might be a bit embarrassing for them or not important from their point of view, but it is from the teacher's point of view.

If nothing else, for those people that changed some of their thinking about what they need to do—in that case, that would have helped their child, and that would have helped that child's children. It's sort of like what makes the world go round really—that engagement and understanding and collaboration.

There were some interesting issues. We had issues with head office people from the Education Department—one or two Directors General who were not very happy with the idea because it took the control mechanism out of the way they saw the system should work.

But there was a change. The worst example of that was only in power for the first year or so of my work, and the next person was a lot more convivial with that idea.

And what I discovered during all that time was that the facilitative role of the teacher was really the same role I needed to play in the work I was doing. So it was getting people to think differently about stuff.

I remember there were many days when at 7:30 or 8:00 in the morning I would have a meeting down at a school somewhere with teacher aides and cleaners to just find out from them a little bit about some of the issues. Then I'd go have a chat to the principal. Then I might have an appointment at head office with the regional director, and then I might have a meeting with the minister for education that afternoon.

So that happened more than once. And you've got to figure out how to manage conversations at different levels.

## The Same Process, Different Contexts

**Michael:** I know—probably seven or eight years after all of this, you were working often as a consultant and you used to get me to type up reports and do data entry and stuff. But I remember I learned a lot as a consequence of doing that.

One of the most interesting things—and I think you articulated this to me, but it was also very obvious what was going on—you'd go and do some consulting job for an organization, and the top management would be worried about some issue. You would go and talk to everybody about that issue, and you eventually realized that what was going on was top management couldn't talk really all that well about the issue because all those people are a bit scared of them. They're a bit worried about the relationship.

And very often whatever the actual problem was, it would have prevented them from being able to have the conversation. So your job seemed to be to establish all those relationships, get people to trust you, get people to actually talk to you, then you sort of write it all down and you can show it in a non-confrontational way to the people purportedly running the show.

And at that point, what they do with that information is to some extent up to them, but all of a sudden they have this knowledge. It's just so funny to hear you describing yourself doing this in a school context—what I later saw you doing was really very similar.

**Howard:** Yeah, that's right. And I found some interesting times when—after I got to know people, when I was in the business area doing consulting work with businesses or government departments and I'd got the trust of people—they would often have conversations on the side to say, how did you learn to do this? What's your training for all this?

And eventually, when I got to know people really quite well, I'd say, well, very similar process to what I used to use with my year three class at Clontarf Primary School.

And of course you had to be careful—people need to understand you were sort of half joking, but not. It really was the same stuff, just different language.

**Michael:** Yeah.

**Howard:** Not even different purposes—the purposes were the same.

One of the processes I really enjoyed doing—you talked about the one-to-one stuff before. I remember talking to Bill Mitchell from Australia Post. He had been intrigued by my approach before he had the capacity to actually fund me. He said, when I get some money, I'm going to get you to come and do that with my future leaders.

I said, okay, Bill, I'm happy to do that, provided you're happy with the idea of funding one-to-one time between me and each of these people, three times at least during the whole process. Because that's a fairly expensive exercise—that's four to five hours with twenty people, three times. That's not just a couple hundred bucks.

So Bill agreed. And I saw that as being—you just sit down at a coffee table or a cafe somewhere and the conversation might start off with: what are we doing here?

And they'd say, "well, what do you mean, what are we doing here?"

I said, "well, why? What are you doing here?"

"Well, I'm part of the program."

We'd be smiling—I knew it was a bit of a joke, but not quite.

I said, "but have you chosen to be here?"

"Yeah, yeah, yeah."

But then some people would say, "well, not really—my boss asked me to be here."

So you'd have a different conversation with them compared to the others.

And then eventually you get all that data, collate it all, and I'd take it back to a workshop—not all of it was ever disclosed at a workshop—but I'd get people to sit in pairs or trios and say: of all that data, what's the most interesting thing for you? And then what's the most interesting thing you think Australia Post needs to do about that?

So I'd say, you think I'm going to go through a nine month program on leadership—well I am, but I'm not telling you what the content of that's going to be at the moment. You're going to tell me, based on what you've already told me and what your collective wisdom is about this stuff. We've got a process design, but the content design is still to come.

And of course, anybody who sat down and thought about it for a bit of time would know that most people come up with similar things when asked these questions. And I think the important thing is to be asking questions that don't actually have defined answers.

So my skill, if anything, was to not pretend to have an answer, but have them disclose what the answer is, and then we work on all that.

I did a lot of that with schools in the parent participation area as well—all that appreciative inquiry stuff. What's the most important good thing that the school does at the moment? And how do you contribute to that? How do students contribute to that? Not get into problems to start with at all. Just get into what we already know.

Because David Cooperrider, with his appreciative inquiry, knew that people are unlikely to want to change what they do and how they do it until they've had their current work recognized.

**Michael:** Hmm.

**Howard:** So that question was important—how are you contributing to that?—because they're already more engaged. People say, oh, well, I don't really do much. Well, I'm sure you do something, so just tell me one thing.

And by the time you're finished half an hour later, there's three or four really important things. And out of those, one thing might pop up, which might be: I think I'm doing well at this part, but I reckon I need to be better at that.

So: which part of that do you reckon you need to be better at? Are there any others around that you think could benefit from that?

So you start building up your agenda for what the actual content of the program is. And by the time you finish that first workshop—the outset workshop—you actually had an agenda for the whole year, give or take a few gaps for things that arise.

And people were really buoyed by the idea that they had actually come up with all this. Because I'd then say, you thought I was going to tell you about this—you're telling me now, and I agree that is stuff. There might be a few other new things that we haven't thought of yet, so let's leave space for that.

And that would make them feel more confident. And those who were there because their boss had told them to be there came out of that process with a different perspective on what their role might be. Previously their role was to do what they thought their boss wanted them to do. Anyway, enough of that—it's a whole week's worth of downloading on that one.

## Where Did This Approach Come From?

**Michael:** How did you get into that? I mean, some of this you got intuitively with your grade threes. But a bunch of it is also actual process that you've picked up along the way.

**Howard:** I don't know, really.

**Michael:** It's the kind of thing I can easily have imagined your dad doing little bits and pieces of, and maybe actually your mum as well. She was a different kind of person, but she was quite a systematic person. I always had that sense.

**Howard:** Yeah. I sort of tend to go to the end first. So I would have said at some point: what's the end that we want here?

When I was in the student union, running meetings there, it would be: so what's the purpose of being here? What's the end product?

And it might be agreement about such and such, or it might be about developing new facilities. So then you sort of went back from there. What sort of information do we need in order to make a good decision about that? Who are the key people involved with that?

So I would do that intuitively. And I realized later on when I did my masters—my MBA and my M in Ed Admin—that there was a lot of organizational psychology around all this. So that helped fine-tune it.

I wrote a booklet on Collaborative Action Learning. I'd come across the Action Learning stuff, which was about identifying a problem and then looking forward to what that might look like as a solution, then how do you fill the gaps between the two.

But I changed it around a little bit to say not so much that you had a problem, but you had an issue or an opportunity or a question that was unanswered. I mean, it's a scientific process, really. What work do you do? Why do you do that? Well, because I'm inquisitive about this, or curious about this.

Like open science, for example—why would you bother doing that when a lot of other people aren't doing it?

So it's not unusual to have—for me, it was not so much the content of what the outcome might be, but almost the process or the tentative solution to something. It may not end up being the end product solution.

When we did the Samford Futures process here in Samford, I just used the same mechanism for that. The questions we asked people, one to ones—same stuff. Slightly different language probably, but the same process.

**Michael:** I remember you saying you basically tried to talk to every single person in Samford.

**Howard:** Well, we certainly gave them the opportunity to talk to us. And we ended up—out of 4,000 households, I think we had 800 households that responded in some specific way.

Usually if you're taking a survey of something and you get better than 4%, you're doing pretty good. A lot of people make judgements based on that. In our case, we had a whole lot more than that.

But it took us three years, which was—people had to be quite patient really in Samford. The early parts were workshops held in the little neighbourhood areas, and then a wrap-up workshop. Each of those workshops identified priorities that seemed important, and each of those priorities got volunteers attached to them to go through another process of unfolding.

It's almost like a process within a process, and my job was to make sure people were doing that properly and they weren't falling into the trap of just being only expert-led.

Sure, some expert leading—but who says what the expert is? For new things it might be somebody's intuitive thought about something, and if you don't have the opportunity for that intuition to come forward, you miss out on all that and you just rehash all the stuff we already knew. **Michael:** But it'll happen anyway.
