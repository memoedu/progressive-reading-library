### I – For most of human history, the busiest people were the lowest status.

Aristotle said we work so that we can have leisure, and that without it no one becomes wise.

Seneca said that the busiest people were often the least accomplished, because they spent their days on everything except their own lives.

Leisure used to be a mark of status while busyness was a mark of obligation. Busyness belonged to slaves, serfs, and laborers who worked from sunrise to sundown.

At some point in the last century, that flipped.

“I’m so busy” became a badge of honor, even when most people knew they were working on nothing meaningful, with nothing to show except time invested in someone else’s vision.

### II - The historical normal is to be self-employed, working a job was a temporary glitch.

> Specialization is in fact only a fancy form of slavery wherein the ‘expert’ is fooled into accepting his slavery by making him feel that in return he is in a socially and culturally preferred, ergo, highly secure, lifelong position.  
>   
> – Buckminster Fuller

During Industrialization, everything became a factory.

Schools included.

The sole purpose of a school system is to prepare children for the jobs of its time, and the jobs at the time were factory jobs - and I can’t help but wonder how education is going to look when the jobs of our time drastically change, or just become meaningless. I believe we are heading into a Second Renaissance, but I’ll save that for later.

Bells marked the start and end of the day. Kids are grouped by age, like batches. Subjects are split up, like an assembly line. Grades and tests were quality checks. Students were rewarded for doing what they were told, on someone else’s schedule, toward someone else’s goal.

Everyone reading this likely went through this system.

And since the mind is shaped through conditioning, and our identity latches onto that which provides us security and comfort, we are all in some way unconsciously tied to a style of work that was only an experiment.

The modern concept of a job is only 100-200 years old. Homo sapiens are 300k years old. Working 9-5, being paid a wage, and having your worth attached to your title is not normal, and it definitely isn’t beneficial to how our brains are wired. Life quickly becomes mechanical.

Work used to mean _owning_ your work.

In 1800, most Americans were farmers, farm workers, or ran a family business. The word “job” meant _one piece of work_ until the late 1700s. It did not mean 40+ years of your life.

The Renaissance ideal was the “universal man” who did many things well. Craftsmen worked under a guild, progressing from apprentice to journeyman to master. “Masterpiece” first meant the work you made to prove you were a master.

I believe history is repeating itself, as it always does, and we are entering the era of the craftsman. A craftsman’s worth was determined by _how_ they used their tools. Today a laptop, the internet, and AI are cheap tools anyone can own. The craft they pursue, their vision for their work, and obsession over the details is what makes the difference. Anyone can build anything, but attention is finite, so only those who treat their work as their life’s work, pouring their heart and soul into what they do will rise above.

In _The Third Wave,_ Alvin Toffler had a similar theory.

He called it the Electronic Cottage, which he saw as the third wave of work.

The first wave was farming, people mostly made what they used. The second wave was the factory, which split people into producers and consumers. The third wave is information and computers, where work moves back home. We once again become _creators._

His argument is that a lot of work was “cottage industry,” where families spun, wove, and made goods at home. The factory pulled work out of the home and into one big building. He predicted that computers would reverse that and bring work home again (I’m sitting in my office, in my underwear, at 10:09 am, writing this, by the way).

> During the First Wave most people consumed what they themselves produced. They were neither producers nor consumers in the usual sense. They were instead what might be called prosumers.

I believe that’s what a creator is. Not a content creator per se, although social media is where a creator would find their audience in today’s world, until social media is replaced with another form of media.

A friend I have online sells cotton apparel because he is against polyester clothing. I can get my apparel from him. Another online friend teaches philosophy, I can get my education from him. Another sells software. Another sells and ships baked goods. So on and so forth. I can pursue my interests and find a wedge in a digital tribe that I can contribute best to - we call this a “niche.” The creator economy has had its ups and downs, but at the end of the day, I see it as a digital society where we can learn, earn, create, and contribute to each other’s lives. That is the modern renaissance lifestyle.

Marshal McLuhan called this the _global village_ where our specialist and fragmented civilization begins to retribalize.

That means the work you choose must be something you have a deep interest in, and allows for near-infinite upward movement.

All of these things are arguing the importance of one thing - _leverage_.

> Leverage is getting more of a result per hour of effort, because something other than your time keeps doing the work after you stop.

Creative thinking, entrepreneurship (freedom of work), and experience are necessary pre-requisites to leverage. It is the only way to compete in a world of ‘everyone can do anything.’ Committing to anything but your life’s work is a death sentence.

At a job, the employee mindset you adopt conditions your mind to be anti-leverage. And most people have this way of thinking deeply ingrained in them.

That’s leverage.

You have posts that generate traffic, and you have a product that sells while you sleep. Both are in alignment with your life’s work.
