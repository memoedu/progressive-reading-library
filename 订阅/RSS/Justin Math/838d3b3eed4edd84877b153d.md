We expected solving the Riemann hypothesis to immediately deliver a colossal breakthrough in mathematical understanding. Instead, we have an enormous, impenetrable machine-verified proof.

That’s the big reveal. Riemann was a smokescreen for a bigger monster problem lurking in the background: sprawling complexity.

And you know what? I’ve never been more excited about the future of mathematics.

The thing about hard problems is that they’re heads of a Hydra. Every time you slay one, more pop up. That’s how progress works. Solutions beget opportunities.

So take a long walk, get your grief out, eat a pint of ice cream or whatever it is you do when you’re feeling let down after big expectations… and strap in for an even BIGGER mathematical challenge:

**Characterizing what it means for a proof or program to be human-cognitively irreducible, and developing techniques & tools for automatically simplifying it toward that limit.**

How do you take sprawling yet functional complexity and automatically compress it down into the essential nuggets of insight?

That’s the problem of the decade. It’s the bottleneck for using AI to build new mathematical theory, the bottleneck for using AI to build production software for novel applications… it’s increasingly the bottleneck for using AI to accelerate innovation anywhere, really.

For the first time in God-knows-how-long, the next great frontier in mathematics finally coincides with the biggest challenge facing technologists. Mathematicians seek elegant proofs. Technologists seek maintainable systems. It’s the same holy grail: cognitive compression.

Forget a million-dollar mathematical prize. This is a trillion-dollar problem that industry actually cares about.

It may be too early to say this, but I’ll say it anyway: the solution of the Riemann hypothesis, and the enormous challenge of making its sprawling proof human-comprehensible, may mark the start of the next golden age – one whose breakthroughs pay dividends far beyond mathematics itself.
